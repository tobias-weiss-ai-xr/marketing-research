Abstract. The diffusion of digital technologies has shifted brand marketing from mass
communication toward individualized consumer dialogue, and personalization has become a
central capability for competitive differentiation. This paper examines how global brands
design and implement personalization strategies in digital marketing environments, using
L'Oréal, the world's largest cosmetics group, as a case. Drawing on a qualitative, single-case
design and secondary data, including corporate reports, press releases, and peer-reviewed
research, the study analyzes L'Oréal's personalization initiatives across four dimensions:
data infrastructure and artificial intelligence (AI)-based diagnostics, augmented reality (AR)
virtual try-on services, omnichannel customer relationship management, and personalized
content delivered through influencers and social platforms. The findings indicate that
L'Oréal's approach rests on proprietary technology ownership, systematic integration of AI
and AR into the consumer journey, and organizational structures that connect data science
with brand management. The case also reveals tensions involving privacy, algorithmic bias,
and digital exclusion that condition the effectiveness of personalization. The study
contributes an integrated framework that links AI capabilities to personalization touchpoints
and brand outcomes, and offers managerial guidance for consumer goods firms seeking to
scale personalization without eroding consumer trust.
Keywords: personalization, digital marketing, artificial intelligence, brand strategy, L'Oréal
1. Introduction
The relationship between brands and consumers has been redrawn by digital technologies. Where
firms once addressed anonymous mass audiences, they now operate inside dense networks of search
engines, social platforms, mobile applications, and online stores, and each interaction within these
networks leaves behavioural data that can be collected, analysed, and acted upon almost
instantaneously [1]. This shift has made personalization one of the most consequential capabilities in
contemporary marketing, because the same infrastructure that carries brand messages also records
how individual consumers respond to them. Survey evidence suggests that 71% of consumers now
expect personalized interactions, that 76% become frustrated when firms fail to deliver them, and
that companies excelling at personalization generate 40% more revenue from these activities than
average players [2]. At the same time, artificial intelligence (AI) has lowered the cost of tailoring

products, messages, and services to individuals, prompting predictions that both marketing strategies
and customer behaviours will be restructured around machine intelligence [3].
Few industries illustrate this transformation as clearly as beauty and cosmetics, a category in
which product fit is highly individual yet traditionally difficult to verify before purchase. L'Oréal,
the world's largest beauty company, has positioned itself explicitly as a Beauty Tech enterprise that
uses science and technology to craft beauty experiences with unprecedented levels of
personalization [4]. In 2024, the group recorded sales of EUR 43.48 billion, of which 28.2% were
generated through e-commerce channels, and reported more than 110 million uses of its Beauty Tech
services [4]. These figures suggest that personalization at L'Oréal is not a peripheral experiment but
a structural component of the business model, which makes the company an instructive setting for
studying how personalization strategy is actually built.
The purpose of this paper is to examine how a global consumer brand operationalizes
personalization in a digital marketing environment, and with what consequences. Three questions
guide the analysis: first, through which digital touchpoints does L'Oréal implement personalization;
second, which technological and organizational capabilities support these initiatives; and third, what
tensions and risks accompany them. The study adopts a qualitative, single-case design based on
secondary data. The remainder of the paper reviews the literature on personalization and AI in
marketing, describes the methodology, presents the case background and the findings, and closes
with a discussion of theoretical and managerial implications, limitations, and directions for future
research.
2. Literature review
2.1. Personalization in digital marketing
Personalization lacks a single accepted definition. In an early conceptual analysis, Vesanen
described it as the adaptation of the marketing mix to an individual customer based on information
the firm holds about that customer, and organized the field around the execution of personalized
marketing, the personalized marketing output, and the value created for both the customer and the
marketer [5]. For customers, the expected benefits include a better preference match, improved
service and communication, and the experience of being treated as an individual; the corresponding
sacrifices involve privacy risk, spam exposure, invested time, and possible extra fees [5]. For the
firm, personalization promises higher response rates, loyalty, satisfaction, and differentiation from
competitors, offset by technology investments and the risk of irritating customers with poorly judged
marketing output [5].
Digital environments have amplified both sides of this value equation. Digital marketing spans
touchpoints across search, display, social, and mobile channels, and rests on data infrastructures that
permit fine-grained targeting and near-real-time optimization [1]. Practitioner research indicates that
the commercial stakes are material: faster-growing firms derive a considerably larger share of their
revenue from personalization than slower-growing peers, and leading organizations treat
personalization as an enterprise-wide capability supported by dedicated talent, agile operating
models, and fit-for-purpose marketing technology, rather than as a narrow promotional tactic [2].
Personalization, in this reading, is simultaneously a consumer-facing experience and an
organizational production system.

2.2. Artificial intelligence as an enabler of personalization
AI has moved personalization from rule-based targeting toward machine-driven customization. A
strategic framework proposed by Huang and Rust distinguishes mechanical AI, which automates
repetitive marketing functions; thinking AI, which processes data to arrive at decisions; and feeling
AI, which analyses interactions and human emotions [6]. In their scheme, thinking AI carries the
core burden of personalization at the marketing action stage, mechanical AI underpins
standardization and automation, and feeling AI supports relationalization [6]. Davenport and
colleagues similarly argue that AI will transform marketing strategy across intelligence levels and
task types, while cautioning that it performs best when it augments rather than replaces human
managers [3].
Firm-level evidence is accumulating. Work on personalized engagement marketing describes how
AI curates otherwise overwhelming choice for individual consumers, narrows information, and
effectively reorders the customer journey in both developed and developing markets [7]. A study of
US-listed firms finds that a stronger AI focus, measured from corporate filings, is associated with
improvements in net profitability, net operating efficiency, and returns on marketing-related
investment [8]. Taken together, this stream suggests that AI-powered personalization functions
simultaneously as a marketing instrument that shapes consumer experience and as a driver of firm
performance, which is why global brands increasingly treat it as a board-level topic rather than a
channel tactic.
2.3. Personalization, consumer response, and brand outcomes
Consumer research has begun to document how personalized digital experiences translate into
brand-level outcomes. In the beauty category, virtual try-on (VTO) technology built on augmented
reality (AR) allows shoppers to preview cosmetics on their own faces before purchase; survey
evidence from Indonesia shows that AR content quality and service quality raise purchase intention
through the mediation of brand trust, indicating that the experiential and relational effects of AR are
at least as important as its informational function [9]. AI-based diagnostics extend personalization
from simulation to measurement. An algorithm developed by L'Oréal researchers, which grades
facial signs from selfie images, was validated against assessments by 50 dermatologists across a
diverse sample of 1,041 women in the United States and achieved strong correlations for most signs
[10]. Related conceptual work argues that the next generation of AI marketing applications should
incorporate artificial empathy, so that automated interactions do not feel cold to consumers and can
bridge the gap in affective and social customer experience [11].
Personalization also operates through human intermediaries. Online influencer marketing
leverages the follower networks, personal positioning, communication content, and follower trust
accumulated by influencers to make brand communication feel individual and authentic [12].
Because followers perceive influencer content as personal recommendation rather than as
advertising, this channel complements algorithmic personalization in ways that pure brand
messaging cannot easily replicate [12]. For a multi-brand group, influencers therefore function as a
human personalization layer that can be tuned by brand, segment, and platform.

The literature is nonetheless candid about the costs of personalization. AI-driven marketing raises
policy questions concerning privacy, bias, and ethics, and these questions intensify when the data

algorithmic bias that may lead to exclusionary recommendations, manipulative personalization
practices, reduced consumer autonomy, and the digital exclusion of consumers with low digital
literacy or limited access to technology as salient ethical concerns [13]. These findings echo the
older insight that consumer sacrifices, above all privacy risk, are built into personalization from the

Three observations motivate the present study. First, the literature remains fragmented between
firm-level analyses of AI capability and performance [6-8] and consumer-level studies of specific
technologies such as AR try-on or conversational agents [9-11]. Second, large corporate
implementations that span diagnostics, VTO, customer relationship management (CRM), and
influencer communication are rarely examined as an integrated system, even though consumers
encounter them as a single brand experience. Third, the beauty industry, where product-individual fit
matters exceptionally but is hard to verify, is underrepresented in mainstream marketing scholarship
despite being an early adopter of AR and AI. This paper addresses these gaps through a case study of
L'Oréal.
3. Methodology
The study employs an exploratory, qualitative, single-case design. Such a design is suitable when a
contemporary phenomenon has to be examined within its real organizational context, when the
researcher relies on documentary rather than experimental evidence, and when the boundaries
between phenomenon and context are blurred. L'Oréal was selected as a revelatory case because it is
the global market leader in beauty, has articulated an explicit Beauty Tech strategy for several years,
and discloses unusually rich information about its digital operations in audited and official
publications [4].
Data were drawn from four types of secondary sources. First, the group's 2024 annual report,
annual results release, and investor communications provided financial and organizational facts [4].
Second, official press releases documented strategic decisions, notably the acquisition of the AR
specialist ModiFace [14]. Third, peer-reviewed publications authored or co-authored by the
company's own researchers documented the scientific validation of its diagnostic algorithms [10].
Fourth, industry research on personalization practice supplied comparative context [2]. The
documents were read iteratively and coded inductively around four themes that emerged from the
literature: data infrastructure and diagnostics, virtual try-on, omnichannel CRM, and personalized
content. Triangulation across source types was used to reduce single-source bias, and all quantitative
figures reported below were taken from audited or official corporate disclosures.
Two limitations of the design are acknowledged at the outset. Secondary data cannot fully capture
internal decision processes, and a single case does not support statistical generalization. The aim of
the analysis is analytical generalization to concepts and frameworks rather than to populations, and
the findings should be read accordingly.
4. Case background
L'Oréal, headquartered in Clichy, France, has been devoted to beauty for more than a century and
today sells the products of 37 international brands in more than 150 countries [4]. In 2024, the group
achieved sales of EUR 43.48 billion, representing growth of 5.1% like-for-like and 5.6% reported,
reached a record operating margin of 20.0%, and employed more than 90,000 people worldwide [4].
The portfolio spans four divisions, namely Consumer Products, Luxe, Dermatological Beauty, and

5.1. Data infrastructure and AI-based diagnostics
The foundation of the strategy is the conversion of consumer data into individual insight. E-
commerce transactions, brand applications, loyalty programmes, and diagnostic sessions generate
behavioural and physiological signals that feed recommendation engines operating across the
group's brand portfolio. Consistent with the thinking AI role is to process data into decisions [6],
these systems move beyond demographic segments toward individual-level product routines, so that
two consumers visiting the same brand site can receive different routines, sequences, and offers
based on their histories and diagnostic results.
Skin diagnostics illustrate the approach most clearly. Tools of the SkinConsult AI type analyse a
selfie, grade several facial signs automatically, and return a personalized skincare routine together
with product recommendations. The scientific credibility of such diagnostics has been tested
formally rather than asserted: in a cross-sectional study of 1,041 women in the United States, the
algorithm's gradings correlated strongly with the assessments of 50 dermatologists for five of seven
facial signs, although agreement was weaker for pigmentation signs on the darkest skin tones [10].
Validation of this kind serves marketing directly, because a recommendation grounded in a clinically
benchmarked measurement is more defensible than one derived from a short questionnaire, and it
simultaneously exposes an inclusion problem, since weaker accuracy for darker skin tones implies
that some consumer groups receive systematically less reliable personalization [10]. The company's
own scientists publish this validation work in dermatology journals, which turns a technical quality-
control exercise into a public trust signal.

5.2. Augmented reality virtual try-on across the consumer journey
The second cluster restores a version of try-before-you-buy to digital channels. ModiFace's
technology allows consumers to try make-up, hair colour, and related products virtually on their own
faces, and it has been deployed on brand websites, e-retailer platforms, social media, and in-store
screens [14]. For an experience good whose fit is normally verified at a cosmetics counter, AR try-
on reduces the perceived risk of online purchase and lowers the cost of exploring alternatives.
Survey evidence from an independent market indicates that the quality of such AR experiences
raises purchase intention through the mediation of brand trust, which suggests that virtual try-on
builds a relational asset and not merely a convenience [9].
Scale changes the economics of this touchpoint. With more than 110 million uses of Beauty Tech
services reported in 2024 [4], the try-on layer doubles as a data-collection mechanism: every session
records preferences, shades, and abandonments, and these signals feed back into recommendation
models, closing the learning loop envisioned in the personalized engagement marketing literature
[7]. Virtual sampling may also substitute for physical testers, which aligns the personalization
agenda with the group's sustainability positioning and reduces waste in the product discovery
process [4]. In this sense, AR try-on is simultaneously a conversion tool, a research instrument, and
a sustainability practice.
5.3. Omnichannel CRM and loyalty personalization
The third cluster manages the relationship over time. With 28.2% of consolidated sales generated
online [4], the group maintains consumer relationships across owned e-commerce sites, pure
players, retailer platforms, brand applications, and physical counters. Mechanical AI automates
routine interactions such as replenishment reminders, service messages, and loyalty
communications, while CRM systems consolidate purchase histories and service records into a
single consumer view that follows the individual across channels [6, 7]. The strategic intent,
articulated in the group's reporting, is a harmonized and personalized experience regardless of where
the consumer meets the brand [4]. The case evidence suggests that omnichannel consistency is what
converts isolated personalized moments into a durable relationship, because diagnostics performed
in one channel reappear as recommendations and services in another.
5.4. Personalized content, influencers, and social commerce
The fourth cluster concerns what consumers actually see and hear. Content is tailored to segments,
platforms, and occasions, and influencers act as trust-bearing intermediaries whose
recommendations are perceived by followers as personal advice rather than as advertising [12]. The
group's position as one of the world's largest advertisers gives it the scale to combine algorithmic
targeting with human-mediated authenticity [4], and its conversational services increasingly
incorporate affective design considerations of the kind described in the artificial empathy literature
[11]. This mix matters because purely algorithmic personalization can feel surveillant, whereas
influencer-mediated personalization feels chosen; the two mechanisms therefore complement rather
than substitute for each other.

5.5. Organizational enablers
The case highlights three organizational enablers behind the four touchpoint clusters. The first is in-
house ownership of core technology: by acquiring ModiFace rather than renting equivalent services,
L'Oréal secured control of its AR and AI stack and embedded it in a Digital Services Factory that
serves all brands, which shortens deployment cycles and keeps consumer data within the group [14].
The second is talent: approximately 8,000 digital, technology, and data specialists complement the
marketing organization and anchor the required skills internally [4]. The third is the governance
bridge between marketing and research, exemplified by validation studies published by the
company's scientists in peer-reviewed journals [10]. These observations align with firm-level
evidence that AI orientation is associated with superior profitability and marketing returns [8], and
with the argument that AI creates most value when it augments human marketers rather than
replaces them [3].
5.6. Tensions: privacy, bias, and digital exclusion
The same mechanisms that create value generate risks. Facial images constitute sensitive biometric
data, so diagnostic and try-on services operate under strict data-protection expectations, and
transparency as well as consent are preconditions of consumer trust in AI-driven journeys [13].
Algorithmic bias is a second tension: the weaker diagnostic agreement for darker skin tones
documented in validation research [10] echoes the concern that personalization systems may serve
some consumer groups less well than others, which is ethically problematic and commercially self-
defeating for a group whose growth increasingly depends on diverse markets [4, 13]. Third, AI-
driven personalization may reduce consumer autonomy through overly curated choice or exclude
consumers with limited digital literacy and technology access [13]. Within the case, these tensions
are treated not as external criticism but as boundary conditions that the firm actively manages
through scientific validation, inclusive design programmes, and published commitments to
responsible AI [4, 10].
6. Discussion
Returning to the research questions, the findings show that L'Oréal operationalizes personalization
through four interlocking touchpoint clusters, namely diagnostics, virtual try-on, omnichannel CRM,
and personalized content, rather than through any single tool. The underlying capability combines
data infrastructure, proprietary AI and AR technology, and organizational structures that connect
data science with brand management. The tensions accompanying these initiatives confirm the older
conceptual insight that personalization is a managed balance between relevance and intrusion, in
which consumer benefits and sacrifices are two sides of the same coin [5].
The case extends prior theory in three ways. First, it shows concretely how mechanical, thinking,
and feeling AI map onto the operations of a global brand: automation dominates CRM and service
communication, analysis dominates diagnostics and targeting, and affective design is emerging in
conversational services [6, 11]. Second, it connects the firm-level and consumer-level strands of the
literature, because the group's sustained margin performance alongside documented consumer-
response mechanisms such as AR-driven brand trust [9] is consistent with the claim that AI focus
improves firm performance and marketing returns [8]. Third, it demonstrates that scientific
validation can function as a marketing asset, since a dermatology study becomes a trust signal for

For managers, three lessons emerge from the case. Owning core personalization technology, as in
the ModiFace acquisition, secures strategic control, brand-wide scalability, and data governance in
one step [14]. Investing in formal validation addresses the credibility and inclusion problems that
undermine many consumer-facing AI services, and publishing the results converts a cost into a
differentiator [10]. Finally, privacy protection, bias monitoring, and attention to digitally excluded
consumers should be designed into personalization programmes from the outset rather than
retrofitted after failures, because trust is the currency that makes repeated personalized interaction
acceptable to consumers [13].
7. Conclusion
This paper has examined how brand personalization is strategized and implemented in a digital
marketing environment through a case study of L'Oréal. The evidence shows a coherent architecture
in which AI-based diagnostics, AR virtual try-on, omnichannel CRM, and influencer-mediated
content are combined under an explicit Beauty Tech strategy, supported by proprietary technology, a
large digital workforce, and scientific validation of consumer-facing algorithms. The company's
2024 results, including a record operating margin of 20% and 28.2% of sales transacted online,
indicate that this architecture is commercially viable at global scale [4].
The study contributes an integrated framework that links AI capability layers, personalization
touchpoints, and brand outcomes under ethical boundary conditions, and it illustrates the value of
triangulating corporate, technical, and consumer-level evidence when studying digital marketing
strategy. Several limitations remain: the analysis rests on secondary data and a single case, the most
recent initiatives have not yet produced independently measurable consumer outcomes, and no
causal claims about individual consumer behaviour are made. Future research should test the
proposed framework quantitatively, compare L'Oréal with digital-native beauty brands that lack
physical distribution, and examine how generative AI reshapes the creation of personalized content
at brand scale.
