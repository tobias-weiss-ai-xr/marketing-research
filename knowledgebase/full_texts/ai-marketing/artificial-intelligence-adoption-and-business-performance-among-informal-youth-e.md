Abstract

Informal enterprises absorb most youth employment in Sub -Saharan Africa, yet it remains unclear whether
artificial intelligence (AI) improves their performance or widens the digital divide. This study extends the
Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) by linking perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU) to
AI adoption and to financial (FP), marketing (MP) and operational (OP) performance among informal youth
entrepreneurs in Zambia. Survey data from owner -operators aged 18 –35 were analysed  with confirmatory
factor analysis and covariance-based structural equation modelling, using a cluster bootstrap because the data
file contained paired, near-identical records. PEOU, but not PU, predicted AI adoption. AI adoption was positively
associated with all three performance dimensions, most strongly with operational performance. PEOU had
significant indirect effects on performance through AI adoption; however, when we added direct paths from
PEOU to performance, these indirect effects were no longer  significant, and PEOU was directly associated with
marketing and operational performance. Measurement quality was modest, particularly for the AI adoption
scale, and model fit was below conventional benchmarks, so the findings should be read as explorator y. In
informal, resource-constrained settings, usability, rather than perceived usefulness, appears to be the gateway
to AI adoption, and AI's performance benefits are associated with how easily entrepreneurs can use it.

INTRODUCTION
In Zambia, most young workers earn their living in enterprises that have never registered
with a tax authority, filed a formal business plan or obtained institutional credit ( International
Labour Organisation [ILO], 2023 ; Zambia Statistics Agency, 2023 ). These entrepreneurs may be
among the hardest for national digital -transformation strategies to reach, because many digital -
inclusion channels rely on formal registration and credit systems from which informal operators
are largely absent ( Begazo et al., 2023 ). Artificial intelligence (AI) is quickly changing how
businesses of all sizes compete, learn and sustain performance. As a family of technologies that can
perceive, reason, and automate tasks that traditionally require human cognition, AI offers benefits
beyond simple automation, extending into strategic decision-making (Shepherd & Majchrzak, 2022;
Fossen et al., 2024). In entrepreneurship theory, AI is viewed as an external enabler that supports
opportunity recognition, market sensing, customer engagement and operational workflows
(Cristofaro & Bañón-Gomis, 2026; OECD, 2025a). Research on digital technology adoption in small
and medium -sized enterprises (SMEs) similarly links advanced digital tools, including machine
learning and generative AI, to lower costs, faster customer response, higher sales and more
customised services (Fossen et al., 2024; UNCTAD, 2025).
However, this literature has largely examined formal SMEs in high -income contexts or
structured enterprises with systematic business processes, leaving a gap in understanding how

these mechanisms operate in informal, resource -constrained settings ( Shepherd & Majchrzak,

registered SMEs, where organisational routines, workforce skills and governance systems support
technology integration ( Alisyahbana et al., 2025 ; OECD, 2025a ; UNCTAD, 2025 ). Informal
enterprises, characterised by low registration rates, limited access to finance and inconsistent
management practices, face distinct constraints that may hinder AI adoption ( Begazo et al., 2023;
Nsokolo et al., 2025 ). Research on digital technology in informal micro -businesses in Zambia and
the wider region shows that basic tools such as mobile money, social media and simple apps are
widely used for transactions and marketing, whereas AI -specific tools such as genera tive text or
image models and automated analytics remain largely unexplored ( Aryeh-Adjei et al., 2025 ;
Sikalumbi et al., 2025). Evidence is also lacking on whether AI adoption in these contexts translates
into tangible performance outcomes, because adoption alone does not guarantee effective use or
value capture (Fossen et al., 2024; OECD, 2025a).
Understanding AI adoption among informal youth entrepreneurs matters for three reasons.
First, young people make up the majority of Zambia's population; youth unemployment and
underemployment remain high, and informal enterprises provide most youth employm ent (ILO,
2023; Zambia Statistics Agency, 2023); therefore, productivity in this segment affects employment
and poverty reduction. Second, Zambia's National Digital Transformation Strategy 2023 –2027
emphasises digital inclusion and the adoption of advanced technologies. However, evidence on how
such s trategies reach micro and informal enterprises is limited ( Ministry of Technology and
Science, Zambia, 2023 ). Third, development agencies increasingly promote AI as a lever for
enterprise performance while cautioning that, without complementary skills, infrastructure and
governance arrangements, technology adoption can widen rather than close existing inequalit ies
(OECD, 2025b; UNCTAD, 2025).
The study contributes in three ways. Theoretically, it extends TAM by situating it within the
informal economy, where formal procedures, organisational  structures and predictable routines
are weak or absent ( Alisyahbana et al., 2025 ; Fossen et al., 2024 ), and by linking AI adoption to
performance outcomes rather than to intentions alone. Practically, it offers evidence for
entrepreneurship-support organisations, educators and policymakers on how to support AI use
among informal youth ventures ( OECD, 2025b; UNCTAD, 2025). For policy, it provides Zambian
evidence relevant to implementing the National Digital Transformation Strategy.
Accordingly, the study has three objectives: (1) to examine whether perceived usefulness
and perceived ease of use predict AI adoption among informal youth entrepreneurs in Zambia; (2)
to assess the relationships between AI adoption and financial, marketin g and operational
performance; and (3) to test whether AI adoption mediates the relationships between perceived
usefulness, perceived ease of use and each performance dimension.

LITERATURE REVIEW
Technology Acceptance Model
AI adoption by informal youth entrepreneurs remains under-theorised, particularly in Sub-
Saharan Africa, where owner -managed micro -enterprises dominate economic activity.
Organisational-level frameworks such as the technology –organisation–environment (TOE)
framework explain the contextual determinants of adoption, but the cognitive mechanisms behind
individual-level acceptance require a complementary lens. In owner -operated enterprises, the
adopter and the organisation  are effectively the same person, which makes an individual -level
model appropriate. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) offers a parsimonious and well -
validated framework for explaining how beliefs about usefulness and ease of use shape intention
and actual use (Davis, 1989; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000).

TAM posits that perceived usefulness (PU), the belief that using a system enhances job
performance, is the strongest predictor of technology adoption ( Davis, 1989 ). Among informal
youth entrepreneurs, PU reflects beliefs about whether AI tools improve sales conversion, customer
engagement, operational efficiency and decision quality. Recent generative-AI studies confirm that
performance-oriented beliefs remain cent ral to adoption intentions ( Sharma et al., 2025 ; Song et
al., 2025). Perceived ease of use (PEOU) refers to the extent to which using a technology is free of
effort (Davis, 1989), and TAM proposes that PEOU also shapes PU because easier technologies are
more readily seen as useful. Where digital skills and infrastructure are limited, the ease of
prompting, mobile compatibility and reliability of AI outputs become critical determi nants of
sustained use (Ibrahim et al., 2025; Zavodna et al., 2024).
Although TAM was developed to explain acceptance and use, PU is fundamentally
performance-focused. If AI adoption is motivated by the belief that it improves business outcomes,
it is logical to ask whether its use is in fact associated with better performance. This study therefore
models PU and PEOU as antecedents of AI adoption and links adoption to performance outcomes,
extending TAM into an under-explored informal-economy context.

Perceived Usefulness and AI Adoption
Contemporary research confirms that PU remains a central determinant of AI -adoption
decisions. In studies of generative AI tools, PU predicts both behavioural intention and intensity of
use, even when users face skill and infrastructure constraints ( Sharma et al., 2025 ; Song et al.,
2025). The effect of PU is not uniform across contexts, however: evidence from resource -
constrained SME settings indicates that structural barriers such as connectivity, cost and digital
literacy can prevent perceived benefits from translating into actual use (Zavodna et al., 2024 ),
suggesting that the strength of the PU –adoption relationship depends on infrastructural and
institutional context. For informal youth entrepreneurs facing resource shortages, time pressures
and competitive markets, the perception that AI can improve performance is nonetheless likely to
matter for the decision to adopt it.
H1: Perceived usefulness of AI is positively associated with AI adoption among informal youth
entrepreneurs in Zambia.

Perceived Ease of Use and AI Adoption
In the AI domain, PEOU refers to whether users see AI tools as understandable, easy to
learn, intuitive and simple to incorporate into daily tasks. Technologies that lower learning effort
and operational barriers are more likely to be tried and integrated into routines. Recent studies
confirm that ease -of-use perceptions influence generative -AI adoption among SMEs and young
users, although the effect is often weaker than that of PU and varies by context (Bahaw et al., 2025;
Ibrahim et al., 2025 ). Evidence from digitally engaged SME populations suggests that once users
cross a threshold of basic digital literacy, ease of use ceases to predict adoption and infrastructural
constraints become binding (Alisyahbana et al., 2025). In contrast, in informal settings where cost
and connectivity dominate, PEOU may be attenuated regardless of how easy a tool is (Aryeh-Adjei
et al., 2025). Most research has focused on formal firms and student populations ( Mwaanga et al.,
2022), providing limited insight into informal youth entrepreneurship. In Zambia, where
informality dominates employment and digital-skills gaps persist, ease of use raises concerns about
whether informal youth entrepreneurs can understand, access, trust, and apply AI under resource-
limited conditions.
H2: Perceived ease of use is positively associated with AI adoption among informal youth
entrepreneurs in Zambia.

AI Adoption and Business Performance
AI adoption is increasingly treated as a driver of business performance among SMEs and
entrepreneurial ventures (Yesuf et al., 2025). Machine learning, predictive analytics and generative
AI help entrepreneurs automate processes, analyse market data, improve customer engagement
and make better decisions (Le Dinh et al., 2025). Studies generally find that AI adoption improves
financial performance (FP) through productivity gains, cost reductions and increased revenue
(Wamba-Taguimdje et al., 2020 ; Abrokwah-Larbi & Awuku -Larbi, 2024; Magableh et al., 2024 ),
although financial gains may lag behind improvements in internal processes (OECD, 2025a).
AI adoption also affects marketing performance (MP). AI tools enable businesses to analyse
customer data, personalise messages, produce promotional content and enhance customer
engagement (Ledro et al., 2025; Zahara et al., 2023), improving segmentation and responsiveness
to consumer needs (Beyari & Hashem, 2025). For entrepreneurs operating in social-media-driven
markets, faster customer communication and more efficient promotion can raise customer
satisfaction and loyalty (Afriyie et al., 2019). The literature further links AI adoption to operational
performance (OP): automation, process optimisation, inventory management and decision support
streamline daily operations and reduce time spent on repetitive tasks (Andayani et al., 2024; Cimino
et al., 2025 ). Operational improvements are often the first observable benefit of AI, which is
particularly valuable for small businesses whose owners manage several functions at once.
These benefits are not automatic. They depend on entrepreneurs' skills, resources and
ability to embed AI in daily activities, and studies of SMEs in emerging and resource -constrained
settings report null or even negative short -run effects where implementation disrupts workflows
or costs outweigh gains ( Boonmee et al., 2025 ; Sánchez et al., 2025 ). Because AI's effects may
therefore differ across outcomes, this study tests them separately for the financial, marketing and
operational dimensions.
H3: AI adoption is positively associated with financial performance among informal youth
entrepreneurs.
H4: AI adoption is positively associated with marketing performance among informal youth
entrepreneurs.
H5: AI adoption is positively associated with operational performance among informal youth
entrepreneurs.

The Mediating Role of AI Adoption
Because TAM positions technology use as the behavioural channel through which beliefs
about a technology affect outcomes, AI adoption is expected to transmit the effects of PU and PEOU
to business performance. Beliefs alone cannot improve sales, marketing reach or efficiency unless
they lead entrepreneurs to use AI in their businesses. In line with the third objective, the following
hypothesis is proposed:
H6: AI adoption mediates the relationships between (a) perceived usefulness and (b) perceived
ease of use and the financial, marketing and operational performance of informal youth
entrepreneurs.

RESEARCH METHOD
Study Setting and Sampling
The study focuses on informal youth entrepreneurs aged 18 to 35 years in Zambia. Because
Zambia's informal sector lacks a comprehensive business register, no probability sampling frame
was available. Hence, the study adopted a purposive, multi -site strateg y targeting informal
enterprises in urban and peri-urban trading areas. Eligible participants were owner-operators aged
18–35 who identified their enterprise as informal (unregistered or not fully tax -compliant,
following the Zambia Statistics Agency's ope rational definition) and had been trading for at least
three months. Trained enumerators approached entrepreneurs at their places of business,
explained the study's purpose, the voluntary nature of participation, the confidentiality of
responses, and the right to withdraw, and obtained written informed consent before administering
the structured questionnaire on paper or a tablet. Of the 320 questionnaires administered, 300
were returned and usable (94% response rate).
All respondents completed every scale, including the 122 respondents (40.7%) who
reported that they did not currently use AI tools; for these respondents, the AI adoption items
capture the absence or low intensity of use, and the PU and PEOU items capture their expectations
of AI tools. Data screening identified two further issues. First, 24 records contained at least one
missing item response; these were excluded listwise, leaving 276 records for the measurement and
structural models. Second, records 151–300 closely matched records 1–150: within each pair, the
31 item responses were identical or differed on no more than two items. Because such records
cannot be treated as statistically independent, all inferential statistics were computed with a cluster
bootstrap in which each matched pair was resampled together, so that standard errors and
confidence intervals reflect approximately 150 independent responses rather than 300.

Measures
All constructs were measured with multi -item scales adapted from validated instruments
and scored on a five -point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). PU and PEOU
items were adapted from Davis (1989)  and Venkatesh et al. (2003) . AI adoption items were
developed with reference to recent work on AI adoption in SMEs ( Le Dinh et al., 2025 ; OECD,
2025a). Financial, marketing and operational performance were adapted from Wiklund and
Shepherd (2005) , Trainor et al. (2014) , Venkatraman (1989)  and Le Dinh et al. (2025) . The
questionnaire contained 26 items measuring six constructs; 24 were retained after measurement

Data Analysis
Descriptive statistics and data screening were conducted in SPSS. Confirmatory factor
analysis (CFA) and covariance -based structural equation modelling (SEM) were conducted in
Python using the semopy package ( Igolkina & Meshcheryakov, 2020 ) with maximum -likelihood
estimation. SEM was chosen over multiple regression because it estimates the measurement and
structural models simultaneously, corrects for measurement error in latent constructs and allows
direct and indirect effects to be tested within one framework. The analysis followed Anderson and
Gerbing's (1988)  two-step procedure: the measurement model was validated first, and the
structural model was then estimated. In the structural model, PU and PEOU were allowed to covary,
and the disturbances of the three performance constructs were allowed to covary becaus e the
performance dimensions are expected to share causes other than AI adoption.
Standardised coefficients are reported. Their standard errors, two-sided p-values and 95%
bias-corrected (BC) confidence intervals were obtained from 5,000 cluster-bootstrap resamples, in
which the 150 matched record pairs were resampled with replacement. Resamples yi elding
improper solutions (a standardised structural coefficient with an absolute value above 1) were
discarded (187 of 5,000 for the hypothesised model). Indirect effects were computed as the
products of their constituent standardised  paths and judged significant when the BC interval
excluded zero ( Zhao et al., 2010 ). Variance explained (R²) is reported for each endogenous
construct. Heterotrait–monotrait (HTMT) ratios were calculated from the item correlation matrix
following Henseler et al. (2015). Model fit was evaluated against conventional criteria (CFI and TLI
≥ 0.90, RMSEA ≤ 0.08, SRMR ≤ 0.08; Hu & Bentler, 1999; Kline, 2023).

Reliability and Validity
Indicator reliability was assessed through standardised factor loadings. Two AI adoption
items, AI3 and AI4, loaded weakly or negatively (0.19 and −0.08 in a CFA with all five items) and
were removed ( Kline, 2023 ). Because both removed items captured analytical and decision -
support uses of AI, the refined three -item scale represents adoption mainly in terms of frequency
of use, marketing use and integration into daily operations. The remaining loadings ranged from
0.271 to 0.791 (Table 1). Twenty of the 24 loadings exceeded 0.60; PU4 (0.598), PEOU3 (0.588) and
AI2 (0.578) fell just below this level, and AI5 loaded weakly (0.271). These items were retained for
content validity because removing AI5 would leave a two-item adoption scale.
Cronbach's alpha and composite reliability (CR) exceeded 0.70 for PU, PEOU, FP, MP and
OP (α = 0.752–0.802; CR = 0.755–0.805) but not for AI adoption (α = 0.538; CR = 0.545). Average
variance extracted (AVE) reached 0.50 only for OP (0.509); it ranged from  0.437 to 0.471 for PU,
PEOU, FP and MP and was 0.311 for AI adoption. Following Fornell and Larcker (1981), convergent
validity is adequate when AVE is below 0.50, but CR exceeds 0.60, which holds for every construct
except AI adoption. Discriminant validity was supported by HTMT ratios, all below 0.85 (maximum
0.801 for PU–PEOU; Henseler et al., 2015), but the Fornell–Larcker criterion was not met for four
pairs, in which the latent correlation exceeded the square root of at least one AVE: PU –PEOU
(0.790), FP–MP (0.767), PEOU–OP (0.678) and AI–OP (0.571) (Table 2). The AI adoption measure
is therefore the weakest part of the model, and results involving it are interpreted with caution.

Common Method Bias
Because all constructs were measured with self -reports from a single respondent per
enterprise, common method bias (CMB) was addressed procedurally and statistically (Podsakoff et
al., 2003). Procedurally, respondents were assured of anonymity and confidentiality, were told that
there were no right or wrong answers, and answered the predictor items (PU, PEOU, AI adoption)
and outcome items (business performance) in separate sections of the questionnaire. Statistically,
three tests were conducted. First, in Harman's single-factor test, all 24 retained items were entered
into one unrotated factor analysis; the first factor accounted for 31.3% of the total variance, below
the 50% threshold. Seco nd, a single -factor CFA fit substantially worse than the six -factor
measurement model (Δχ²(15) = 502.97, p < .001). Third, full-collinearity variance inflation factors
for the six constructs, computed from construct composite scores, ranged from 1.19 to 1.96, below
the 3.3 threshold (Kock, 2015). Because Harman's test is weak on its own ( Podsakoff et al., 2003),
the three tests are considered jointly; together they indicate that CMB is unlikely to account for the
results.

Additional Diagnostic Tests
Three further checks were conducted. First, the variance inflation factor for PU and PEOU
in the AI adoption equation was 1.64, below the 3.3 and 5.0 thresholds ( Hair et al., 2022 ; Kock,
2015); each performance equation has a single predictor, so collinearity does not arise there.
Second, univariate skewness ranged from −1.36 to 0.79, and excess kurtosis from −0.70 to 3.09,
below the levels that Kline (2023) treats as severe (|skewness| > 3; |kurtosis| > 10), but Mardia's
multivariate kurtosis was 123.30 (critical ratio = 28.99), indicating multivariate non -normality.
Because maximum-likelihood standard errors can be biased under non -normality, all inferences
rely on bootstrap confidence intervals, which do not assume normality ( Kline, 2023 ). Third,
because 122 respondents did not use AI tools, the structural model was re-estimated for current AI
users only (n = 168 complete records); the results are reported with the hypothesis tests.

Measurement Model
The six-factor measurement model showed mixed fit: χ²(237) = 659.92, χ²/df = 2.78, CFI =
0.830, TLI = 0.802, RMSEA = 0.081 (90% CI [0.073, 0.088]), SRMR = 0.061. The χ²/df ratio and SRMR
met conventional criteria, and RMSEA was marginal, but CFI and TLI fell below 0.90 (Hu & Bentler,
1999; Marsh et al., 2004). Together with the reliability and validity results above, this indicates that
the measurement model is adequate for exploratory purposes but not strong, with the AI adoption
scale as the main weakness.

Discussion
The finding that PEOU, but not PU, predicts AI adoption departs from classical TAM studies,
in which PU is typically the dominant predictor ( Ibrahim et al., 2025 ; Sharma et al., 2025 ). The
departure aligns with research showing that simplicity and accessibility can outweigh perceived
performance benefits among resource-constrained and non-expert users (Alisyahbana et al., 2025;
Zavodna et al., 2024). PEOU appears to act as a threshold factor that reduces cognitive burden and
builds confidence before users can evaluate usefulness, particularly among those encountering AI
for the first time ( Ibrahim et al., 2025 ). Informal youth entrepreneurs typically lack structured
training, technical support, and time to learn complex systems, so they favour  tools they can use
immediately. Notably, PEOU dominated PU despite the relatively high educational attainment of
this sample, suggesting that contextual constraints rather than cognitive ability may shape
adoption. Two further explanations should be consi dered. PU and PEOU are highly correlated in
these data, and TAM's PEOU → PU path was not modelled, so part of PU's contribution may be
absorbed by PEOU; the non -significant PU coefficient indicates that PU adds little unique
explanation once PEOU is controlled, not that usefulness is irrelevant. In addition, perceived risks
such as fear of costly mistakes with an unfamiliar tool may suppress adoption despite recognised
usefulness, although perceived risk was not measured here (Zavodna et al., 2024).
In the hypothesised model, AI adoption was positively associated with all three
performance dimensions, and most strongly with operational performance. This pattern aligns with
the view that operational improvements are often the first observable benefit of AI, as automation
and improved information processing reduce the time owner-operators spend on repetitive tasks
(Andayani et al., 2024 ; Cimino et al., 2025 ). The associations with financial and marketing
performance align with evidence that AI use supports pricing, customer communication, and
promotional content that increase revenue and visibility ( Abrokwah-Larbi & Awuku-Larbi, 2024;

409

Le Dinh et al., 2025; Magableh et al., 2024). These associations should not be read causally. The data
are cross -sectional, so better -performing enterprises may be better able to adopt AI, and the
associations weakened markedly when we analysed only AI users, suggesting they reflect
differences between users and non-users rather than gains that increase with intensity of use.
The mediation results show that the role of AI adoption as a channel from ease of use to
performance is sensitive to model specification. When PEOU could affect performance directly, the
indirect effects disappeared, and PEOU remained directly associated w ith marketing and
operational performance. One interpretation is that ease of use reflects a broader digital capability
(comfort with smartphones, apps and online platforms) that supports marketing and operations
whether or not AI tools are used. This exte nds research framing digital technologies as capability
enablers in resource-constrained environments (Dwivedi et al., 2023 ; Nambisan et al., 2019 ) and
suggests that, in informal settings, the ability to use technology may matter more than adopting any
single tool.

Theoretical Implications
First, the study shows that the relative importance of TAM's core constructs is context -
dependent. Contrary to the assumption that PU is the primary determinant of adoption (Venkatesh
et al., 2003 ), PEOU was the only significant predictor of AI adoption among informal youth
entrepreneurs, supporting more contextualised adoption models that incorporate environmental
and socioeconomic constraints (Alisyahbana et al., 2025). Second, the study adds evidence from the
informal sector to the still-limited literature on AI and entrepreneurship in developing economies
(Fossen et al., 2024 ; Le Dinh et al., 2025 ). Third, the mediation analysis cautions against treating
technology adoption as the automatic mechanism linking TAM beliefs to outcomes: ease of use was
largely associated with performance directly, suggesting that TAM -based performance models
should acc ount for the broader capabilities ease -of-use beliefs may capture. Finally, the study
shows that young informal entrepreneurs are already engaging with AI, supporting arguments that
informal enterprises can be digitally adaptive (Begazo et al., 2023).

Policy and Practical Implications
Because ease of use is the clearest determinant of AI adoption, technology developers,
trainers and policymakers should treat simplicity as a design requirement rather than a secondary
feature. AI solutions for informal entrepreneurs should minimise technical complexity and focus on
practical applications such as automated messaging, pricing support and basic record -keeping,
designed for mobile devices and low -bandwidth conditions. Training programmes should
emphasise hands-on use and build general digital confidence, since ease of use was associated with
performance even beyond AI adoption itself. Policymakers can support adoption by expanding
affordable internet access, supporting innovation hubs and incubation centres , and providing
incentives for digital tools, which are critical for bridging the digital divide within the informal
sector (Begazo et al., 2023; UNCTAD, 2025).

CONCLUSIONS
This study examined factors associated with AI adoption and whether adoption is
associated with business performance among informal youth entrepreneurs in Zambia, addressing
an evidence gap left by research focused on formal SMEs in high-income countries. In answer to the
first objective, PEOU, not PU, predicted AI adoption, indicating that usability is the gateway to AI
adoption in this informal context. For the second objective, AI adoption was positively associated
with financial, marketing, and operatio nal performance, strongest with operational performance,
although these associations weakened when we analysed only AI users. In answer to the third

objective, AI adoption carried significant indirect effects of PEOU on performance in the
hypothesised model. However, these did not survive the addition of direct paths, which showed
PEOU to be directly associated with marketing and operational performance. The study advances
the field by situating TAM in the informal economy and showing that, for informa l youth
entrepreneurs, ease of use matters more for adoption than perceived usefulness. Given the modest
measurement quality and model fit, these conclusions should be treated as exploratory.

LIMITATIONS AND FURTHER RESEARCH
Several limitations qualify these findings. First, the cross -sectional design limits causal
inference: SEM tests theoretically grounded associations but cannot establish direction or capture
the gradual, iterative nature of AI adoption (Boonmee et al., 2025). Longitudinal studies are needed
to examine how adoption and its performance effects evolve as entrepreneurs gain experience.
Second, the purposive sample limits generalisability  to formal SMEs and to other countries with
different institutional support and technological readiness; comparative studies across countries
and sectors are recommended (OECD, 2025a).
Third, the data file contained paired, near -identical records and 24 records with missing
responses. Although cluster bootstrapping prevents paired records from inflating precision, the
effective sample is about 150, which limits statistical power and contributed to unstable estimates
in the supplementary model; future studies should verify data entry against source questionnaires
and use larger independent samples. Fourth, measurement quality was modest: AVE fell below 0.50
for five constructs, discriminan t validity was weak under the Fornell –Larcker criterion for four
construct pairs, notably PU–PEOU and FP–MP, model fit was below conventional benchmarks, and
the three-item AI adoption scale had low reliability. Future research should develop and pretest AI
adoption measures for informal settings that capture distinct types of use (communication,
marketing, analytics and operations).
Fifth, the study relies on self -reported data. Although statistical tests indicated that
common method bias is unlikely to explain the results, self -reported performance may not fully
capture actual outcomes, particularly given informal record -keeping; fut ure studies should use
objective data such as mobile -money transaction records where feasible ( Sánchez et al., 2025 ).
Sixth, the model includes only TAM variables and omits organisational  readiness, environmental
pressures and institutional support; integrating TOE, the resource -based view and dynamic -
capabilities perspectives, and modelling TAM's PEOU → PU path, would provide a fuller account
(Dwivedi et al., 2023; Sánchez et al., 2025). Finally, sector-specific studies in agriculture, retail and
services are needed, because readiness, adoption challenges and performance outcomes are likely
to differ across sectors.
