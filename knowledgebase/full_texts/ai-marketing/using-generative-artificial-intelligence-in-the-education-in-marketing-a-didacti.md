Abstract. Students increasingly rely on Generative Artificial Intelligence (Generative AI) tools when
completing academic assignments, which fundamentally transforms learning dynamics in higher education.
From a peda gogical perspective, this shift raises critical questions regarding students’ attitudes toward
Generative AI, their level of competence in using such tools, and the implications for teaching and learning
in applied disciplines such as Marketing. Drawing on constructivist and self -regulated learning
perspectives, the present study conceptualizes Generative AI as a cognitive and metacognitive support that
mediates knowledge construction rather than replacing human learning processes. Based on a targeted
didactic experiment conducted as a workshop within an undergraduate Marketing course, the study
addresses three interrelated research questions: (1) how frequently students use Generative AI tools and
how they assess their own level of preparedness; (2) how stu dents evaluate the educational benefits of
Generative AI usage; and (3) which factors they perceive as critical for the successful integration of
Generative AI into academic work. Empirical data were collected through a twophase design combining
artifact analysis, expert evaluation, and a structured questionnaire (n = 54). The results indicate a high
frequency of Generative AI usage: 43% of students report using Generative AI tools often or continuously,
while 44% use them occasionally. Most respondents ass ess their level of Generative AI competence as at
least “decent,” with ChatGPT being universally used. Students identify productivity gains, support in
information synthesis, and assistance with preparatory tasks as key benefits, while emphasizing prompt
formulation, prior knowledge, and output validation as essential success factors.

Keywords: Generative AI; marketing education; higher education; didactic experiment; self -regulated
learning.

Introduction
The rapid diffusion of Generative Artificial Intelligence (Generative AI) tools in higher education
has profoundly altered the way students search for information, process knowledge, and complete
academic assignments. In practice, Generative AI is no longer a marginal or experimental
technology: for many students, it has become an integral element of everyday learning activities.

This development poses significant challenges and opportunities for university teachers,
particularly in applied fields such as Marketing, where analytical reasoning, strategic thinking, and
contextual understanding are central learning outcomes. Within the framework of the newly
established (2024) Gamification Transformational Laboratory, a team of lecturers and researchers

marketing education. Rather than approaching Generative AI solely as a technological innovation,
the present study adopts a pedagogical perspective, viewing Generative AI as a learning mediator
that interacts with students’ prior knowledge, learning strategies, and self -regulatory capacities.
From this standpoint, understanding how students actually use Generative AI, how they perce ive
its benefits, and which competencies they consider essential becomes a prerequisite for designing
effective learning environments. The primary goal of the experiment was to gain a deeper
understanding of students’ attitudes toward Generative AI and the ir level of knowledge and skills
in using such tools. Based on these insights, the study seeks to inform the intentional design and
management of learning dynamics so that the educational potential of Generative AI is fully and
responsibly realized. The study contributes to the literature on Marketing education by providing
empirically grounded insights into how Generative AI tools are embedded in students’ learning
strategies and by outlining didactic implications for university teachers. The findings supp ort the
need for pedagogically guided integration of Generative AI that fosters critical thinking, self -
regulation, and responsible use rather than purely instrumental adoption. By moving beyond a
purely descriptive account of Generative AI usage, the pape r positions itself within the broader
academic discourse on constructivist learning, self -regulated learning, and technology -enhanced
education. In doing so, it aims to contribute to the emerging body of research on the pedagogical
integration of Generative AI in higher education and, more specifically, in Marketing education.

Literature review
Over the past decade, scholarly interest in the application of Artificial Intelligence in education has
increased significantly, with a particularly sharp rise foll owing the public diffusion of Generative
AI tools. Bibliometric analyses confirm a rapid growth in publications addressing AI -supported
teaching and learning across diverse educational contexts (Afzaal et al., 2024; Delen et al., 2024;
López-Chila et al., 2024). Despite this expansion, the literature remains fragmented, and stable,
theory-driven findings are still emerging due to the complexity of educational systems and the
dynamic evolution of AI technologies.

Generative AI and Higher Education: Current Research Trends
Existing research identifies three major trends in the integration of AI in education. First, AI
applications have expanded from highly specialized domains (e.g., medicine or computer science)
to broader educational fields, including business and social sciences (Afzaal et al., 2024). Second,
there is increasing emphasis on collaboration between educators, researchers, and developers in
designing AI -supported learning environments (Delen et al., 2024). Third, AI is increasingly
viewed as a driver of systemic transformation, influencing curriculum design, assessment practices,
and educational management (Totseva & Mavrodieva, 2024).
In higher education, AI is associated with opportunities for personalization, efficiency, and
adaptive learning (Kuleto et al., 2021; Beloev et al., 2024). Generative AI supports content
generation, summarization, translation, data processing, and individualized feedback, which may
reduce learning time and enhance productivity (Surugiu et al., 2024). At the same tim e, concerns

Theoretical Perspectives: Constructivism, Self-Regulated Learning, and Generative AI
To move beyond a descriptive understanding of Generative AI usage, it is essential to situate
empirical observations within established educational theories. Two particularly relevant
theoretical perspectives are constructivism and self-regulated learning, both of which offer a robust
framework for interpreting how students interact with Generative AI in academic contexts. From a
constructivist standpoint, learning is understood as an active process in which learners construct
knowledge through engagement with problems, information, and social interaction. In this sense,
Generative AI can be conceptualized as a cognitive tool that supports knowledge construction by
providing scaffolding, alternative representations, and rapid access to synthesized informati on.
Rather than transmitting knowledge, Generative AI facilitates exploration, hypothesis generation,
and iterative refinement of ideas, especially in complex, illstructured tasks typical of Marketing
education. Self-regulated learning theory further enriches this perspective by emphasizing learners’
capacity to plan, monitor, and evaluate their own learning processes. When students use Generative
AI effectively, they engage in key self -regulatory activities such as goal setting (defining what to
ask the sy stem), strategic planning (selecting appropriate tools and prompts), monitoring
(evaluating the relevance and quality of AI -generated outputs), and reflection (validating and
integrating results into their own reasoning). Consequently, the educational value of Generative AI
depends less on the technology itself and more on students’ metacognitive skills and prior
knowledge. These theoretical lenses imply that students’ attitudes toward Generative AI, their
perceived competence, and their actual usage patter ns are interrelated components of a broader
learning dynamic. Positive attitudes and frequent usage are likely to be associated with higher
levels of selfregulation and domain knowledge, whereas uncritical or purely instrumental use may
undermine deep learning. This theoretical linkage informs the conceptual logic of the present study
and guides the interpretation of the empirical findings.

Generative AI in Business and Marketing Education
Within the fields of Economics, Management, and Marketing, empirical research on AI-supported
learning remains comparatively limited. Thematic reviews indicate that only a small share of AIin-
education publications explicitly address business-related disciplines (Kuleto et al., 2021). Existing
studies primarily focus on l ecturers’ adoption of AI tools and their perceptions of pedagogical
impact (Surugiu et al., 2024), as well as on factors influencing students’ willingness to use AI
technologies (Sova et al., 2024). Research in Marketing education suggests that Generative AI is
increasingly used for content creation, market analysis, and strategic decision support. Ivanov
(2023) demonstrates that, at present, students perceive only marginal differences between human-
and AI-generated marketing content, highlighting the need for instructional designs that emphasize
critical evaluation
rather than passive consumption. Mengov and Zinovieva (2024) further argue that future
managers must develop “expert intuition” and judgment capabilities that cannot be outsourced to
AI systems.  From an educational perspective, marketing tasks such as market entry analysis,
segmentation, and strategy formulation are particularly suitable for constructivist, problem -based
learning supported by Generative AI. These tasks require the integration of diverse data sources,
contextual reasoning, and iterative decision -making, all of which can be enhanced – but not
replaced – by Generative AI tools.

Research Gap and Positioning of the Study
Despite the growing body of literature, several gaps remain. Firs t, many studies focus on
technological capabilities rather than on learning processes and student agency. Second, empirical
evidence linking Generative AI usage to self -regulated learning and competence development is
still scarce, particularly in the context of Marketing education. Third, there is a lack of didactically
grounded experiments that combine authentic academic assignments with systematic reflection on
students’ experiences. The present study addresses these gaps by examining students’ Generativ e
AI usage within a structured didactic experiment embedded in a real Marketing course. By
explicitly linking empirical indicators (frequency of use, perceived competence, benefits, and
success factors) to constructivist and self-regulated learning perspectives, the study contributes to
a more theoretically informed understanding of Generative AI in higher education.

Methodology
Overview of the Didactic Experiment
A didactic experiment was conducted for the purposes of the present study in order to explore how
undergraduate students in Marketing use Generative AI tools in authentic academic settings. The
experiment follows the understanding of didactic experimentation as a targeted pedagogical
intervention in which specific conditions are intentionally desi gned, the resulting effects are
systematically observed, and conclusions are drawn regarding teaching and learning processes
(Bijkov & Kraevski, 2007). The methodological design is exploratory and mixed method in nature,
combining qualitative and quantitat ive approaches. Given the innovative and rapidly evolving
character of Generative AI in higher education, the study does not aim to establish causal
relationships but rather to provide an empirically grounded, theoretically informed analysis of
students’ usage patterns, perceptions, and self -assessed competencies. The experiment seeks to
answer the following research questions: – How frequently do students use Generative AI tools
when completing academic assignments, and how do they assess their level of pr eparedness? –
How do students evaluate the educational benefits of using Generative AI? – Which factors do
students perceive as critical for the successful use of Generative AI in academic work?

Structure and Duration of the Experiment
The didactic experi ment was embedded in a compulsory Marketing course for fourth- year
undergraduate students enrolled in the bachelor’s programs in Business Administration and
Economics and Finance at the Faculty of Economics and Business Administration (FEBA), Sofia
University “St. Kliment Ohridski”. The experiment was conducted over a five-week period,
from 10 November to 18 December 2024, and was structured into two sequential phases.

Phase One: Generative AI Supported Academic Assignment and Workshop
As part of the Marketing course, students were assigned a complex, problem-based task simulating
a real-world consulting scenario. A manufacturing company seeks to enter the European market
and hires the students, acting as consultants, to conduct a marketing analysis and provide strategic
recommendations. Students were required to address the following questions: – What
environmental factors should be considered when developing a marketing plan for the European
market? – How should the European market be segmented? – What is the potential and
attractiveness of the identified segments, and which segment(s) should be targeted? – At what stage
of the product life cycle is the European market for the product? – Which market entry strategy is
most appropriate? – How should pricing, distribution, and communication policies be defined? The

discussion component, allowing for questions, argumentation, and defence of positions. The
workshop format served a dual didactic purpose: it functioned both as an assessment mechanism
and as a reflective learning environment in which students articulated and critically discussed their
use of Generative AI. Four lecturers acted as independent evaluators. Based on written reports,
presentations, and participation in discussions, each evaluator completed an assessment form. The
evaluation criteria included: – completeness and persuasiveness of responses; – quality of analysis
and justification; – visualization and presentation quality; – reliability of data and correctness of
citations; – clarity and precision of expression; – active participation in discussion. Each criterion
carried a predefined weight, and the final team score was calculated as the sum of points awarded
by the evaluators.

Phase One Data Sources and Analytical Logic
Data from Phase One were collected through document and artifact analysis (student reports and
presentations), structured observation during the workshop, and expert evaluation by lecturers. A
qualitative content analysis was conducted to identify recurring themes related to students’
Generative AI usage, perceived benefi ts, and strategies. These themes informed the development
of notional indicators used in Phase Two.

Phase Two: Questionnaire Survey
Based on the notional indicators identified during Phase One, a structured questionnaire was
developed to collect individual-level data. The questionnaire was administered between 11 December
and 18 December 2024 and completed by students on a voluntary basis. A total of 54 valid
questionnaires were collected out of 59 participating students, resulting in a response rate of 92%.

Data Analysis Approach
Quantitative data from the questionnai re were analysed using descriptive statistical methods
(frequencies, percentages, and distribution patterns), which are appropriate given the exploratory
nature of the study and the sample size. Qualitative responses to open-ended questions were analysed
thematically to enrich and contextualize the quantitative findings. Inferential statistical techniques
(e.g., regression analysis or ANOVA) were not applied, as the study does not aim to test causal
hypotheses. Instead, the emphasis is placed on transparency, reproducibility, and analytical coherence
between research questions, data collection methods, and analytical procedures.

Limitations of the Study
Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the study is based on a relatively small and
context-specific sample (n = 54) drawn from two bachelor’s programs within a single faculty.
Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as indicative rather than generalizable. Second,
the results rely substantially on students’ self -assessment and self -reported data, which may be
influenced by social desirability or overconfidence. Opportunities for external validation are
limited, despite the inclusion of expert evaluation during the workshop. Third, the dynamic nature
of Generative AI technologies implies tha t students’ skills, perceptions, and usage patterns may

Results and discussions
Frequency and Intensity of Generative AI Usage
The empirical findings reveal a high frequency of Generative AI usage among undergraduate
students in Marketing-related programs. According to the survey data, 11% of respondents report
using Generative AI tools continuously when completing  academic assignments, 32% use them
often, and 44% use them sometimes. Only 13% indicate rare or no usage. These results confirm
that Generative AI has become an integral component of students’ learning practices rather than an
occasional or experimental resource. From a constructivist perspective, such frequent use suggests
that students actively incorporate Generative AI into their problem-solving processes, particularly
when dealing with complex, open- ended tasks such as market analysis and strategy form ulation.
Compared with earlier studies conducted shortly after the initial diffusion of Generative AI tools
(e.g., Sova et al., 2024; Tsankov & Damyanov, 2024), the observed usage frequency is relatively
high. This may reflect both the increasing maturity of Generative AI technologies and the specific
academic context of Marketing education, where information synthesis and analytical reasoning
are central learning activities.

Students’ Self-Assessed Competence in Using Generative AI
Regarding self -assessed competence, 67% of students report using Generative AI at a “decent
level,” while 24% assess their competence as “high,” and 5% claim very advanced skills. Only 4%
indicate difficulties or non -use. These findings align with self -regulated learning theory,  which
emphasizes learners’ perceived self-efficacy as a key determinant of strategic learning behaviour.
Students who consider themselves competent are more likely to experiment with different prompts,
critically evaluate outputs, and integrate AI-generated content into their own reasoning. Lecturers’
observations during the workshop confirm that students’ self -assessments are largely realistic. In
comparison with findings reported by Surugiu et al. (2024) and Sova et al. (2024), the students in
the present study appear better prepared to work with Generative AI tools. This higher level of
perceived competence may be explained by the authentic, problembased design of the assignment,
which required students to move beyond superficial content generation and e ngage in iterative
refinement, validation, and justification of AI-supported outputs.

Generative AI Tools Used by Students
All respondents report using ChatGPT, making it the dominant tool in the sample. Other tools are
used less frequently: Gemini (48%), Copilot (35%), DALL-E (26%), Perplexity (24%), and small
language models (13%). Tools such as Scopus AI, LLaMA, and domain- specific applications are
rarely used (4%). This distribution reflects both accessibility and task relevance. Text -based large
language models are particularly well suited to marketing assignments that involve analysis,
synthesis, and strategic reasoning. The limited use of specialized or academic AI tools suggests
that students prioritize convenience and versatility over disciplinary specificity. These findings are
consistent with prior research indicating that students predominantly adopt Generative AI tools that
offer immediate cognitive support and low entry barriers (Surugiu et al., 2024; Tsankov &
Damyanov, 2024). From a didactic standpoint, this underscores the need for instructors to explicitly
introduce and scaffold the use of more specialized AI resources where appropriate.

Didactic Implications and Theoretical Integration
Taken together, the results illustrate a dynamic interaction between students’ attitudes toward
Generative AI, their self -assessed skills, and their learning strategies. Frequent usage, positive
evaluations, and emphasis on critical success factors suggest that Generative AI is embedded within
students’ self-regulated learning processes rather than used as a passive shortcut. From a theoretical
perspective, the findings provide empirical support for conceptualizing Generative AI as a
mediating artifact in constructivist learning environments. Its pedagogical value emerges when it
is integrated into as signments that require analysis, reflection, and justification – conditions that
were intentionally designed in the present didactic experiment. At the same time, the results
highlight the necessity of pedagogical guidance. Without explicit instructional f raming, there is a
risk that students may over -rely on Generative AI or use it instrumentally, thereby limiting deep
learning. This reinforces the role of university teachers as designers of learning environments that
foster critical, responsible, and reflective use of AI technologies.

Conclusion
Based on a two-phase didactic experiment conducted within undergraduate programs in Business
Administration and Economics and Finance, the present study examined how students use
Generative Artificial Intelligence tools when completing academic assignments in Marketing. The
findings indicate a high frequency of Generative AI usage: 43% of participants report using such
tools often or continuously, while 44% use them occasionally. Students also report relatively high
levels of competence, with the majority assessing their skills as at least “decent.” ChatGPT emerges
as the dominant tool, while other Generative AI resources remain less widely known and used.
From a pedagogical perspective, the results demonstrate that  Generative AI is primarily used to
support preparatory activities, information synthesis, and initial orientation on complex
assignments. At the same time, students clearly recognize that successful Generative AI usage
depends on human competencies such a s effective prompt formulation, critical validation of
outputs, prior domain knowledge, and an understanding of how AI systems function. These
findings reinforce the view that Generative AI does not replace learning but reshapes the conditions
under which learning occurs.

The main contribution of the study lies in its theoretically informed, empirically grounded analysis
of Generative AI usage in Marketing education. By integrating constructivist and self -regulated
learning perspectives with data from a real -world didactic experiment, the study moves beyond
descriptive accounts and offers insights into the learning dynamics associated with Generative
AIsupported tasks. Specifically, it demonstrates how students embed Generative AI into their

Implications for University Teachers and Institutions
The findings have several practical implications for university teachers. First, instructors should
explicitly address Generative AI usage within course design by clarifying expectations, ethical
standards, and evaluation criteria. Second, learning activitie s should be structured to require
justification, reflection, and validation of AI -generated outputs, thereby supporting self -regulated
learning. Third, educators themselves require ongoing professional development to remain
informed about the capabilities and limitations of evolving Generative AI tools. At the institutional
level, universities should move beyond restrictive or purely regulatory approaches and instead
develop pedagogically informed frameworks for Generative AI integration. This includes
providing access to appropriate tools, supporting interdisciplinary collaboration, and embedding
AI literacy within curricula.

Several limitations of the study must be acknowledged. The sample size is relatively small and
context-specific, which limits the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the reliance on
selfreported data introduces potential biases related to self -perception and social desirability.
Future research should address these limitations through longitudinal designs that track changes in
students’ Generative AI usage, competencies, and learning outcomes over time. Comparative
studies across disciplines, institutions, and demographic groups would further enhance
understanding of contextual differences. In addition, future work could employ experimental or
quasi-experimental designs to examine the causal effects of specific pedagogical interventions
involving Generative AI. As Generative AI continues to evolve, ongoing empirical and theoretical
research will be essential to ensure that its integration into higher education supports meaningful
learning, equity, and academic integrity rather than undermining them.
