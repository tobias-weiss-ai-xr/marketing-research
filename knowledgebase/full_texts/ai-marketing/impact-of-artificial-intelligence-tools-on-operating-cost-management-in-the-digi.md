Abstract: Small and medium -sized enterprises (SMEs) are rapidly adopting artificial intelligence
(AI) tools in marketing, yet evidence on how these tools change marketing operating costs remains
fragmented and dominated by large -firm cases. This paper develops a tra nsparent, activity-based
cost model of monthly marketing operating expenditure (OPEX) and evaluates it through a Monte
Carlo experiment with 10,000 draws per scenario. Three scenarios are compared for a representative
SME marketing unit: a manual baseline (S0), partial AI adoption through off-the-shelf software-as-
a-service tools (S1), and integrated AI adoption built on a CRM and marketing-automation core (S2).
Costs comprise labour, agency spending, media-spend wastage, tooling, amortised implementation
and training, and AI oversight. For a three-person team, mean monthly OPEX falls from USD 8,879
(S0) to USD 8,362 under S1 (−5.8%) and to USD 8,523 under S2 (−3.9%). Although S2 removes far
more gross cost (USD 2,150 vs. 1,135 per month), it also adds USD 1,795 of AI-related cost, so its net
advantage is smaller and far less certain (probability of positive savings 79.0% vs. 99.1% for S1).
Scale is decisive: integrated AI does not pay off for one- or two-person teams, and it overtakes partial
AI only from roughly four to five marketing employees. Sensitivity analysis shows that the share of
freed labour hours actually converted into cost savings and the recurring licence burden dominate
outcomes. The findings support a staged, measurement-led adoption strategy.
Keywords: Artificial intelligence; marketing digitalisation; small and medium -sized enterprises;
operating cost management; Monte Carlo simulation; total cost of ownership.
1. Introduction
1.1 Background and problem statement
Small and medium -sized enterprises form the numerical backbone of almost every
national economy, and marketing is one of the first functions in which they encounter data-
driven technology. Social media advertising, e -mail automation, customer relationship
management (CRM) platforms, and, more recently, generative and predictive AI have
become default infrastructure for customer acquisition. Scholars argue that AI is reshaping
marketing strategy, customer experience, and the internal division of labour between
people and machines [1], [2]. For an SME, however, the decisive managerial question is
rarely whether AI is strategically interesting; it is whether a given tool will lowe r or raise
the monthly cost of running marketing, and how confident the owner can be in that answer
before signing a subscription or commissioning an integration.
The question is difficult because AI changes cost structure rather than simply cost
level. Automation can compress the hours needed for content drafting, reporting, and

implementation and training outlays, and new oversight duties such as prompt
engineering, quality assurance, and data governance. Economists have long observed that
general-purpose technologies deliver measurable productivity gains only after
complementary investments in skills, processes, and organisation have been made, which
explains why expectations and statistics often diverge [3]. In marketing, this logic implies
that visible automation savings may be offset by less visible adaptation costs, particularly
in organisations too small to spread fixed costs across many users.
Despite the practical importance of these trade -offs, most published evidence on AI
in marketing is either conceptual, focused on effectiveness outcomes such as conversion
and engagement, or drawn from large multinational firms with dedicated data teams [4 ],
[5]. Studies that explicitly model the operating cost side for SMEs, with transparent
assumptions that managers can adjust to their own circumstances, are scarce. This gap
matters because SMEs cannot easily absorb failed technology investments, and vend or
claims rarely disclose the distribution of possible outcomes. The present paper addresses
this gap by treating the AI adoption decision as a problem of operating cost management
under uncertainty.
1.2 Related literature and research gap
The literature relevant to this study can be organised into five streams: conceptual
frameworks, machine -learning applications, innovation and strategy, firm value, and
adoption theory. The first stream develops conceptual frameworks that classify AI
capabilities and their implications for marketing. Davenport et al. [1] propose a
multidimensional framework distinguishing levels of intelligence and forms of AI
deployment, while Huang and Rust [2] structure AI applications in marketing along
research, strate gy, and action, using a typology of mechanical, thinking, and feeling
intelligence that builds on their earlier analysis of AI in service [6]. These works clarify
what AI can do, not what it costs. The second stream reviews machine -learning methods
and applications in marketing, connecting computational techniques to managerial insight
[4] and mapping the deployment of AI across marketing functions [5]. The third stream
examines AI within innovation management and business strategy, emphasising
organisational capabilities, governance, and the risks of technology-led transformation [7],
[8].
The fourth stream concerns firm-level value and performance. Empirical work on AI-
based transformation projects indicates that value creation operates through process
improvements and depends on organisational context [9], and economy -level modelling
suggests that benefits may be unevenly distributed between early and late adopters and
between large and small organisations [10]. Multi -author perspectives further stress that
AI adoption raises technological, organisational, and ethical challenges that cannot  be
reduced to a purchase decision [11]. The fifth stream provides the theoretical lenses for
adoption itself: diffusion of innovations [12], the technology acceptance model [13], and
the technology –organisation–environment (TOE) framework [14]. These lens es explain
why and when SMEs adopt, but they are largely silent on the economics of running AI
once adopted.
Two methodological traditions from cost management are particularly useful for
closing the gap. The total cost of ownership (TCO) concept insists that the relevant cost of
a technology includes acquisition, operation, and maintenance rather than the purcha se
price alone [15]. Time-driven activity-based costing (TDABC) estimates process costs from
the time required to perform activities and the cost per unit of capacity supplied [16].
Combined, they suggest modelling marketing as a set of activities with known time shares,
letting AI reduce the time each requires, and adding the full set of AI -related costs. What

1.3 Research questions and contributions
The study is organised around four research questions. RQ1: How does the adoption
of AI tools change the level and composition of monthly marketing operating cost in a
representative SME? RQ2: How uncertain are these effects, and how long does it take for
one-off investments to be recovered? RQ3: How does the size of the marketing team
moderate the cost effect of partial versus integrated AI adoption? RQ4: Which cost and
adoption parameters most strongly determine whether AI reduces operating cost?
The paper makes three contributions. First, it offers a compact and fully specified cost
model that links AI-induced time reductions at the activity level to the monthly OPEX of
an SME marketing unit, bridging TCO and TDABC logic with the AI -in-marketing
literature. Second, it introduces the notion of a realisation factor, the share of freed working
hours that is actually converted into avoided cost, and shows that it is among the most
influential drivers of outcomes; this concept captures the indivisibilit y of labour in small
firms. Third, it provides distributional evidence (probability of savings, payback, scale
thresholds) that can guide staged adoption. The remainder of the paper is organised in the
IMRAD structure: Section 2 describes the methods, Sect ion 3 reports results, Section 4
discusses implications and limitations, and Section 5 concludes.

2. Methods
2.1 Research design
The study uses a scenario-based simulation design. Rather than surveying a small and
possibly unrepresentative sample of firms, it builds an explicit cost model with
probabilistic parameters and evaluates it through repeated random sampling. Monte Carlo
methods, introduced by Metropolis and Ulam [17], are appropriate when the outcome of
interest is a nonlinear function of many uncertain inputs and when the decision -maker
needs the distribution of outcomes rather than a point forecast. The design therefore
answers conditional questions, namely what operating cost outcomes follow if plausible
parameter ranges hold, and it should not be read as an empirical estimate of average
savings in any specific national or sectoral population. All assumptions are declared  in
Section 2.4 so that readers can substitute their own values.
The reference organisation is an SME marketing unit with three full -time-equivalent
(FTE) employees, a monthly paid -media budget of USD 1,000 per FTE, and a moderate
reliance on an external marketing agency. Three scenarios are compared. S0 is the manual
baseline, in which marketing activities are performed with conventional tools and limited
automation. S1 represents partial AI adoption: subscription -based generative -AI,
analytics, and scheduling tools are added without deep integration into the firm's dat a
systems. S2 represents integrated AI adoption: an AI -enabled CRM and marketing -
automation platform is implemented, with predictive segmentation, automated campaign
orchestration, and conversational agents.
2.2 Conceptual framework
Figure 1 presents the conceptual framework that guides the cost model. It draws on
the TOE perspective [14], treating firm size, data readiness, skills, vendor pricing, and
regulation as moderators, and on the process view of marketing in the AI literature [1], [4].
Five families of AI tools (generative AI, predictive analytics, conversational agents,
programmatic advertising optimisation, and AI -enabled CRM) act on five marketing
processes (content production, campaign execution, analytics and reporting, segmentation
and targeting, and coordination). Each process transformation feeds five cost mechanisms:
labour-hour reduction, agency substitution, media -spend wastage reduction, and two
cost-increasing mechanisms, namely licence, cloud, and API fees and the combined burden

2.3 Cost model
Monthly operating cost under scenario s is defined as the sum of six components,
following the TCO principle that all recurring and amortised costs attributable to the
technology must be counted [15]:
 Cs = Ls + Gs + Ws + Ts + Ks + Os (1)
where L is marketing labour, G external agency spending, W media -spend wastage,
T tooling (licences, cloud, and API usage), K amortised implementation and training, and
O the cost of AI oversight and rework. Following the logic of time -driven costing [16],
labour cost is derived from the time share of each marketing activity and the proportional
time reduction that AI achieves in that activity:
 Ls = N · c · (1 − ρ · Σa wa ra,s) (2)
where N is the number of FTE, c the fully loaded monthly cost per FTE, w a the share
of working time devoted to activity a, ra,s the proportional time reduction achieved in that
activity under scenario s, and ρ the realisation factor. The realisation factor is central:
because labour is lumpy, an SME that frees 20% of a marketer's time cannot usually reduce
payroll by 20%, so only a fraction ρ of the freed hours becomes avoided cost, while the
remainder is absorbed as slack or redeployed to non-cost-saving work. The time shares are
fixed at 35% for content production, 20% for campaign execution, 15% for analytics and
reporting, 10% for segmenta tion and targeting, and 20% for coordination and approvals.
Agency spending and media wastage are modelled as:
 Gs = G0 · (1 − σs);     Ws = B · ω · (1 − δs) (3)
where G0 is baseline agency spending, σ the share of agency work substituted by AI,
B the paid-media budget, ω the baseline share of media spend that is wasted (for example,
poorly targeted impressions), and δ the proportional reduction in that wastage owing to

AI-enabled targeting and bid optimisation. One-off implementation cost I and training cost
E are amortised over 36 months, and oversight is proportional to post-automation labour:
 Ks = (Is + Es) / 36;     Os = θs · Ls (4)
The saving relative to the baseline is Δs = C0 − Cs, and the simple payback period in
months is PB s = 36 · K s / (Δs + Ks), that is, one-off outlays divided by the operating saving
before amortisation. Tooling, implementation, and training costs are scaled with team size
through sub-proportional factors (0.6 + 0.4 N/3 for tooling and 0.7 + 0.3 N/3 for one -off
costs), reflect ing the fixed -cost element of SaaS subscriptions and integration work,
whereas agency spending and media budget scale proportionally with N.
2.4 Parameters and distributions
Every uncertain input is represented by a triangular distribution defined by a
minimum, a most likely value, and a maximum. The triangular form suits situations in
which only expert-style bounds are available and is easy for practitioners to modify. The
ranges in Table 1 were set to be plausible for a small marketing unit in a mid -income
market, with tool -related costs reflecting the price bands of commercially available SaaS
and CRM products and time reductions set conservatively, in line with the view tha t AI
gains are heterogeneous across tasks and are typically larger for routine analytical and
content tasks than for coordination [6], [1]. The parameters are assumptions, not estimates
from primary data, and their purpose is to make the model's logic expl icit; the sensitivity
analysis in Section 3.4 examines how much the conclusions depend on them.

2.5 Simulation procedure and sensitivity analysis
For each scenario, 10,000 independent parameter vectors were drawn using a fixed
random seed (42) and the model was evaluated for each draw, yielding empirical
distributions of monthly OPEX and of the saving relative to S0. Reported statistics
comprise means, standard deviations, the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles, the probability
that the saving is positive, and the median payback period. To answer RQ3, the procedure
was repeated for team sizes from one to eight FTE, with agency spending and media
budget scaled proportionally and tooling and one -off costs scaled sub -proportionally as
described above. To answer RQ4, global sensitivity was assessed by computing Spearman

3. Results
3.1 Level and composition of operating cost
For the reference three -FTE unit, the manual baseline S0 has a mean monthly OPEX
of USD 8,879 (standard deviation 775), of which labour accounts for USD 6,993 (78.8%),
and agency spending, media wastage, and basic tooling for the remainder. Under partial
AI adoption (S1), mean OPEX falls to USD 8,362, a mean saving of USD 517 or 5.8%. Under
integrated AI adoption (S2), mean OPEX is USD 8,523, a mean saving of USD 355 or 3.9%.
Figure 2 decomposes these totals and shows how the composition shifts: labour, age ncy,
and wastage components shrink, whereas tooling, amortised implementation and training,
and oversight components appear or grow.

Figure 2. Mean monthly marketing operating cost by component and scenario
(three-FTE team). Error bars show the 5th–95th percentile range of total OPEX.
The decomposition reveals an important asymmetry. Integrated AI (S2) removes
more gross cost than partial AI: labour falls by USD 1,526 (21.8%) compared with USD 867
(12.4%) under S1, and agency spending and wastage fall proportionally even more (Table
2 r eports the gross totals). However, the cost of the AI itself is much higher under S2:
tooling rises by about USD 997 relative to the baseline, amortised implementation and
training add USD 505, and oversight adds USD 293, for a total of roughly USD 1,795 o f
added cost, compared with about USD 618 under S1. Net of these costs, S1 saves USD 517
and S2 USD 355. In other words, at this scale, the additional automation ambition of S2 is
largely consumed by the additional cost of delivering it.

3.2 Uncertainty, risk, and payback
The distributions show that the expected saving is only part of the story. Under S1,
the 90% interval of the percentage saving runs from 1.7% to 10.0%, and the probability that
partial AI lowers OPEX is 99.1%. Under S2, the interval runs from −4.2% to 11.7%, and the
probability of positive savings falls to 79.0%; in about one case in five, integrated AI raises
operating cost relative to the manual baseline. The median payback period of one -off
outlays is 6.5 months for S1 but 20.9 months for S2, and 79.0% o f S2 draws recover their
investment within the 36 -month amortisation horizon. The dispersion of OPEX is similar
across scenarios, so the added risk of S2 lies in its thinner margin: adverse parameter
combinations are more likely to reverse the sign of the saving.
3.3 Effect of team size
Team size moderates the results strongly, as Figure 3(a) shows. For a one -person
marketing function, partial AI is roughly cost -neutral (mean change −0.1%, probability of
positive saving 49.3%), and integrated AI is clearly value-destroying (mean change −17.3%,
probability of saving 1.0%). At two FTE, S1 already delivers a 4.3% mean saving, while S2
remains slightly negative (−1.5%). From three FTE, both options are positive on average,
but S2 overtakes S1 in mean saving only at about four FTE (6.6% vs. 6.5 %), and it does so
decisively from five FTE (8.2% vs. 7.0%). At eight FTE, S2 saves 10.7% (about USD 2,533
per month) against 7.7% for S1. The pattern reflects the fixed -cost character of integration
and licensing, which is diluted only when more users share the platform.

4. Discussion
4.1 Interpretation of the main findings
The central finding is that AI can lower the operating cost of SME marketing, but that
the magnitude is modest, sensitive to scale, and highly dependent on managerial
conversion of efficiency into savings. A mean saving of 5.8% for partial adoption and 3.9%
for integrated adoption at three FTE is meaningful but far below the dramatic reductions
sometimes suggested in vendor communication, and the result is consistent with the
productivity-paradox argument that visible technological potential is partly offse t by
adaptation and complementary costs [3]. Most of the additional gross savings under S2 are
consumed by higher licence fees, amortised integration, and oversight.
The finding that partial adoption is the safer option for very small teams, while
integrated adoption becomes attractive only at around four to five FTE, adds nuance to the
diffusion and adoption literature. Rogers' diffusion framework [12] and the technol ogy
acceptance model [13] predict adoption on the basis of perceived advantage and ease of
use, and the TOE framework [14] adds organisational and environmental determinants.
The present results suggest that an additional economic condition should be consi dered:
the scale threshold at which fixed integration and licensing costs can be spread over
enough users. Below this threshold, an SME may find AI useful and easy to use and still
be worse off if it selects an over -engineered solution. This qualifies the assumption that
deeper integration is always better [9], [10].
4.2 Theoretical implications
The study contributes to theory in three ways. First, it connects the conceptual AI -in-
marketing frameworks [1], [2], [4] with cost -management theory by translating capability
typologies into activity -level time reductions and full -cost accounting [15], [1 6]. Second,
the realisation factor provides a parsimonious construct for a phenomenon that is well
known to practitioners but rarely formalised: efficiency gains and cost savings are different
quantities, and the gap between them is larger in small firms b ecause of labour
indivisibility. Third, evaluation of AI investments should be distributional: because the
probability that S2 reduces cost is only 79.0% at three FTE despite a positive mean, point
estimates give a misleading impression of certainty.
4.3 Managerial implications
Several practical recommendations follow. First, SMEs with one or two marketing
employees should begin with low -cost, subscription-based tools targeted at the highest -
yield activities (content production and analytics) and defer deep integration until scal e
justifies it. Second, managers should plan how freed hours will be converted into value
(reduced agency contracts, avoided hires, or redeployment to revenue work); without such
a plan the realisation factor stays low. Third, vendors with transparent, sca lable pricing
should be preferred. Fourth, oversight and training should be budgeted as explicit
recurring line items rather than hidden overhead. Finally, adoption should be staged with
measured checkpoints, in line with the feedback logic of Figure 1: a pilot with a defined
baseline OPEX, scaled up only if realised savings meet pre-agreed thresholds.
4.4 Limitations and directions for future research
The study has several limitations. The most important is that the results derive from
a simulation with assumed parameter distributions and not from primary firm-level data;
they show what follows from plausible assumptions but do not establish empirical

averages. Future research should calibrate the distributions with observed cost and time -
use data from SMEs and test whether the scale thresholds hold in practice. Second, the
model is static: it ignores learning effects and tool price changes, which are s alient in the
fast-moving generative AI market. Third, the model omits revenue effects, customer -
experience gains, and risk -related costs such as data -protection compliance; a fuller
evaluation would embed it in a profit or net -present-value framework. Finally, inputs are
drawn independently although some are correlated in reality, and the reference firm is
stylised; extensions could therefore incorporate correlated inputs, dynamic adoption
paths, and sector-specific calibrations informed by adoption theory [13], [14].

5. Conclusion
This paper examined how AI tools affect the operating cost of marketing in small and
medium-sized enterprises using an activity -based, full -cost model evaluated through
Monte Carlo simulation. For a reference three -person marketing unit, partial AI adoptio n
reduced mean monthly OPEX by 5.8% with a 99.1% probability of positive savings and a
median payback of about 6.5 months, whereas integrated adoption reduced it by 3.9% with
a 79.0% probability of positive savings and a median payback of about 20.9 months .
Integrated AI removes more gross cost but adds substantially more AI -related cost, and it
becomes clearly superior only when the marketing team reaches about five FTE. The
realisation of freed hours as actual savings and the recurring licence burden are the
dominant determinants of results. For SME managers, AI-enabled marketing digitalisation
is a cost -management decision that should be staged, measured, and matched to
organisational scale; for researchers, the cost side of AI in SMEs deserves distributi onal,
empirically calibrated analysis, for which the model reported here offers a transparent
starting point.
