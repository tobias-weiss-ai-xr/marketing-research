Abstract:
Generative AI (GenAI) has moved from a novelty demo to a wo rking part of marketing teams
it now drafts ad copy, designs images, writes product descriptions, and holds one -to-one
conversations with customers.  This report studies how GenAI  is changing branding and
marketing: what makes it different from older, prediction -based AI, where it is used across the
funnel, and what strategic value it creates. It examines Coca-Cola's Create Real Magic platform
as the major case study a genuine, wel l-documented example that shows both the creative
upside of GenAI and the reputational risk of leaning on it too heavily, as seen in the backlash
to Coca-Cola's 2024 AI Christmas ad.  It also looks at Indian examples, including Cadbury's
AI-personalised Diwali campaign with Shah Rukh Khan, to show GenAI adoption in Indian
marketing is not just a trend borrowed from the West.  The present research  proposes a simple
governance framework and sets out what marketing managers should do differently in a
GenAI-enabled workplace. The central argument: GenAI is very good at multiplying marketing
output, but it is human judgement not the model  that turns that output into brand value.
Keywords: Generative Marketing, Marketing Management, AI in Marketing, Digital
Marketing, Branding, Digital Branding.
1. INTRODUCTION
One example that makes the discussion around GenAI quite clear is Coca -Cola's Christmas
advertising. In November 2024, the company released a version of its famous "Holidays Are
Coming" advertisement made almost entirely with generative AI. The response was mixed, and
some viewers described the advertisement as "soulless" and even "a dystopian nightmare"
(NBC News, 2024). Coca-Cola later released an improved version, but it still received criticism
(TechRadar, 2025). This shows an important point for marketers: producing something quickly
and cheaply is useful, but it does not automatically make people connect with a brand. In simple
terms, generative AI refers to AI systems that can create new content such as text, images,
audio and video when a user gives them an instruction or prompt. Large language models and
image or video generation models are common examples. This is different from older AI
systems that mainly analysed information, classified it or made p redictions.

AI itself is not new to marketing. Marketers have already used it for activities such as ad
bidding, recommendations and customer analysis. The major change after ChatGPT became
publicly available in November 2022 was that AI also became capabl e of creating the actual
marketing material, rather than only helping marketers analyse data.
The adoption of GenAI has also been quite fast. According to Digital Applied (2026), the share
of marketers using GenAI in at least one regular workflow increased  from 51% in early 2024
to 87% in early 2026.
For this assignment, I am looking at GenAI through the usual marketing idea that promotion is
basically a way for a company to communicate with its customers (Kotler & Keller, 2021).
GenAI does not remove this basic idea. It mainly changes how the communication is created
and how quickly it can be delivered to a large number of people.
The report moves from concept to application to risk to a real case study, before narrowing
down to Indian marketers and a marke ting manager's day-to-day decisions.
3. CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK
3.1 What Is Generative AI?
Generative AI is a category of artificial intelligence trained on very large datasets of text,
images or audio, which learns underlying patterns well enough to generate  new, original -
looking output from an instruction (a "prompt"). Tools like ChatGPT, Gemini, Midjourney,
and Adobe Firefly fall in this category. What makes them useful for marketing is simple: they
turn a written brief into a first draft of copy, an image concept, or a video storyboard in seconds
instead of days.

3.3 Evolution of AI in Marketing
Generative AI is not something that suddenly appeared out of nowhere. It is part of a longer
development in marketing technology, starting with simple rule -based systems and later
moving towards predictive AI and now systems that can actually generate content.

a) Rule-based stage (pre-2010s): simple if-then logic for tasks like email segmentation.
b) Predictive/analytical stage (2010s):  machine learning improved targeting, scoring and
bidding, but only ranked or predicted it did not create.
c) Early generative stage (2014 –2019): GAN-based tools began producing images, mostly
in research and niche design use.
d) Mainstream GenAI stage (2022 onward):  ChatGPT's launch made large language
models accessible to non -technical marketers, and brands like Coca -Cola began GenAI
pilots within months.
e) Current stage (2024 –2026): GenAI is now embedded in everyday marketing workflows
and starting to run multi-step "agentic" tasks rather than single prompts.
4. Applications in Branding & Marketing
GenAI is also not limited to one particular marketing  activity. It can be used in many parts of
a marketing team's work. Some of the most useful areas are content creation, advertising, social
media, personalisation, storytelling and customer engagement.
4.1 Content Creation
a) Preparing first drafts of blog po sts, product descriptions, and email copy, which a human
editor then refines for brand voice.
b) Creating product and lifestyle images without arranging a physical photo shoot   useful for
e-commerce catalogues with thousands of SKUs.
4.2 Advertising
a) Creating many versions of ad copy and creatives for A/B testing within a single campaign
brief.
b) Coca-Cola's own "Create Real Magic" platform combined OpenAI's GPT -4 and DALL-E
to let both the brand and the public generate advertising assets from Coca -Cola's visual
archive (Coca-Cola Company, 2023)   discussed in depth in Section 8.

Page 5
c) Programmatic ad platforms increasingly use GenAI to auto-generate creative that matches
a target audience segment in real time.
4.3 Social Media
a) Creating captions, hashtags and different  versions of posts for each platform (LinkedIn vs
Instagram vs X).
b) Turning a single campaign asset into several localised or platform -specific formats within
minutes.
4.4 Personalisation
a) Changing product descriptions and offers according to a customer's  browsing behaviour
or location.
b) Cadbury's AI -personalised Diwali campaign generated location -specific ads naming
individual local retailers, reaching over 1,800 small businesses across multiple Indian
cities (Exchange4media, 2021)   examined further in Section 9.
4.5 Brand Storytelling
a) Building interactive, participatory campaigns where consumers co -create brand content
rather than only consuming it   exactly what Coca-Cola attempted by inviting the public to
generate art using its brand assets.
b) Risk: stor ytelling generated too quickly, without enough human craft, can feel hollow
rather than emotional   the core criticism of Coca -Cola's 2024 holiday ad (NBC News,
2024).
4.6 Customer Engagement
a) Chatbots that can answer product questions and help customers during the buying process,
on the brand's own website or WhatsApp.
b) 24/7 multilingual communication is especially useful in India because customers speak
many different languages.

is available.
6. Strategic Benefits
https://doi.org/10.33774/coe-2026-433tc Content not peer-reviewed by Cambridge University Press. License: CC BY 4.0

Page 7
a) Speed is one of the biggest advantages. A first draft of an ad, email or social m edia post
that could take hours can now be prepared in a few minutes. This gives the team more time
to edit the work and focus on the actual campaign.
b) Cost can also be an advantage, especially for smaller brands and local businesses   like the
1,800+ retailers reached by Cadbury's campaign   can access celebrity -level, personalised
advertising that would otherwise be unaffordable (Exchange4media, 2021).
c) Personalisation is another major benefit. GenAI can create many small variations of the
same message, something manual copywriting cannot do at that volume.
d) It also makes experimentation easier because marketers can test more creative versions
before spending a large amount on media, improving the odds of finding what works.
e) Another interesti ng benefit is that tools such as Create Real Magic allow ordinary
consumers to take part in creating brand content, rather than leaving all the creative work
to agencies   turning marketing into genuine co -creation (Forbes, 2023).
7. Risks and Limitations
7.1 Hallucination
a) GenAI models can state incorrect facts, prices, or claims confidently and fluently, with no
built-in signal separating a correct answer from a wrong one.
b) For a marketing chatbot or AI -written product page, a hallucinated claim about
specifications, discounts, or availability becomes a real, publishable brand promise unless
a human checks it first.
7.2 Copyright
a) GenAI models are trained on large amounts of internet content, and it is often unclear
whether that training used licensed materia l   a live legal question, not a settled one.
b) The New York Times' copyright lawsuit against OpenAI and Microsoft, filed December
2023, is still active in 2026 and remains one of the most closely watched tests of whether
training AI on copyrighted material counts as fair use (LegalClarity, 2026).
7.3 Privacy

Personalisation depends on customer data, and prompts fed into GenAI  tools can
unintentionally expose personal or confidential customer information if teams are not careful
about what they paste into external AI tools.
7.4 Bias
Because GenAI learns from existing data, it can reproduce demographic, cultural, or gender
stereotypes present in that data   a real risk for brand imagery meant to represent customers
fairly.
7.5 Brand Consistency
AI-generated content can drift in tone unless guided by brand guidelines, style prompts, and
human review   inconsistent voice across channels weakens brand identity over time.
7.6 Consumer Trust

wheels that appeared to glide instead of turn (NBC News, 2024; TechRadar, 2024). Consumer
trust in a beloved brand asset can be damaged, not helped, by visible over -reliance on AI.

8. Major Case Study: Coca-Cola's "Create Real Magic"
8.1 Background
a) In February 2023, Bain & Company announced a services alliance with OpenAI, and
Coca-Cola became the first company to sign on (Bain & Company, n.d.).
b) A month later, Coca -Cola launched "Create Real Magic"    a platform built by OpenAI
and Bain that combined GPT -4 and DALL -E, letting digital creatives generate original
artwork using Coca -Cola's own brand assets, such as the contour bottle, the Spencerian
script logo, and its Santa Claus and polar bear imagery (Coca -Cola Company, 2023).
8.2 Strategy and AI Application
a) The platform was consumer -facing, not agency -only: anyone could visit
createrealmagic.com, use branded creative elements as a starting canvas, and generate
art with AI (Marketing Dive, 2023).
b) Top submissions were featured on digital billboards in Times Square and Piccadilly
Circus, and thirty creators were invited to a workshop at Coca -Cola's Atlanta
headquarters (Coca-Cola Company, 2023).
c) Strategically, this let Coca -Cola treat its "highest -profile advertising assets" as a
shared creative canvas rather than a tightly controlled property   a genuine shift in
how a heritage brand approached brand control (Marketing Dive, 2023).
8.3 Outcome
a) The platform received over 120,000 AI -generated submissions, and global CMO Manolo
Arroyo described the company as "just scratching the surface" of what GenAI could do for
its marketing model (VentureBeat, 2023; Marketing Dive, 2023).
https://doi.org/10.33774/coe-2026-433tc Content not peer-reviewed by Cambridge University Press. License: CC BY 4.0

Page 10
b) Coca-Cola went on to use GenAI well beyond this platform, including a five -year, $1.1
billion generative AI deal with Microsoft (Advertising Week, 2025).
c) The story did not stay entirely positive: Coca -Cola's 2024 fully AI -generated Christmas
advert drew backlash for feeling "soull ess" and for flaws like truck wheels that glided
instead of rotating (NBC News, 2024). Its improved 2025 version, using animals instead
of human characters, reduced but did not eliminate the criticism (TechRadar, 2025).
8.4 Marketing Lessons
a) Co-creation wo rks when brand assets are strong.  Create Real Magic succeeded partly
because Coca-Cola's visual identity is instantly recognisable   GenAI amplified an existing
brand story rather than inventing a new one.
b) Novelty wears off; craft does not.  Goodwill from the 2023 platform did not automatically
transfer to the 2024 AI -made ad, because audiences judge finished brand films by
emotional craft, not by which tool made them.
c) Partnership matters.  Coca-Cola leaned on Bain & Company for strategy and OpenAI for
the technology (Bain & Company, n.d.)   a sensible model for brands without in -house AI
expertise.
d) Scale needs a safety net.  With 120,000+ submissions, Coca -Cola needed clear content -
moderation and brand -safety rules   a governance question every open GenAI camp aign
has to answer (Section 11).
9. INDIAN MARKET PERSPECTIVE
a) Indian businesses are not lagging in GenAI adoption   Adobe's 2025 India survey found
that 23% of Indian businesses already show measurable ROI from GenAI, the highest
reported figure in the Asia-Pacific region (Adobe, 2025).
b) Executives in the same survey pointed to faster content ideation and production (73%) and
improved productivity (67%) as the biggest benefits so far (Adobe, 2025).
c) Cadbury's "Not Just A Cadbury Ad" is arguably India's best-known GenAI marketing case.
In its 2021 edition, Mondelez India worked with Ogilvy and the AI video platform

Lucknow, Indore and Pune (Exchange4media, 2021).

11. GENAI GOVERNANCE FRAMEWORK
The risks discussed earlier, along with the Coca -Cola example, show why companies need
some basic rules for using GenAI. Instead of making a new decision for every campaign, a
company can create a common process that employees can follow.

https://doi.org/10.33774/coe-2026-433tc Content not peer-reviewed by Cambridge University Press. License: CC BY 4.0

Page 13
Figure 3: A six-step governance framework for using generative AI responsibly in marketing.
a) Policy first: define what GenAI  can and cannot be used for, including brand -voice rules
and mandatory disclosure of AI-assisted content, before any team starts using it informally.
b) Risk-tier every use case:  a low -stakes internal brainstorming prompt needs far less
oversight than a customer-facing chatbot answer or a public ad   treat them differently.
c) Keep a human in the loop:  every AI draft that reaches a customer should pass a fact
check, a legal/copyright check, and a bias check before publishing   not after a complaint
arrives.
d) Require disclosure and approval:  as regulators pay closer attention to AI -generated
advertising claims, a documented sign-off step protects the brand as much as the customer.
e) Monitor after launch:  track not just performance metrics but also complaints and signs
of factual drift, since a model's outputs can change as underlying tools are updated.
f) Name an accountable owner:  every AI -generated asset should have one person
responsible f or it   "the AI made a mistake" is not a defence that regulators, courts, or
customers are likely to accept (a pattern consistent with the copyright and liability concerns
discussed in Section 7).
12. Implications for Marketing Managers
a) Budget for review t ime, not just generation time.  The cost saving from GenAI is partly
offset by the human hours needed to fact -check, edit, and approve output   plan for both.
b) Build prompt and brand -voice guidelines.  A well -written prompt with brand tone,
audience, and cons traints produces far better first drafts than a vague one   this is now a
core marketing skill.
c) Reassess agency and freelancer briefs.  Some routine content work will shift in -house as
GenAI lowers the cost of a first draft; agencies that add value will offer strategy and craft,
not just production.
d) Treat every GenAI campaign as a brand-risk decision, not only a creative one   Coca-Cola's
case shows even a well-resourced, admired brand can misjudge audience reaction to visible
AI use.
e) Keep training current,  since tool limitations (hallucination rates, bias patterns, copyright
terms) change quickly and a governance framework set once will fall behind.

13. FUTURE OF GENAI IN MARKETING
a) Agentic marketing: GenAI tools are moving from answering single prompts to running
multi-step tasks with less supervision, such as drafting, testing, and optimising a campaign
end to end.
b) Better grounding:  future tools are likely to reduce hallucination by linking o utputs to
verified brand and product data, rather than relying purely on general training data.
c) Clearer copyright and disclosure rules:  as cases like the New York Times' lawsuit
against OpenAI move toward resolution, marketers can expect clearer legal boun daries
and probably new licensing costs (LegalClarity, 2026).
d) Video generation will mature quickly.  Coca-Cola's own progress between its criticised
2024 ad and its improved 2025 version shows how fast AI video quality is advancing
(TechRadar, 2025).
e) Human craft becomes the differentiator, not the tool itself.  As GenAI access becomes
near-universal among competitors, brands that stand out will use it as a starting point for
better human storytelling, not a replacement for it.
14. CONCLUSION
a) Generative AI has genuinely changed branding and marketing   not as a passing trend, but
as a working tool now embedded in content creation, advertising, personalisation, and
customer engagement.
b) The benefits discussed in this assignment are significant. G enAI can save time, reduce
some production costs and make personalisation possible at a scale that would be difficult
for a small marketing team to manage manually.
c) At the same time, the Coca -Cola case shows that simply using advanced AI does not
guarantee a successful campaign. Coca -Cola's 2023 co -creation initiative received a very
different response from its largely AI -made 2024 Christmas advertisement, even though
both involved generative AI.
d) The important difference was the way the technology was used.  Human judgement,
creative direction and understanding of the brand still played a major role in how the final
content was received.
