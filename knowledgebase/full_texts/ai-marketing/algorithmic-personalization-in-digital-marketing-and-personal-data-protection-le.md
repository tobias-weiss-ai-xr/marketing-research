This article examines the legal boundaries of algorithmic personalization in
digital marketing within the framework of the European Union's General Data
Protection Regulation (GDPR), the ePrivacy Directive, and emerging
regulatory instruments such as the Digital Services Act (DSA) and the Artificial
Intelligence Act (AI Act). Through a doctrinal and comparative legal
methodology, the study critically analyzes three interconnected regulatory
dimensions: the validity and effectiveness of consent as a legal basis for data-
driven advertising; the legal constraints governing profiling and automated
decision-making under Articles 4(4) and 22 GDPR; and the normative limits
of targeted advertising in the context of fundamental rights to privacy and
data protection. Drawing on regulatory guidance from the European Data
Protection Board (EDPB), the Article 29 Working Party, and relevant case law
from the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU), the analysis reveals
that the consent -based model suffers from struct ural deficiencies, including
information asymmetry, consent fatigue, and the commodification of personal
data. Profiling, while defined and regulated, remains insufficiently
constrained due to ambiguities in the scope of solely automated decisions and
the lack of robust explainability requirements. The article proposes a rights -
based regulatory framework that moves beyond the notice -and-consent
paradigm toward substantive protections incorporating purpose limitation,
data minimization, algorithmic transpare ncy, and meaningful human
oversight. The findings contribute to the ongoing scholarly and policy debate
on reconciling innovation in digital marketing with the protection of
individuals' fundamental rights in the data-driven economy.

1. Introduction
The digital economy has undergone a profound transformation driven by the capacity of commercial
entities to collect, aggregate, and process vast quantities of personal data for the purpose of
delivering algorithmically personalized marketing content. Algo rithmic personalization refers to the

automated process by which digital platforms and advertisers deploy machine learning algorithms,
predictive analytics, and behavioral tracking technologies to tailor commercial communications,
product recommendations, and pricing strategies to individual c onsumers based on inferred
preferences, browsing histories, geolocation data, and psychographic profiles (Zuiderveen Borgesius,
2016; Veale & Edwards, 2018). This practice has become the cornerstone of the contemporary
advertising ecosystem, in which personal data functions as both the currency of digital exchange and
the raw material for value extraction (Zuboff, 2019).
The economic incentives underlying algorithmic personalization are substantial. Research by Aridor,
Che, and Salz (2023) demonstrates that privacy regulation such as the GDPR produced a 12.5%
reduction in trackable consumers, yet the remaining opt -in users  became more predictable and
valuable to advertisers, illustrating the complex interplay between privacy regulation and the data -
driven advertising market. The tension between the commercial benefits of personalization and the
fundamental rights of individuals to privacy and data protection constitutes one of the most pressing
regulatory challenges of the twenty-first century (Acquisti, Brandimarte, & Loewenstein, 2020).
The European Union has positioned itself at the forefront of data protection regulation through the
adoption of the General Data Protection Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2016/679, hereinafter "GDPR"),
which entered into force on 25 May 2018. The GDPR establi shes a comprehensive legal framework
governing the processing of personal data, including specific provisions addressing consent (Articles
6 and 7), automated decision -making and profiling (Articles 4(4) and 22), and transparency
obligations (Articles 13 a nd 14). These provisions are supplemented by the ePrivacy Directive
(Directive 2002/58/EC), which regulates the use of cookies and similar tracking technologies, and by
the emerging Digital Services Act (DSA, Regulation (EU) 2022/2065) and the Artificial Intelligence Act
(AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689), which introduce additional constraints on algorithmic systems
used in digital advertising.
Despite this extensive regulatory architecture, significant normative and practical gaps persist. The
consent-based model that underpins much of the GDPR's legitimacy framework has been subject to
sustained scholarly criticism. Południak -Gierz (2019) demon strates that the GDPR's consent -based
protection in the context of personalization during the pre -contractual stage resulted in a
multiplication of the information presented to consumers, paradoxically undermining informed
decision-making. Similarly, the provisions governing profiling and automated decision-making, while
representing a significant normative advance, remain hampered by interpretive ambiguities
regarding the scope of "solely automated" decisions and the extent of the right to explanation (Brkan,
2019; Veale & Edwards, 2018).
This article aims to provide a comprehensive legal analysis of the regulatory limits governing
algorithmic personalization in digital marketing. The research addresses three interconnected
questions: (1) To what extent does the consent mechanism under the GDPR provide effective
protection against invasive data-driven advertising practices? (2) What are the legal constraints and
shortcomings governing profiling and automated decision-making in the context of digital marketing?
(3) How do emerging regulatory instruments reshape the normative framework for targeted
advertising? The analysis contributes to the scholarly debate by proposing a rights-based framework

2. Methodological Framework
This study employs a doctrinal legal research methodology combined with a comparative regulatory
analysis. Doctrinal research, understood as the systematic exposition, interpretation, and critical
evaluation of legal norms, constitutes the primary methodological approach (Hutchinson & Duncan,
2012). The analysis focuses on the normative framework established by the GDPR, the ePrivacy
Directive, the Digital Services Act, and the Artificial Intelligence Act, complemented by authoritative
interpretive guidance from the European Data Protection Board (EDPB), the Article 29 Working Party,
and relevant jurisprudence from the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU).
The comparative dimension of the study draws on scholarly literature from multiple jurisdictions,
including analysis of the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) and Brazil's Lei Geral de Proteção de
Dados (LGPD), to situate the European approach within the broader global regulatory landscape. The
literature review was conducted using academic databases including Scopus, Web of Science, and
specialized legal research platforms, with search parameters encompassing the period from 2016 to
2026. Sources were verified for authenticity and scholarly quality using the Scite and Consensus
academic research tools.
The analytical framework is structured around three principal normative pillars: (i) the legal basis of
consent and its structural limitations in the digital advertising context; (ii) the regulatory framework
governing profiling and automated decision-making; and (iii) the specific legal constraints applicable
to targeted advertising. Each pillar is examined through the lens of the fundamental rights to privacy
and data protection enshrined in Articles 7 and 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the
European Union.
3. Theoretical and Conceptual Framework
3.1 The Data-Driven Advertising Ecosystem
The contemporary digital advertising ecosystem operates through a complex infrastructure of
intermediary entities and technologies that facilitate the collection, aggregation, and monetization
of personal data. As Ullah et al. (2022) comprehensively docume nt, this ecosystem encompasses
advertising networks, demand-side platforms, data management platforms, and analytics companies
that process user data to construct behavioral profiles enabling targeted ad delivery.
Seabra, Glatzl, and Lifschitz (2024) provide empirical evidence of the mechanisms of what Zuboff
(2019) terms "surveillance capitalism," demonstrating through network traffic analysis how various
entities track and harvest digital footprints during web nav igation. This data -driven model creates
what Büchi et al. (2020) characterize as substantial power asymmetries between users and platforms,
where inferences and the increasingly automated nature of decision -making based on user data
become essential aspect s of profiling. Figure 1 illustrates the structure of this ecosystem and the

3.2 Informational Self-Determination and the Privacy Paradox
The theoretical foundation of data protection regulation in the European tradition rests on the
concept of informational self -determination (informationelle Selbstbestimmung), as articulated by
the German Federal Constitutional Court in its landmark 1983 C ensus Decision. This principle posits
that individuals possess a fundamental right to determine the disclosure and use of their personal
data. Within the GDPR framework, consent serves as the primary mechanism through which this
principle is operationalize d, premised on the assumption that individuals are capable of making
rational decisions regarding their data when provided with adequate information (Clifford, Graef, &
Valcke, 2018). However, extensive behavioral research has documented a persistent gap between
individuals' stated privacy preferences and their actual data-sharing behavior, a phenomenon known
as the "privacy paradox." Acquisti, Brandimarte, and Loewenstein (2020) synthesize decades of social
science research demonstrating that consumers fundamentally care about online privacy and take
numerous actions to protect it, yet find it prohibitively difficult to attain desired levels of privacy
through individual action alone. This finding has profound implications for the consent-based model,
as it suggests that the conditions for autonomous, informed decision -making are structurally
undermined in the digital advertising context.
3.3 Profiling as a Socio-Legal Construct
Profiling, as defined in Article 4(4) GDPR, encompasses any form of automated processing of personal
data consisting of the use of personal data to evaluate certain personal aspects relating to a natural
person, particularly to analyze or predict aspects c oncerning that person's performance at work,
economic situation, health, personal preferences, interests, reliability, behavior, location, or

Journal of Intelligent Decision Making and Information Science
Volume 3(8s), (2026) 1318-1332
1322
movements. Mann and Matzner (2019) argue that with increased algorithmic complexity, biases
embedded in profiling systems become more sophisticated and difficult to identify, control for, or
contest. Their analysis highlights a critical limitation of priva cy and data protection rights: their
individual focus does not protect against the discriminatory potential of algorithmic profiling that
operates at the group level.
Kaltheuner and Bietti (2018) observe that profiling is a relatively novel concept in European data
protection law, now explicitly defined in the GDPR. Through profiling, highly sensitive details can be
inferred or predicted from seemingly innocuous data, leading to comprehensive profiles that may or
may not be accurate or fair. These profiles are increasingly deployed to make or inform consequential
decisions, from credit scoring to hiring, policing, and targeted advertising.

The first deficiency pertains to information asymmetry and cognitive overload. The GDPR mandates

the broader digital economy. Zelianin (2022) documents how companies primarily choose consent or
legitimate interests to process personal data, transforming consent into a gateway for data extraction
rather than a genuine expression of autonomous choice.
4.3 Consent and the ePrivacy Framework
The relationship between GDPR consent requirements and the ePrivacy Directive introduces
additional complexity. Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive requires prior informed consent for the
storage of, or access to, information stored in a user's terminal  equipment, effectively mandating
consent for the use of cookies and similar tracking technologies. The CJEU's judgment in Planet49
(Case C-673/17) confirmed that active consent is required for the placement of cookies, rejecting pre-
ticked checkboxes. The  interplay between GDPR and ePrivacy consent creates a layered consent
architecture that, while theoretically robust, poses significant practical challenges and has given rise
to the proliferation of cookie consent banners designed to manipulate users thro ugh dark patterns
(Nouwens et al., 2020).

5.2 Interpretive Challenges: "Solely Automated" and "Significant Effects"
The application of Article 22 to digital marketing practices is complicated by interpretive difficulties.
Binns and Veale (2021) raise five distinct complications relating to interpreting Article 22 in the
context of multi-stage profiling systems, including the potential for selective automation on subsets
of data subjects despite generally adequate human input, and the ambiguity around where to locate
the decision itself. They argue that the nature of these challenges will make it difficult for courts or
regulators to distil a set of clear, fair, and consistent interpretations.
The notion of decisions that "similarly significantly affect" the data subject remains underspecified.
The Article 29 Working Party's guidelines suggest that targeted advertising can in certain
circumstances constitute a decision with significant effects, particularly where it affects the prices
offered, manipulates vulnerable individuals, or results in discriminatory treatment. Wiedemann
(2022) proposes a two-step approach in which profiling is considered step one and decision -making
step two, with important implications for the allocation of legal responsibility.

GDPR obliges the controller to inform the data subject of th e reasons why an automated decision
was taken. However, De Laat (2022) conjectures that in a climate of intensified protection of

6. Targeted Advertising: Regulatory Boundaries and Emerging Constraints
6.1 The Legal Qualification of Targeted Advertising
Targeted advertising involves the delivery of commercial messages to individuals based on their
observed online behavior, inferred characteristics, or predicted preferences. From a data protection
perspective, this practice necessarily involves the process ing of personal data, including browsing

Journal of Intelligent Decision Making and Information Science
Volume 3(8s), (2026) 1318-1332
1327
histories, click patterns, purchase records, geolocation data, and derived profiles, all of which fall
within the scope of the GDPR. Van der Hof and Lievens (2020) highlight the particular vulnerabilities
of children, documenting how complex revenue models  create value by feeding children's data into
algorithms to profile them and offer personalized advertising.
6.2 The Digital Services Act and the Prohibition of Profiling-Based Advertising
The Digital Services Act (DSA) introduces significant new constraints. Article 26 DSA requires
providers of online platforms to clearly label advertisements and disclose targeting parameters.
Crucially, Article 28(2) DSA prohibits the presentation of adver tisements based on profiling using
special categories of personal data as defined in Article 9(1) GDPR, and Article 28(3) extends
additional protection to minors. These provisions represent a significant normative shift from the
consent-based paradigm toward substantive prohibitions.
6.3 The Artificial Intelligence Act and High-Risk Algorithmic Systems
The AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) establishes a risk -based regulatory framework with
implications for algorithmic personalization. Article 5(1)(a) prohibits AI systems that deploy
subliminal techniques beyond a person's consciousness to materially distort behavior. Article 5(1)(b)
prohibits the exploitation of vulnerabilities of specific groups due to age, disability, or social or
economic situation. These prohibitions have direct implications for personalization systems that
employ manipulative techniques or exploit vulnerable populations.

8. Discussion: Toward a Rights-Based Regulatory Framework
8.1 The Inadequacy of the Notice-and-Consent Paradigm
The analysis reveals fundamental inadequacies in the notice -and-consent paradigm. The structural
deficiencies of consent, including information asymmetry, cognitive overload, consent fatigue, and
the manipulation of choice architecture through dark pattern s, systematically undermine
autonomous, informed decision-making. The privacy paradox documented by Acquisti, Brandimarte,
and Loewenstein (2020) further demonstrates that even privacy -conscious individuals struggle to
translate their preferences into effective privacy-protective behavior.
The commodification of personal data within the digital advertising ecosystem creates economic
incentives that structurally favor data extraction over data protection. When consent is transformed
from a genuine expression of autonomous choice into a transa ctional prerequisite for accessing

Journal of Intelligent Decision Making and Information Science
Volume 3(8s), (2026) 1318-1332
1329
digital services, its normative function as a guardian of informational self -determination is
fundamentally compromised.
8.2 Strengthening Profiling Regulation
The interpretive challenges surrounding Article 22 GDPR require legislative and judicial clarification.
The multi-stage profiling systems analyzed by Binns and Veale (2021) demonstrate that the binary
distinction between automated and human decision -making is increasingly inadequate. A more
nuanced approach would recognize the spectrum of automation and establish graduated obligations
corresponding to the degree of algorithmic influence.
The right to explanation requires substantive strengthening. The tension between algorithmic
transparency and intellectual property protection, documented by De Laat (2022) and Battaglini et
al. (2019), necessitates legislative guidance on balancing these competing interests, including
standardized explanation frameworks and independent algorithmic audit mechanisms.

A comprehensive rights -based framework should integrate: (i) strengthened purpose limitation
requirements preventing repurposing of data for unrelated advertising; (ii) enhanced data
minimization obligations; (iii) robust algorithmic transparency requireme nts; (iv) mandatory human
oversight mechanisms for high -impact decisions; (v) independent algorithmic audit and
accountability structures; and (vi) effective enforcement mechanisms including significant
administrative fines and collective redress options.
9. Conclusions
This article has provided a comprehensive legal analysis of the regulatory limits governing algorithmic
personalization in digital marketing within the European data protection framework. The analysis
demonstrates that while the GDPR, supplemented by the ePrivacy Directive, the DSA, and the AI Act,
establishes a multi-layered regulatory architecture, significant normative and practical gaps persist.
The consent-based model suffers from structural deficiencies that undermine its effectiveness as a
mechanism for protecting informational self -determination. The provisions governing profiling and
automated decision -making are hampered by interpretive ambi guities and the tension between
algorithmic transparency and intellectual property protection. The emerging prohibitions introduced
by the DSA and the AI Act signal a welcome movement toward substantive protections, but their
implementation and enforcement remain to be fully tested.
The article proposes a rights-based regulatory framework that moves beyond the notice-and-consent
paradigm toward substantive protections incorporating purpose limitation, data minimization,
algorithmic transparency, and meaningful human oversight. Such a framework acknowledges that in
the context of pervasive algorithmic personalization, individual consent alone cannot bear the
normative weight of legitimizing data processing practices that systematically affect fundamental
rights. The protection of individuals in the data-driven economy requires a combination of individual
rights and structural regulations that constrain the design and deployment of algorithmic systems,
ensuring that innovation in digital marketing proceeds within boundaries that respect human dignity,
privacy, and the right to data protection.
Future research should address the empirical dimensions of enforcement effectiveness, the impact
of emerging technologies such as federated learning and privacy -enhancing computation on the
regulatory calculus, and the development of sector-specific codes of conduct that operationalize the
principles identified in this analysis.
