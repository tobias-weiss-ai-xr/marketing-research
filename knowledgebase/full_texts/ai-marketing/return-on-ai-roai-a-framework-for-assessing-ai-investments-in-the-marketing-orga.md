Abstract
Artificial intelligence (AI) is reshaping marketing as firms increasingly delegate activities and decisions previously per -
formed by marketers to AI systems. As AI assumes a greater role in market-facing decisions, understanding the value firms
realize from these investments becomes increasingly important. Building on marketing accountability and resource-based
theory, we develop a Return on AI (RoAI) framework that conceptualizes AI investment through the people, processes,
and capabilities that enable its deployment and governance. RoAI evaluates incremental benefit while accounting for
value retention, evidence durability, and the full costs of deployment over time. The framework identifies a distinctive
consequence of delegated judgment, wherein AI can improve current performance while reducing the experiential prac -
tice through which marketers develop and maintain the judgment required to evaluate and redirect the AI solution. This
mechanism helps explain heterogeneity in AI returns across firms and over time. We develop seven testable predictions
and an agenda for research on creating, appropriating, and sustaining value from AI.

Introduction
Marketing organizations increasingly budget for artificial
intelligence (AI) as a recurring line item. 1 A 2025 Boston
Consulting Group (BCG) survey, for example, found that
71% of chief marketing officers planned to invest more than
$10 million annually in generative AI over the next three
years, up from 57% one year earlier (Boston Consulting
Group, 2025). Similarly, BCG’s 2026 AI Radar, based on
responses from nearly 2,400 executives, reported that large
firms expected AI spending to rise from approximately 0.8%
to 1.7% of revenue, and 94% of the organizations surveyed
said that they would continue investing even if AI did not
deliver returns in 2026 (Boston Consulting Group, 2026).
The surveys identify that firms are prioritizing personaliza -
tion, video generation, and agentic systems as AI use cases.

systems that use data, algorithms, and model-based learning to gener-
ate predictions, recommendations, content, or decisions that support,
automate, or perform tasks that ordinarily require human intelligence.

Notwithstanding this optimism, marketing organiza -
tions still lack a way to comprehensively assess the return
on their AI investments. Firms continue to treat AI adop -
tion and deployment, productivity, revenue growth, or cost
savings as evidence that an AI investment has succeeded,
even though each of these outcomes captures only part of
the investment’s economic consequences. As a result, some
firms conclude that their AI investments deliver no value,
whereas others overstate the value delivered. A widely dis -
cussed review of 300 publicly disclosed enterprise initia -
tives concluded that 95% of the organizations examined had
realized no measurable return on generative AI spending
estimated at $30 billion to $40 billion (Challapally et al.,
2025). McKinsey’s 2025 global survey found, in turn, that
marketing and sales were among the functions that most fre-
quently reported AI-related revenue gains, yet only 39% of
respondents attributed any enterprise-level earnings impact
to AI, and most of those attributed less than 5% of earnings
to it (McKinsey & Company, 2025). Similarly, PwC’s 2026
survey of 4,454 chief executives found that 56% reported
no significant financial benefit from AI during the preced -
ing year, whereas 12% reported both revenue and cost ben-
efits (PwC, 2026). These findings are informative, but they
record outcomes at a single point in time and say little about
why returns vary so widely across firms that adopt similar
AI use cases.
Marketing accountability research provides the nec -
essary starting point for answering this question. Prior
literature shows that firms should evaluate marketing expen-
ditures through their incremental effects on cash flows, cus-
tomer equity, and firm value (Hanssens & Pauwels, 2016;
Katsikeas et al., 2016; Rust et al., 2004). The same work
calls for a credible counterfactual, an explicit evaluation
horizon, and measures that connect marketing activity to
economic outcomes. These principles remain essential for
understanding the returns on AI investments. Applying these
principles to AI, however, requires revisiting three assump-
tions that were often implicit in evaluations of earlier mar -
keting technologies. First, the performance of an AI system
can change after launch as the model, its inputs, customer
behavior, or the provider’s architecture evolves. Second,
the budget allocated to the investment at authorization can
overlook later expenditures on workflow redesign, verifica-
tion, monitoring, employee development, and governance.
Third, the value created by a deployment can accrue across
functions or be captured by model providers, infrastructure
vendors, scarce technical labor, and customers. As a result,
an analysis that holds performance, cost, or value capture
constant can misstate the investment’s value even when its
initial outcome measure is accurate.
To explain why these assumptions become so impor -
tant, we situate the AI investment within the marketing
organization. We define the marketing organization as the
configuration of people, processes, and capabilities through
which a firm makes and implements market-facing deci -
sions. People contribute expertise, experience, and judg -
ment. Processes coordinate recurring activities such as
segmentation, campaign design, pricing, service, and reten-
tion. Capabilities represent the accumulated ability to com -
bine these resources in ways that produce market outcomes.
The resource-based view suggests that a technology’s value
depends on the complementary assets through which the
firm deploys it (Barney, 1991; Bharadwaj, 2000; Ray et al.,
2004). Hence, the relevant AI investment includes the AI
system together with the data, redesigned processes, over -
sight routines, managerial attention, and capability devel -
opment that support its productive use. Accordingly, firms
that license similar models can realize very different returns
because these complements differ in quality and require
time to accumulate (Dierickx & Cool, 1989; Cohen &
Levinthal, 1990).
This organizational lens also clarifies why AI presents
a distinctive value-assessment problem. Roughly once
a decade for the past six decades, a new technology has
reshaped the marketing organization (e.g., mass media,
customer databases, customer relationship management
systems, digital channels, and marketing analytics) (Kumar
& Reinartz, 2016; Wedel & Kannan, 2016). Each changed
the resources available to marketers, and each left the judg-
ment with them. We summarize these changes along four
dimensions, which we label as reach, memory, speed, and
judgment. Earlier technologies usually had a primary locus
of influence. For example, mass media expanded aggregate
reach, databases extended organizational memory, digital
channels accelerated interaction, and analytics strength -
ened the information available for managerial judgment.
Predictive, generative, and agentic AI systems, by compari-
son, affect all four dimensions within the same deployment
while exercising part of the judgment that links information
to action. The behavior of the AI system thus becomes part
of the investment outcome.
Delegating judgment also reduces the practice through
which marketers develop and maintain it. A retention team,
for example, learns to recognize a failing offer strategy by
designing, executing, and reviewing campaigns. When an
AI agent assumes those activities, campaign performance
may improve even as the team has fewer opportunities to
exercise the judgment needed to oversee the agent. The
organization can therefore become less able to direct, chal -
lenge, or correct the system while its current performance
remains strong. In conventional resource-accumulation
models, capability erosion follows when investment lapses
(Dierickx & Cool, 1989). Delegated judgment creates a dif-
ferent pattern, where the firm continues to invest and see the

Against this background, we discuss AI as an organi -
zational investment whose performance, cost, and conse -
quences change over time. Existing research offers detailed
accounts of AI capabilities, task allocation, consumer
responses, ethical risks, and organizational adoption (Dav -
enport et al., 2020; Hermann et al., 2024; Huang & Rust,
2021; Plangger et al., 2022). Recent conceptual agendas
also examine generative AI and the changing scope of mar-
keting work (Cillo & Rubera, 2025; Grewal et al., 2025).
These streams, however, do not provide an integrated
account of how an AI deployment creates and sustains value
for the firm. An AI system can improve targeting, conver -
sion, or employee productivity and still produce a disap -
pointing return once the analysis includes implementation,
oversight, and costs. Conversely, an early deployment can
appear uneconomic even as it builds the data, routines, and
expertise that improve later investments. A framework for
AI investment therefore has to explain not just what an
application delivers, but whether that value lasts and stays
with the firm.
Accordingly, we develop Return on AI (RoAI) as a frame-
work for assessing the full economic value of AI invest -
ments in marketing. Although its economic logic extends
beyond marketing, we develop the framework in the mar -
keting organization because firms have far less experience
with technologies that perform market-facing judgment
than with those that expand reach, memory, or speed. The
consequences of that delegation can be substantial for both
current performance and the capabilities required to gov -
ern future decisions. RoAI begins with gross incremental
benefit relative to a credible counterfactual and adjusts that
benefit for the share retained by the firm, because external
partners may appropriate part of the value created by the
deployment. A durability weight captures how much confi -
dence the firm should place in benefits projected into later
periods as the system and its environment change. RoAI
also includes the full economic cost of infrastructure and
use, process transformation, governance and oversight,
and capability development or maintenance. Direct pro -
vider payments are captured within the cost, whereas the
retained-share captures external claims not already counted
as direct costs. Expressed as a benefit–cost multiple, RoAI
equals 1.00 at break-even; values above 1.00 indicate that
evidence-adjusted benefits retained by the firm exceed the
resources committed over the stated horizon.
Because these consequences emerge at different times,
the framework organizes them into short- and long-horizon
benefits and costs. Short-horizon benefits include productiv-
ity gains, faster decisions, and incremental revenue from the
focal application. Short-horizon costs include technology
use, process conversion, employee learning, and the mana -
gerial training/attention needed to make the deployment
operational. Over a longer horizon, the deployment can
create reusable data, routines, complementary capabilities,
and cross-functional spillovers. It can also create provider
dependence, customer adaptation, continuing governance
obligations, or erosion of capabilities previously sustained
through direct practice. A pilot may, for example, show a
strong conversion lift well before later monitoring costs or
customer adaptation appear. Given the multitude of factors,
we argue that two deployments can thus produce different
evaluations simply because they include different conse -
quences or evaluate them over different horizons.
Building on this temporal structure, we connect the four
outcome categories to the people, processes, and capabili -
ties that constitute the marketing organization. This cross-
classification identifies where each component of RoAI
arises and the organizational mechanisms that can raise or
reduce an AI investment’s value. It yields seven predictions
about employee responsibilities, process redesign, adop -
tion sequence, value retention, governance, capability ero -
sion, and interfirm divergence. We state each so that future
research can test it using deployment records, workflow
logs, cost data, and longitudinal marketing outcomes.
The framework makes three contributions. First, it
extends marketing accountability research by treating
return as an evolving property of an organizational invest -
ment rather than as a launch-period outcome. This perspec-
tive expands the evaluation beyond initial performance and
the authorized budget to include the benefits, costs, and
value-capture consequences that emerge as the deployment
operates and scales. Second, it extends resource-based the -
ory by identifying an erosion-under- success mechanism.
When AI assumes the decisions through which marketers
develop and maintain judgment, the capability needed to
oversee the system can weaken even while the deployment
continues to perform well. Oversight can therefore become
more demanding just as the experiential basis for providing
it contracts, allowing the investment’s return to deteriorate
without any decline in the technology’s performance. Third,
the framework advances research on AI-enabled technology
investment by explaining why firms that license the same
model can realize different economic returns. Shared access
may narrow differences in the technology itself, but hetero-
geneity in data, processes, governance, and accumulated
expertise determines how much value each firm creates,

We organize the rest of the article as follows. We first
define the marketing organization as a configuration of peo-
ple, processes, and capabilities. We then trace how earlier
technologies changed its reach, memory, speed, and judg -

managerial implications.
The marketing organization
A theory of RoAI requires a clear account of the organi -
zational resources through which an AI investment creates
value. This account identifies what the deployment changes,
shows how those changes enter the RoAI equation, and
helps explain why firms using similar systems can realize
different returns. We therefore begin by defining the market-
ing organization and its underlying resource configuration
before examining how technology has reshaped it. With -
out this foundation, claims that AI transforms marketing
leave unclear both what is being transformed and how those
changes affect economic value.
More formally, the marketing organization comprises
the specialized resources through which a firm develops
and maintains demand-side assets. It identifies and under -
stands customers, communicates value, sets prices, man -
ages channels, and builds relationships (Moorman & Rust,
1999). These activities create brands, customer relation -
ships, and channel partnerships that support future cash
flows (Srivastava et al., 1998, 2001). Their economic value
is well established. Stronger brand portfolios, for example,
are associated with higher and more stable returns (Morgan
& Rego, 2009), while deliberate management of customer
equity improves performance relative to a transaction-
focused approach (Kumar & Reinartz, 2016; Rust et al.,
2004). Marketing expenditure therefore supports both cur -
rent market activity and assets that generate future revenue.
Resource-based reasoning explains why the return to
an AI investment depends on the organization in which
the technology is deployed. Performance differences arise
from resource bundles that accumulate over time and are
difficult to trade, reproduce, or imitate (Barney, 1991;
Kozlenkova et al., 2014; Peteraf, 1993). In marketing, these
resources include the human capital, capabilities, culture,
structures, metrics, and incentives identified by Moorman
and Day ( 2016). For our analysis, we organize them into
people, processes, and capabilities. People contribute judg -
ment and market knowledge, processes coordinate recurring
decisions, and capabilities develop as these resources are
combined through experience. Because AI can alter each
element and the complementarities among them, its return
depends not only on the technology itself but also on how
the surrounding resource bundle is reconfigured.
More specifically, People encompasses the individuals
who perform marketing work, together with their skills,
judgment, and market knowledge. Processes are the recur -
ring arrangements that coordinate market-facing decisions,
including campaign-planning cycles, pricing reviews,
lead-management procedures, decision rights, metrics, and
incentives. Capabilities are firm-specific capacities that
develop as people carry out these processes over time. Mar-
ket sensing and the ability to translate customer insight into
coordinated action are familiar examples (Day, 1994, 2011;
Moorman & Day, 2016). Meta-analytic evidence shows
that marketing capabilities contribute more strongly to firm
performance than comparable capabilities in research and
development or operations (Krasnikov & Jayachandran,
2008). Capabilities are especially important to RoAI because
they accumulate gradually yet can deteriorate before the
loss appears in reported performance. For example, a pric -
ing capability built through years of promotion experiments
resides across analysts, review practices, and accumulated
experience rather than in any single employee or procedure.
This embeddedness makes the capability difficult to imitate,
but also vulnerable when AI changes or removes the activi-
ties through which it is sustained (Dierickx & Cool, 1989).
People, processes, and capabilities also develop jointly.
Employees gain experience through recurring processes,
and repeated coordination converts some of that experience
into organizational capability. Those capabilities, in turn,
shape how the firm designs and executes subsequent pro -
cesses. At any point, the marketing organization therefore
reflects a history of investment and learning (Dierickx &
Cool, 1989; Kozlenkova et al., 2014). Because these ele -
ments are complementary, the contribution of one depends
on the condition of the others (Milgrom & Roberts, 1990).
When AI performs part of a marketing decision process,
for instance, the affected role changes, the process must be
redesigned, and employees may receive less of the expe -
rience that maintained the associated capability. The eco -
nomic consequence therefore extends beyond the system’s
purchase price and any immediately displaced labor.
The marketing organization is narrower than the full set
of marketing activities performed within the firm. Product
teams conduct experiments, sales teams manage customer

becomes productive only through organiza- tional use.

redesigned around its strengths and limitations, and learn -
ing from experience becomes part of the firm’s capabilities.
These adaptations help explain why firms acquiring simi -
lar technologies often realize different returns (Bharadwaj,
2000; Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 2000; Melville et al., 2004; Ray
et al., 2004; Tippins & Sohi, 2003).
How technology has reshaped the
marketing organization
Today’s marketing organization emerged through a suc -
cession of technological innovations. Database market -
ing, customer relationship management (CRM) systems,
digital channels, mobile and social platforms, and market -
ing analytics each altered the resource bundle described
above (Kumar & Reinartz, 2016; Wedel & Kannan, 2016).
Although these innovations differed in purpose and archi -
tecture, their organizational consequences can be compared
along four dimensions: reach, memory, speed, and judg -
ment. These correspond to successive requirements of a
market-facing decision. The organization must be able to
address a customer, know something about that customer,
respond within a meaningful timeline, and select an action.
Each dimension therefore captures a change in what the
organization can do and the resources required to do it.
Table 1 summarizes the four dimensions, the innovations
that primarily altered them, and their organizational con -
sequences. Together, they provide the historical reference
point for identifying what is distinctive about AI.
The first dimension, reach, is the set of customers whom
the marketing organization can address individually. For
much of the twentieth century, mass media produced broad
aggregate exposure but little individual addressability. Firms
communicated with segments because one-to-one contact
was prohibitively expensive. Database marketing began to
relax this constraint. Direct channels expanded individual
contact, and digital and mobile platforms eventually made
individual targeting economically feasible at scale (Wedel &
Kannan, 2016). As reach expanded, firms created specialist
roles in acquisition and targeting, developed customer lists
and channel-governance processes, and built more refined
segmentation capabilities.
The second dimension, memory, concerns the customer
information the organization can retain, connect, and retrieve
across interactions. CRM systems advanced this dimension
by converting scattered transaction records into durable cus-
tomer histories that could inform subsequent touch points
(Kumar & Reinartz, 2016). Loyalty programs, behavioral
tracking, and third-party data extended the breadth and con-
tinuity of those histories. Analysts consequently assumed a
larger organizational role, while data-governance processes
developed around the collection and use of customer infor -
mation. These changes made the customer-linking capabili-
ties identified by Day (1994) feasible on a much larger scale.

market signal and a marketing response. Marketing analyt -
ics and experimentation platforms compressed that timeline
from quarters to days, while programmatic systems reduced

The fourth dimension, judgment, concerns evaluating
possible actions and choosing the response that advances
the firm’s objectives. As Agrawal et al. (2018) explain, pre-
diction models estimate what is likely to occur, whereas
judgment weighs the consequences of acting on that esti -

information or predictions. A CRM system recalled the
customer’s history, an analytics model scored the lead, and
a dashboard displayed the trend, but a marketer generally
selected the action. Programmatic bidding was an important
exception because it delegated high-frequency choices to
an algorithm. Even there, however, the organization speci -
fied the decision space and objective in advance, the actions
were repetitive, and errors were comparatively inexpensive
to reverse. Judgment therefore captures how much of the
evaluation and choice the technology itself performs.
Viewed together, the four dimensions reveal an uneven
historical pattern. Reach, memory, and speed advanced
repeatedly, with each advance leading firms to create roles,
redesign processes, or build capabilities. Judgment changed
much less. With limited exceptions such as programmatic
bidding, technology informed or accelerated decisions
while marketers retained responsibility for selecting the
action. Firms consequently developed considerable experi -
ence adapting their organizations to changes in reach, mem-
ory, and speed, but far less experience organizing around
technology that performs a substantial portion of marketing
judgment. This asymmetry matters once an AI deployment
changes all four dimensions at the same time.
A second feature of this history is the concentration of
change. Earlier innovations usually had one primary dimen-
sion. Database marketing mainly expanded reach, whereas
CRM systems mainly deepened memory. Each produced
secondary effects, but much of the existing decision archi -
tecture could remain in place while the organization adapted
around the principal change. This limited the number of
complementarities that had to be reconfigured simultane -
ously. Because people, processes, and capabilities create
value jointly, a deployment that changes several dimensions
at once presents a more demanding coordination problem
than one whose principal effect is localized (Milgrom &
Roberts, 1990).
How is AI different
Earlier marketing technologies typically concentrated their
effects in one or two of the four dimensions in Table 1,
while leaving most judgment with people. AI differs in
two respects. First, a single deployment can affect reach,
memory, speed, and judgment simultaneously. The same
system can expand the customers a firm addresses, deepen
the information used in an interaction, shorten the response
cycle, and participate in selecting the action. This breadth
requires several complementary resources to be reconfig -
ured within the same initiative. Second, AI can assume part
of the judgment itself, a change with limited organizational
precedent because earlier marketing technologies rarely
participated in framing and executing broad market-facing
decisions. This section develops these two differences and
identifies three properties of AI systems that alter the return
calculation.
The changes in reach, memory, and speed largely extend
trajectories that firms already recognize. Generative systems
can produce individualized content at low marginal cost
and expand addressability (Davenport et al., 2020; Huang
& Rust, 2021). Models trained on interaction histories can
identify patterns that employees could not readily retrieve
from customer records, deepening the information available
for subsequent decisions. AI systems can also compress
selected response cycles from days or hours to near-contin-
uous adjustment. Firms that adapted to database marketing,
CRM systems, or marketing analytics will recognize each
of these changes individually. What is less familiar is their
combination within a single deployment and the accompany-
ing transfer of judgment to the system. Changes in judgment
have less precedent. Recommender systems select products
to display, and programmatic systems select impressions to
purchase (Wedel & Kannan, 2016). In these settings, how -
ever, people typically defined the alternatives, specified the
objective, and created the content shown to customers. The
algorithm then made a bounded choice within a problem the
organization had already framed. Contemporary AI systems
can participate in both framing and executing the response.
A single system may, for example, identify customers to tar-
get, compose the campaign, set the bid, route a complaint,
and draft the reply (Agrawal et al., 2018). AI can therefore
perform part of the judgment that previously connected
information to market-facing action.
The extent of this delegation varies across AI applica -
tions. Predictive systems generally provide forecasts or
scores while people retain the final decision. Generative sys-
tems produce content or recommendations that people may
review before release, whereas agentic systems can execute
sequences of actions with greater autonomy. As the share of

The relevant distinction is therefore not simply whether AI
is used, but how much of the decision process it performs.
This transfer of judgment makes three properties espe -
cially important for assessing return. First, system behavior
can change after deployment. Second, the organizational
investments required for productive use can continue to
emerge as the deployment operates and scales. Third, the
value created can be distributed across internal functions
and external parties. Each affects a different component of
the return calculation, and each becomes more consequen -
tial as AI assumes greater responsibility for market-facing
decisions.
The first property is behavioral change after deployment.
Earlier marketing technologies were comparatively stable
artifacts. A CRM system, for example, generally performed
the same computation late in its life as it did at launch,
even as the market around it changed. AI systems are less
stable because their outputs are probabilistic, their inputs
evolve, and providers can update underlying models on
schedules the adopting firm does not control (Agrawal et
al., 2018; Grewal et al., 2020; Huang & Rust, 2021). For
instance, consider a customer-retention agent (hereafter, the
retention agent). It identifies customers at risk of leaving,
composes individualized offers, selects timing and chan -
nel, and allocates campaign spending. Suppose the agent
increases retention in the first quarter. Its performance may
later weaken because the initial customer pool was more
receptive, customers learn that lapsing triggers a discount,
or the provider changes the model. Evidence that accurately
describes launch-period performance may therefore provide
weaker support for benefits projected into later periods.
The second property is the emergence of additional orga-
nizational investment after deployment. Earlier technologies
were often costly to implement, but firms could generally
identify the principal expenditure categories before launch.
AI deployments can reveal new requirements through use.
Employees may need verification protocols, managers may
need rules for reliance and override, and multiple agents
may require coordination standards that were unnecessary
during the pilot. These requirements increase process-trans-
formation and governance costs even when the original
software budget remains unchanged. Walmart’s consolida -
tion of dozens of specialized agents into four coordinating
agents illustrates the mechanism at portfolio scale, where
common standards for routing decisions became necessary
only after coordination itself became a material organiza -
tional problem (Bousquette, 2025).
Metered-usage pricing illustrates why an AI investment
can have an uncertain cost path. AI providers often charge
by the token, the unit of text a language model processes,
making the unit price known at purchase. Total expenditure,
however, depends on model calls, context length, tool use,
retries, and self-checking—quantities shaped partly by task
and workflow design and often observable only in produc -
tion. Lower unit prices can therefore coincide with higher
aggregate spending. Bain estimates that between December
2024 and December 2025, the price per token fell by more
than half while token consumption increased more than
fourfold, causing aggregate expenditure to rise despite the
lower unit price (Wang et al., 2026; Rogelberg, 2026). The
cost path therefore depends on implementation architecture
and usage as well as the provider’s posted price.
Usage based pricing also makes internal metrics and
incentives economically important. A firm that rewards
employees or business units for AI use may encourage addi-
tional consumption even when that use is not tied to incre -
mental benefit (IBM, 2026; Sherry, 2026). Under metered
pricing, greater use immediately increases the deployment’s
economic cost, Ct, while any corresponding increase in
gross incremental benefit, Δ Bt, still requires credible evi -
dence. Usage measures can support effective management
when they are linked to economic outcomes. When activ -
ity itself is treated as evidence of value, however, adoption
is easily mistaken for return. Evaluating usage therefore
requires establishing whether additional consumption pro -
duces an incremental economic benefit.
The third property is distributed value capture. Many AI
systems rely on models and infrastructure that competitors
can access on similar terms. As access becomes more com-
mon, differentiation may weaken and marketing outputs
may become more similar across firms (Liu et al., 2025).
Provider and infrastructure payments enter Ct. Other exter-
nal claims on revenue, data, or derivative use reduce λ t, the
share of benefit retained by the firm, when those claims have
not already been counted as direct costs. Limited portabil -
ity and dependence on external providers can increase such
claims. Benefits realized by finance, service, operations, or
product remain within the firm and belong in ΔBt when they
are attributable to the marketing deployment. Assessing
return therefore requires identifying where value is created,
how much the firm retains, and whether external claims
have already been included in cost (Challapally et al., 2025).
Delegated judgment makes each of these properties more
consequential. When technology primarily supplied infor -
mation, a marketer usually interpreted the output before the
firm acted. Changes in information quality, implementation
requirements, or differentiation therefore passed through a
human decision maker before affecting the market. When
an AI system selects and executes the action, changes in
system behavior can instead translate directly into cus -
tomer facing decisions. Errors may spread across many

interactions before aggregate measures reveal them. The
firm must therefore continue monitoring delegated deci -
sions and maintain the capacity to intervene. Earlier auto -
mated systems limited this exposure because their decisions
were narrower and more readily reversible. AI systems can
assume decisions whose consequences extend across cus -

Together, these three properties explain why a calcula -
tion made at launch cannot fully assess an AI investment.
Gross benefit can change as the system and its environ -
ment evolve. The resources required for productive use can
expand after approval, and the share of value retained by the
firm can change as relationships with providers, customers,
and other parties evolve. A comprehensive assessment must
therefore follow benefits, costs, and value capture over time.
To understand the organizational mechanisms that shape
these paths, we next examine how AI redistributes market -
ing decisions and responsibilities.
How AI is reshaping the marketing
organization
AI systems affect value by redistributing marketing deci -
sions and responsibilities among the system, marketers, and
adjacent functions. The retention agent makes this redistri -
bution concrete. Before adoption, employees identify laps -
ing customers, design win back offers, schedule contact, and
allocate campaign spending. After adoption, the agent per -
forms much of this sequence. Employees therefore assume
new responsibilities for directing and overseeing the system
while participating less often in the decisions through which
marketing judgment develops. These mechanisms become
more economically important as the share of the decision
delegated to AI increases.
Delegation can first raise gross incremental benefit (Dav-
enport et al., 2020; Huang & Rust, 2021). Labor required per
campaign can fall, campaign volume can rise, and individu-
alized offers can be produced at a scale a human team could
not match. These gains may be especially visible during a
pilot, when receptive customers remain in the addressable
pool and experienced employees devote unusual attention
to implementation. Governance requirements may also
appear modest because the deployment has not yet reached
sustained scale. A pilot can therefore demonstrate operat -
ing potential while providing incomplete evidence about the
magnitude and persistence of economic value.
At the same time, delegation creates new responsibili -
ties around the decisions the system performs. A firm must
translate its retention strategy into an objective the agent can
pursue. Maximizing win back conversion alone, for example,
may encourage excessive discounting unless margin, customer
value, and brand considerations are also incorporated. Employ-
ees must verify offers because errors in terms or voice may
reach customers before aggregate measures reveal them.
Managers also need rules for reliance and override,
together with evidence that the system continues to operate
under the conditions for which it was approved (Korst et
al., 2025; Singla et al., 2025). Objective specification, veri-
fication, monitoring, investigation, and correction therefore
become complementary investments, even when they are
distributed across existing roles and do not appear on the
technology invoice.
As routine decisions move to the system, the judgment
retained by employees can become more demanding. Once
the agent assumes frequent and well specified campaign
decisions, marketers remain responsible for questions the
system cannot resolve (Agrawal et al., 2018). They must
assess whether the retention strategy remains appropri -
ate, whether the pattern of offers strengthens or weakens
the brand, and whether changes in system behavior require
intervention. These judgments concern a system whose
behavior may change partly through provider actions and
whose market effects may become visible only after many
customer interactions have occurred. The marketer’s role
therefore shifts toward setting direction, interpreting excep-
tions, and governing a system that performs a growing share
of market facing decisions.
Repeated delegation can also alter the organization’s
marketing capabilities. A retention team develops judgment
about recoverable customers and appropriate offers through
years of campaign design, execution, and review. That
capability resides across people, processes, and accumu -
lated experience. When the agent performs those decisions,
employees exercise the relevant judgment less often, reduc-
ing the practice that sustains the capability. The decline may
remain hidden while the system performs well and experi -
enced employees remain available. The organization may
discover the loss only when effective oversight requires
judgment that is no longer being regularly exercised. This
pattern differs from conventional resource accumulation
models. Dierickx and Cool (1989) describe asset stocks that
deteriorate when the investment needed to maintain them
declines. Under delegated judgment, however, investment
in the AI deployment can continue while the capability
required to govern it weakens because the system has dis -
placed the practice that sustained that capability. Capability
erosion can therefore emerge without a decline in deploy -
ment performance.
Over time, it may reduce future benefit, increase depen -
dence on external expertise, or raise the cost of rebuilding
lost judgment.

keting. Because marketing connects customers to the rest of

retention agent affect other functions. Discount decisions
influence revenue forecasts, contact decisions affect service

launch, while specification, verification, monitoring, inves -

Changes in human judgment and organizational capability
may take longer to appear, and cross functional effects place
additional benefits and costs outside the initiating budget. A
return framework must therefore identify each consequence,
when it becomes material, and where its economic effect is
realized. The next section translates these requirements into
the RoAI equation and its four outcome categories.
Evaluating the return on AI
Our organizational analysis identifies consequences that
become economically important at different points in
time. Delegation can generate measurable benefits soon
after launch, while governance, coordination, and capabil -
ity development often add costs as the deployment scales.
Capability erosion may affect benefits or increase depen -
dence only after a longer period. We therefore organize
these consequences into short-term benefits, short term
costs, long-term benefits, and long-term costs. These labels
refer to when an outcome becomes economically material
in a particular setting rather than to fixed calendar intervals.
Table 2 applies the four categories to the retention agent
and illustrates how this temporal structure supports a fuller
assessment of value. We first introduce the RoAI equation,
then explain its conceptual foundations, and finally examine
each outcome category.
The framework contains four time varying terms. Gross
incremental benefit, ΔBt, is the economic value attributable
to the deployment in period t relative to a credible coun -
terfactual, before AI specific deployment costs and external
appropriation are deducted. It includes attributable benefits
realized anywhere within the firm. Effects on customer wel-
fare or other external parties are addressed in the societal
extension in Web Appendix A. For the retention agent, the
counterfactual is the prior process described in the preced -
ing section. The observed outcome under the AI augmented
process already reflects adoption, workflow quality, and
organizational translation. Weakness in any of these areas
therefore reduces ΔBt or increases the cost of producing it
rather than creating a separate reduction in retained share.
The remaining terms capture value retention, evidentiary
strength, and cost. The retained share, λ t, is the portion of
incremental benefit captured by the firm after external claims
that have not already been recorded as direct costs. Evi -
dence durability, dt, reflects how informative the evidence
available on the evaluation date remains for estimating ben-
efit in period t as the system, its users, or its environment
change. Full economic cost, Ct, includes infrastructure and
usage fees, workflow transformation, governance, over -
sight, and capability development or maintenance. Both ΔBt
and Ct include attributable consequences in adjacent func -
tions when they are caused by the marketing deployment.
A payment included in Ct cannot also reduce λ t, because
doing so would count the same external claim twice. In a
prospective analysis, all four trajectories are conditional on
the information available at the time of evaluation. We omit
that additional date subscript for readability. Return on AI

by the firm, after accounting for the strength of the support-

horizon. Subtracting one yields the corresponding net return
on cost. The equation does not itself establish causality, so
a credible research design must identify Δ Bt. The analysis

factual against which the deployment is evaluated. Real -

Once incremental benefit for a completed period has been
established, that realized amount remains unchanged. The
durability weight applies only to evidence used to estimate
benefits in periods whose outcomes are not yet known. Web
Appendix A states the formal properties of each term, speci-
fies conditions that prevent double counting, and provides a
numerical illustration.
Evidence durability requires additional explanation
because it differs from two familiar financial adjustments.
Financial discounting places amounts from different peri -
ods on a common present value basis, while a risk premium
compensates investors for bearing risk. The durability
weight serves neither purpose. It is an epistemic adjust -
ment that reflects how strongly existing evidence supports
an estimate for period t when the model, customer base, or
operating process may have changed since that evidence
was collected. As the relevance of the evidence declines,
dt reduces projected incremental benefit toward the coun -
terfactual. A continuation decision made partway through a
deployment therefore combines realized periods, for which
dt equals one, with projected periods, for which dt may be
below one and can increase after revalidation. Firms can
strengthen dt by maintaining held out evaluation suites,
running regression tests after provider model updates, and
monitoring drift in inputs and outputs. These activities enter
Cgov as the direct cost of keeping the evidence current. Put
simply, dt captures what the firm knows about future benefit,
while financial discounting captures what a future dollar is
worth today.
Because these mechanisms become material at different
times, every estimate of RoAI depends on the evaluation
horizon. A calculation ending after four quarters can sup -
port a different funding conclusion from one extending to
twelve. Early observations emphasize delegation benefits
and launch expenditures. Longer horizons incorporate reus-
able assets, continuing governance requirements, changes in
value capture, customer adaptation, and capability erosion.
The choice of T is therefore substantive because it deter -
mines which economic consequences enter the assessment.
We recommend reporting RoAI across several horizons so
that decision makers can observe its trajectory rather than
only a single endpoint. Web Appendix A develops this prop-
erty formally and illustrates it with the retention agent.
Survey evidence reinforces the importance of horizon
choice. A Deloitte survey of 1,854 executives across Europe
and the Middle East found that most respondents expected
a typical AI use case to require two to four years to gen -

expected technology investments to pay back within seven
to twelve months, and only 6% reported AI payback within
one year (Michalski et al., 2025). Applying the shorter win-
dow can end the evaluation before several benefits and costs
become observable, making an investment appear weak
simply because the horizon is incomplete. Similarly, PwC’s
2026 finding that 56% of respondents reported no significant
cost or revenue benefit in the prior year describes outcomes
over a particular observation period and leaves the eventual
economics of those deployments unresolved (PwC, 2026).

pendently of the horizon over which it is observed.

Conceptual foundations
Resource based theory explains why the four outcome
categories can develop differently across firms even when
they adopt similar AI systems. Economic value depends
not only on access to the technology but also on the orga -
nizational resources through which it is deployed (Barney,
1991; Wernerfelt, 1984). Because many AI systems are
available to competitors on comparable terms, access alone
can generate economic benefit without creating a durable
relative advantage (Mata et al., 1995; Peteraf, 1993). Pro-
ductive use instead depends on complementary resources
that accumulate through path dependent investment and
cannot be purchased fully formed (Dierickx & Cool, 1989).
Data infrastructure, governance routines, and deployment
specific judgment, for example, may become more valu -
able as they mature, while deterioration in those resources
may remain hidden until performance or control weakens.
Appropriation also depends on these complements because
parties that control scarce resources can claim part of the
value the deployment creates (Milgrom & Roberts, 1990;
Teece, 1986).
Applied to AI, this reasoning connects the timing of
returns with organizational heterogeneity and value capture.
As access to a common technology spreads, differences in
the return attributable to the technology itself may narrow.
Returns can nevertheless diverge because firms begin with
different resource bundles and develop complementary
assets at different rates. The same logic applies when those
resources deteriorate. A firm that preserves the judgment
needed to direct an AI system, for example, may respond
more effectively to provider changes or customer adapta -
tion than one whose internal capability has weakened. RoAI
therefore reflects both the common properties of the tech -
nology and the firm specific resources through which it is
used. The erosion under success mechanism introduced ear-
lier extends this logic by showing that an important comple-
ment can deteriorate even while the deployment continues
to perform well. We return to this mechanism in the discus-
sion of long term costs and in the research agenda.
Short-term benefits
Short term benefits arise when an AI deployment improves
the economics of an existing marketing process. For the
retention agent, these gains can include lower labor require-
ments per campaign, greater campaign volume, more indi -
vidualized offers, and earlier contact with customers at risk
of leaving. Firms often observe such changes first through
operating measures. They become economic benefits, how-
ever, only when the analysis establishes that they are incre-
mental and translates them into margin, cost reduction,
avoided loss, or another firm level outcome. McKinsey’s
2025 findings illustrate the prevalence of reported operat -
ing gains. Among respondents using AI in marketing and
sales, 43% reported revenue increases of 5% or less and
24% reported larger increases (McKinsey & Company,
2025). These figures do not establish investment level
return because they do not identify the counterfactual, full
economic cost, retained share, or persistence of the gain.
Operating improvement is therefore an input to the assess -
ment rather than evidence of return on its own.
Observed gains also provide incomplete information
about how benefits will develop after scaling. Pilot results
are often generated while the most receptive custom -
ers remain available, experienced employees continue to
absorb exceptions, and implementation receives unusually
close attention. The underlying model may also remain
unchanged during the observation period. Under these con-
ditions, the measured ΔBt can accurately describe pilot per-
formance while providing weaker evidence about benefits
in later periods. The durability weight dt captures this dis -
tinction by reducing the weight placed on projected benefits
as the relevance of the original evidence declines, without
assuming that future performance must deteriorate.
Finally, the source of the benefit influences whether the
resulting advantage can persist. An AI system may continue

to generate absolute economic value after competitors gain
comparable access, even when the technology itself no
longer differentiates the firm (Barney, 1991; Mata et al.,
1995). Firms using the same foundation models may, for
example, target similar customers or produce increasingly
similar content. More durable differences are likely to arise
from customer data, organizational routines, governance
practices, and accumulated knowledge about how to direct
the deployment. Evaluating short term benefit therefore
requires distinguishing the economic gain produced by the
investment from the resources that determine whether that
gain can be sustained.
Short-term costs
Short term costs extend beyond licenses and metered usage
to integration, data preparation, and the labor and coordi -
nation required to make delegation reliable. Employees
must specify objectives, examine output, monitor behav -
ior, investigate anomalies, and exercise override authority.
These responsibilities are attributable to the deployment
whether they are performed within existing roles or charged
to functions outside marketing. Because firms rarely cre -
ate separate accounting categories for this work at launch,
the approved budget can understate Ct even when spending
remains within plan. A complete assessment must therefore
capture both the invoices generated by the deployment and
the organizational work required to operate it. Web Appen-
dix B develops these governance responsibilities, explains
why they persist as long as delegated judgment creates
material exposure, and traces their effects on the terms in
Eq. 1.
Current expenditures also differ in the resources they
leave behind. Model usage that produces no incremen -
tal value is current consumption. A verification protocol,
override process, or governance routine, by contrast, can
become a complementary organizational asset when it
improves reliability or reduces the cost of later deployments
(Brynjolfsson et al., 2021; Milgrom & Roberts, 1990). The
vendor invoice alone cannot reveal this distinction. The ana-
lyst must determine whether the expenditure merely sup -
ports current use or also creates a resource that can generate
future value. Evidence that similar information technologies
produce different returns across firms is consistent with the
importance of such complementary resources (Bharadwaj,
2000; Ray et al., 2004). Cost classification should therefore
reflect not only where the expenditure is recorded but also
what organizational resource it creates or preserves.
The same type of expenditure can consequently repre -
sent current consumption in one deployment and capability
building in another. Both increase current Ct, but their later
effects can differ substantially. An expenditure that builds
a reusable complement may increase future Δ Bt, reduce
future Ct, or strengthen value capture by improving porta -
bility or bargaining power. Treating all current expenditures
as economically equivalent can therefore lead managers
to eliminate governance or learning activities that support
later returns. An informative cost record should identify
the amount spent, the purpose of the expenditure, and the
resource created or preserved by the spend.
Long-term benefits
Long term benefits arise when deployment experience cre -
ates resources or spillovers whose economic effects per -
sist beyond the period in which they were generated. Data
infrastructure, verification protocols, and governance rou -
tines can reduce the cost or improve the reliability of later
deployments. Information produced by the focal deploy -
ment can also improve decisions elsewhere in the firm.
Transaction histories from the retention agent may, for
example, improve revenue forecasting in finance or capac -
ity planning in service when they provide information that
the prior workflow did not generate. Customer relationships
may strengthen as well when AI supported contact remains
relevant, consistent, and appropriately timed (Kumar &
Reinartz, 2016; Rust et al., 2004). Each effect belongs in
RoAI only when it is incremental, attributable to the deploy-
ment, and not already captured elsewhere in the calculation.
The most defensible source of durable advantage, how -
ever, is likely to reside in the complementary resources
that accumulate around the deployment. Competitors may
license the same generally available technology, but they
cannot as readily acquire data infrastructure calibrated to
a particular customer base, governance routines refined
through experience, or managerial judgment developed
through repeated use. These resources reflect an organiza -
tional history that rivals cannot reproduce simply through
purchase (Dierickx & Cool, 1989). Their economic value
also depends on co-specialization. As Teece (1986) argues,
firms capture more of the return when they control the com-
plements required for productive use and are less dependent
on providers or scarce external specialists. The same logic
applies when a firm deploys a licensed AI system.
The interdependence that makes these resource bundles
difficult to imitate can also make their contribution difficult
to diagnose within the firm (Rai, 2020). Performance may
depend on the model, proprietary data, workflow design,
governance, or interactions among them. When a plausi -
ble but incorrect model output causes a poor outcome, for
example, managers may address the visible symptom with -
out identifying the underlying source of failure. Such inter-
nal causal ambiguity has an economic cost when it slows
learning, prolongs ineffective intervention, or impedes the

Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science
development of complements that could improve future
RoAI. Inimitability is therefore most valuable when the firm
can still understand and manage the components on which
the broader capability depends.
Firms consequently need internal diagnosability with -
out sacrificing the external inimitability of the integrated
capability. Component level evidence can help distin -
guish failures originating in the model, data, workflow, or
governance process. The broader capability can remain
socially complex because its value continues to depend on
how those components are coordinated (Makadok, 2001).
Greater internal diagnosability can therefore support learn -
ing and preserve long term value without making the overall
resource bundle easier for rivals to reproduce. This distinc -
tion becomes especially important when we turn to long
term costs, where deterioration may remain hidden until the
organization needs to intervene.
Long-term costs
Long term costs are often difficult to observe because they
can affect several terms in Equation 1. Capability erosion,
for example, can raise Ct by forcing the firm to rebuild
expertise, reduce ΔBt by weakening its ability to direct the
system, or reduce value capture by increasing dependence
on external parties. The central mechanism is reduced
practice. The judgment required to govern an AI system is
often developed and maintained through the same decisions
that the system begins to perform. For the retention agent,
marketers learn to recognize a failing strategy by design -
ing, executing, and reviewing campaigns over time. When
delegation removes those experiences, current performance
can improve even as the basis of future oversight weakens.
Evidence from an adjacent setting is consistent with this
mechanism. In a multicenter study of endoscopists, unas -
sisted adenoma detection fell from 28.4 to 22.4 percent after
several months of AI assisted colonoscopy while assisted
performance remained strong (Budzyń et al., 2025). Prac-
titioner evidence points in the same direction. In an enter -
prise survey, 43% of leaders anticipated skill atrophy even
though 89% reported that AI augmented employee activity
(Korst et al., 2025). Strong current performance can there -
fore coexist with deterioration in the organizational capacity
needed for future oversight.
Provider dependence and customer adaptation create
additional long term costs and can compound this erosion.
Providers can change prices, model behavior, portability,
or contractual terms, thereby increasing Ct or reducing the
share of value retained by the firm. Customers may also
learn that lapsing triggers an attractive offer and adjust their
behavior, reducing subsequent ΔBt. An organization whose
internal judgment has weakened is less able to recognize
strategic customer response, evaluate provider induced
changes, or negotiate from a position of technical under -
standing. Internal expertise and oversight therefore remain
economically important long after deployment.
The same accumulation process that makes a market -
ing capability valuable also makes its deterioration costly
to reverse. Tacit knowledge, social complexity, and path
dependence make the loss difficult to observe and the under-
lying resource difficult to reconstruct (Barney, 1991; Dier-
ickx & Cool, 1989). A decade of accumulated experience
cannot be recreated within one quarter simply by increasing
expenditure because time compression diseconomies apply
to rebuilding as well as to initial development. The conse -
quences become especially severe when weakened internal
judgment coincides with a provider price change, model
change, or other event that requires informed intervention
(Teece, 1986). By the time the economic cost becomes vis -
ible, the organization may already have lost part of the capa-
bility required to respond effectively.
These mechanisms also show why the four outcome cat-
egories cannot be evaluated independently. Excess short
term consumption can crowd out verification or governance
investment and increase the likelihood of later failure.
Capability erosion can make a decline in Δ Bt more diffi -
cult to diagnose because the source may lie in the system,
in customer adaptation, or in the organization responsible
for directing the deployment. New oversight requirements
can increase Ct when the firm purchases monitoring tools
or external expertise, while growing dependence on those
external complements may later weaken value capture.
Conversely, expenditures recorded as current costs can cre-
ate routines that reduce the cost or improve the reliability of
subsequent deployments. A consequence that first appears
in one category can therefore alter the economic effects
recorded in another.
Such interactions explain why technical improvement
and economic value can diverge. A system may become
more accurate or capable even as RoAI declines because
usage grows faster than benefit, the retained share falls, or
the organization loses the judgment needed to convert tech-
nical performance into market value. Organizational learn -
ing and capability erosion can also occur at the same time.
A team may, for example, develop stronger monitoring rou-
tines while exercising less of the underlying customer judg-
ment. Model performance, adoption, and activity therefore
provide useful information, but none is sufficient on its own
to establish return.
Across settings, the four categories provide a common
structure without implying a common return trajectory. An
investment may generate immediate gains, reveal additional
costs through use, create reusable assets, or expose the firm
to risks that become more important over time. The relative

Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science
importance of these consequences depends on the setting.
Every evaluation must therefore identify when each con -
sequence becomes economically material and specify the
horizon over which RoAI is calculated. The framework
structures that assessment while leaving the realized pattern
to empirical evidence.
The relevant definition of benefit also depends on the eco-
nomic objective and the counterfactual. An efficiency objec-
tive may focus on incremental contribution after changes in
contact cost. A growth objective may require customer mar-
gin or lifetime value over a longer horizon. A risk reduction
objective, such as screening advertising placements for brand
safety, concerns avoided losses that are less directly observed
than realized revenue. Evaluations of the same deployment can
therefore reach different conclusions when they use different
objectives, counterfactuals, or horizons. A claim about return
becomes interpretable only when these choices are explicit.
Earlier marketing technologies exhibited some of the same
pressures. CRM data became stale, implementation costs
exceeded technology budgets once organizational change
was included, and vendors captured part of the value cre -
ated (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 2000). AI makes the assessment
more demanding because several of these pressures can arise
within the same deployment and can stem from changes in
the system itself. The framework therefore anticipates that
return estimates may take longer to stabilize and may be more
sensitive to organizational choices than was typical for ear-
lier technologies. It does not imply that AI investments earn
lower returns on average. Firms that account for the full cost,
preserve essential judgment, and build valuable complements
may generate substantial and durable value.
The importance of each outcome category also depends
on how much of the marketing decision the AI system per -
forms. Predictive systems provide forecasts or scores that
employees interpret. Generative systems produce marketing
content that employees approve or revise. Agentic systems,
including the retention agent, execute connected sequences
of decisions (Bornet et al., 2025; Davenport & Ronanki,
2018; Huang & Rust, 2021). As delegation increases, the
potential economic benefit expands, but so do the organi -
zational consequences that require governance. The three
system types therefore call for different evaluation designs.
For a predictive system, the relevant counterfactual is
the prior decision process into which the score or forecast
is introduced. The primary questions concern outcome
observability, adoption, and the value created by actions
taken in response to the prediction. For a generative system,
the counterfactual is the prior production process, and the
assessment must connect production metrics, inference vol-
ume, and human revision to market response. For an agentic
system, the counterfactual is an alternative decision pol -
icy, and the assessment must address objective alignment,
policy level causality, reversibility, and continuing gover -
nance. Long horizon concerns differ accordingly. Predictive
systems place greater emphasis on data quality and model
maintenance, generative systems on content convergence
and provider dependence, and agentic systems on customer
adaptation and capability erosion. Table C.1 in Web Appen-
dix C summarizes these implications.
Capability erosion illustrates why the degree of delegation
matters. It may remain limited when employees continue to
exercise judgment around a churn score, yet become substan-
tial when an agent selects customers, designs offers, and allo-
cates spending. Early enterprise evidence is consistent with
this ordering. Among the 57% of respondents in the Deloitte
survey who reported using agentic systems, only 10% said
they were already realizing significant returns. Half expected
returns within three years, and a further third expected to wait
three to five years (Michalski et al., 2025). This pattern is con-
sistent with a setting in which both potential benefit and gov-
ernance requirements are substantial, and it illustrates why an
evaluation ending after four quarters may capture only part of
the investment’s economics. Web Appendix C develops these
differences further and derives four evaluation regimes from
evidence durability and retained share, two terms that firms
can influence without fully controlling.
The outcome categories identify which economic con -
sequences belong in the assessment. The elements of the
marketing organization identify where those consequences
originate and which terms in Equation 1 they are likely to
affect. Role redesign can alter labor cost, delegation benefit,
and the judgment available for oversight. Process redesign
can alter the quality and cost of specification, verification,
coordination, and override. Capability change may remain
invisible in quarterly reports until the organization requires
expertise that it no longer possesses. Crossing the four out -
come categories with people, processes, and capabilities
therefore converts the value assessment framework into a
research agenda focused on the organizational mechanisms
that shape RoAI. The next section develops that agenda.
RoAI and the marketing organization: A
research agenda
RoAI becomes a research agenda when its economic terms
are linked to the organizational resources that shape them.
Changes in people can affect labor cost, the benefits of del -
egation, the counterfactual available for evaluation, and
the judgment retained for oversight. Processes determine
how objectives are translated into market facing action and
therefore influence both gross incremental benefit and the
costs of verification, coordination, and override. Capabili -
ties determine whether productive knowledge accumulates,

Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science
whether the firm can protect the value it creates from exter-

tem or its environment changes. Crossing the four outcome
categories with people, processes, and capabilities therefore
identifies the organizational mechanisms through which
each term in Eq. 1 can vary.
This organizing logic follows an established tradition
in marketing scholarship. Contingency research identified
a common set of conditions that later studies could com -

accountability research likewise developed constructs such
as customer equity and market based assets that connected
marketing activity to financial outcomes (Hanssens & Pau -
wels, 2016; Katsikeas et al., 2016; Rust et al., 2004). Subse-
quent synthesis became possible because studies addressed
related questions using a shared conceptual vocabulary
(Morgan et al., 2019). RoAI serves a similar purpose by
placing heterogeneous organizational consequences within
a common economic structure, allowing evidence to accu -
mulate across deployments, firms, and settings.
Existing AI research agendas provide important foun -
dations for this effort. They examine technological capa -
bilities, task allocation, generative systems, and consumer
experience (Cillo & Rubera, 2025; Davenport et al., 2020;
Grewal et al., 2025; Hermann et al., 2024; Huang & Rust,
2021; Plangger et al., 2022). A complementary agenda is
needed to explain the economic value firms realize from
AI as an organizational investment. Such an agenda must
account for why the same technical system can generate dif-
ferent benefit trajectories, cost structures, evidentiary con -
ditions, and value capture outcomes across organizations.

People and RoAI
For people, the research agenda begins with the redistri -
bution of decision responsibility. AI adoption can change
staffing and role content, but its economic consequences
extend well beyond payroll. Marketing roles often contain
judgment that is not captured in formal task descriptions,
including knowledge of exceptions, customer responses,
and the limits of established processes. When an AI system
assumes part of a role, the firm must determine where that
judgment will reside and who will govern the decisions now
performed by the system. Continuing roles may therefore
require substantial redesign, while responsibilities embed -
ded in eliminated roles must be reassigned. Labor savings
constitute economic benefit only after the analysis accounts
for these continuing responsibilities and for any judgment
lost through the reorganization.
Role changes also affect the counterfactual used to esti -
mate incremental benefit. During a pilot, employees may
resolve exceptions, correct outputs, and provide intensive
support that will not be available under the scaled operat -
ing model. Their contribution can make the system appear
more productive while remaining absent from the stated
cost. Once the original team has been disbanded, the prior
process may also become difficult or impossible to recon -
struct. This problem is already visible in practice. Salesforce
reported that customer support headcount declined from
roughly 9,000 to 5,000 roles as Agentforce agents absorbed
the corresponding conversations, thereby removing much of
the prior process against which the agents’ incremental con-
tribution might otherwise be evaluated (Salesforce, 2024;
Fortune, 2025). A phased rollout, preserved holdout process,
or other defensible design should therefore be established
before reorganization (Angrist & Pischke, 2009; Imbens &
Rubin, 2015). Without such a design, the firm may attribute
to the system outcomes that the prior team would have pro-
duced while overlooking responsibilities that employees
continued to perform during the pilot.
Preserving the counterfactual does not, however, resolve
the measurement problem created by continuing human
responsibilities. Objective specification, verification, anom-
aly investigation, monitoring, and override rarely receive a
common accounting treatment. Firms may centralize these
activities in dedicated roles or distribute them across mar -
keters, analysts, legal staff, and information technology
employees. The chosen arrangement affects Ct directly and
can also influence Δ Bt and dt through the quality of con -
trol. Employee responses to algorithmic error illustrate this
tradeoff. Skepticism may prevent a costly mistake, whereas
excessive avoidance can increase review effort without
improving decisions (Dietvorst et al., 2015). Research
should therefore examine when these responses improve
control and when they merely add cost, as well as their con-
sequences for benefit and evidence durability.
Over longer horizons, the design and productivity of
continuing roles become more informative than the initial
reduction in headcount. As prediction or execution becomes
less costly, complementary human judgment can become
more valuable (Agrawal et al., 2018). Realizing that value
requires deliberate role design. Responsibilities for objec -
tive setting, verification, exception handling, monitoring,
and override must be assigned, supported, and evaluated.
System logs can help identify who performs each respon -
sibility and whether intervention improves outcomes. The
complementarity argument therefore predicts stronger RoAI
when labor displacing deployments are accompanied by
continuing roles that explicitly direct and govern the system.
Role redesign also has a developmental consequence.
Marketing judgment develops through repeated exposure
to customers, campaigns, experiments, and decisions whose
outcomes become observable over time. When AI assumes
a substantial share of those decisions, employees lose some
of the practice through which they prepare for more senior
responsibilities. Not every automated activity contributes
meaningfully to development, but entry level and inter -
mediate decisions can provide the feedback through which

Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science
marketers learn how market actions produce outcomes
and when established rules cease to apply. Labor market
evidence suggests that this developmental pathway may
already be narrowing. Using payroll records, Brynjolfsson
et al. (2026) find that employment among workers aged 22
to 25 in occupations most exposed to AI declined by about
11% between late 2022 and mid 2026, while employment
for the same age group in the least exposed occupations
increased by about 10%. Survey evidence also indicates
executive concern about deskilling, which we examine
further in the capability predictions below (Goel et al.,
2026). Because marketing capabilities accumulate gradu -
ally through experience, an organization may be unable to
reconstruct them quickly once the developmental pathway
that sustained them has weakened (Dierickx & Cool, 1989).
Two predictions follow from these mechanisms. P1
concerns the reliability of pilot evidence. As a larger share
of the marketing decision is delegated to AI, the differ -
ence between pilot RoAI and RoAI after scaling should
increase. Greater delegation makes employee absorption of
exceptions during implementation more consequential and
increases the likelihood that continuing governance require-
ments are omitted from the pilot estimate.
P1 The gap between pilot RoAI and post-scaling RoAI
increases as the share of the marketing decision del -
egated to AI increases.
P2 concerns organizational complementarity. Labor-dis -
placing AI investments should generate stronger RoAI
when continuing roles explicitly assign responsibility for
objective specification, verification, investigation, moni -
toring, and override. Delegation does not eliminate these
responsi- bilities. Instead, it redistributes them, and their
value depends on whether they are clearly assigned and
adequately supported (Milgrom & Roberts, 1990).
P2 Among labor-displacing AI investments, RoAI is higher
when continuing roles explicitly assign responsibility
for objective specification, verification, investigation,
monitoring, and override.
Together, P1 and P2 change how staffing reductions should
be interpreted. Eliminating a role does not eliminate all of
the responsibilities or judgment embedded in that role, and
transferring decisions to an AI system creates new require -
ments for specification, monitoring, and control. Workforce
decisions should therefore identify the judgment being
delegated, the judgment that remains with employees, the
assignment of continuing responsibilities, and the process
through which future marketers will develop relevant expe-
rience. The associated labor and capability costs should also
enter the economic evaluation of the deployment. Treating
them as unspecified organizational adjustment would under-
state the resources required to sustain RoAI.
Processes and RoAI
For processes, the research agenda begins with suitability
for delegation. A marketing process is a stronger candidate
when its decision inputs and objectives can be codified,
feedback arrives quickly, and poor decisions can be reversed
at limited cost. Programmatic bidding combines these
characteristics and therefore provides the narrow histori -
cal exception discussed earlier. Processes with delayed or
difficult-to-reverse consequences require more verification
and managerial intervention, which can absorb a substan -
tial share of the gross benefit from delegation. An adoption
sequence based only on projected labor savings is there -
fore unlikely to maximize RoAI. Research should examine
whether codifiability, feedback speed, consequence delay,
and reversibility predict the persistence of delegation gains
across otherwise comparable processes.
Process design also encompasses the measures and incen-
tives that govern AI use. Metered systems create a direct
connection between activity and Ct, yet organizations some-
times treat activity itself as evidence of adoption or progress
(IBM, 2026; Sherry, 2026). Such measures can encourage
greater use without establishing incremental value. The
token consumption pattern discussed earlier illustrates the
problem. Higher consumption may support more valuable
applications, so usage alone does not indicate waste. It also
cannot establish return. Process measures should therefore
connect AI activity to both the economic outcome produced
and the resources consumed in producing it.
The criteria used to evaluate a deployment can also
change as the organization learns. Revising a measure is
informative when new evidence shows that the original
measure did not adequately capture the economic objective.
The same revision becomes problematic when it preserves a
favorable assessment by weakening the connection to ben -
efit, cost, or value capture. In the enterprise survey cited
earlier, 72% of respondents reported using structured return
measures rather than adoption alone (Korst et al., 2025).
Future research can examine changes in evaluation criteria
over time, the reasons given for those changes, and sub -
sequent economic outcomes to distinguish organizational
learning from measurement distortion. Maintaining a doc -
umented history of evaluation criteria can also strengthen
later assessments.
Such measurement discipline becomes especially impor-
tant when firms redesign processes around AI. In PwC’s
2026 survey, firms reporting both revenue gains and cost
reductions were two to three times more likely than other

1 3
Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science
respondents to report extensive use of AI in demand genera-
tion or strategic decision-making (PwC, 2026). This asso -
ciation is consistent with complementarity theory, which
predicts greater value when technology adoption is accom -
panied by changes in the surrounding process (Brynjolfsson
et al., 2021; Milgrom & Roberts, 1990). It does not estab -
lish that process redesign caused the observed difference,
because firms undertaking more extensive redesign may
also have possessed stronger capabilities before adoption.
Staggered redesigns across units or activities would provide
a more credible test of whether process redesign improves
RoAI. A longer horizon raises a related question about what
one deployment contributes to the next.
Data infrastructure, monitoring routines, verification
protocols, and governance processes can reduce the cost or
improve the reliability of subsequent investments (Cohen
& Levinthal, 1990; Dixit & Pindyck, 1994). The extent of
this carryover makes the adoption sequence economically
important. Predictive systems may, for example, create data
and monitoring assets that support later generative or agen-
tic deployments. The value of those assets depends on their
transferability. A resource calibrated to predictive accuracy
may provide little support for editorial verification or real-
time agent oversight. Research should thus identify which
complements transfer across deployments, how much
future Ct they reduce, and whether those savings exceed the
resources required to create them. Questions about sequenc-
ing extend recent research that situates generative AI within
innovation and marketing processes and distinguishes the
activities that AI systems support from those they perform
(Cillo & Rubera, 2025; Grewal et al., 2025). Business
units within the same firm often adopt AI types in differ -
ent sequences, creating useful variation while holding many
firm-level conditions relatively constant. Such comparisons
can test whether reusable complements created by an earlier
deployment improve subsequent RoAI while accounting for
a unit’s general propensity to adopt new technology. The
adoption sequence can therefore reveal whether organiza -
tional resources created through one deployment retain their
economic value in another.
As deployment dependence increases, contract manage -
ment becomes another important process. Provider terms
may receive substantial attention at adoption, but their
economic importance can increase as usage expands and
switching becomes more difficult. A provider controlling
a scarce complement can raise prices, alter model behav -
ior, restrict portability, or revise contractual terms (Teece,
1986). Price changes affect Ct directly, contractual claims
can reduce λ t, and weaker differentiation can reduce Δ Bt.
Periodic review of prices, usage, portability, and model
changes therefore forms part of the process through which
the firm protects its return. Retained share should thus be
reassessed alongside gross incremental benefit rather than
treated as fixed at adoption.
Customer adaptation requires similar attention. A reten -
tion process may become less effective when customers
learn that lapsing generates an attractive offer, even if the
agent becomes more proficient at executing its policy. Trans-
action histories can reveal the prevalence of strategic laps -
ing and the rate at which it reduces incremental margin. The
process is inherently adaptive because the firm’s decision
rule influences customer behavior, which in turn changes
the effectiveness of that rule. Evaluation criteria, operating
policies, and the relevant counterfactual therefore require
periodic reassessment as customer behavior changes.
Two predictions follow from these process mecha -
nisms. P3 concerns dependence on shared AI foundations.
Greater reliance on external models and infrastructure
should weaken RoAI through higher provider related costs
or reduced value capture. This effect should become stron -
ger as dependence increases and weaker when portability,
provider competition, or renegotiation options constrain the
provider (Teece, 1986).
P3 Greater dependence on shared AI foundations lowers
RoAI. This relationship is weaker when firms have
greater portability, provider competition, or renegotia -
tion options.
P4 concerns adoption sequence. Predictive first adoption
should improve the RoAI of subsequent generative or agen-
tic deployments when the earlier deployment creates data,
monitoring, or governance assets that can be reused. The
advantage should diminish when those complements are
specific to the original deployment.
P4 The positive effect of predictive first adoption on the
RoAI of subsequent generative or agentic deployments
increases with the transferability of the data, moni -
toring, and governance assets created by the earlier
deployment.
Together, P3 and P4 show that process design affects RoAI
beyond the focal workflow. Contracting, portability, con -
sumption management, customer adaptation, and the trans -
fer of organizational assets across deployments all shape the
economic value that the firm ultimately realizes from AI.
Capabilities and RoAI
Capabilities, the third element of the agenda, warrant sepa -
rate attention because they accumulate more slowly than
roles or formal processes and can deteriorate without an
immediate decline in reported performance. They shape

1 3
Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science
how effectively the organization converts technical func -
tionality into economic benefit, how much of that value the
firm retains, and how well it responds when the system or its
environment changes. Capability development and erosion
can therefore explain differences in RoAI that the technical
system alone cannot. The empirical challenge is to detect
these changes before their economic consequences become
fully visible.
One way to identify the role of capabilities is to com -
pare return trajectories across firms that use the same AI
system. Similar trajectories would suggest that the technol -
ogy accounts for much of the economic outcome, whereas
increasing divergence would indicate a growing role for
firm-specific resources and governance. Existing evidence
is consistent with this possibility. Firm-level studies link the
performance effects of AI to conditions within the adopting
organization (Mishra et al., 2022; Mu & Zhang, 2025), while
field research in retailing shows that technology and orga -
nizational practice can coevolve along firm-specific paths
(Bonetti et al., 2023). Cross-sector survey evidence also
reveals substantial dispersion. In the enterprise survey cited
earlier, the share of respondents reporting positive returns
ranged from 88% in technology and telecommunications to
54% in retail, where more complex physical operations can
slow the organizational changes required for productive use
(Korst et al., 2025). The unresolved questions are when such
divergence begins, which complementary resources account
for it, and whether those resources primarily affect Δ Bt, λt,
or Ct.
Capability development also requires current expen -
diture, making the distinction between organizational
investment and current consumption especially important.
Spending contributes to absorptive capacity by helping
the firm recognize, assimilate, and apply learning across
deployments (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990). High levels of AI
use, however, need not produce such development. Employ-
ees may route more decisions through the system without
becoming better at evaluating, directing, or learning from it.
The invoice cannot distinguish between these cases. Empir-
ical analysis must therefore examine what an expenditure
leaves behind, whether a reusable data asset, a governance
routine, stronger diagnostic ability, or no enduring organi -
zational resource.
Over longer horizons, capabilities are also a plausible
source of persistent heterogeneity in RoAI. The underlying
model may be available to many firms under similar licens-
ing terms, whereas customer-specific data infrastructure,
governance routines refined through experience, and judg -
ment about deployment develop within the organization.
Rivals cannot readily reproduce this accumulation through
purchase alone (Dierickx & Cool, 1989; Teece, 1986).
Research should therefore identify which complements
remain difficult to imitate after accounting for the shared
technology and estimate the incremental return associated
with those resources. Doing so would clarify how firms
built on the same technical foundation can continue to real-
ize different levels of economic value.
The same interdependence that protects a capability from
imitation can, however, make its source of value difficult to
diagnose internally. Performance may depend jointly on the
model, data, workflow, governance, and human judgment,
making it difficult to determine which component is respon-
sible when outcomes change (Makadok, 2001; Rai, 2020).
This creates a tension between external inimitability and
internal diagnosability. Research should examine when the
protection created by causal ambiguity outweighs the learn-
ing costs it imposes on the focal firm. It should also identify
diagnostic practices that enable managers to isolate compo-
nent failures without dismantling the social complexity of
the broader capability. Greater internal diagnosability can
therefore support learning and sustained RoAI while pre -
serving the inimitability of the integrated resource bundle.
Evidence from adjacent settings provides initial support
for the idea that reduced practice can weaken capability even
while assisted performance remains strong. The endoscopy
study discussed earlier offers the clearest example. A longi-
tudinal study in professional services likewise documented
self-reinforcing skill erosion after judgment was automated,
despite continued system performance (Rinta-Kahila et al.,
2023). Executive evidence is consistent with the same con -
cern. Half of the leaders in a 2026 BCG survey reported
observing deskilling, with judgment, problem framing, and
creative thinking among the capabilities viewed as most
vulnerable (Goel et al., 2026). These studies do not establish
the rate or form of erosion in marketing, but they are con -
sistent with the practice-based mechanism proposed here.
The timing and severity of erosion should depend on the
type and frequency of the experience displaced by AI. A per-
ceptual or detection skill exercised repeatedly may weaken
relatively quickly once practice declines. Deliberative judg-
ment distributed across people and processes may deteriorate
more slowly and become visible only when an unusual deci-
sion or system failure requires it. Marketing researchers can
examine these trajectories by linking longitudinal skill assess-
ments, intervention records, and model revalidation to deploy-
ment histories. The central empirical question is whether the
organization retains the capacity to evaluate and redirect the
system when established conditions no longer hold.

retical basis for this divergence (Barney, 1991; Mata et al.,
1995).
P5 Among firms using the same AI system, divergence in
realized RoAI increases over time as differences in their
complementary assets and governance routines increase.
P6 concerns process embeddedness, defined as the extent
to which the focal process is connected to established roles,
routines, and information flows. Greater embeddedness
should increase the resources required for initial recon -
figuration and therefore depress pilot RoAI. After scaling,
however, those same connections can support learning,
coordination, and control when they have been successfully
adapted (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Teece et al., 1997).
The expected effect of embeddedness therefore depends on
deployment stage.
P6 The relationship between process embeddedness and
RoAI becomes more positive as an AI deployment
moves from pilot to scaled operation, provided that the
surrounding roles, routines, and information flows are
successfully adapted.
P7 concerns capability erosion. Erosion should be more pro-
nounced when delegation removes frequent or consequen -
tial opportunities to exercise judgment, and less pronounced
when firms preserve meaningful human responsibility for
selected decisions (Dierickx & Cool, 1989). The timing of
this decline may also vary across perceptual, creative, and
deliberative skills, providing an important boundary condi -
tion for empirical research.
P7 Capability erosion increases with the frequency and
consequentiality of judgment delegated to AI. This
relationship is weaker when firms preserve meaningful
human responsibility for selected decisions.
Testing P7 requires an outcome that current performance
metrics cannot provide. We propose a capability audit con -
ducted on the same schedule as model revalidation. The audit
would include a retained set of decisions that employees
continue to make without the system, periodic assessment
of unassisted performance on those decisions, and evalua -
tion of override quality rather than override frequency. The
trajectory of unassisted performance provides the erosion
measure, while retained human responsibility provides the
condition that P7 predicts will slow that decline. Preserving
such responsibility has an economic cost because it sacrifices
part of the delegation benefit, making the forgone benefit
the price of maintaining the capability. Rotating employ -
ees through verification, exception handling, and selected
decisions can distribute that cost while preserving relevant
experience. Pairing capability audits with model revalida -
tion also allows the organization to assess human capacity
while assessing system performance. Because accumulated
judgment cannot be rebuilt quickly after prolonged periods
of reduced practice, maintaining the capability is likely to
be less costly than reconstructing it after substantial erosion
(Dierickx & Cool, 1989). Table 4 summarizes the seven pre-
dictions, their theoretical basis, and an illustrative test for
each.
Tables 3 and 4 complete the transition from framework
to testable theory. The former organizes research around the
economic terms and organizational mechanisms specified
by RoAI, while the latter states predictions that can be eval-
uated empirically. Evidence that the gap between pilot and
post-scaling RoAI does not increase with delegated judg -
ment would, for example, weaken P1. Convergence among
firms using the same system would weaken the capability
argument in P5, while evidence that process embeddedness
has the same effect across deployment stages would weaken
P6. Such findings would help distinguish the proposed orga-
nizational explanation of RoAI from an account in which
technical characteristics explain most of the return.
Future extensions to the RoAI framework
The RoAI framework focuses on the organizational and
economic consequences of AI investments in marketing, but
its logic also accommodates questions that arise as deploy -
ments extend across functions, stakeholders, and institu -
tional boundaries. We highlight two extensions that retain
the framework’s core structure while broadening the range
of consequences it can assess.
The first extension concerns cross-functional interdepen-
dence. For example, the retention agent discussed earlier can
potentially affect finance forecasts, service staffing, product
learning, and information technology requirements. These
consequences belong in RoAI when they are attributable
to the marketing deployment, even when another function
records the resulting benefit or cost. The relationship also
runs in the opposite direction because data infrastructure,
product systems, and operational capabilities controlled
elsewhere in the firm can determine whether the market -
ing deployment becomes productive. Future research can
therefore examine the decision rights, coordination arrange-
ments, and transfer pricing rules through which functions
allocate investment, risk, and return. Such work would
clarify how organizational boundaries influence the value
ultimately realized from an AI investment.

The second extension concerns consequences that initially
fall outside the firm’s private return. Marketing AI systems
could affect customers whose data inform the system, employ-
ees whose responsibilities change, and markets in which gener-
ated content circulates (Rust, 2020; Verhoef et al., 2021). These
consequences fall within a firm-centered evaluation when they
subsequently affect benefits, value capture, or costs. A deploy-
ment that weakens customer trust, for example, can reduce
later ΔBt even while current response measures remain favor-
able because trust is a market-based asset (Aguirre et al., 2015;
Martin & Murphy, 2017; Srivastava et al., 1998). Environmen-
tal or regulatory burdens can similarly originate outside the
firm and later enter Ct through compliance, litigation, restricted
use, or mandated redesign (Strubell et al., 2019; Tabassi, 2023).
External effects can therefore become part of private returns
when they feed back into the firm’s economics over time.

Conclusion
Marketing organizations are making larger and more durable
commitments to AI, yet they still lack an integrated method
for assessing the value of those investments. Improvements
in revenue, conversion, content production, or employee
productivity are important inputs to evaluation, but none
establishes return on its own. A complete assessment also
requires a credible counterfactual, attention to how much
value the firm realizes and retains, a full accounting of eco-
nomic cost, and a horizon long enough to capture the con -
sequences of continued use. The RoAI framework brings
these requirements together within a common economic
structure.
RoAI treats an AI deployment as an organizational
investment in a configuration of people, processes, and
capabilities. Its numerator begins with incremental benefit
and adjusts for the share retained by the firm. A durabil -
ity weight reflects how strongly current evidence supports
benefits projected into later periods, while conventional
financial discounting places economic flows from differ -
ent periods on a common basis. The denominator captures
infrastructure and use, process transformation, governance
and oversight, and capability development. Because RoAI is
a benefit–cost ratio, 1.00 provides the economically mean -
ingful reference point. A deployment can therefore perform
well on operating measures while remaining below break-
even once the full investment is considered.
The historical analysis explains why AI requires this
broader treatment. Earlier marketing technologies expanded
reach, extended organizational memory, increased speed, or
strengthened the information available for judgment while
leaving most judgment with people. AI can affect all four
dimensions within a single deployment and perform part of
the judgment that connects information to market action.
Delegation, therefore, changes more than task volume. It
creates ongoing requirements for verification, exception
handling, monitoring, and governance while reducing the
practice by which employees develop and maintain the
judgment needed to recognize failure or to redirect strategy.
An organization can consequently become less able to gov-
ern the system even while the system continues to perform
well. This possibility is not anticipated by conventional
accumulation models and may remain invisible in current
operating measures. Because these consequences emerge at
different times, the evaluation horizon becomes a substan -
tive part of the return assessment.
The four outcome categories make this temporal logic
explicit. Short-horizon benefits and costs capture the imme-
diate economics of putting the deployment into use. Long-
horizon benefits include reusable assets, organizational
learning, and cross-functional spillovers, while long-hori -
zon costs include provider dependence, customer adapta -
tion, continuing governance requirements, and capability
erosion. The same deployment can therefore appear more or
less attractive as the evaluation horizon changes. The direc-
tion cannot be assumed in advance because it depends on
the magnitude and timing of consequences across all four
categories. RoAI structures this assessment without pre -
suming the resulting return trajectory.
The research agenda identifies the organizational mech -
anisms that shape these outcomes. For people, the central
questions concern the allocation of decision responsibility,
the development and retention of expertise, and the human
effort required to govern delegated judgment. For pro -
cesses, the relevant mechanisms include process suitability,
verification, exception handling, consumption management,
dependence on external complements, and the sequence in
which different forms of AI are introduced. For capabilities,
research must distinguish resources that accumulate through
use from those that deteriorate as direct practice declines.
Seven predictions translate these mechanisms into testable
relationships across deployments, organizations, and time.
From a managerial perspective, the framework identifies
four requirements for evaluating an AI investment. Firms
should specify the counterfactual and evaluation horizon
before projecting return, account for the full economic cost
of the deployment, including oversight and capability main-
tenance, reassess the share of value retained after launch, and
verify that the expertise required to intervene remains avail-
able. Table 5 translates these requirements into a scorecard
that assigns each term in Equation 1 a measure, data source,
owner, and review cadence. Strong operating performance
does not eliminate the need for continued review because
provider terms, customer behavior, employee expertise, and
system behavior can all change after deployment.
RoAI also has limitations that offer several opportunities
for future research. Its current formulation is conceptual,
so the temporal trajectories of gross incremental benefit,

evidence durability, retained value, and capability change

How quickly marketing judgment deteriorates, and
whether deliberative skills erode in the same way or at the
same rate as perceptual skills, remains an open empirical
question. Cross functional consequences also warrant more
detailed modeling because the benefits and costs attribut -
able to an AI deployment can arise in different parts of the
firm. Similarly, the societal extension developed in Web
Appendix A requires empirical validation to determine
when customer welfare, worker outcomes, or environmen -
tal burdens feed back into private return through regulation,
trust, litigation, or redesign. More broadly, the seven predic-
tions should be tested across technologies, organizational
settings, and markets to establish the boundary conditions
of the framework.
Overall, the value of an AI investment cannot be inferred
from system performance alone. It depends on how the tech-
nology interacts with the people, processes, and capabilities
through which the organization deploys and governs it. Mar-
keting scholars are well positioned to study this interaction
because the discipline connects customer and market out -
comes with organizational capabilities and accountability. A
theory of RoAI can therefore do more than assess whether
an AI deployment produces an immediate economic gain. It
can explain why similar systems generate different returns
across firms, why those returns change over time, and which
organizational choices allow favorable returns to endure.
