Abstract
The Bayān al-Sharʿ encyclopedia, authored by the Omani jurist Muḥammad ibn Ibrāhīm al-Kindī (d.
508 AH / 1115 CE), stands as one of the most comprehensive juridical compilations within the Ibāḍī
intellectual heritage. Far from being a purely doctrinal  and legal reference, this monumental work
serves as a valuable reflection of Omani social and economic life during the Islamic Middle Ages. A
critical reading of its contents reveals an exceptional wealth of material that extends well beyond
jurisprudence, offering insights into the classification of sales, their spatial contexts, the agents
involved, and the diverse forms of commercial transactions. Such dimensions render the text an
indispensable source for reconstructing the economic structures of Oman at a time when it was deeply
integrated into the commercial networks of the Indian Ocean and the  Arabian-Persian Gulf. This
study aims to reconsider Bayān al-Sharʿ as a historical source that unveils key aspects of Omani
economic and social realities during its period of compilation. By focusing on the contractual
formulas of sales and examining thei r implications, the research seeks to highlight the intersections
between legal norms and economic practices, as well as their broader sociocultural significance.
Ultimately, al-Kindī’s encyclopedia provides a remarkably detailed model of commercial activity in
Oman, outlining sales contracts, ethical framework of trade, regulatory norms, and organizational
structure. Moreover, it underscores the diversity of sales practices and the circulation of both local
and imported goods, thereby testifying to the central role of Oman in the global trade system of the
medieval Islamic era.
Keywords: Bayān al-Shar, economic, jurisprudence, Oman, sales, trade
Introduction
The importance of Oman i legal encyclopedias, spanning different periods, lies not merely in
their purely jurisprudential significance, but also in their status as the most authentic sources for
writing Oman’s history across all its historical and civilizational fields. The book  “Bayan al-Shar”,
written by the scholar Muhammad bin Ibrahim Al-Kindi, serves as a prime example for tracing the
daily life of the Omani society within its multiple domains , such as agriculture, crafts, and trade
during the Islamic medieval era.

.4  Bayān al-Sharʿ: Historical Utilization and Methodological Limits
Before discussing the methodological obstacles in researching the encyclopedia Bayan al-
Shar',6it is essential to provide the introduction of the scholar -jurist. He is Muhammad bin Ibrahim
bin Sulaiman bin Muhammad bin Abdullah bin Al-Miqdad Al-Kindi Al-Samdi Al-Nizwi, a scholar
of the latter half of the 5 th century Hijri, who lived into the early 6 th century Hijri and died in 508
AH/1115 AD. He studied under Sheik Abu Ali al -Hassan bin Ahmad bin Muhammad bin Uthman
al-Nizwi.7
Many researchers interested in Oman i history and its notable figures have provided sufficient
biographies of the author, saving us the task.8 They mentioned that Sheik al-Kindi was among those
who diligently sought knowledge, having studied under Sheik Abu al -Hassan bin Ahmad bin Nasr
al-Hajari. The Sheik was prolific in knowledge and broad in learning, precise in observation and
understanding, and profound in perception. H e was among the most prominent scholars of his time
and left numerous Omani-authored works and concise treatises on jurisprudence and creed. He also
left scattered legal responses (fat awa) and a poem “ al-'Abiriyyah,” which describes paradise and
what Allah has prepared therein for its inhabitants, spanning 83 verses .9
As for the book Bayan al-Shar, it consists of 72 volumes. It was organized and its introduction
was written by his cousin Abu Bakr Ahmad Bin Abdullah Al-Kindi (d. 557 ah/1162 ce), the author
of the book “al-Musannaf,”10 since Sheik Al-Kindi passed away before he could arrange it.
The volumes of the book comprise several chapters distributed between the jurisprudence of
worship ('Ibadat) and the jurisprudence of transactions (Mu'amalat). It begins with chapters on

jurisprudential context. Although the jurist-scholar focused on the jurisprudential-legal perspective—
his primary domain—the topics constitute valuable historical material that can be utilized to extract
data seldom provided by other Omani sources. These case studies present various aspects of
commercial transactions. They also address many issues related to the details of daily life in Omani
market during the Islamic era, as well as several aspects of the economic affairs of Omani society
across a g eographical sphere defined by his correspondence with jurists and scholars from other

Al-Kindi’s method in Bayan al-Shar' is systematic and classificatory. He arranged issues across
jurisprudential chapters, joined theory with practice, drew on authenticated narrations from the Ibadi
imams and sheiks, and added discussions reflecting varied interpretations.
Despite this, the methodological problem remains regarding the  methods of investing in and
employing this type of writing in the field of historical studies. It does indeed constitute a real obstacle
for the modern historian, since the content is jurisprudential and not aimed at the purpose of historical
documentation. This makes it difficult to separate the boundaries of jurisprudence and history within
the case and to verify its authenticity, given the absence of several indicators, including the additions
made by scribes and scholars who succeeded one another in la ter periods comprising additions,
comments, and annotations. This requires caution and care when dealing with the original text of the
book. However, it is easy to distinguish them through the occurrence of indicative phrases, such as
“from the added addition of the work,” “from  other than the book,” “from the margin,” and “from
the book of the sheikhs.” 13

5. Regarding the Types of Sales Formulae
5.1. The Spaces of Commerce
The sales-related issues presented by Al-Kindi frequently contain references to the locations and
spaces of commercial exchange and dealings .20 The marketplace ( al-suq) constituted the primary
space for buying and selling activities in his time. These were places where various goods and
merchandise gathered and commercial transactions and deals were conducted. Business operations ,
such as measuring, storage, cash sales, purchases, and barter took place there. They also served as
centers for craft and professional activities, typically located in the city center, following the pattern
of landmarks in the Islamic city.21
As it is historically known, the marketplace, besides being the primary intermediary in the
processes of production, distribution, and consumption, represents a vital space for investing and
circulating capital. Consequently, the  suq is considered as one of the central components in the
economic, social, and religious fabric of the Islamic society. While it serves the function of
intermediation between production and consumption, it also transcends this role by being a space
where exchange is practiced, rules of interaction are formulated, oversight is exercised, commercial
transactions are regulated, and the responsibilities of the actors involved are defined.
In the Omani context, the presence of the market in traditional jurisprudential texts is evident as
a reflection of an active and organized local economic structure. Al -Kindi’s Bayān al-Sharʿ stands
out among these texts, as it contains a collection of regulations to organize market mechanisms and
their vital role. Al-Kindi dedicated multiple chapters to market rulings, reflecting its central position
in the economic life of Islamic Oman and highlighting the jurisprudential regulatory tools that
accompanied local commercial activity.
There are mentions of shops (dukākīn) , which flouri shed with the trade of goods and
commodities brought from both within and outside Oman, reflecting the mobility of Omani
merchants traveling to and from various regions .22 The Omani ships docked in places such as the
Levant (al-Shām), Iraq, the Hijaz, Persia, Yemen, Daybul, 23 India, and China.24 In this context, Al-
Kindi transmitted the response of the scholar Muḥ ammad ibn Maḥbūb regarding zakāh, who stated
that “as for all wealth brought by its owners to Oman for trade or otherwise from the lands of Islam,
such as Iraq, Persia, Aden, and Daybul... ”25  Residences (manāzil) also served as spaces for buying

5.2. Actors in Sales Transactions
Based on some examples within the issues presented in  Bayān al -Sharʿ, various active
categories involved in the sales process within Omani trade during the Islamic era have been
identified. There is no doubt that the types of merchants in Oman varied according to the amounts of
their capital, the social status of their owners, and the methods they employed for investment, whether
they were from the elite or the common people, and whether engaged in direct trade or within a
framework of brokerage and agency.
Large-scale merchants occupied the foremost position due to the financial wealth they
possessed, which grew through multiple resources. Some of them were even from the ruling
authority, as indicated by some issues concerning the sales transactions of sultans and thei r aides.28
Among them were those who owned a private market  or possessed shops (dukākīn) .29 Al-Kindi
highlights in his  Bayān that agency (al -wakālah), as a juristic mechanism, played a crucial role in
regulating buying and selling activities within medieval Omani society. Agents (al-wukalā’) served
as vital intermediaries between merchants and the markets. He defined agency as a legal proxy in
which the agent acts on behalf of the principal within the bounds of the authorization granted to him,
bound by the conditions of legal capacity (al-ahliyyah) and trustworthiness (al-amānah). He indicates
that a sale for less than the authorized price is invalid unless ratified by the principal and any excess
profit belongs to the principal. Furthermore, the agent is considered a trustee (amīn) and is only liable
in cases of transgression (al -taʿaddī) or negligence (al-taqṣīr). Al-Kindi explicitly prohibited  all
forms of fraud or deception that use agency to circumvent prohibitions against usury (al-ribā) or
excessive uncertainty (al-gharar).30
Furthermore, many legal cases revealed issues related to agency in buying and selling , 31
shedding light on several matters concerning the role of agents in facilitating certain transactions. It
indicates that the agency system was used by Omanis in their daily sales, particularly in cases of
travel, maritime trade, and other circumstances.32

Based on the examined issues concerning sales formulae in  Bayān al-Sharʿ, it is evident that
sales (bay') commonly involved tangible goods ('ayn) and were divided into types. Among them
was cash sale, known as  al-bay' al-muṭlaq (the absolute sale), which is the exchange of a tangible
good for debt (i.e., money). This was the most widespread type, as it allows a person to exchange
using his own money without being subject to constraints imposed by others. Another type is barter
sale (al-Muqāyaḍah / al-Mu'āwaḍah),41 which is the exchange of one tangible good for another.
Then, there is the exchange of currencies, called  ṣarf (currency exchange). We limit ourselves here
to mentioning a sample of these.
Barter sale (al-bay' bi al -muqāyaḍah) is considered as one of the oldest forms of financial
exchange in Islamic jurisprudence, involving the direct exchange of one commodity for another. It
was the foundational form of transaction before the widespread use of currency. Although currency
is not a product of the modern era, its circulation was limited in some environments. This type of sale
was considered the most prevalent method for exchanging goods among the people of Oman, likely
due to the scarcity of circulating currency and the prevalence of agriculture and pastoralism in the ir
way of lif e. Several issues discussed by Al-Kindi revealed fatawa from Omani scholars regarding
cash sales in various contexts across the country. 42 However, what is notable regarding the sales
formula is the scrutiny applied to ensure that the currency was sound and valid. An example is the
issue mentioned by Abu Al-Hawari: a man knows a dirham to be debased; is it permissible for him
to buy food with it? He said that it is not permissible for him.43 This suggests that currency dealings
were approached cautiously due to the risk of metal adulteration.

monetary metals, as in wheat for barley or dates for dates. It is permissible once the general conditions
hold, that is, mutual consent, a clear agreement, precise identification of both items, and immediate
exchange (al-taqābuḍ) completed in the same session.
Al-Kindi emphasized in this regard that barter s ales must be free from excessive uncertainty

6. Conclusion
To summarise, this sample of commercial legal issues (masāʾil al -buyūʿ) in Bayān al -Sharʿ
reveals aspects of the Omani society ’s sociocultural specificity through the contexts in which it
appeared, marked by a distinctive legal -jurisprudential framework. Though this type of case
(nawāzil) often lacks a detailed narrative of the incident itself, it preserves the positions of the
scholarly elite (al-nukhbat al-ʿālimah) on issues in their society, making it an exceptionally suitable
resource to study the era’s intellectual output and Omani economic life.
The current study demonstrates the multiplicity of sales methods and forms in Om an through
the jurisprudential encyclopedia Bayan al-Shar by Muhammad ibn Ibrahim al-Kindi. These included
cash sales, bartering, and advance sales (sadaf/salaf). Additionally, it revealed other aspects that
formed an integrated system of local and global commercial exchange and transaction methods.
Furthermore, the diversity of jurisprudential forms for commercial contracts presented by
Muhammad ibn Ibrahim al-Kindi in Bayan al-Shar does not merely reflect theoretical jurisprudential
reasoning (ijtihad). Rather, it serves as a living testament to the economic vitality experienced by the
Omani society during the medieval period. The adoption of contracts such as salam  (forward
manufacture) and nasi'ah (deferred payment) indicated the existence of advanced financing models
that enabled merchants to meet production and marketing requirements at the time. Meanwhile, sales
