Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly used in digital marketing to personalize
communications, recommend products, and automate customer interactions.
Despite these capabilities, consumer trust in AI-powered marketing remains
uncertain. This exploratory study examines consumer perceptions of AI-driven
digital marketing using survey data collected from 500 U.S. adults. Findings
suggest that consumers generally recognize the usefulness of AI-powered
marketing but remain cautious about whether organizations using AI act in
consumers’ best interests. Respondents expressed concerns regarding privacy,
data use, and organizational trustworthiness, while transparency and data
security emerged as important trust-building factors. Moderate daily AI users
(2–5 hours) reported higher levels of trust in companies employing AI-driven
marketing practices than light users (<2 hours). Overall, the findings suggest
that consumer acceptance of AI’s functional benefits does not necessarily
translate into trust in organizations that deploy these technologies. Implications
for marketing managers and directions for future research are discussed.
1. Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) has become a defining feature of contemporary
digital marketing. Organizations increasingly rely on AI-driven systems to
personalize advertising, recommend products, automate customer service
interactions, and predict consumer preferences at scale (Davenport et al.,
2020). These capabilities allow marketers to process behavioral data with a
level of precision that was previously unattainable.
Despite these advances, the growing presence of AI in marketing has
generated significant consumer anxiety regarding privacy, transparency, and
ethical data use. Consumers are increasingly aware that AI systems collect
and analyze personal information to influence purchasing behavior, often
through processes that are unclear and difficult to evaluate. Trust, defined
as the expectation that exchange partners will act reliably and benevolently,
has emerged as a central variable in understanding consumer responses to AI-
powered marketing (Morgan & Hunt, 1994).
In AI-mediated marketing environments, trust is not limited to
organizational credibility. Consumers must also evaluate the reliability and
fairness of algorithmic systems that operate invisibly behind personalization
interfaces (Marthews & Tucker, 2019). This opacity creates a fundamental

The present study makes a focused empirical contribution by

forces combine to produce cautious rather than trusting consumer postures
toward AI-powered marketing. The study uses statistical comparisons to
identify whether consumer trust attitudes lean positive or negative, whether
AI adoption changes perceptions of companies across specific dimensions,
and how key attitudes relate to one another. Rather than testing a fully
specified causal model, the study aims to characterize the trust landscape and
generate hypotheses for future research.
The study addresses the following research questions:
RQ1: Do consumer trust attitudes toward AI-powered marketing lean
more positive or negative?
RQ2: Does AI adoption influence perceptions of companies?
RQ3: How are key consumer attitudes toward AI-powered marketing
related to one another?
RQ4: What broader patterns can be used to characterize the overall AI
trust landscape?
2. Literature Review
The following review draws on four streams of relevant theory and
research. Rather than presenting these as parallel frameworks, we integrate
them around a central question: why might consumers accept AI marketing
as useful while remaining skeptical of the organizations using it? Privacy
calculus theory explains the cost-benefit logic governing data sharing. The
personalization paradox and creepy marketing research show how data-driven
personalization can backfire. Trust-in-automation theory explains how
familiarity with technology builds confidence over time. Commitment-Trust
Theory grounds the organizational trust dimension. Together, these
perspectives set up the AI trust gap this study investigates.
2.1. Consumer Trust and Commitment-Trust Theory
Morgan and Hunt’s (1994) Commitment-Trust Theory identifies trust as
central to cooperative exchange, arguing that parties who trust one another
are more likely to commit to long-term relationships and less likely to engage
in opportunistic behavior. In digital environments, where consumers cannot
directly observe organizational practices, trust plays an especially critical role
in reducing perceived risk and sustaining engagement.

In AI-enabled marketing contexts, trust extends beyond the organization
itself to encompass confidence in the technological systems generating
recommendations and automating interactions. Consumers must develop
trust in AI systems they cannot inspect, making transparency and perceived
benevolence especially consequential (Murtarelli et al., 2021). Perceived
trustworthiness predicts consumer willingness to engage with personalized
services and share personal information (Kim & Park, 2013).
Trust has also been shown to shape important marketplace outcomes
beyond attitudes and behavioral intentions. Chaudhuri and Ligas (2016)
found that trust, rather than satisfaction, was the mechanism through which
favorable perceptions of merchandise value translated into consumers’
willingness to pay premium prices. Their findings position trust as a relational
pathway connecting positive evaluations of a firm to concrete economic
behavior. Extending this logic to AI-powered marketing, trust may similarly
determine whether favorable perceptions of AI-driven personalization
translate into deeper consumer engagement and sustained relationships.
2.2. Trust in Automation and Artificial Intelligence
Trust in AI can be understood as a specialized form of trust in automated
systems more broadly. Hoff and Bashir (2015) propose that trust in
automation develops through the interaction of dispositional tendencies,
situational context, and accumulated experience with the technology itself.
Their framework suggests that people evaluate automated systems not only
according to objective performance but also according to perceived reliability,
predictability, transparency, and the degree of control retained by the user.
Because consumers rarely observe the algorithms responsible for
personalization and recommendation directly, trust becomes an especially
important mechanism for managing the uncertainty inherent in these
interactions.
A broad review of the AI trust literature by Glikson and Woolley (2020)
identifies trust as one of the most consistent predictors of AI acceptance
across contexts. Their synthesis finds that trust in AI is shaped by perceived
fairness, explainability, competence, and accountability, while concerns about
bias, privacy intrusion, and insufficient transparency tend to erode it.
Together, these findings suggest that consumer trust in AI-powered
marketing depends not only on technological performance but also on
broader perceptions of how, and for what purpose, AI systems are deployed.
2.3. Privacy Calculus and Data-Sharing Behavior
Privacy calculus theory proposes that individuals weigh potential benefits
against privacy risks when deciding whether to disclose personal information
(Dinev & Hart, 2006). In AI-driven personalization contexts, this calculus
is especially salient: personalized marketing may improve relevance and
convenience, but it depends on data collection practices that many consumers
experience as intrusive or threatening.

This tension has been described in the marketing literature as the
personalization paradox. Aguirre et al. (2015) found that while consumers
value the relevance and convenience that personalization provides, they
simultaneously grow uncomfortable when firms appear to know an excessive
amount about them. Their findings indicate that personalization tends to
strengthen consumer experience when data practices are transparent and
expected, but undermines trust when consumers perceive the underlying data
collection as surveillance rather than service.
Related concerns have been explored through the concept of creepy
marketing. Moore et al. (2015) argue that consumers label marketing
practices as creepy when firms use personal information in ways that exceed
what consumers consider socially normative. Their qualitative work identifies
three underlying dimensions of this reaction: a sense of privacy invasion,
stalking-like surveillance, and violations of broader social norms. These
dimensions are particularly relevant to AI-driven marketing, where
sophisticated personalization systems can easily create the impression that a
firm knows too much, even when no rule has technically been broken.
The benefit-risk tradeoff is context-sensitive and individual-specific (Awad
& Krishnan, 2006). Concerns about data misuse, algorithmic opacity, and
lack of consumer control have been shown to reduce willingness to disclose
personal information even when material benefits are offered (Martin et al.,
2017).
2.4. AI-Specific Trust Challenges
Algorithm aversion, the tendency to reject algorithmic recommendations
even when they outperform human judgment, is well documented in the
behavioral literature (Dietvorst et al., 2015). In marketing and decision-
making contexts, algorithm aversion may manifest as skepticism toward AI-
generated recommendations, resistance to personalization systems, or general
wariness of AI-powered interactions regardless of their functional quality
(Jussupow et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023; Yalcin et al., 2022).
Transparency has emerged as a key factor shaping AI trust. Puntoni
et al. (2021) show that consumers approach AI interactions through an
experiential lens, evaluating not only outcomes but the process through
which AI arrives at them. Opaque machine-learning systems that cannot
explain their recommendations create a transparency gap that undermines
confidence even when outcomes are satisfactory. This transparency gap
motivates the present investigation.
While neither Banerjee et al. (2025) nor Dang et al. (2025) directly
examined AI-powered marketing, their findings offer conceptual extensions
relevant to this context. Banerjee et al. (2025) found that perceived
authenticity shapes consumer engagement with social media influencer
content, and Dang et al. (2025) found that trending content elements in
short-form advertisements generated greater engagement than content
without such features. By extension — and not as a direct finding of those

3. Methodology
3.1. Research Design and Orientation
This study employed a quantitative, cross-sectional survey design to
examine consumer attitudes toward AI-powered digital marketing.
Consistent with the objectives of exploratory research, the study seeks to
identify directional patterns and relationships that may inform future theory
development rather than test a fully specified causal model (Stebbins, 2001).
Because cross-sectional survey designs provide limited evidence regarding
temporal ordering and causality, the findings should be interpreted as
hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory (Rindfleisch et al., 2008).
3.2. Sample and Data Collection
Data were collected through a nationally distributed online consumer
panel administered by Qualtrics. The final analytical sample consisted of 500
U.S. adult consumers who completed the full survey (N = 553 initiated;
53 excluded for incomplete responses). All respondents confirmed familiarity
with digital marketing and awareness of AI prior to entering the main
survey. An English-language fluency check required participants to compose
a written description of a product displayed on screen; respondents who
failed were excluded. Demographic composition: gender was approximately
equal (48.6% male, 51.4% female); age ranged across all adult cohorts with the
largest segment aged 55–64 (23.0%); racial composition was 64.8% Caucasian,
13.4% African American, 8.8% Hispanic, and 6.8% Asian; the majority held
at least a college degree.
One limitation of the survey design is that respondents were asked about
‘AI-powered digital marketing’ generally, rather than a specific AI application.
Different respondents may have had different AI applications in mind when
answering, which introduces heterogeneity in the referent and should be
considered when interpreting the findings.
3.3. Measures
The survey instrument was developed based on constructs from the
consumer trust and AI acceptance literatures. Items were adapted from
previously validated scales where available; study-specific items were
developed for constructs without established measures. All Likert-scale items
used a 7-point format (1 = Strongly Disagree to 7 = Strongly Agree). Table

3.4. Data Preparation
Prior to analysis, a data quality check identified a coding error in the
individual trust items (Q2): the data export had recorded a value of 9 in place
of the intended value of 7 (Strongly Agree). This was identified by examining
the range of values against the documented 1–7 scale and confirmed via the
value label mapping in the SPSS export file. All instances were corrected prior
to analysis. No other variables showed irregularities.
3.5. Reliability
Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha for
multi-item scales and Spearman-Brown coefficients for two-item measures.
The AI Digital Marketing Attitude scale demonstrated good reliability (α
= .855), while the General Company Trust (α = .870) and AI Company
Trust (α = .894) scales demonstrated strong reliability. The Individual Trust
measure exhibited lower internal consistency (α = .688), consistent with
reviewers’ observation that the four items (self-trustworthiness, interpersonal
trust, personal ethics, and trust in AI) may capture related but conceptually
distinct dimensions. Accordingly, these items are analyzed and interpreted
individually rather than as a composite. For the two-item measures, AI Trust
Impact demonstrated excellent reliability (Spearman-Brown = .933) and Data
Sharing Willingness also demonstrated excellent reliability (Spearman-Brown
= .953).

3.6. Analytical Approach
Analyses were conducted in Python using the SciPy and Statsmodels
libraries. One-sample t-tests compared each construct’s average against the
scale midpoint of 4.0 (addressing RQ1). Paired-samples t-tests compared how
the same respondents rated general companies versus AI-using companies
across seven trust dimensions, with Bonferroni correction applied for
multiple comparisons (addressing RQ2). Cohen’s d and 95% confidence
intervals are reported alongside significance tests to communicate practical
effect magnitude. Pearson correlations and a multiple linear regression model,
including variance inflation factor (VIF) diagnostics to assess
multicollinearity, examined attitudinal interrelationships and predictors of
AI company trust (addressing RQ3). One-way ANOVAs with Tukey HSD
post hoc tests examined whether trust differed across subgroups defined by
AI usage frequency, age, and gender, alongside descriptive privacy analyses
(addressing RQ4). Common method bias was assessed via Harman’s single-
factor test applied to the set of primary perceptual variables.

that consumer trust toward AI-powered marketing is best characterized as
cautious and conditional. Consumers accept AI’s functional utility while
remaining skeptical about the organizations deploying it — precisely the AI
trust gap this study investigates.

4.4. Research Question 4: What Broader Patterns Characterize the AI
Trust Landscape?
To address RQ4, one-way ANOVAs with Tukey HSD post hoc tests
examined whether AI company trust differed across consumer subgroups,
and descriptive analyses characterized privacy concerns and trust-building
preferences. Table 6 and Figure 3 present the group-comparison results.
The overall ANOVA for daily AI usage was significant, F(3, 489) = 6.15, p
< .001. Tukey HSD post hoc tests revealed that only the difference between
the less-than-2-hours group (M = 3.93, n = 407) and the 2-to-5-hours group
(M = 4.51, n = 70) was statistically significant (p = .001). No other group
comparisons reached significance, including those involving the 6-to-10-hours
(n = 10) and greater-than-10-hours (n = 6) groups. Given the extremely small
sample sizes in these latter two groups, conclusions about their means should
not be drawn, and future research should examine high-usage consumers with
an appropriately powered design.

5. Discussion
This study examined consumer trust in AI-powered digital marketing
through an exploratory survey of 500 U.S. adults. The findings are organized
around each research question and connected to the theoretical framework
introduced in the literature review.
5.1. Research Question 1
The findings associated with RQ1 reveal the core AI trust gap: consumers
accept AI marketing as functionally useful while remaining skeptical of the
organizations deploying it. Trust in AI was only marginally above neutral, and
outcome measures — particularly whether AI companies act in consumers’
best interests — were significantly below neutral. This pattern supports
Hoff and Bashir’s (2015) and Glikson and Woolley’s (2020) observation that
functional competence alone does not generate trust when users lack visibility
into how systems operate. It also aligns with privacy calculus theory: even
when consumers recognize the benefits of personalization, concerns about
organizational intentions and data practices suppress trust and data-sharing
willingness.

5.2. Research Question 2
The findings associated with RQ2 show that AI adoption is associated
with a targeted rather than uniform reduction in company trust. After
Bonferroni correction, only Competence and Capability differed significantly
between general and AI-using companies, with small effect sizes (d =
0.145–0.225). No significant differences were found on Humanity, Integrity,
Transparency, or Reliability. This selective pattern suggests that consumers
question the organizational judgment and execution quality behind AI
adoption — not the ethics or relational character of the firm. This finding
is consistent with Glikson and Woolley’s (2020) observation that perceived
competence is a separable and critical component of AI trust. Importantly,
the non-significant results on human-centered dimensions should not be
interpreted as establishing equivalence; they simply indicate insufficient
evidence of differences in this sample.
5.3. Research Question 3
The findings associated with RQ3 show that attitudes toward AI-powered
marketing are strongly interrelated, and that belief in AI’s benevolent intent
and overall marketing attitude are most strongly associated with AI company
trust. This is consistent with Chaudhuri and Ligas’s (2016) finding that
trust, rather than general satisfaction, mediates the relationship between
positive evaluations and behavioral engagement. Harman’s single-factor test
result (65.8% of variance explained by the first factor) suggests some degree
of common method variance, and readers should be cautious about the
magnitude of reported correlations. The regression findings are best
understood as identifying which attitudes travel together most closely, rather
than establishing causal pathways.
5.4. Research Question 4
The findings associated with RQ4 show that AI usage frequency is
statistically associated with AI company trust, but this relationship is more
limited than initially suggested. Tukey HSD post hoc tests indicate that
only moderate daily AI users (2–5 hours) differ significantly from light
users (<2 hours); higher-usage groups are too small to draw conclusions
from. This pattern is broadly consistent with Hoff and Bashir’s (2015)
emphasis on accumulated experience as a key mechanism in the development
of automation trust. The privacy findings reinforce the personalization
paradox (Aguirre et al., 2015) and creepy marketing concerns (Moore et al.,
2015): more company data knowledge tends to decrease rather than increase
trust for the plurality of consumers, and value-exchange incentives rank last
among trust-building preferences.

6. Managerial Implications
The findings identify several actions marketing managers can take to
address the gap between consumers’ acceptance of AI’s functional benefits
and their trust in the organizations deploying it. The results suggest that
effective AI implementation requires more than demonstrating technological
capability or delivering increasingly personalized experiences. Managers must
also consider how AI use affects perceptions of organizational intent, data
stewardship, consumer control, and competence.
Demonstrate consumer-oriented intent, not just personalization value.
Belief that companies using AI act in consumers’ best interests was strongly
associated with AI company trust. Managers should therefore communicate
not only that AI is being used, but why it is being used and how consumers
benefit from it. For example, firms can explain how AI helps reduce irrelevant
advertising, improve recommendations, simplify service interactions, or
otherwise create identifiable benefits for consumers. This shifts the emphasis
from demonstrating technological sophistication to demonstrating that AI
serves a legitimate consumer purpose.
Make data security and transparency visible. Consumers identified data
security (87.4%) and transparency about data use (83.0%) as the two most
frequently selected actions that would increase their trust in AI-driven
marketing. Opt-out options (78.2%) and clear privacy policies (75.8%) were
also selected substantially more often than providing greater value in
exchange for personal data (47.9%). This pattern suggests that trust may be
built more effectively through risk reduction, transparency, and consumer
control than through attempts to compensate consumers for increased data
collection. Managers should therefore make privacy protections visible at the
points where data are collected and AI-driven personalization occurs, clearly
communicating what information is collected, how it is used, and how it is
protected.
Preserve meaningful consumer control. The strong preference for opt-
out options suggests that consumers value the ability to control their
participation in AI-driven personalization. Managers should make
personalization and data-use controls easy to locate, understand, and modify
rather than treating them solely as legal or privacy-policy disclosures.
Providing consumers with meaningful choices regarding personalization, data
use, and AI-mediated interactions may also help reduce perceptions that
personalization represents surveillance rather than service. This approach is
consistent with the broader privacy findings, in which 44.0% of respondents
indicated that greater company knowledge of their personal data decreases
trust, compared with only 14.6% who indicated that it increases trust.
Do not assume that AI adoption signals greater organizational
competence. AI-using companies were rated significantly lower on
competence and capability than companies generally, although the observed
effect sizes were small. No significant differences after correction were found
on the remaining trust dimensions. For managers, these findings suggest that

adopting AI should not be assumed to enhance consumer perceptions of
organizational expertise. Firms may benefit from demonstrating the quality
and reliability of AI-supported decisions, maintaining appropriate human
oversight, and providing accessible human support when automated systems
fail or produce unsatisfactory results. AI implementation should therefore
be evaluated not only in terms of efficiency and technological performance
but also in terms of how it affects perceptions of the organization using the
technology.
Build familiarity among consumers with limited AI experience. Moderate
daily AI users (2-5 hours) reported significantly higher AI company trust
than light users (<2 hours), although the cross-sectional design does not
establish whether greater AI use produces greater trust or whether consumers
who are more trusting of AI are more likely to use it. Managers could
nevertheless consider introducing consumers to AI through low-risk, easily
understood applications before asking them to rely on AI in interactions
involving more sensitive information or consequential decisions. Product
discovery, basic recommendations, and optional AI-assisted customer service
may provide opportunities for consumers to become more familiar with
AI-enabled interactions. Because the present findings are associative rather
than causal, the effectiveness of such strategies should be tested rather than
assumed.
Consider existing brand trust when implementing AI personalization.
General company trust and AI company trust were very strongly correlated
(r = .874), suggesting that consumers’ existing perceptions of companies
are closely related to how they evaluate those companies in an AI context.
However, the magnitude of this relationship should be interpreted cautiously
given the evidence of potential common method bias in the study. Managers
should therefore view AI trust as part of broader brand and relationship
management rather than as an isolated technology issue. Organizations with
weaker baseline consumer trust should be particularly cautious about
assuming that sophisticated AI personalization will independently overcome
existing trust concerns.
Manage the AI trust gap as a strategic marketing issue. Collectively, these
findings suggest that the central managerial challenge is not convincing
consumers that AI-powered marketing is useful; respondents already
recognized its functional benefits. Rather, the challenge is converting that
perceived usefulness into trust in the organizations deploying the technology.
Managers should therefore evaluate AI marketing initiatives not only through
traditional performance measures such as engagement, conversion,
personalization effectiveness, and efficiency, but also through their effects on
consumer trust. Measures of perceived transparency, data security, consumer
control, benevolent intent, and organizational competence can help firms
identify whether AI implementation is strengthening or weakening customer
relationships. Ultimately, effective AI marketing may depend less on

7. Limitations and Future Research
This study has several important limitations. First, the cross-sectional
design does not support causal inference. The associations between AI usage
and trust, and between attitudinal predictors and AI company trust, could
reflect selection effects rather than causal relationships. Second, Harman’s
single-factor test indicated potential common method bias (first factor =
65.8%), a consequence of collecting all perceptual data from the same
respondents in the same survey. Future research should consider multi-source
designs or procedural remedies such as temporal separation of predictors and
outcomes.
Third, the sample is limited to U.S. adults, restricting cross-cultural
generalizability. Fourth, the survey asked about ‘AI-powered digital
marketing’ generally, without specifying a particular application; different
respondents may have had different technologies in mind, introducing
heterogeneity in the referent construct. Fifth, the regression model included
only attitudinal predictors; personality traits, prior data breach experiences,
and industry-specific factors were not measured.
Sixth, several trust-building preference response categories (Q17) had a
small number of additional responses beyond the five main categories
(Q17_6: n = 17, 3.4%) that were not analyzed in the main body of the paper.
Future research should examine the full range of consumer-identified trust-
building actions more comprehensively.
Future research could address these limitations through longitudinal or
experimental designs that establish causal direction, cross-national
comparisons, and studies that examine specific AI applications (e.g.,
recommendation systems, chatbots, targeted advertising) rather than AI-
powered marketing in general. Structural equation modeling could also test
the theoretical relationships implied by the present correlation and regression
findings.
8. Conclusion
This study documents a clear AI trust gap in consumer perceptions of
AI-powered digital marketing: consumers accept the functional usefulness of
AI while remaining skeptical about the organizations deploying it. AI-using
companies are specifically penalized on competence-related trust dimensions,
not on ethical or relational ones. Belief in AI’s benevolent intent and overall
attitude toward AI marketing are the attitudes most strongly associated with
AI company trust, while age and gender show no significant association.
Moderate daily AI users report higher trust than light users, suggesting a role
for experience in building comfort with AI marketing over time.

These findings suggest that companies hoping to build consumer trust
in AI-powered marketing should invest in visible governance, clear
communication of benevolent intent, and data security assurances rather
than expanded personalization or incentive programs. The gap between AI
marketing practice and consumer trust is real, measurable, and — these
findings suggest — unlikely to close without deliberate attention to how, not
just how well, AI is deployed.
