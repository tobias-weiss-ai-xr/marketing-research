Abstract
Brands increasingly rely on artiﬁcial intelligence to maintain recognizable brand voice across social
media, product communication, and chatbot-based service encounters. This conceptual paper
introduces the Persona-Avatar Continuity-Trust (PACT) Model to explain how consumers' familiarity with
an AI persona in social media may shape trust when they later interact with a recognizably continuous
chatbot avatar. Drawing on parasocial relationship theory, virtual inﬂuencer research, anthropomorphism,
social presence, dual-route trust formation, AI disclosure, expectation-conﬁrmation reasoning, self-
determination theory, self-brand congruity, and cross-cultural marketing, the paper develops an
integrative theory-building framework. The model proposes that threshold-level familiarity with a persona
can pre-activate social presence in later chatbot interactions, enabling a persona carryover effect that
supports affective and cognitive trust. This process is conditional on avatar-persona continuity, truthful
disclosure, perceived competence, performance-expectation ﬁt, consumer digital literacy, persona
selection agency, machine-heuristic preferences, and cultural context. The paper advances eight testable
propositions linking persona continuity to trust formation and downstream outcomes such as
engagement, loyalty, and brand equity. It shifts the unit of analysis from isolated AI touchpoints to
continuous AI-mediated brand relationships across content and conversation. The framework also offers
practical guidance for managing persona drift, AI disclosure, human escalation, data protection, and
meaningful consumer choice. Future research should validate the model through staged experiments,
longitudinal exposure designs, measurement development, non-avatar controls, and cross-cultural ﬁeld
studies.
1. INTRODUCTION
Consumers might repeatedly encounter an AI brand persona before they even have a service need. For
instance, an Indian beauty brand might employ an expressive digital character to share ingredient
narratives on Instagram, address common inquiries, and feature in product education videos. However,
related virtual-idol research also suggests cross-cultural variation, particularly in East Asian contexts
where virtual personae may become linked to mainstream or national identity formation (Song et al.,
2024). When the same character reappears in a chatbot interface, the user may not perceive the
interaction as a cold start with an unfamiliar system. Instead, the interaction can be viewed as an
extension of an existing relationship that is well-known but inherently unequal. This is crucial because AI
brand characters are increasingly assuming multiple organizational roles. While virtual inﬂuencers and
brand avatars are employed for content creation, storytelling, and endorsements, conversational agents
are utilized for providing recommendations and services. Still, theoretical traditions dealing with these
functions have typically examined them in isolation. Research on parasocial relationships, social media
inﬂuencers, and virtual inﬂuencers examines factors such as familiarity, authenticity, disclosure,
engagement, and parasocial response within content environments (Hoffner & Bond, 2022; Lou, 2022;
Lou et al., 2023; Stein et al., 2024). Research on chatbots more frequently examines trust established
within bounded dialogues through dimensions such as warmth, competence, empathy, disclosure, and
perceived reliability (Kull et al., 2021; Cheng et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2022; Prakash & Das, 2024; Wang
Page 2/29

et al., 2026). This separation has signiﬁcant consequences. Evidence suggests that anthropomorphic
and social cues can shape trust-related responses in AI-mediated interactions, while disclosure of non-
human identities may yield different outcomes depending on whether the context involves customer-
service interactions or digital endorsements (De Cicco et al., 2021; Mozafari et al., 2022; Wang & Qiu,
2024). Still, these studies do not determine whether prior familiarity with the same recognizable AI
persona alters the initial conditions of chatbot trust formation.
A consumer familiar with a digital persona may approach the interaction with expectations of warmth,
tone, competence, and authenticity that are absent in encounters with an unfamiliar chatbot. This study
addresses this theoretical gap by introducing the Persona-Avatar Continuity-Trust (PACT) Model. In the
model, persona refers to the content-phase identity through which a brand's AI character becomes
familiar, and avatar refers to the conversational instantiation through which that character responds in
an interactive setting. Continuity refers to perceived consistency in visual cues, linguistic style, values,
disclosure, and expected conduct across contexts. The central claim is intentionally provisional:
persona-speciﬁc parasocial familiarity may pre-activate perceived social presence in a later chatbot
encounter, thereby altering the route and rate at which trust is formed. This proposed mechanism is
termed the persona carryover effect, referring to the transfer of relational warmth and readiness from
asynchronous content exposure to synchronous or quasi-synchronous interactions within the same
consumer-persona relationship. The effect remains conditional on a temporal trade-off: functional
competence, including accuracy and safety, is a prerequisite for initial trust before relational warmth can
become dominant. The machine heuristic may also moderate the process, because some users prefer
nonanthropomorphic interfaces that they perceive as more objective, systematic, or eﬃcient (Sundar &
Kim, 2019; Yang & Sundar, 2024). The model culminates in eight propositions, including the ﬁnal claim
that affective and cognitive trust serve as primary drivers of brand engagement, loyalty, and equity. Three
conditions make the proposed effect falsiﬁable: familiarity must reach a meaningful threshold; transfer
should add to, rather than replace, competent within-chat interaction; and it should be speciﬁc to
familiarity with the persona rather than general awareness of the brand. PACT also identiﬁes a negative
counterpart. When a consumer recognizes that the chatbot is intended to be a familiar persona but
experiences its style, values, disclosure, or competence as inconsistent, the prior relationship may
increase rather than reduce risk. This study refers to this as the continuity violation effect. The model
further considers whether consumer persona selection agency, disclosure architecture, performance
expectation ﬁt, AI aversion, digital literacy, and cultural context alter these relationships. The study is
guided by three research questions:
(RQ1) Under what conditions might parasocial familiarity with an AI brand persona transfer into a later
chatbot encounter?
(RQ2) Through which social presence and cognitive-affective trust pathways might such transfers
inﬂuence brand-related outcomes?

(RQ3) When might continuity, disclosure, performance, and consumer choice strengthen, weaken, or
reverse the proposed effects?
The manuscript proceeds by stating its conceptual development approach, describing the data basis of
the conceptual synthesis, reviewing the theoretical foundations, presenting the four-stage model and
propositions, and specifying an empirical validation agenda and governance implications.
2. CONCEPTUAL DEVELOPMENT APPROACH AND STATUS OF
CLAIMS
This article is written as an integrative conceptual paper rather than a systematic literature review or
empirical study. Its purpose is to unify previously isolated theoretical perspectives around a speciﬁc unit
of analysis: the recognizable AI brand persona that evolves from feed-based content to conversational
services. This study does not purport to provide a comprehensive overview of all AI marketing research,
nor does it report observed effect sizes. It employs published evidence to formulate plausible premises
and generates new propositions that require further testing. The conceptual synthesis revolves around
six key questions: whether audiences can develop parasocial familiarity with artiﬁcial or virtual ﬁgures;
how anthropomorphic, authentic, and brand-value cues foster such familiarity; how social presence and
trust operate within conversational AI; why disclosure effects vary across contexts; how continuity can
create expectation conﬁrmation or violation; and how consumer choice may enhance trust through
autonomy and self-congruity. While classical theories offer construct deﬁnitions, newer peer-reviewed
studies provide empirical anchors for AI and virtual inﬂuencer contexts. To enhance theoretical
parsimony, established empirical premises are treated as foundational rather than being listed
separately. The eight propositions below synthesize the paper's core theoretical contributions: content-
to-conversation persona carryover, continuity violation, dual-route trust conversion, disclosure
architecture, performance-expectation ﬁt, persona selection agency, digital-literacy boundary conditions,
and the inﬂuence of affective and cognitive trust on brand outcomes.
Following guidance on conceptual-article design, the paper is positioned as a hybrid of theory synthesis
and model-building: it integrates constructs from adjacent literatures, speciﬁes a new cross-touchpoint
mechanism, and translates that mechanism into propositions that can be empirically challenged
(MacInnis, 2011; Gilson & Goldberg, 2015; Jaakkola, 2020). The value of the article is therefore assessed
in terms of theoretical clarity, internal coherence, novelty, and capacity to guide future empirical work
rather than statistical representativeness.
By design, the model prioritizes content by investigating what occurs when social media familiarity
precedes chatbot interaction. While a chatbot-ﬁrst approach is a plausible option included in the
research agenda, it is not assumed to be symmetrical. Similarly, the proposed Indian validation context is
justiﬁed as a substantive empirical setting for digital brand relationships rather than as proof that the
propositions have already been established in India. This positioning clariﬁes the scope of the
conceptual claim: PACT advances testable relationships to be validated rather than presenting them as

3. DATA
This article is conceptual and theory-building in nature. No primary empirical dataset was collected, and
no human participants, customer records, platform logs, private organizational data, or experimental
responses were used to generate the manuscript's propositions. The data basis of the study is therefore
a secondary conceptual source corpus rather than a newly created empirical sample.
The source corpus consists of the cited works listed in the References section, including peer-reviewed
journal articles, foundational theory papers, conference proceedings, scholarly books, arXiv preprints
used only as emerging technical evidence, and one publicly available industry report on the Indian
internet context. These sources were selected because they directly inform the theoretical traditions
used in the PACT model: parasocial relationship theory, virtual inﬂuencer research, anthropomorphism
and social presence, dual-route trust formation, disclosure and persuasion knowledge, expectation-
Page 7/29

conﬁrmation and expectancy-violation logic, self-determination theory, self-brand congruity, digital
literacy, and cross-cultural marketing.
The data collection procedure followed an integrative conceptual synthesis process rather than a
systematic review protocol. Relevant sources were identiﬁed through targeted literature searching,
backward and forward citation chaining, and screening for conceptual relevance to AI brand personae,
virtual inﬂuencers, chatbots, disclosure, social presence, trust, and consumer-brand relationship
outcomes. Sources were retained when they supplied either a foundational theoretical construct,
empirical evidence for a mechanism used in the model, or a boundary condition relevant to content-to-
conversation continuity. Because the objective is theory construction rather than comprehensive
evidence mapping, the process was not designed as a scoping review, did not use PRISMA-ScR
procedures, and does not claim exhaustive coverage of every empirical study in AI marketing.
The analytical procedure involved qualitative concept mapping and theory synthesis. Constructs were
compared across the selected literatures, overlapping mechanisms were consolidated, and relationships
were organized into the four-stage Persona-Avatar Continuity-Trust architecture. The propositions were
developed by linking the accumulated source evidence to theoretically plausible but as-yet untested
relationships. No statistical analysis, machine-learning model training, scraping, survey analysis, or
proprietary analytics platform was used in the study.
Data accessibility is limited to the cited sources themselves. Public reports and open-access preprints
can be accessed from their issuing organizations or repositories, while journal articles and books may
require publisher access or institutional subscription. No third-party permissions, data-use agreements,
or ethical approvals were required because the manuscript does not reproduce restricted datasets,
collect personal data, or analyze identiﬁable human-subject information. Any future empirical validation
proposed in the manuscript should obtain informed consent, proportionate data protection safeguards,
platform permissions where applicable, and institutional ethics approval whenever human participants
or behavioral data are involved.
4. THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS
4.1 Parasocial familiarity and mediated AI personae
Horton and Wohl (1956) deﬁned parasocial interaction as a sense of intimacy experienced at a distance
from media ﬁgures. Subsequent parasocial research shows that such perceived relationships can
become meaningful features of media use without becoming reciprocal interpersonal relationships
(Rubin et al., 1985; Hoffner & Bond, 2022). While contemporary digital platforms offer features such as
comments and reactions that suggest responsiveness, an asymmetry often persists, allowing
consumers to feel closeness without reciprocal social awareness. Lou (2022) characterizes this
dynamic as a trans-parasocial relation within inﬂuencer contexts. For an AI brand persona, this implies
that consumers distinguish software from humans and do not equate the relationship with interpersonal
Page 8/29

friendship. Instead, repeated exposure to a consistent character can foster familiarity, an expected tone,
and a feeling of relational warmth. Research comparing virtual and human inﬂuencers suggests that
consumers can develop parasocial responses to virtual ﬁgures, particularly when they perceive similarity,
human-likeness, authenticity, or credible persona cues (De Cicco et al., 2024; Lou et al., 2023; Stein et al.,
2024). PACT therefore treats parasocial familiarity as a potential antecedent relational resource rather
than simply as an outcome of content engagement.
4.2 Persona authenticity, brand-value inheritance, and
anthropomorphism calibration
Virtual personas are intentionally crafted rather than naturally emergent brand representatives. Their
perceived authenticity may therefore depend less on claims of human origin and more on the coherence
of their narrative, voice, values, and transparent admission of artiﬁciality. Allal-Chérif et al. (2024)
suggest that the effectiveness of AI-powered virtual inﬂuencers cannot be reduced to appearance alone,
because narrative and perceived authenticity are central to their marketing performance. Similarly, Lou et
al. (2023) show that consumers may perceive virtual inﬂuencers as “authentically fake,” accepting their
staged or artiﬁcial nature when the persona is narratively coherent and transparently constructed.
Recent work further links anthropomorphised virtual inﬂuencer cues and perceived authenticity to
consumer trust and engagement, including both cognitive and affective trust-related outcomes (Goel &
Garg, 2025). PACT introduces brand-value inheritance to capture consumers’ perception that an AI
persona recognizably embodies the brand’s values, personality, and narrative. This is a consumer-
perceived construct rather than a claim about technical training data. Brand-value inheritance may foster
parasocial familiarity because a consistent persona is easier to recognize, interpret, and remember
across content and service contexts. Anthropomorphism calibration is treated as a distinct design
condition: humanlike cues can enhance social legibility, but excessive realism or overly humanlike
expectations may make deﬁciencies more salient and create discomfort or trust risk (Epley et al., 2007;
Mori et al., 2012; Uysal et al., 2022; Gutuleac et al., 2024; Dabiran et al., 2024).
4.3 Social presence and dual-route trust formation in
chatbot interaction
Social presence refers to the extent to which a mediated interaction is experienced as socially present or
personally involving. In digital service contexts, social presence has been linked to consumer trust, and
in chatbot contexts, responsiveness, conversational warmth, competence, empathy, reliability, and
relational cues may contribute to trust-related evaluation (Gefen & Straub, 2004; Nordheim et al., 2019;
Kull et al., 2021; Cheng et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2022; Prakash & Das, 2024; Wang et al., 2026). Trust
should not be treated as a unitary judgement in this context. Cognitive trust concerns competence,
reliability, accuracy, and appropriate oversight, whereas affective trust concerns perceived benevolence,
warmth, emotional comfort, and relational conﬁdence (McAllister, 1995; McKnight & Chervany, 2001;
Dunn & Schweitzer, 2005; Johnson & Grayson, 2005). The PACT extension suggests that parasocial
familiarity may alter the initial balance between these trust routes. An unfamiliar chatbot may initially be
Page 9/29

judged mainly through competence and reliability cues; a recognized, familiar AI persona may make
affective trust cues salient earlier, provided that the system performs adequately. This distinction
matters for PACT because persona continuity is expected to change the sequencing of trust formation,
not to replace functional competence as a requirement for trustworthy service.
4.4 Disclosure architecture and persuasion knowledge
Disclosure
evidence is not consistently positive or negative. In customer-interaction contexts, Luo et al. (2019)
found that revealing a chatbot's identity before a sales conversation can decrease purchases by lowering
perceived knowledge and empathy. De Cicco et al. (2025) similarly show that non-human identity cues
can shape consumer responses through social presence and speciesism activation.
In contrast, Wang and Qiu (2024) found that AI technology transparency can have beneﬁcial effects in
digital endorser contexts, while Kirkby et al. (2023) found that AI-disclosed brand voice does not
necessarily reduce perceived brand authenticity or brand attitude. Liu et al. (2025) further suggest that
intelligence disclosure interacts with the temporal phase of the customer journey.
PACT therefore treats disclosure as a four-dimensional architecture encompassing timing, speciﬁcity,
setting, and product involvement. Timing concerns when consumers are informed about AI involvement.
Speciﬁcity concerns whether disclosure merely labels the agent as AI or clariﬁes human oversight,
escalation, and data use. Setting distinguishes service interactions from content, endorsement, or brand-
voice contexts. Product involvement is treated as a boundary condition because consumers may
process AI disclosure differently when a product is personally relevant, risky, or consequential rather
than low-involvement or routine.
4.5 Persona selection agency, autonomy, and congruity
Many conversational interfaces use generic agents. A brand featuring multiple AI personas could allow
consumers to choose the character they prefer to interact with. According to self-determination theory,
autonomy, competence, and relatedness foster engagement and positive experiences (Deci & Ryan,
1985). PACT does not presume that every option is advantageous, because an abundance of choices or
poorly differentiated personas can be counterproductive (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000; Scheibehenne et al.,
2010). It predicts a bounded agency advantage, in which selecting a familiar persona that is meaningful
and manageable makes the interaction feel more self-directed and relationally appropriate. Still, the
PACT model must recognize that the desire for a customizable or relational persona is not universal.
Some users may rely on a machine heuristic, preferring an AI’s perceived objectivity, procedural
neutrality, security, or systematic eﬃciency over anthropomorphic warmth (Sundar & Kim, 2019; Yang &
Sundar, 2024). For these consumers, a functional, non-relational interface may foster greater trust than a
familiar persona, indicating that persona carryover effects are limited by individual preferences for
machine-like neutrality. Self-brand congruity offers an additional pathway through which a chosen
persona may be viewed as consistent with a consumer’s self-concept, thereby reinforcing identiﬁcation
Page 10/29

and affective trust (Sirgy, 1982; Fournier, 1998). Since autonomy can be immediate while identiﬁcation
often develops through repeated experiences, PACT treats these pathways as related but not
interchangeable.
4.6 The cross-touchpoint gap
While the literature offers plausible evidence for the component mechanisms of PACT, it has not yet
established the continuity pathway as a whole. Research on virtual inﬂuencers typically assesses
content exposure, chatbot studies usually examine interactions after they begin, and disclosure research
often evaluates cues within a speciﬁc context. PACT redirects attention to the transition between
settings: whether a consumer perceives the chatbot as the same persona encountered in prior content,
transfers parasocial familiarity into the interaction, and treats consistency or inconsistency as
consequential for trust. This cross-touchpoint bridge is the model’s primary focus and represents a
critical empirical challenge for future research.
5. THE PACT MODEL: A STAGE-BY-STAGE CONCEPTUAL
ARCHITECTURE
5.1 Organizing logic
The PACT model is organized as a process architecture rather than an assertion of a deterministic
consumer response. It proposes that a recognizable AI brand persona can accumulate familiarity during
content exposure, carry that familiarity into interaction through perceived continuity, and thereby change
the conditions under which trust and brand-related behavior emerge. The model is represented in four
stages: Persona Genesis, Familiarity Accumulation, Continuity Transfer, and Trust and Outcomes. The
core mechanism is social presence pre-activation: before substantive dialogue begins, recognition of a
familiar persona may make the chatbot feel more socially present. However, this is moderated by a
temporal trade-off, where functional competence is a prerequisite for initial trust before relational
warmth becomes dominant. Pre-activation is a mechanism that gains eﬃcacy as the interaction
progresses. The persona carryover effect is expected to operate only above a familiarity threshold and
remains vulnerable to persona drift as a continuous technical challenge. Figure 1 presents the
parsimonious PACT conceptual research model and maps the eight integrative propositions to the model
architecture.

5.2 Stage 1: Persona Genesis - identity construction
Page 11/29

Persona genesis refers to the process by which a brand develops an AI character that maintains
consistent recognition over time. Brand value inheritance refers to the perceived alignment between
brand identity and persona expression, anthropomorphism calibration involves the extent of human-like
visual, linguistic, and behavioral cues, and visual-linguistic identity design provides the recognizable
features that facilitate future continuity. At this stage, authenticity stems not only from design but also
from continuous management. Practitioners must contend with persona drift, deﬁned here as the
technical and experiential risk that an AI agent deviates from its expected identity, tone, values, or
conduct across multi-turn conversations. Current technical evidence on persona drift and persona
ﬁdelity draws partly on arXiv preprints and should therefore be treated as emerging rather than settled
(Brandt, 2025; Brandt & Wang, 2025; Li et al., 2024; Shin et al., 2025).
5.3 Stage 2: Familiarity Accumulation - relational build-up
Through repeated interactions, a consumer may grow accustomed to the persona's tone, narratives, and
expected replies. The intensity of parasocial familiarity reﬂects the strength of this non-reciprocal
relational orientation, with perceived persona warmth and narrative immersion proposed as its
antecedents. Crucially, this construct differs from brand familiarity, because recognizing a brand's
products does not equate to feeling acquainted with its recurring AI character.
5.4 Stage 3: Continuity transfer-recognition in interaction
Continuity transfer begins when consumers encounter the chatbot and recognize it as the same persona
previously encountered in feed-based content. Visual, linguistic, narrative, and ethical consistency are
expected to facilitate recognition, pre-activate social presence, and make the transition from content to
conversation feel coherent. This logic is consistent with expectation-conﬁrmation reasoning: prior
expectations become consequential when later experience conﬁrms or disconﬁrms them (Oliver, 1980).
Still, continuity is not guaranteed by reusing the same avatar or brand name. Large language models and
role-playing language agents can drift from assigned identity across extended interaction, producing out-
of-character behavior, instruction drift, or persona-ﬁdelity failures (Li et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Shin
et al., 2025). Persona selection agency may strengthen continuity transfer by making the interaction feel
self-selected and relationally appropriate. In contrast, a recognizable but inconsistent persona may
violate user expectations more strongly than a neutral, unfamiliar chatbot. Under PACT, this creates a
continuity-risk mechanism: the same prior familiarity that enables persona carryover may also intensify
negative reactions when the chatbot's tone, competence, values, or disclosure practices conﬂict with the
persona consumers previously encountered.
5.5 Stage 4: Trust and brand outcomes
The interaction leads to assessments of both cognitive and affective trust. Cognitive trust is grounded in
perceived competence, accuracy, reliability, transparency, disclosure quality, data security, and
appropriate escalation arrangements. Affective trust is grounded in warmth, perceived care, emotional
comfort, benevolence, and social presence (Johnson & Grayson, 2005; Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Shang et
al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026). PACT does not assume that either trust route always dominates. Instead, it
Page 12/29

proposes that prior parasocial familiarity changes the relative salience and sequencing of cognitive and
affective trust during the chatbot encounter. For an unfamiliar chatbot, consumers may initially rely more
heavily on competence and reliability cues. For a recognized AI brand persona, affective cues such as
warmth, familiarity, and relational comfort may become salient earlier, but only if baseline performance
is adequate. If the chatbot fails on accuracy, reliability, or escalation, prior familiarity may not protect
trust and may even increase disappointment. Downstream outcomes include continued chatbot use,
willingness to rely on the chatbot when appropriate, reduced need for human support in low-risk service
situations, openness to relevant recommendations, engagement, loyalty, and brand-equity effects.
Existing chatbot research supports links between chatbot service quality, cognitive trust, affective trust,
satisfaction, and customer loyalty, but PACT extends this logic by asking whether these outcomes are
strengthened or weakened when the chatbot is recognized as a previously encountered AI brand persona
(Chen et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2026). The core constructs and their proposed observable indicators are

6. INTEGRATIVE PROPOSITION DEVELOPMENT
PACT is advanced through eight integrative propositions. The earlier component relationships have not
been discarded; rather, they are retained as mediation logic, boundary conditions, controls, or theoretical
rationale within the propositions to which they are most closely connected. Prior empirical ﬁndings are
therefore used as grounding and are not numbered as independent propositions.
6.1 Persona genesis and relational familiarity
P1. Perceived brand value inheritance and calibrated anthropomorphism are expected jointly to
strengthen parasocial familiarity with an AI brand persona through perceived authenticity and warmth;
Page 14/29

this positive relationship may weaken where humanlike cues become incongruent with the persona's
performance capacity or disclosed artiﬁciality.
Theoretical rationale. This proposition preserves the design and authenticity pathways in a single
upstream claim. It recognizes that a coherent persona may invite familiarity without assuming that
greater realism is always beneﬁcial (Epley et al., 2007; Moore, 2012). It also treats the machine heuristic
as a boundary condition: for users who value objectivity, systematic processing, and perceived lack of
human bias, calibrated machine-like neutrality may be more trustworthy than relational warmth (Sundar
& Kim, 2019; Yang & Sundar, 2024).
6.2 Persona-speciﬁc carryover
P2. Relative to equivalent non-persona brand exposure, threshold-level parasocial familiarity with an AI
brand persona is expected to increase perceived social presence at the beginning of a later chatbot
encounter and reduce perceived interaction friction when the chatbot is recognized as that persona.
Theoretical rationale. P2 retains threshold familiarity and social presence pre-activation. Crucially,
competence acts as a functional gateway for the persona carryover effect, as baseline accuracy and
reliability are necessary for trust to transition from purely cognitive to affective domains.
6.3 Continuity and its violation
P3. Perceived cross-channel continuity between the content-phase persona and its chatbot instantiation
is expected to strengthen the persona carryover effect, whereas recognized discontinuity is expected to
generate a continuity violation that may reduce trust below an unfamiliar chatbot baseline.
Theoretical rationale. This proposition consolidates the positive consistency condition and the negative
violation prediction. It explicitly includes persona drift as a primary technical driver of continuity violation,
where LLM inconsistency triggers disconﬁrmation of the established relational baseline.
6.4 Dual-route trust conversion and outcomes
P4. In chatbot encounters, social presence pre-activation is expected to increase affective trust, whereas
perceived competence and reliability support cognitive trust; further, parasocial familiarity is expected to
increase the relative contribution of affective trust to continuance, engagement, and loyalty outcomes.
Theoretical rationale. P4 retains the paper's mediator and trust-route salience claims while avoiding
separate propositions for already established trust dimensions. It remains conditional: relational
readiness may facilitate trust formation, but does not excuse poor performance (Cheng et al., 2022;
Johnson & Grayson, 2005; Prakash & Das, 2024).
6.5 Disclosure architecture
P5. The trust consequences of truthful AI disclosure are expected to depend jointly on timing, speciﬁcity,
and communication setting: pre-interaction and speciﬁc disclosure should better preserve cognitive trust
Page 15/29

than delayed or vague disclosure. By contrast, disclosure in moralized or cause-related communication
may heighten scepticism, particularly among consumers high in AI aversion.
Theoretical rationale. This integrative proposition preserves the timing, context, speciﬁcity, and
scepticism logic in one architecture rather than treating each design dimension as an independent
theoretical contribution (Luo et al., 2019; Wang & Qiu, 2024; De Cicco et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Qiu et
al., 2025).
6.6 Performance-expectation ﬁt
P6. The positive inﬂuence of humanlike persona cues on cognitive trust is expected to depend on
chatbot competence and reliability, such that salient service failure produces a sharper trust decrement
where a highly anthropomorphic or already familiar persona has raised consumer expectations.
Theoretical rationale. P6 preserves the competence mediation and mismatch penalty while expressing
them as one performance-contingent proposition. It guards against interpreting warmth or familiarity as
a substitute for functional quality.
6.7 Consumer persona selection agency
P7. Meaningful selection of a familiar AI persona, rather than assignment to an otherwise equivalent
avatar, is expected to increase affective trust, engagement and continuance through perceived autonomy
and self-brand congruity.
Theoretical rationale. This proposition retains both agency and congruity mechanisms within a
manageable claim. It treats choice as bounded: agency is likely to be beneﬁcial only where options are
intelligible, non-manipulative, and relevant to the consumer (Deci & Ryan, 1985; Fournier, 1998; Sirgy,
1982).
6.8 Digital-literacy boundary condition
P8. Consumer digital literacy is expected to moderate PACT trust formation, strengthening the inﬂuence
of speciﬁc transparency and competence information on cognitive trust while attenuating reliance on
anthropomorphic social cues alone.
Theoretical rationale. P8 retains a theoretically meaningful consumer-level moderator without
multiplying propositions for every contextual variation. Cross-cultural, linguistic, and service-risk
variations remain appropriate boundary tests in the empirical programme rather than additional
propositions (van Deursen & van Dijk, 2011; Petty & Cacioppo, 1986).

7.2 The decisive sequential continuity experiment
Study 3 should expose participants to a branded content stream through repeated encounters instead of
a single vignette. Participants would be randomly assigned to either an AI persona content condition or
an informational brand-content control condition, with the latter matched on brand, product, message
volume, and visual quality. After a suﬃcient interval, they should engage in a service task via either a
persona-consistent chatbot or one that is deliberately inconsistent. A distinct choice manipulation can
compare the selection of a persona with assignment to the same persona. Before interpreting outcome
tests, manipulation checks must evaluate the intensity of parasocial familiarity, brand familiarity, cross-
channel recognition, perceived persona consistency, and the clarity of perceived AI disclosure. Persona
carryover will be supported only if the avatar exposure condition demonstrates signiﬁcantly higher initial
social presence and lower interaction friction than the non-avatar brand control, thereby exerting an
indirect inﬂuence on affective trust and behavioral outcomes. A continuity violation would require a
planned contrast in which the familiar yet inconsistent condition is trusted less than an unfamiliar
chatbot baseline. Since brief studies may fail to generate suﬃcient parasocial familiarity (Boerman & van
Reijmersdal, 2020), a non-signiﬁcant result should not be automatically interpreted as disconﬁrmation. A
null result stemming from weak familiarity manipulation would mainly indicate a design ﬂaw, whereas a
null result derived from validated high familiarity, recognition, and suﬃcient statistical power would offer
greater theoretical insight.
7.3 Measurement and analysis considerations
Wherever feasible, established measures should be employed to assess cognitive and affective trust,
parasocial interaction, social presence, engagement, and loyalty. To enhance empirical rigor and reduce
potential bias in self-reported data, studies may supplement traditional psychometric scales with
automated, objective multi-turn evaluation frameworks, including validated LLM-as-a-judge scoring, to
assess Prompt-to-Line Consistency and verify persona adherence throughout the dialogue. New or
signiﬁcantly adapted scales are necessary to measure brand value inheritance, cross-channel persona
recognition, perceived interaction friction, disclosure speciﬁcity, and persona selection agency. These
measures should initially undergo expert review, cognitive interviews, and a pilot conﬁrmatory factor
Page 19/29

analysis. When constructs shift from human media ﬁgures to explicitly disclosed AI personae, the
wording must avoid implying that consumers believe the agent is sentient or genuinely caring.
Additionally, multi-group analysis (MGA) within the structural equation modeling (SEM) framework can
test statistically signiﬁcant differences across consumer segments. This approach is appropriate for
validating moderating roles such as digital literacy and cultural background by comparing path
coeﬃcients across groups, provided measurement invariance is established ﬁrst.
The empirical program should incorporate longitudinal design and multi-turn evaluation to test whether
persona continuity and trust formation remain stable across repeated interactions. Automated
evaluation, including LLM-based scoring, may be used as an auxiliary tool for assessing persona
consistency, but such scores should be validated against human-coded consistency ratings and
participant recognition checks.
The statistical method should be selected after conﬁrming measurement speciﬁcations. Covariance-
based SEM is appropriate when the study is conﬁrmatory and constructs are modeled reﬂectively with
adequate distributional properties. PLS-SEM may be appropriate when prediction-oriented objectives,
complex models, or well-justiﬁed composite measures dominate (Hair et al., 2021). In either case,
studies should report measurement validity, indirect-effect conﬁdence intervals, robustness checks,
model comparisons, and preregistered contrasts. Multi-group comparisons should be preceded by
measurement-invariance tests.
To support credible inference, experiments should be preregistered, include power analyses suited to
moderated mediation and planned contrasts, and report exclusions, attrition, and unsuccessful
manipulations. Field studies should collect behavioral evidence, such as chatbot continuation or human
escalation, only with informed consent and proportionate data protection.
7.4 Indian and cross-cultural validation opportunity
The Indian digital consumer setting is theoretically valuable because brands increasingly operate in a
large, mobile-ﬁrst, multilingual, and culturally heterogeneous interaction environment. Recent evidence
from the Internet and Mobile Association of India and Kantar (2025) indicates that India has one of the
world's largest active internet populations and that Indic-language use is central to digital consumption.
India should nevertheless be treated as a validation context rather than presumed evidence for PACT. A
robust design would recruit participants in India and at least one comparison market, test measurement
equivalence, examine language and platform familiarity, and assess whether trust pathways, disclosure
expectations, or persona-selection beneﬁts differ across settings.
8. DISCUSSION
8.1 Theoretical contribution
Page 20/29

PACT's main contribution lies in its proposed shift in the unit of analysis. While existing research offers
valuable insights into virtual inﬂuencers, chatbot trust, and disclosure, consumers are increasingly
encountering these elements not as isolated applications but as a uniﬁed, recognizable AI brand persona
throughout their journey. By theorizing the shift from feed to conversation, PACT introduces temporal
and cross-channel structure to the formation of AI trust. The model's adaptability to diverse cultural
contexts also presents an important area for further investigation. In markets such as East Asia, trust
building may integrate symbolic elements, including cultural alignment and collective identity, alongside
the affective and cognitive pathways outlined in the PACT model. Identifying these symbolic elements is
a potential expansion of the framework, thereby strengthening its applicability to global digital branding
strategies.
Second, the persona-carryover and continuity-violation propositions render continuity empirically
signiﬁcant. Familiarity is not always advantageous. Maintaining a coherent and capable persona can
reduce interaction friction and accelerate affective trust, whereas inconsistency or inadequate
performance may lead to heightened disappointment. This symmetry enhances the model's falsiﬁability
and ethical signiﬁcance. The technical challenge is that large language models can unintentionally
deviate from assigned personas during multi-turn conversations. Such drift can prevent the model from
reaching the intended continuity stage because even minor inconsistencies may provoke the continuity
violation effect and erode relational equity.
Third, PACT incorporates disclosure ﬁndings while avoiding the reduction of transparency to a mere cost
of trust. PACT suggests maintaining truthful disclosure while testing timing, speciﬁcity, and
communication setting as design features. This approach aligns with consumer autonomy, as trust built
on continuity should not rely on concealing artiﬁcial identities or gathering relational data without
explanation.
Fourth, the consumer persona selection agency expands AI brand relationship thinking beyond the
passive assignment of a bot. By connecting meaningful choice to autonomy and self-brand congruity,
PACT initiates a research agenda exploring the conditions under which consumer-controlled relational
interfaces can foster trust without devolving into manipulative personalization. However, a balanced
perspective must acknowledge the machine heuristic. Some users may prefer non-personiﬁed AI
because they perceive it as more objective, systematic, and less susceptible to human-like bias (Sundar
& Kim, 2019; Yang & Sundar, 2024). This argues that extensive personalization is not always beneﬁcial,
offering a more reﬁned perspective for future studies on user preferences between machine-like
neutrality and anthropomorphic warmth.
8.2 Managerial and governance implications
Managers may be tempted to interpret a popular AI persona as an asset that can be deployed
indiscriminately across all consumer touchpoints. PACT suggests greater caution. Where the same
persona moves from content to service, design governance should preserve recognizable voice and
values while acknowledging the technical challenge of persona drift. Practitioners must actively manage
Page 21/29

this drift to keep an AI consistently in character and ensure that promised warmth is supported by
competent service capabilities and accessible human escalation.
Disclosure
should occur before substantive interaction and should be understandable rather than merely legalistic.
However, practitioners must consider the potential differential impacts of disclosure based on product
involvement. For high-involvement products, explicit AI labeling can lead to a sharper decline in trust
compared to low-involvement categories. Consumers should be informed that they are interacting with
an AI persona, whether a human can intervene, how data generated in the conversation may be used,
and how to opt for an alternative channel. For a familiar persona, disclosure should also avoid implying a
human memory or relationship that the system does not possess.
Choice architecture requires similar restraint. Offering a small number of clearly differentiated personas
may support autonomy; nudging consumers toward a persona based on opaque proﬁling could
undermine it. Brands should therefore treat persona-selection data as sensitive interaction preference
information and avoid inferring vulnerability, identity or emotion without appropriate permission and
safeguards.
8.3 Limitations and directions for future research
The proposed model has several limitations. First, it is conceptual and therefore does not demonstrate
that persona carryover exists, reaches a practically important magnitude, or generalizes across products
and cultures. Second, its content-ﬁrst pathway may not describe journeys in which consumers initially
meet a chatbot and later follow the persona in content environments. Reverse or reciprocal transfer
should be examined rather than assumed.
Third, parasocial familiarity is diﬃcult to induce ethically and ecologically in short experiments.
Longitudinal content exposure and studies of real followers may be required. Fourth, high-stakes service
contexts such as health, ﬁnance, or complaints make product involvement a critical moderator; for these
high-involvement products, users may exhibit a strong human-labeling bias that limits or negates the
affective advantage of familiarity. Fifth, personas may generate adverse outcomes, including
dependency, stereotyping, identity manipulation, privacy intrusion, or reluctance to seek human
assistance.
Future research should examine trust repair after persona failure, the effects of language and cultural
relational norms, the point at which persona choice becomes burdensome, consumer responses to
continuity disclosure, and the behavior of vulnerable or younger users. Importantly, studies should
evaluate not only engagement and purchase intentions but also technical solutions for mitigating
persona drift and maintaining identity consistency, alongside informed choice, appropriate escalation,
and consumer welfare.
9. CONCLUSION
Page 22/29

AI brand personas may increasingly accompany consumers from content consumption into
conversation. PACT proposes that this transition matters: parasocial familiarity developed in a feed may
pre-activate social presence when the same persona appears as a chatbot, thereby inﬂuencing how trust
begins to form. The proposed effect is neither automatic nor inherently beneﬁcial; it is conditional on
recognizable continuity, competence, transparent disclosure, and meaningful consumer control.
Taken together, the model answers RQ1 by proposing that transfer is most likely when persona
familiarity exceeds a meaningful threshold and the chatbot is recognized as the same persona. It
answers RQ2 by specifying social presence pre-activation as the bridge from familiarity to affective trust,
while competence and reliability sustain cognitive trust. It answers RQ3 by identifying continuity,
disclosure architecture, performance-expectation ﬁt, persona selection agency, machine-heuristic
preferences, digital literacy, product involvement, and culture as conditions that can strengthen, weaken,
or reverse the proposed effects.
By grounding eight integrative propositions in relevant prior evidence and setting out a staged validation
program, this paper offers a cautious but testable framework for studying continuous AI brand
relationships. Its central normative position is equally important: if brands design continuity as a trust
resource, they should govern that continuity transparently and preserve the consumer's ability to
understand, choose, and escalate the interaction.
