Abstract
Generative	artificial	intelligence	(GenAI)	is	transforming	consumer	decision-making	by	enabling	large	language	models	to
function as collaborative AI assistants or autonomous AI agents. These developments signal a shift from AI as a passive
tool	to	an	active	market	participant,	ultimately	evolving	into	AI	customers	that	navigate	offers	independently,	interact	with
firms	directly,	and	shape	purchase	decision	processes.	This	research	note	examines	the	implications	of	AI	customers	for
marketing practice and scholarship, arguing that their growing role necessitates the emergence of machine marketing as a
new subdiscipline dedicated to understanding, predicting, and shaping the behavior of AI-based decision-makers.

Generative	artificial	intelligence	(GenAI)	is	one	of	the	most
notable technological transformations of our time. As con -
sumers increasingly turn to GenAI tools, including large
language	 models	 (LLMs)	 such	 as	 ChatGPT	 or	 Gemini,	 to
interpret, synthesize, and generate information, these AI
systems are evolving into AI assistants that collaborate with
users, support decision-making, and facilitate the comple -
tion of complex tasks. For example, Expedia’s ChatGPT-
based conversational planner guides users from initial
trip	ideation	all	the	way	to	booking	hotels	and	flights	(see
also Expedia 2023).	 Similarly,	 OpenTable’s	AI	 assistant,

focus	group	discussions	(Bilendi,	2025).
AI systems now transcend simple information retrieval
and delivery to support users across the entire consumer
journey.	While	AI	 assistants	 act	 as	 active	 collaborators	 in
consumers’ decision processes, AI agents can independently
perform delegated tasks and execute actions within pre -
defined	constraints.	This	shift	has	far-reaching	implications
for marketers and marketing research. For both AI assistants
and AI agents, consumers delegate part of their decision
authority to an AI system, such as selecting an information
source	 (e.g.,	 brand-owned	 websites,	 comparison	 websites,
product	tests),	choosing	a	retailer,	or	completing	the	check-
out process. As consumers increasingly delegate decisions
to AI assistants and AI agents, these systems become AI
customers that marketers must recognize as part of their tar-
get audience and that marketing scholars must incorporate
into future market and consumer research.
Recent literature in marketing research has largely
focused on how consumers interact with AI systems and
how these AI systems change customer journeys and refer -
rals	(e.g.,	Aggarwal	et	al.	2024; Blut et al. 2024; Gelbrich
et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2024).	At	the	same	time,	research	in
machine psychology, a nascent foundational interdisciplin -
ary	field	that	seeks	to	explain	how	AI	systems	process	infor-
mation and make decisions, suggests that human and AI
decision-making	can	differ	considerably	(Hagendorff	2023).
These studies indicate that AI assistants and AI agents can-
not	be	treated	as	“automated	humans”	but	that	they	are	dis-
tinct actors in the marketplace that follow their own utility
patterns.
Tying in with these developments, we make three pri -
mary contributions in this research note. First, we introduce
and conceptualize AI customers as distinct market actors
that increasingly participate in and shape consumer deci -
sion-making. Second, we explain how the growing use of
AI assistants and AI agents transforms traditional consumer
decision processes by redistributing decision authority
between humans and AI systems. Third, we argue that these
developments necessitate a new research agenda focused on
understanding	and	influencing	such	AI-supported	consumer
behavior.
The use of AI assistants and AI agents for shopping
tasks will have far-reaching implications for marketers and
marketing researchers who now need to understand how
humans interact with AI systems and how these interac -
tions	shape	decision-making.	At	the	managerial	level,	firms
must understand how to market to AI customers and how
to address AI’s algorithmic priorities and human prefer -
ences equally. Building on these insights, we conclude that
machine marketing should emerge as a distinctive subdis -
cipline in marketing research dedicated to understanding,
predicting,	 and	 influencing	 the	 behavior	 of	 AI	 customers
in	marketplace	contexts.	By	defining	the	concept	of	the	AI
customer and outlining its theoretical and managerial impli-
cations, this research note provides an initial foundation for
this	emerging	field.
The rise of AI customers
How AI systems turn into AI customers
Recent advances in GenAI have substantially expanded the
capabilities of AI systems, enabling increasingly autono -
mous	forms	of	human-AI	interaction	(Mienye	et	al.	2025).
At the core of these systems are large language models
(LLMs),	which	generate	outputs	by	predicting	likely	token
sequences based on patterns learned from large-scale train -
ing	data	(Brown	et	al.	2020; Radford et al. 2018; Vaswani
et al. 2017).	 While	 early	 LLM	 applications	 were	 largely
limited to conversational interactions, recent developments
have extended their capabilities substantially. Through
retrieval-augmented	generation	(RAG),	for	example,	LLMs
can access and incorporate external information beyond
their	training	data	(Lewis	et	al.	2020).	Memory	mechanisms
allow systems to retain information about prior interactions,
user preferences, and contextual factors across sessions. In
addition, tool-use capabilities enable LLMs to interact with
external software systems, databases, search engines, and
web	services	through	APIs	(Wang	et	al.	2024).
These technological advances have enabled a new gener-
ation of consumer-facing AI systems that actively support or
perform decision-making. Two prominent examples are AI
assistants and AI agents, which combine LLM-based rea -
soning	with	planning,	memory,	and	action	capabilities	(Xi
et al. 2025).	AI	assistants	support	consumers	throughout	the
decision process but require continuous human interaction,
guidance,	 and	 confirmation.	 For	 example,	 they	 can	 gather
and compare information, make recommendations based
on explicit user preferences, and provide decision support
through interactive sales dialogue. AI agents extend these
capabilities	 by	 autonomously	 pursuing	 user-defined	goals.
They can decompose complex objectives into subtasks,
gather and evaluate information from multiple sources, iter-
atively	refine	decisions,	and	execute	actions	with	minimal
or no human intervention. For example, an AI agent tasked
with purchasing a product may search across retailers,
compare prices and product characteristics, assess alterna -
tives	against	user-defined	criteria,	and	complete	a	transac-
tion through integrated payment and commerce systems
(Gonzalez	et	al.	2026).	As	a	result,	AI	systems	increasingly
move beyond supporting consumer decisions to actively

For marketers, a key implication of these latest devel -
opments is that AI systems may increasingly function as
intermediaries	 between	 firms	 and	 consumers.	 Whereas	AI
assistants may shape consumers’ consideration sets and
evaluations through personalized recommendations, AI
agents may autonomously participate in purchase processes
by searching for products, comparing alternatives, and exe-
cuting transactions.
As AI systems increasingly evaluate, recommend, select,
and transact on consumers’ behalf, they evolve from deci -
sion-support technologies into AI customers  that actively
participate	in	marketplace	exchanges	(Fig.	1).	AI	customers
thus constitute market actors that emerge through the inte -
gration of AI systems into consumer decision-making pro -
cesses and span a continuum from supportive AI assistants
to more autonomous AI agents.1
AI customers in consumer decision-making
AI customers act as intermediaries between the human
consumer	 and	 the	 firm	 throughout	 the	 customer	 journey,
from initial information search and preference building
over check-out to post-purchase interactions such as refund
negotiations. Their role extends beyond executing del -
egated tasks and fundamentally reshapes how consumers
make decisions, the degree of autonomy they grant AI sys -
tems, and how human and AI preferences evolve through
repeated interactions. Accordingly, we discuss AI custom -
ers as decision-making partners, examine the implications
of increasing delegation and autonomous decision-making,
and consider the long-term co-evolution of human and AI
preferences.

researchers will need to understand under which circum -

Delegation and autonomous decision-making
Although AI customers will often operate as decision-sup -
port systems, consumers may increasingly delegate decision
tasks	to	them.	With	increased	decision	authority,	AI	custom-
ers act with higher autonomy and can initiate actions or
complete	transactions	within	prespecified	boundaries	with-
out human involvement.
Prior work has shown that consumers’ willingness to
delegate choice tasks to AI systems depends on perceived
autonomy	and	control.	Specifically,	reduced	perceived	deci-
sion autonomy lowers the adoption likelihood of recom -
mender	systems	(Ahmad	Husairi	and	Rossi	2024).	Research
also suggests that contextual factors, such as task complex-
ity and initiation mode of delegation, shape whether con -
sumers accept or resist autonomous AI decision-making
(Adam	 et	 al.	 2024; Spitzer et al. 2025).	 Key	 questions,
therefore, include which types of decisions consumers are
most	 willing	 to	 delegate,	 how	 perceived	 task	 difficulty	 or
risk	influences	this	willingness,	and	how	autonomous	deci-
sion-making	by	AI	customers	affects	consumer	satisfaction,
trust,	and	perceived	control	(Candrian	and	Scherer	2022).
This development also introduces novel dynamics of
trust,	 agency	 attribution,	 and	 influence	 that	 may	 diverge
from	 those	 observed	 in	 human-to-human	 persuasion	 (see
also Mortati and Mundstock Freitas 2026).	 Investigating
these dynamics can help determine whether established mar-
keting	mechanisms,	such	as	source	credibility	(Ismagilova
et al. 2020; Sternthal et al. 1978),	message	framing	(Levin
and Gaeth 1988),	and	affective	appeals	(Gulati	et	al.	2024),
operate similarly when decision-making is supported by
algorithmic entities. For example, would scarcity or popu -
larity appeals that typically impact human decision-making
(Wu	and	Lee	2016)	equally	impact	AI	agent	decision-mak-
ing? Initial research touching upon this domain has started
to	examine	how	AI	agent	decision-making	is	influenced	by
persuasive marketing cues, such as scarcity appeals, top row
positioning,	or	strike-through	pricing	(Allouah	et	al.	2025;
Sabbah and Acar 2026).	Such	research	helps	to	address	the
emerging question of whether communication directed at AI
customers constitutes a distinct form of persuasion.
Relatedly, considering AI customers as market actors
raises the question of how AI customers themselves process
marketing information. Emerging evidence suggests that
LLMs are susceptible to various types of biases regarding
judgment	(e.g.,	probability	weighting;	Hintze	et	al.	2025),
information	 processing	 (e.g.,	 framing	 effects;	 Binz	 and
Schulz 2023),	and	response	behavior	(e.g.,	social	desirability
bias; Salecha et al. 2024),	as	well	as	decision-making	heuris-
tics	(e.g.,	confirmation	bias;	Macmillan-Scott	and	Musolesi
2024).	However,	while	LLMs	sometimes	mimic	parameters
of human information processing and behavior, this is not
always	the	case	(e.g.,	Kuric	et	al.	2026; Sarstedt et al. 2024).
Understanding	 these	 similarities	 as	 well	 as	 differences	 in
human and agentic information processing is critical for
explaining how AI customers make decisions and whether
these	 decisions	 parallel	 human	 decision-making	 (Sarstedt
et al. 2026).	Researchers	have	even	discussed	deliberately
implementing human-like cognitive biases in AI systems to
more	 closely	 mirror	 human	 decision-making	 (Besta	 et	 al.
2025;	Hagendorff	and	Fabi	2023).	Should	such	approaches
become widespread, both researchers and practitioners will
need	to	carefully	evaluate	how	these	design	choices	affect
AI customer behavior and marketplace outcomes.
Co-evolution of human and AI preferences
Over time, human consumers may develop increasingly
close relationships with their AI systems, resulting in recip-
rocal	influence	and	preference	co-evolution	(Pedreschi	et	al.
2025).	AI	customers	continuously	learn	from	user	feedback
and	interaction	histories,	enabling	them	to	refine	recommen-
dations and adapt their decision-making processes. In turn,
consumers may adjust their preferences and behaviors in
response to the alternatives and information provided by AI
systems—a phenomenon already observed in interactions
with	recommender	systems	(Dean	and	Morgenstern	2022).
As these relationships deepen, AI customers may increas-
ingly mirror aspects of their human counterpart’s personal -
ity, values, and decision-making. Conversely, consumers
may become dependent on AI-generated recommendations
and delegate an expanding range of decisions. In some cases,
individuals may even form emotionally meaningful rela -
tionships with AI systems, as illustrated by the popularity
of	applications	such	as	Replika	(Possati	2022).	Such	devel-
opments raise important questions regarding the agency
and persuasive power of AI systems, as well as the broader
ethical implications of a future in which interactions with
AI increasingly complement, or potentially replace, human
relationships. Marketers will therefore need to understand
not	 only	 how	 AI	 customers	 affect	 consumption	 decisions
but also how long-term human–AI relationships shape con-
sumer welfare and marketplace behavior.
Introducing machine marketing
A	recent	industry	survey	by	Oteh	et	al.	(2026b)	indicates	that
85% of respondents are open to integrating AI systems into
their purchase journey, with 32% of respondents being open
to completely delegating decision-making to AI systems
(i.e.,	let	AI	choose	what	to	buy).	These	figures	underscore
that	explaining,	predicting,	and	influencing	the	behavior	of
AI customers will become a major competitive advantage

for companies. In the future, companies will not only need
to market to humans, but also to AI customers.
Machine marketing	will	emerge	as	a	new	field	dedicated
to	 understanding,	 predicting,	 and	 influencing	 the	 behav-
ior of AI customers. Machine marketing examines how AI
systems in the form of AI customers interpret and respond
to	 marketing	 stimuli,	 aiming	 to	 understand	 and	 influence
their behavior in marketplace contexts. Machine marketing
explores how such systems make or recommend consump -
tion choices, mediate interactions between companies and
consumers, and engage with other AI agents as active actors
in marketplace dynamics. By focusing on the relationship
between prompt design, input environments, and resulting
market behaviors, machine marketing extends traditional
marketing	theory	into	the	realm	of	AI	assistants	and	(semi)-
autonomous AI actors.
Machine marketing conceptualizes AI customers as dis -
tinct entities in the marketplace that act on behalf of human
consumers	or	firms.	That	is,	machine	marketing	considers
AI	customers	as	a	separate	target	group	that	notably	differs
from human customers and thus requires uniquely focused
marketing campaigns and tailored marketing strategies. As
AI customers operate through AI-mediated evaluation and
delegated decision-making, they interpret product informa -
tion, compare alternatives, infer consumer preferences, and
potentially select products on consumers’ behalf. Machine
marketing	concerns	how	firms	adapt	their	offerings	as	well
as communications to be understood, correctly evaluated,
and selected by AI customers as non-human decision inter -
mediaries. Machine marketing thus extends established
platform logics, which emphasize strategies tailored to
human consumers as decision-makers, such as attention
and	 matching	 mechanisms	 (Meyer	 et	 al.	2024).	 Similarly,
it extends prevailing paradigms of customer experience
research,	 which	 focuses	 on	 human	 “customer’s	 cognitive,
emotional, behavioral, sensorial, and social responses to a
firm’s	offering”	(Lemon	and	V erhoef	2016,	p.	71),	as	well	as
recommender-system research, which examines how algo -
rithms predict human purchase behavior based on human
purchase data so as to generate recommendations for human
customers	 (Bodapati	 2008).	 While	 these	 perspectives	 can
inform and potentially be applied to machine marketing,
the concept reframes both the object and target of market -
ing.	 Specifically,	machine	 marketing	 shifts	 attention	 from
human-tailored value propositions to machine-readable
offerings,	from	human-facing	persuasion	to	AI-facing	influ-
ence, and from direct consumer demand to demand that is
filtered,	translated,	or	reshaped	by	AI	systems.
Accordingly, machine marketing raises new questions
about	segmentation	(targeting	AI	customers	versus	humans),
persuasion	(how	AI	customers,	acting	as	gatekeepers,	inter-
pret,	 and	 prioritize	 messages),	 and	 competition	 (between
human-	 and	 machine-driven	 decision-making	 processes).
As such, the paradigm of machine marketing represents
both a methodological and conceptual frontier for market -
ing research, necessitating a framework to study exchange
processes in increasingly hybrid human-machine markets.
In Fig. 2, we suggest such a framework in which we place
machine marketing as relevant to all customer journey
stages	(Lemon	and	V erhoef	2016).	This	placement	mimics
the ubiquity of AI customers as representations of human

Machine marketing will be particularly prominent in
the early stages of the customer journey through genera -
tive	engine	optimization	(GEO,	Aggarwal	et	al.	2024)	and
the emerging practice of generative engine advertising
(GEA).	 Analogous	 to	 search	 engine	 optimization	 (SEO),
GEO	aims	to	increase	the	likelihood	that	a	firm’s	products,
services, or content are retrieved, cited, and incorporated
into AI-generated responses. By contrast, GEA is the paid
counterpart	to	GEO,	enabling	firms	to	promote	products	or
services through sponsored placements embedded in AI-
generated responses or recommendations. GEO involves
testing representative consumer prompts to understand how
AI	 systems	 reference	 a	 company’s	products	 and	 offerings
and optimizing digital content to increase their inclusion in
AI-generated responses, whereas GEA focuses on purchas -
ing visibility within these conversational interfaces.
In	practice,	GEO	requires	firms	to	adapt	both	the	structure
and the distribution of their digital content. This includes
publishing clear, structured, and factual content that gen -
erative models can easily interpret. GEO also encourages
building a brand’s presence in widely referenced, machine-
readable sources, such as community platforms or public
knowledge	 bases	 (e.g.,	 Reddit	 and	 Wikipedia),	 because
these sources are often used by AI models when generating
answers	(Aggarwal	 et	 al.	2024; Chen et al. 2025; Nestaas
et al. 2024).	 GEO	 directly	 influences	 which	 products	 and
services reach consumers’ attention and enter their consid -
eration set.
Making a purchase decision will then be a dyadic process
that may involve discussing choice options with an AI assis-
tant.	The	final	decision,	therefore,	will	be	heavily	influenced
by this AI-human-collaboration, making machine market -
ing a counterpart of traditional marketing that targets human
consumers. For example, the shift from SEO to GEO comes
with changes in consumers’ search strategy in that they
move from generic search terms as in the search engine case
(e.g.,	“I	am	looking	for	a	coat”)	to	more	nuanced	prompts
(“I	am	looking	for	a	bordeaux	colored	coat	that	has	knee-
length”).	 These	 prompts	 may	 be	 paired	 with	 multimodal
inputs,	for	example,	by	providing	pictures	or	asking	to	find
a coat that matches one’s hair color or body shape. Com -
panies must optimize their product descriptions to ensure

Studying the behavior of AI customers
A crucial aspect in the machine marketing era is to under -
stand the behavior of AI customers. Researchers could draw
on silicon samples—data generated by LLMs to simulate
human-produced	outputs	for	research	purposes	(Sarstedt	et
al. 2024)—or	LLM-based	 digital	twins	for	this	task	 (Tou-
bia et al. 2025).	Since	LLM-based	data	generation	relies	on
a similar computational architecture as AI customers, this
correspondence may facilitate generalizations from silicon
samples and digital twins to AI customers. However, instead
of assessing the overlap between silicon and human sam -
ples, which is a common practice in current silicon sam -
pling	research	(Gao	et	al.	2025; Imschloss et al. 2025; Loru
et al. 2025; Sarstedt et al. 2024),	the	aim	must	be	to	under-
stand AI customers as distinct entities. Researchers have
proposed that such an endeavor necessitates the establish -
ment	of	an	academic	area	of	machine	psychology	(Hagen-
dorff,	2023),	with	important	implications	for	the	marketing
discipline. Rather than interpreting the internal representa -
tions and design components of the neural networks that
underly LLMs, machine psychology would examine how
AI customers perceive, decide, act, and interact in ways that
resemble human behavior. As AI customers learn from feed-
back, adapt to user preferences, and coordinate with humans
and other machines, their actions can be analyzed through
psychological concepts such as motivation, learning, social
interaction,	 and	 persuasion	 (Hutson	 2024).	 The	 machine
psychology	 perspective	 thereby	 offers	 machine	 marketing
with frameworks that help anticipate how AI customers
behave in complex environments, how consumers inter -
pret and respond to them, and how human-AI relationships
develop over time.
Conclusion
The emergence of machine marketing closely parallels
the rise of SEO and SEA several decades ago. Back then,
marketers	 learned	 that	 reaching	 consumers	 required	 first
appealing to the algorithms that determined what informa -
tion people would see. Today, a similar shift is underway as
AI assistants and AI agents increasingly act as intermediaries
between	firms	and	consumers.	These	systems	filter,	recom-
mend,	and	even	decide	on	products	and	services,	effectively
becoming new gatekeepers of market access. Just as SEO
required understanding how search engines interpreted and
ranked content, machine marketing will require understand-
ing how AI customers process prompts, represent prefer -
ences, and make decisions. This transition signals a broader
move from marketing to humans toward marketing through
and to machines. This development will reshape marketing
strategies, metrics, and ethics in the digital marketplace.
Companies have already responded to this shift by moving
from SEO to GEO and by allowing popular AI systems such
as ChatGPT to access and interact with their online stores.
With	this	transition,	AI	customers	will	become	an	impor-
tant target for companies to market their products and
services. They blur the boundaries between tools and col -
laborators:	 they	 act	 (semi-)autonomously	 within	 bounded
parameters but typically remain contingent on human inten-
tion and oversight. This hybrid mode of operation under -
scores the role of AI customers not as replacements for
human cognition or labor, but as extensions of it. Given their
likely prominent role in future consumer decision-making
settings, we suggest that marketers and researchers should
treat AI customers as a distinct population whose behavioral
mechanisms need to be understood. Silicon samples may
offer	insights	into	this	regard	by	mimicking	the	behavior	of
AI customers, even if they do not mimic human behavior
perfectly in all its facets. As consumers and AI systems will
increasingly interact, AI customers will become more sensi-
tive to human behavior, just as human consumers may adapt
their behaviors considering AI-based recommendations and
purchase decisions.
