Abstract: As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes increasingly embedded in banking sector; a critical challenge
is understanding how organizations can translate employee-oriented investments into productive human-AI
collaboration. This study examines how internal marketing turns organizational investment into AI-enabled
employee capabilities and productive knowledge, and when this conversion is constrained. A quantitative, cross-
sectional survey (N = 341) was conducted among employees across operational, supervisory and managerial
roles in Pakistan’s banking sector who had experience using or interacting with AI-enabled technologies at
work. The findings show that internal marketing positively enhances AI readiness, which subsequently
strengthens AI-augmented knowledge sharing. This knowledge sharing positively contributes to self-perceived
productivity, while AI readiness and AI-augmented knowledge sharing sequentially mediate the relationship
between internal marketing and productivity. AI anxiety negatively moderates the relationship between AI-
augmented knowledge sharing and self-perceived productivity, weakening this positive association at higher
levels of anxiety. The study extends social exchange theory to AI-enabled work by theorizing that AI does not
create productivity automatically; rather, its value emerges through an employee-level conversion process that
goes beyond AI adoption alone. Importantly, this conversion is psychologically conditioned by AI anxiety, which
determines the extent to which AI-enabled knowledge can be translated into productivity gains. The findings
can help managers maximize the productive value of AI by embedding role-specific AI development and enabling
AI-augmented knowledge-sharing mechanisms within broader reskilling and career-support practices that build
employee readiness while reducing anxiety about AI-driven change.

Introduction
Employees constitute a critical source of organizational advantage in knowledge -intensive and rapidly
changing business environments because organizational effectiveness increasingly depends on how
effectively employees acquire, exchange and apply knowledge (Wang & Noe, 2010). This issue is particularly
important in banking, where digital transformation is reshaping service delivery , decision -making and
employees’ everyday work. In Pakistan, this transformation has become increasingly visible. The State Bank
of Pakistan (SBP) reported 9.1 billion retail payment transactions in FY2025, with 88% conducted through
digital channels, while mobile banking application users increased from 18.7 million to 24.1 million (State
Bank of Pakistan [SBP], 2025). Alongside digitalisation, the growing use of artificial intelligence (AI) is
creating new possibilities for automation, information processing, decision support and knowledge-intensive
work (Bankins et al., 2024). Consequently, the human capability to adapt to AI-enabled work is becoming as
important as technological investment itself.
This transformation raises an important organizational question: how can organizations prepare employees
to effectively leverage AI rather than merely introduce AI technologies into existing work systems? AI readiness

employees as internal customers of the company and the employees' behavior as internal marketing of the
company towards the internal customers (Ahmed & Rafiq, 2003). Resources for adapting to organisational
change can be provided for employees throu gh training and development, internal communication,
managerial support and empowerment. In the banking sector, internal marketing has been correlated with
positive employee and service experiences (Sharma et al., 2025), and recent studies have found that internal
marketing is related to the organizational knowledge-sharing behavior (Nguyen et al., 2024). These practices
can thus go beyond traditional employee outcomes in an AI-driven workplace and empower employees to
embrace new technologies.
This is in line with the social exchange theory (Cropanzano & Mitchell, 2005) which suggests that if an
organization treats an employee well, the employee will feel obligated to return the favor and treat the
organization favorably. In this way, internal marketing is an investment that an organization makes in the
ability and welfare of its employees. In return, employees could become more prepared to use AI, take part
in knowledge-related tasks and leverage AI to enhance their performance. AI research in recent years also
illustrates the importance of social exchange processes to understand employees' reactions to AI-supported
working environments (Khan et al., 2025). AI readiness is therefore positioned as the first mechanism through
which internal marketing may influence employee outcomes. AI-ready employees are better equipped to
understand, accept and integrate AI into their work processes, allowing them to exploit AI beyond simple
task automation. However, readiness alone may not generate value unless employees can use AI to enhance
how they acquire, process and exchange knowledge. Traditional research establishes knowledge sharing as
an important mechanism for organizational learning and performance (Wang & Noe, 2010). In AI-enabled
workplaces, intelligent technologies can retrieve information, synthesise knowledge, generate insights and
support communication, thereby changing how employees participate in knowledge processes. Accordingly,
this study conceptualises AI-augmented knowledge sharing as employees’ use of AI-enabled capabilities to
enhance the acquisition, processing, exchange and application of work-related knowledge. Emerging research
indicates that AI can facilitate employee knowledge sharing through enhanced learning opportunities (Hu et
al., 2025), while recent research also highlights the changing dynamics of knowledge sharing in AI-enabled
workplaces (Zhong & Wang, 2025).
The value of AI-augmented knowledge sharing should ultimately be reflected in employees’ work
outcomes. Employees who can efficiently access, process and exchange AI-supported knowledge may be
better positioned to solve problems and accomplish work tasks, thereby strengthening self-perceived
productivity. Knowledge sharing has consistently been associated with employee performance through
mechanisms such as employee engagement and knowledge utilisation (Engidaw et al., 2024). Recent AI
research similarly suggests that knowledge sharing constitutes an important pathway through which AI can
enhance employee performance (Khan et al., 2025). Thus, the present study proposes a sequential process
in which internal marketing strengthens AI readiness, AI readiness facilitates AI -augmented knowledge
sharing, and AI-augmented knowledge sharing enhances employees’ perceived productivity. However, the
translation of AI-enabled knowledge into productivity may depend on employees’ psychological responses to
AI. AI can simultaneously provide valuable work resources and gen erate concerns about technological
uncertainty, job security and changing work roles. Research on attitudes towards AI at work demonstrates
that employees can experience both positive orientations towards AI and negative reactions such as anxiety

mediation model in which AI readiness and AI-augmented knowledge sharing explain the relationship
between internal marketing and self-perceived productivity, while AI anxiety weakens the positive
relationship between AI-augmented knowledge sharing and self-perceived productivity. By examining these
relationships among employees in Pakistan’s banking sector, the study extends internal marketing research
into the AI-enabled workplace, advances knowledge-sharing research by recognising the augmenting role of
AI and highlights the psychological conditions under which AI-enabled knowledge can generate employee
productivity.

Literature Review and Hypotheses
Social Exchange Theory
Social exchange theory provides the overarching theoretical foundation for understanding how
organizational practices translate into employee attitudes and behaviours. The theory proposes that social
relationships develop through reciprocal exchanges in which favourable treatment generates an expectation
of reciprocity (Cropanzano & Mitchell, 2005). Within organizations, employees evaluate the resources and
support provided by their employer and may reciprocate through positive attitudes, discretionary behaviours
and greater contribution to organizational goals. Internal marketing represents such an organizational
investment because training, communication, support and empowerment provide employees with resources
that facilitate their development and work effectiveness (Lings & Greenley, 2005). Social exchange theory is
particularly relevant to AI-enabled work because organizations increasingly provide employees with
technological and developmental resources while employees are expected to acquire new capabilities and
adapt their work practices. Recent research has applied this theory to explain positive employee responses to
AI, including knowledge sharing and performance (Khan et al., 2025). The theory therefore provides a
coherent explanation of the proposed sequence: organizational investment through internal marketing can
strengthen AI readiness; AI-ready employees can reciprocate through AI-augmented knowledge sharing; and
such knowledge-related contributions can facilitate productive outcomes. At the same time, AI anxiety may
increase the perceived psychological costs associated with AI engagement, weakening the conversion of AI-
enabled resources into productivity.

Internal Marketing and AI Readiness
Internal marketing encompasses organizational practices through which employees are developed, informed,
supported and empowered (Brown, 2025). Its underlying premise is that organizations must attend to their
internal market before employees can effectively contribute to external value creation (Gounaris, 2006).
Empirical work has identified training, internal communication, leadership, rewards and empowerment
among important dimensions of internal marketing (Kaur & Sharma, 2015). These practices are particularly
relevant when employees face substantial technological change because organizational support can provide
the knowledge and confidence required to adapt to new work demands. AI readiness reflects employees’
preparedness and capability to understand and integrate AI into their work. Recent research indicates that

AI readiness involves both technological and human capabilities and represents an important prerequisite for

AI Readiness and AI-Augmented Knowledge Sharing
Knowledge sharing involves the exchange and application of information, expertise and experience among
organizational members and represents a fundamental mechanism through which individual knowledge
becomes an organizational resource (Argote & Ingram, 2000;Sharma et al., 2025). However, the emergence
of AI is changing the way employees acquire, process and exchange knowledge. AI-enabled tools can assist
employees in retrieving information, synthesising dispersed knowledge, generating content and supporting
problem-solving, thereby augmenting rather than simply replacing employees’ knowledge activities (Akhtar,
2022; Zhang et al., 2025). This readiness should therefore shape the ways in which employees can and will
use these capabilities to enhance their knowledge-related work. Staff feeling ready to use the AI benefit from
an increased likelihood of trying the AI-enabled tools, critically assessing the information produced by AI,
and using the AI in their knowledge-sharing processes. AI's ability to facilitate employee knowledge sharing
is supported by recent empirical studies, which also show that AI integration can create learning opportunities
for employees (Hu et al., 2025). Recent studies also indicate AI-enabled work environments create novel
opportunities and challenges related to the utility, sharing and assessment of knowledge (Zhong & Wang,
2025). Therefore, employees should be able to participate in AI-powered knowledge sharing with the ability
and confidence provided by AI readiness. In line with the social exchange theory, the employees who gain
and build valuable AI-related skills may look back and make an active contribution to the company by sharing
their knowledge. Hence:
H2: AI readiness has a positive effect on AI-augmented knowledge sharing.

AI-Augmented Knowledge Sharing and Self-Perceived Productivity
Knowledge sharing enables employees to access expertise, minimize repetitive work and enhance problem
solving, all of which help individual employees to perform their jobs better (Mesmer-Magnus & DeChurch,
2009). AI can be utilized in the workplace to improve the value of knowledge sharing as employees can
access, combine and apply knowledge more easily and quickly. Thus, AI can be used to augment human skills
by expanding the capacity of information processing and boosting decision-making skills of the employees
(Akhtar et al., 2022; Jarrahi, 2018). There is recent empirical evidence that backs up this relationship. AI use
has been associated with employee performance through knowledge sharing and work engagement (Khan et
al., 2025), while experimental evidence demonstrates that generative AI can substantially improve the speed
and quality of professional work (Malik et al., 2022; Noy & Zhang, 2023). Research in the Pakistani context
has also linked AI with employee productivity through knowledge -sharing mechanisms, although this
evidence has primarily examined healthcare employees rather than banking employees (Mushtaq & Akhtar,
2024; Shaikh, 2023). AI-augmented knowledge sharing can therefore provide employees with timely and
relevant knowledge that facilitates task completion and problem-solving, enhancing their perceptions of
productivity. Under social exchange theory, employees who effectively reciprocate organizational and
technological resources through knowledge contributions can derive performance-related benefits from those
exchanges. Hence:
H3: AI-augmented knowledge sharing has a positive effect on self-perceived productivity.

Serial Mediating Role of AI Readiness and AI-Augmented Knowledge Sharing
The preceding relationships suggest that the influence of internal marketing on employee productivity may
unfold through a sequential process in which each mechanism enables the next. Internal marketing provides
developmental, informational and relational resources that help employees prepare for technological change
and develop the confidence and capabilities required to work with AI (Valtonen et al., 2025). This AI
readiness, in turn, enables employees to integrate AI more effectively into the acquisition, processing and
exchange of work-related knowledge (Li et al., 2025). When such knowledge activities are augmented by AI,
employees can access, organise and share knowledge more efficiently, creating resources that can
subsequently support task accomplishment and enhance perceived productivity. This sequential logic is
consistent with evidence that internal marketing supports knowledge sharing and organizational
effectiveness (Yang, 2015) and that its effects on performance can operate through knowledge-oriented
mechanisms (Rahimiaghdam, 2020; Sameeni & Qadeer, 2015). More recent AI research likewise indicates
that knowledge-sharing mechanisms can connect AI use with employee performance (Khan et al., 2025;
Mushtaq & Akhtar, 2025). From a social exchange perspective, organizational investment through internal
marketing initiates reciprocity by equipping employees with the resources needed to become AI -ready;
employees can then reciprocate through AI -augmented knowledge contributions that enhance their
productive capabilities. Thus, the effects of internal marketing on productivity are expected to strengthen as
they pass sequentially through AI readiness and AI-augmented knowledge sharing. Therefore:
H4: AI readiness and AI-augmented knowledge sharing sequentially mediate the positive relationship
between internal marketing and self-perceived productivity.

Moderating Role of AI Anxiety
Although AI-augmented knowledge sharing can provide valuable resources for employees, its contribution to
productivity may depend on employees’ psychological responses to AI (Raisch & Krakowski, 2021). AI anxiety
encompasses apprehension associated with learning, using and adapting to AI as well as concerns regarding
its consequences for employment and work roles (Wang & Wang, 2022). Such anxiety can consume
psychological resources and reduce employees’ willingness to engage confidently with AI-enabled work.
Recent research demonstrates that AI anxiety related to job replacement and learning can undermine
employees’ work passion (Chen et al., 2025: Sameeni et al., 2022), while research in Pakistan shows that AI
anxiety can shape employees’ responses to organizational AI transformation (Nazeer & Ahmad, 2025). From
a social exchange theory perspective, employees assess not only the benefits but also the costs associated
with an exchange relationship. AI-augmented knowledge sharing may provide valuable informational
resources, but high AI anxiety can increase perceived risks, uncertainty and psychological costs associated
with using those resources. Consequently, employees may possess and share AI-enabled knowledge without
being equally capable of converting it into productive work outcomes. Evidence that AI-related risks can
condition the positive effects of AI on employee outcomes further supports this boundary -condition

Methodology
This study employed a quantitative, cross-sectional survey design to examine the proposed relationships in
Pakistan’s banking sector. The target population comprised employees working across operational,
supervisory and managerial roles who had experience using or interacting with AI-enabled technologies in
their work. A purposive sampling approach was adopted to ensure that respondents possessed relevant
experience for assessing the study constructs. The data were gathered by means of a self -administered
questionnaire and participation was voluntary and confidential. During the questionnaire a screening
question was added at the outset: ‘Have you worked with or used any AI-enabled technology or application
in your work? Respondents who answered ‘Yes' went on to answer the following measures in the order that
they appear below: internal marketing, AI readiness, AI-augmented knowledge sharing, AI anxiety and self-
perceived productivity. Demographic and employment data were gathered tow ards the end of the
questionnaire to reduce the chance of demographic questions impacting on the answers to the substantive
questions. Of this total 341 responses were eligible for analysis after screening for eligibility and
completeness.
The proposed PLS-SEM model was deemed to have sufficiently large sample size. Contemporary
methodological guidance recommends determining sample adequacy using statistical power rather than
relying solely on the traditional ten-times rule, which provides only a rough minimum guideline (Sameeni et
al., 2024a; 2024b). Thus, the final sample of 341 observations yields a significantly larger number of data
points to estimate the direct, serial mediation and moderation relations proposed. Partial least squares
structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) was employed to analyse the data, which included assessing the
measurement model and testing the hypothesized structural relationships, conducting serial mediation
analyses, and examining the moderating effect of AI anxiety.

Measures
The study employed previously established and validated measures, with wording adapted where necessary
to reflect the AI-enabled banking context. Internal marketing was assessed with the eight-item scale devised
by Kaur and Sharma (2015), which consisted of employee-oriented aspects such as internal communication,
training and development, empowerment, rewards, leadership, and organizational support. The seven items
used to assess AI readiness were adapted from Wang et al. (2026) and included items related to employees'
confidence and cognition, optimism, collaborative learning and working, and comfort in working with AI. AI-
augmented knowledge sharing was operationalised by using six items adapted from Wang and Noe (2010)
to incorporate the role of AI in employees’ acquisition, processing, exchange and application of work-related
knowledge. This approach treats AI-augmented knowledge sharing as a context-specific extension of
established knowledge-sharing behaviour rather than as an entirely independent, previously established
construct. AI anxiety was measured by adapting the AI Anxiety Scale of Wang and Wang (2022), using ten
items to the workplace context, capturing employees’ concerns regarding AI and its implications for their
work. Self-perceived productivity was measured using Bélanger’s (1999) four-item scale for employee
productivity adapted to the AI-enabled work context. All items were assessed using a five-point Likert scale
ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree.

The structural model was assessed to examine the proposed direct relationships. Bootstrapping was used to
determine the significance of the path coefficients. Table 5 reports the direct effects corresponding to H1-H3

predicts AI readiness (β = 0.22, p < 0.001), supporting H1. AI readiness significantly predicts AI-augmented
knowledge sharing (β = 0.19, p = 0.007, supporting H2. AI-augmented knowledge sharing significantly
predicts self-perceived productivity (β = 0.31, p < 0.001), supporting H3. The interaction between AI -
augmented knowledge sharing and AI anxiety is significant (β = 0.27, p = 0.003), supporting H5 and
indicating that the positive association between AI-augmented knowledge sharing and self-perceived
productivity becomes weaker as AI anxiety increases.

Discussion
The findings provide a coherent explanation of how internal marketing contributes to employee productivity
in an AI-enabled banking environment. Rather than indicating a simple relationship between organizational
practices and productivity, the results reveal a sequential process in which organizational support first
strengthens employees' readiness for AI, this readiness facilitates AI-augmented knowledge sharing, and the
resulting knowledge resources contribute to greater self-perceived productivity. At the same time, the
negative moderating effect of AI anxiety demonstrates that the productivity benefits of AI -augmented
knowledge sharing depend partly on employees' psychological responses to AI. The overall results indicate
that the benefits of AI in banking are not just about the technology itself, but through the interaction of
organizational preparation, employee capability, knowledge processes and psychological readiness.
The positive relationship between internal marketing and the AI readiness suggests that organizational
practices that focus on employees can contribute to the human conditions for AI -enabled work. The
traditional focus of internal marketing has been on training, communication, empowerment, support, and
employee development, and past studies have demonstrated the importance of internal marketing to
knowledge sharing and organizational effectiveness (Rahimiaghdam, 2020; Yang, 2015). The current finding
brings this to a technological level. Employees are more likely to grasp and be involved with new technologies
if organizations give them information that is relevant to technology , developmental opportunities and
supportive working conditions. Such an interpretation aligns with the latest studies that have established that
employees' capabilities and organizational conditions are more important to AI readiness than the technology
itself (Wang et al., 2026). So, internal marketing seems to offer a framework for the organization to build an

suggests that readiness also plays a role in how employees use AI to support knowledge-related work. AI-

support for viewing AI readiness as an important antecedent of AI-augmented knowledge sharing.
The positive association between AI-augmented knowledge sharing and self-perceived productivity
highlights the productivity benefits of AI partly in the knowledge processes. This discovery aligns with studies
indicating the impact of knowledge sharing on employee performance (Khan et al., 2025) and evidence that
AI can improve employee outcomes through knowledge-related activities. Shaikh (2023), for example, found
knowledge sharing to be an important mechanism linking AI -related developments with employee
productivity. There are also more recent studies that have suggested that AI -based efforts can enhance
employee performance and that the interplay between human and AI can be part of this link (Wan & Jiang,

a worker is just given access to it. Such improvements in productivity are more likely when employees can
use AI in areas where valuable knowledge is learned, developed, shared, and utilized. The conclusions of the
significant sequential mediation are most comprehensible. The findings show that internal marketing is a
driver of productivity through a system of organizational investment that prepares employees, readiness that
facilitates knowledge sharing with the help of AI, and knowledge sharing that facilitates productive work.
This builds on previous work that found a relationship between internal marketing and knowledge-oriented
outcomes (Rahimiaghdam, 2020; Yang, 2015) and recent studies linking AI with knowledge sharing and
employee performance (Hu et al., 2025; Khan et al., 2025). Importantly, the result indicates that AI readiness
and knowledge sharing augmented by AI are not two stand-alone mechanisms. Their sequential relationship
suggests that employees have to be well-prepared to interact with AI before they can effectively integrate AI
into their knowledge sharing processes. This is especially relevant in the banking industry, where in the
implementation of AI, employees must apply the technological capability and knowledge of the business with
professional judgment.
However, it is crucial to recognize that these positive relationships are qualified by AI anxiety, which has
a negative moderating effect. The more anxious employees get about AI, the less they seem to be able to
translate AI-supported knowledge sharing into perceived productivity . The result aligns with research
indicating that employee experiences of AI anxiety negatively impact employee experiences (Chen et al.,
2025) and with recent studies on the relationship between AI anxiety and negative outcomes in the workplace
(Tong et al., 2025). Furthermore, it aligns with studies showing organization's AI adoption may cause positive
and negative knowledge behaviors based on the employees' interpretation of technological change (Zhu et
al., 2025). From this standpoint, AI-powered knowledge sharing is a potential resource, while AI anxiety is a
psychological cost that can diminish the value employees can get from the resource. The results thus reflect
the ambivalence of opportunities and apprehensions in AI work. In summary , the findings reflect AI-powered
productivity as a process of capability and psychology. The organizational conditions that enable AI readiness
are created through internal marketing; AI readiness allows employees to participate in knowledge sharing
in the context of AI; knowledge sharing is a path to productivity; and the extent to which AI knowledge
resources translate into productive outcomes is determined by the level of AI anxiety . This is aligned with
social exchange theory, as the provision of resources by an organization can be met with employees' capability
growth, knowledge sharing, and productive actions, and perceived psychological costs of the AI can reduce
this exchange.

Theoretical Contributions
This study makes four principal theoretical contributions. First, it builds on the social exchange theory (SET)
to theorize technological preparedness and knowledge contribution as two successive forms of employee
reciprocity in the context of AI-supported work. Simply put, traditional applications of SET typically account
for how positive treatment of the organization leads to positive attitudes or behaviors of the employees. In
the present findings a series of reciprocal responses may begin as an organizational investment, rather than
a single behavioural response. Internal marketing is the investment in the organization, AI readiness is the
readiness of the employee to act on that investment, AI -augmented knowledge sharing represents a
subsequent contribution of knowledge resources, and finally productivity is the desired work -related
outcome. This is a more dynamic implementation of SET to technologically changing workplaces. Second,
the study adds to the growing body of literature on AI readiness, finding an organizational antecedent of
employee readiness. Recent research has increasingly conceptualised AI readiness as an important capability
for successful AI implementation (Wang et al., 2026). But readiness is often considered an employee or
organizational state-of-being. This study theoretically positions readiness as being something that can be
nurtured in an organization through investment in internal marketing, by establishing a positive relationship
between the two. This study theoretically places readiness as something that can be produced by investing in
an organization through internal marketing, as the study showed that there is a positive relationship between
the two. This is a paradigm shift from whether employees are ready for AI to how organizational practices
can make employees ready for AI. Internal marketing is thus not only relevant to employee-organization
relationships, but also to the human aspect of technological change.
Third, the study contributes to the knowledge-sharing theory by conceptualizing the AI-augmented
knowledge sharing as a mechanism connecting employee technological capability and productivity. The
contribution is not to claim that AI-augmented knowledge sharing represents an entirely separate and
established knowledge-sharing tradition. Rather, it extends established knowledge-sharing logic to an
environment in which AI participates in the acquisition, processing and exchange of work-related knowledge.
Existing AI research has shown that AI adoption can encourage knowledge sharing through learning
opportunities (Hu et al., 2025), while human-AI collaboration research highlights the complementarity
between human capabilities and technological capabilities. The present model integrates these ideas by
positioning AI-augmented knowledge sharing after AI readiness and before productivity, thereby clarifying
its role as an intermediate employee-level mechanism. Fourth, the study introduces AI anxiety as a boundary
condition on the productivity value of AI-augmented knowledge sharing. Much of the emerging AI literature
has focused on adoption, readiness, capability and performance benefits. However, employees may
simultaneously experience uncertainty, job-related concerns and learning anxiety. By demonstrating that AI
anxiety weakens the positive relationship between AI-augmented knowledge sharing and productivity, the
study provides a more balanced theoretical account of AI-enabled work. The result supports the emerging
view that AI can have simultaneously enabling and constraining consequences for employees (Chen et al.,
2025; Tong et al., 2025). More importantly, the moderation finding enriches SET by suggesting that the
exchange relationship is influenced not only by resources received from the organization but also by the
perceived psychological costs associated with the technological environment.
Collectively, these contributions connect organizational investment, technological readiness, knowledge
exchange and employee psychology within one theoretically integrated model. The study therefore moves
beyond examining whether AI improves employee outcomes and instead explains how organizational
practices enable AI-related productivity and under what psychological condition those benefits are weakened.

Practical Implications
The findings have several implications for banks operating in Pakistan, where AI adoption is developing
alongside digital transformation and increasing demands for workforce reskilling. First, it is crucial for banks
to consider AI readiness as a goal for their employees, not just IT . The roles of internal marketing and learning-

and-development should be merged into delivering role-specific AI training. Training should be provided to
the various people in the organization who interact with the AI applications, in the context of the specific
risks they may face, not in a generic AI workshop. Secondly, banks need to enhance internal communication
regarding the implementation of AI. Staff need to be briefed on the tasks that will be supported by AI, tasks
that can be automated, tasks that will need human judgment and new skills that will be necessary . This is
significant given evidence from Pakistan suggests that employee anxiety over AI can negatively impact
constructive response to AI adoption by the organization. So, managers should instead frame the adoption
of AI as a reorganisation of the work and an enhancement of capabilities, not just as a technology
replacement.
Third, Pakistani banks need to establish robust internal mechanisms for knowledge sharing with the help
of AI. Employees might submit proven use cases, prompts, workflow enhancements, and frequently asked
questions and verification steps to company knowledge bases. AI specific community of practice, with support
across branches and functions, might help enable the sharing of AI learning. Recent studies have shown that
AI can boost knowledge sharing when employees feel there are opportunities to learn and build their skills.
Fourth, banks should not view AI anxiety as just an anti-technology stance but take proactive steps to tackle
it. Guided practice and accessible support are recommended for employees with learning anxiety, and
transparent reskilling and career-development options for employees who are concerned about losing their
jobs. This is particularly important because recent evidence links AI anxiety with adverse employee outcomes
and suggests that organizational knowledge-sharing can help employees cope with AI-related learning
pressures. Finally, AI implementation should be accompanied by human -oversight and responsible-use
protocols. Employees should know what customer information can be entered into AI systems, which AI-
generated outputs require verification, and who remains accountable for AI-supported decisions. Banks
should also evaluate productive AI use through indicators such as processing time, error reduction, service
quality and knowledge-retrieval efficiency rather than simply measuring how frequently employees use AI.

Limitations and Future Directions
The study has several limitations that provide directions for future research. First, its cross-sectional design
limits conclusions about the temporal ordering of the proposed relationships. Longitudinal or time-lagged
research could examine whether changes in organizational support lead to subsequent changes in AI
readiness, knowledge sharing and productivity, providing stronger evidence for the proposed sequential
mechanism and responding to the need to examine AI-related employee outcomes over time (Savolainen et
al., 2026). Second, the study relies on self-perceived productivity and self-reported measures. Although these
capture employees’ experiences of AI-enabled work, future research could combine employee responses with
supervisor-rated performance and objective indicators such as processing time, error rates, customer -
response time and actual AI-use metrics. Such multi-source designs could strengthen the empirical
assessment of AI-related productivity outcomes. Third, the study focuses on Pakistan’s banking sector, which
may limit generalisability to other industries and national contexts. Future research could replicate the model
across Pakistani industries and compare Pakistan with emerging and developed banking markets to examine
whether institutional and technological differences alter the proposed relationships. Fourth, AI anxiety is
explored as a single phenomenon. Future research could differentiate job-replacement anxiety, learning
anxiety, and other types of AI-related anxiety, while exploring protective factors including AI self-efficacy,
psychological safety, perceived organizational support, and trust in AI. This may explain the times at which
employees are more likely to be able to use AI related resources in effective ways (Tong et al., 2025; Chen et
al., 2025). Lastly, the overall process of AI-enabled knowledge sharing is explored. Research could separate
out the various aspects of knowledge acquisition, creation, exchange and application with AI assistance. Such
granularity can show where productivity gains and psychological expenses arise, especially when the AI-
related knowledge and anxiety can impact employee learning (Liu et al., 2026).
