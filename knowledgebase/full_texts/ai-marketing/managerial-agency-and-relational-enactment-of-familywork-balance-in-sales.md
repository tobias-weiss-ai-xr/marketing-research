A B S T R A C T
Family–Work Balance (FWB) is a critical challenge in sales management and has traditionally been examined
from individual-level perspectives, such as Role and Identity Theory, which largely place responsibility for
achieving balance on employees. However, the boundary-spanning nature of sales work and sustained perfor-
mance pressures suggest that balance is also shaped by managerial practices and employees' perceptions of
vocational fit. Drawing on Vocational Fit Theory, this study reconceptualizes FWB as a higher-order reflective
construct representing employees' evaluative perceptions of balance, emerging from the alignment between
managerial structuring and perceived vocational fit. Using a sequential mixed-methods design, we first con-
ducted 19 semi-structured interviews with sales professionals to clarify the conceptualization of FWB and
identify the interpretive themes underpinning the higher-order construct. These insights informed a quantitative
study involving 314 UK and 159 Greek sales professionals. Results demonstrate that managerial structuring and
perceived vocational fit jointly shape employees' perceptions of FWB and that balance and imbalance are distinct
evaluative states rather than opposite ends of a single continuum. The study contributes to theory by advancing a
relational conceptualization of FWB and extending sales management research with a context-sensitive
perspective on balance in sales work. It also contributes to practice by positioning sales managers as active
balancing agents whose decisions regarding work design, performance expectations, and support influence
employees' ability to sustain both well-being and performance.
1. Introduction & scope for the investigation
Balancing family and work life has long been recognized as central to
employees' effectiveness and sustainable functioning (Cummings et al.,
1975). Family-Work balance (FWB) refers to individuals' perceptions
of their ability to satisfactorily manage the demands, responsibilities,
and expectations associated with both family and work roles (Greenhaus
& Allen, 2011). Although prior literature frequently uses the term
work–family balance, the present study adopts the term family–work
balance (FWB) to emphasize the construct's relational and evaluative
orientation. Empirical research links FWB to enhanced well-being
(Bakalım & Karçkay, 2017), reduced strain, and improved organiza-
tional outcomes, including sales performance (Guedes et al., 2023). At
the same time, FWB is inherently subjective and dynamic, evolving as
personal circumstances, career stages, and work arrangements change
(Kim et al., 2021; Wayne et al., 2021; Wayne et al., 2022). Within
industrial marketing contexts, recent research has increasingly empha-
sized the strategic importance of salesperson well-being and relational
sustainability for long-term performance (Bernuzzi et al., 2022; Habel,
Alavi, & Pick, 2020).
Despite its importance, the dominant explanatory logic in the FWB
literature remains largely individual-centric (Casper et al., 2018; Wayne
et al., 2017). Balance is typically conceptualized as the outcome of
personal coping strategies, boundary management, or individual
resource mobilization, even when contextual variables are acknowl-
edged (Ashforth et al., 2000; Bakker & Demerouti, 2007). Organiza-
tional conditions and managerial actions are frequently treated as
antecedents or moderators rather than as integral components of how
balance is shaped in practice (Hammer et al., 2011; Voydanoff, 2005).
This creates a conceptual asymmetry: employees are seen as the main
agents responsible for balance, while managerial governance is back-
ground. In contrast, research in Industrial Marketing Management

These dynamics are especially prominent in sales environments.
Sales roles operate within performance management systems defined by
targets, monitoring, incentives, and customer responsiveness (Habel,
Alavi, & Pick, 2020; Winter, Zacharias, et al., 2024). Modern sales
work is diverse; some roles remain field-based with extensive travel and
boundary spanning, while others are hybrid or digitally mediated,
supported by CRM systems and remote coordination technologies
(Storbacka et al., 2016; Winter, Habel, & Alavi, 2024). Regardless of
configuration, managers typically exercise discretion over workload
allocation, performance evaluation, schedule flexibility, and availability
standards. Prior research shows that sales control systems and supervi-
sory practices shape salesperson motivation, stress exposure, adaptive
selling behavior, and relational outcomes (Guenzi et al., 2014; Habel,
Kassemeier, et al., 2020; Piercy et al., 2009). What remains insuffi-
ciently theorized, however, is how these governance mechanisms
interact with individual circumstances to shape employees' evaluative
perceptions of balance.
Existing sales research has primarily examined the work–family
interface through conflict-based lenses (Darrat et al., 2010; Mulki et al.,
2006; Netemeyer et al., 1996; Netemeyer et al., 2005). These studies
provide valuable insights into strain, deviance, and turnover, although
the role of managerial agency in shaping balance perceptions remains
comparatively underdeveloped. Emerging research has begun to explore
salesperson well-being, relational stress dynamics, and supervisory
support (Bernuzzi et al., 2022; Guenzi et al., 2014), while work on in-
ternal market orientation and sales management emphasizes the role of
managerial practices in shaping employee outcomes and customer value
(Piercy et al., 2009). Yet a coherent explanation of FWB as an outcome of
alignment between managerial structuring and individual capacity re-
mains underdeveloped.
Role Theory highlights competing role demands (Biddle, 1986;
Crooker et al., 2002), enrichment perspectives emphasize positive
spillover (Carlson et al., 2014; Ollier-Malaterre et al., 2020), Identity
Theory focuses on inter-role tensions (Ashforth et al., 2008; Habel,
Kassemeier, et al., 2020; Ladge & Little, 2019), and Job
Demands–Resources Theory frames imbalance as excessive demands
relative to resources (Bakker & Demerouti, 2007). While analytically
powerful, these frameworks predominantly situate responsibility for
balance within the individual. Rather than displacing these perspectives,
this study reframes them by positioning balance as an outcome of
alignment between what the organization structures and what the in-
dividual is able to enact. This aligns with the pertinent literature high-
lighting that outcomes for frontline employees are shaped by
governance structures and supervisory actions (Habel, Alavi, & Pick,
2020; Habel, Kassemeier, et al., 2020; Piercy et al., 2009).
This study addresses that limitation by proposing a relational
alignment approach to FWB in sales. Drawing on Vocational Fit Theory
(Cable & DeRue, 2002; Kristof-Brown et al., 2005), we conceptualize
FWB as a higher-order reflective construct that represents employees'
evaluative perceptions of the harmony between their personal circum-
stances and the structure, monitoring, and assessment of work within
performance governance systems. Managerial decision-making,
including target calibration, monitoring intensity, flexibility interpre-
tation, and boundary signaling, is treated as an active component of the
balance formation process (Guenzi et al., 2014; Jaworski et al., 1993).
FWB remains an individual evaluative perception, but one that is
embedded within the relational governance systems characteristic of
industrial sales environments.
Two research questions guide this study. First, how do managerial
structuring and perceived vocational fit jointly influence FWB among
sales professionals? Second, does FWB manifest through distinct rela-
tional configurations rather than along a simple continuum of balance
and imbalance? To address these questions, the study adopts a sequen-
tial mixed-methods design. Study 1 uses qualitative inquiry to identify
the interpretive themes through which sales professionals evaluate FWB.
These themes subsequently inform the operationalization of the reflec-
tive dimensions used to measure the higher-order FWB construct in
Study 2. Using data collected from sales professionals in the United
Kingdom and Greece, the quantitative phase examines the relationships
among managerial structuring, perceived vocational fit, and FWB, while
complementary multivariate analyses explore broader relational con-
figurations associated with balance perceptions. This design integrates
qualitative conceptual development with quantitative theory testing to
explain the relational enactment of FWB.
The study makes three contributions. First, it advances FWB theory
by conceptualizing balance as a higher-order reflective construct
emerging from the alignment between managerial structuring and em-
ployees' perceived vocational fit. Second, it extends sales management
research by explicitly linking FWB to sales governance, supervisory
behavior, internal market orientation, and relational performance
management (Bernuzzi et al., 2022; Piercy et al., 2009). Third, it offers
actionable implications for sales leaders by identifying governance-
sensitive mechanisms that help sustain employee well-being alongside
long-term sales performance.
2. Key theoretical foundations of family-work balance
To establish the theoretical foundations of these contributions, the
following review examines how FWB has been conceptualized in the
literature and identifies the conceptual limitations that motivate the
relational framework developed in this study.
The study of FWB has developed across various disciplines, including
sociology, organizational psychology, and management, reflecting
increasing concern about how individuals handle competing role de-
mands (Biddle, 1986; Casper et al., 2018; Greenhaus & Allen, 2011). In
industrial marketing and sales contexts, however, employee well-being
is increasingly viewed as structurally embedded within governance
systems and performance architectures (Habel, Alavi, & Pick, 2020;
Piercy et al., 2009). Building on this perspective, the present study
conceptualizes FWB as a higher-order reflective construct that repre-
sents employees' overall evaluative perception of the balance between
work and personal life. This evaluative perception emerges through the
alignment between managerial structuring and employees' perceived
vocational fit within performance-governed sales environments.
In sales environments, where work may involve field-based activity,
hybrid selling, digital customer interaction, and sustained performance
pressure (Mulki et al., 2006; Winter, Habel, & Alavi, 2024), balancing
work and personal life becomes particularly challenging. Salespeople
occupy boundary-spanning roles characterized by temporal fluidity,
emotional labor, and relational accountability (Jaramillo et al., 2011;
Singh et al., 1996). These conditions heighten demands across domains,
making FWB not solely a personal coordination issue but also a
governance-sensitive organizational challenge.
Role Theory provides one of the earliest and most influential lenses
for understanding this interplay. It conceptualizes employees as occu-
pying multiple roles shaped by social and organizational expectations
(Biddle, 1986; Crooker et al., 2002; Ferdous et al., 2023). In sales con-
texts, these expectations include achieving performance targets, main-
taining customer relationships, and meeting family obligations.
Research within this tradition has largely developed across three related
streams (Greenhaus & Allen, 2011; Wayne et al., 2017): work–family
conflict perspectives emphasizing incompatibilities between competing
role demands, enrichment perspectives emphasizing positive spillover
across domains, and broader balance and fit-oriented approaches

The work–family conflict stream highlights how pressures in one
domain undermine functioning in the other (Bande et al., 2015; Kelly
et al., 2014). For salespeople, long working hours, constant availabil-
ity, and customer-driven interruptions frequently encroach on personal
time, creating strain that undermines satisfaction, engagement, and
relational continuity (Jaramillo et al., 2011; Mulki et al., 2006).
Research on supervisory control and performance pressure further
suggests that such strain is often intensified by sales governance systems
(Guenzi et al., 2014; Piercy et al., 2009).
In contrast, enrichment perspectives emphasize how experiences in
one role can enhance functioning in another, for example when inter-
personal skills developed in family life improve customer interactions or
when professional success generates positive spillover into personal life
(Carlson et al., 2014; Ollier-Malaterre et al., 2020). Broader FWB per-
spectives integrate both conflict and enrichment by emphasizing har-
mony across domains rather than treating work and family as competing
spheres (Crooker et al., 2002; Wayne et al., 2017). Despite these dif-
ferences, these perspectives generally position individuals as primarily
responsible for managing competing demands.
Identity Theory extends this understanding by examining how in-
dividuals negotiate role identities across work and family domains
(Ashforth & Schinoff, 2016; Cameron, 2004; Turner et al., 1979). Job
Demands–Resources Theory complements these perspectives by
emphasizing how limited resources such as time, energy, and emotional
capacity influence work–family dynamics (Bakker & Demerouti, 2007;
Zhou & Buehler, 2016). In sales roles, such resources are frequently
depleted by customer demands, travel, digital monitoring, and perfor-
mance variability (Winter, Zacharias, et al., 2024). Although these
perspectives provide important insight into how balance is experienced,
they largely locate the source of balance within individual coping,
identity negotiation, or resource management.
Rather than treating these perspectives as competing explanations,
the present study integrates them by repositioning FWB as an outcome of
alignment between organizational structuring and individual capacity.
Managerial systems define the conditions under which work is enacted,
while individuals differ in their ability to engage with these conditions
(Piercy et al., 2009; Voydanoff, 2005). FWB therefore emerges from the
degree of alignment between these elements rather than from either
independently. Accordingly, the present framework treats balance as
relationally enacted through the interaction between governance
structures and employees' evaluative perceptions of fit.
This omission is particularly salient in sales organizations, where
governance systems, supervisory behaviors, and internal market orien-
tation practices directly shape employee-level evaluations (Gounaris,
2006; Gounaris et al., 2010; Piercy et al., 2009). To clarify how these

Early empirical work focused on work–family conflict, while later
research incorporated multidimensional and enrichment-based per-
spectives (Carlson et al., 2000; Netemeyer et al., 1996; Netemeyer et al.,
2005). Across these approaches, balance is typically inferred from
individual-level evaluations rather than modeled as a relational
outcome shaped by managerial systems. This reinforces the need for a
framework that explicitly incorporates organizational structuring into
the balance formation process.
Research outside sales demonstrates that managerial support, au-
tonomy, and job design materially influence employees' ability to
manage work and family responsibilities (Hammer et al., 2011). In sales
settings, managers exercise considerable discretion over workload
allocation, target calibration, schedule flexibility, and performance
standards (Bradford & Weitz, 2009; Jaramillo et al., 2011; Singh et al.,
2021). Such discretion does not merely influence balance outcomes but
helps define the structural conditions under which alignment between
work and personal life becomes possible.
While the preceding discussion establishes that managerial struc-
turing shapes the organizational conditions under which balance is
experienced, it does not explain how employees evaluate these condi-
tions. Addressing this question requires a theoretical perspective that
explicitly considers the alignment between individual characteristics
and work environments. Vocational Fit Theory provides this explana-
tory mechanism by conceptualizing balance as emerging from em-
ployees' perceived fit with the conditions created through managerial
structuring.

At its core, VFT conceptualizes fit as an employee's subjective eval-
uation of alignment between personal characteristics and job re-
quirements. Importantly, fit is dynamic and continuously reassessed as
conditions change (Kristof-Brown et al., 2005). This perspective is
particularly relevant for FWB because perceptions of balance depend on
whether individuals feel able to meet work demands without under-
mining their personal lives.
Sales environments intensify the importance of perceived fit because
salespeople operate under conditions of role ambiguity, shifting per-
formance expectations, customer dependence, and relational account-
ability (Singh et al., 1996; Spiro & Weitz, 1990). These conditions make
perceived fit central to how employees interpret the compatibility be-
tween work demands and broader life circumstances (Jaramillo et al.,
2011). Within this framework, perceived vocational fit reflects em-
ployees' perceived capacity to engage with managerial structuring
rather than an isolated personal attribute.
Prior research distinguishes between fit and balance, where fit re-
flects the perceived ability to meet demands and balance reflects
perceived harmony across life domains (Clarke et al., 2004; Voydanoff,
2005). Building on this distinction, the present study conceptualizes
FWB as the evaluative outcome of how well perceived fit aligns with
conditions defined by managerial structuring.
From a relational alignment perspective, managerial agency plays a
central role in shaping perceived fit. Managers influence not only
interpersonal support but also role design, workload, flexibility, and
performance expectations (Jaramillo et al., 2011; Singh et al., 2021).
Managerial structuring therefore defines the conditions to which em-
ployees must adapt, while perceived vocational fit captures their
perceived ability to do so.
Although VFT has been applied in sales research, its systematic
integration into FWB research remains limited. Table 2 summarizes key
studies employing fit-based perspectives in sales contexts and highlights
both their contributions and limitations.
As shown in Table 2, prior studies have largely examined managerial
structuring and vocational fit as separate explanatory perspectives,
without integrating them to explain how employees evaluate balance
within performance-driven sales environments. This gap provides the
conceptual foundation for the present study, which brings these per-
spectives together within a single relational framework.
Building on this integration, FWB is conceptualized as a higher-order
reflective construct representing employees' overall evaluative percep-
tion of balance between work and personal domains. This perception
reflects the extent to which individuals are able to engage with work
conditions without systematic detriment to their personal lives.
Importantly, perceived balance varies across individuals even under
similar structural conditions due to differences in personal circum-
stances, career stages, and priorities (Casper et al., 2018). From a rela-
tional alignment perspective, such variation is expected because balance
depends on how individual capacity interacts with managerial struc-
turing rather than on structural conditions alone.
Within the present framework, FWB constitutes the focal higher-
order reflective construct. Managerial structuring and perceived voca-
tional fit are treated as antecedents, while governance pressures and
perceived barriers shape balance perceptions. Coping mechanisms and
post-work routines are conceptualized as adaptive responses rather than
core dimensions of FWB, whereas attitudinal variables such as job
satisfaction, work engagement, and turnover intentions are treated as
external outcomes used to assess construct validity.

Managerial structuring involves supervising work demands via
workload, targets, monitoring, flexibility, and boundaries, affecting
employees' views of discretion, availability, and role manageability in
sales. Managers in sales environments have significant discretion in how
they assign workloads, set targets, schedule, grant autonomy, and
monitor performance (Jaramillo et al., 2011; Singh et al., 2021; Singh &
Das, 2013). These decisions influence how employees perceive their
roles and the permeability of boundaries. When managerial practices
align work demands with employees' personal responsibilities, they help
reduce role strain and support higher levels of FWB. On the other hand,
strict expectations, heavy monitoring, and unrealistic goals can hinder
employees' ability to balance work and personal life effectively (Mulki
et al., 2006). Therefore, we suggest:
H1. Managerial structuring of work is positively associated with FWB
among sales professionals.
Perceived vocational fit is conceptualized as employees' evaluation
of the alignment between their abilities, needs, and career orientations
and the demands and characteristics of their sales role. Consistent with
VFT, this includes both demands–abilities fit and needs–supplies fit di-
mensions, which together influence whether employees perceive their
work role as compatible with their broader life circumstances. VFT also
suggests that employees' personal assessment of how well their needs,
skills, and job roles align influences their attitudes and work results
(Cable & DeRue, 2002; Kristof-Brown et al., 2005). In sales settings with
changing demands and high performance pressure, perceived fit in-
dicates whether employees feel they can fulfill role expectations without
significantly sacrificing their personal lives. When salespeople view a
strong match between work demands and their personal situations, they
are more likely to experience FWB. Conversely, misalignment can cause
increased stress and diminish their sense of balance. Accordingly, we
propose:
H2. Perceived vocational fit is positively associated with FWB among
sales professionals.
5. Methodology, research design, and findings
A sequential mixed-methods approach was employed to refine the
conceptualization of FWB and empirically validate its higher-order
reflective measurement model. Accordingly, the study pursued two
interconnected objectives. First, Study 1 aimed to clarify the conceptu-
alization of the higher-order reflective FWB construct and operationalize
its reflective dimensions, thereby supporting scale development. Sec-
ond, Study 2 aimed to validate the proposed measurement structure and
formally test the hypothesized relationships between managerial
structuring, perceived vocational fit, and FWB.
5.1. Study 1: Qualitative Exploration
5.1.1. Research design
To explore how FWB is enacted in sales contexts, we employed an
exploratory qualitative design using a theory-informed, data-refined
(abductive) analytic approach. This qualitative phase clarified how sales
professionals interpret and evaluate FWB and informed construct spec-
ification and measurement development for the quantitative phase. It
was not intended to test causal relationships.
This approach was selected to capture salespeople's lived experiences
of balancing vocational demands and personal needs, an issue central to
VFT and its emphasis on perceived alignment between role requirements
and individual circumstances (Miles & Huberman, 1994). Nineteen
semi-structured, in-depth interviews were conducted with sales pro-
fessionals in the United Kingdom. Eligibility criteria required partici-
pants to have at least two years of continuous sales experience and a
minimum of one year with their current employer, ensuring sufficient
familiarity with both the sales role and the organizational context
shaping their experience of balance.
5.1.2. Research protocol and method of analysis
Semi-structured interviewing was selected because it enables sys-
tematic exploration of theoretically informed topics while remaining
open to participants' interpretations and meaning-making (Broun´eus,
2011; Smith & Osborn, 2003). This approach was particularly suited
to examining how salespeople negotiate the interplay between work and
personal life amid organizational and managerial constraints. Consistent
with the relational alignment perspective adopted in this study, the
interview protocol was informed by prior research on sales governance,
managerial control systems, and family-supportive supervisory behav-
iors (Guenzi et al., 2014; Hammer et al., 2009; Jaworski et al., 1993;
Piercy et al., 2009). These studies informed the interview protocol by
identifying sensitizing concepts, including target setting, monitoring
intensity, flexible interpretation, boundary signaling, and perceived
organizational support, while remaining open to refinement during the
abductive analysis. In addition to reflective expressions of balance, the
protocol explored governance pressures, perceived barriers, and coping
responses associated with participants' experiences of balance and
imbalance.
Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached,
defined as the point at which no substantively new relational patterns or
evaluative dimensions emerged in successive interviews (Guest et al.,
2006). Initial participants were recruited from a part-time MBA pro-
gram based on the eligibility criteria. Subsequent participants were

the study's working definition and invited to reflect on its resonance
with their experiences. This sequencing preserved participants' un-
prompted accounts while allowing analytical comparison between lived
meaning and conceptual framing. Only thereafter did the discussion
move to more focused prompts concerning managerial governance
mechanisms and contextual influences.
Data analysis employed abductive template analysis (King, 2004;
King & Brooks, 2016). Template analysis was selected because it com-
bines theory-informed hierarchical coding with flexibility for abductive
refinement and integration. The process began with open coding of
transcripts, allowing descriptive codes to emerge and subsequently be
refined through iterative comparison. A preliminary coding template
informed by FWB and related literature guided the analysis while
remaining adaptable throughout coding (Brooks et al., 2015). Initial
coding remained open to emergent participant meanings, while theo-
retical concepts informed iterative interpretation and refinement of the
coding template through an abductive process (King, 2004; King &
Brooks, 2016).
The coding template was developed through ongoing engagement
with the empirical data. Through iterative comparison, consolidation,
and refinement, lower-order descriptive codes were organized into hi-
erarchical structures of codes, interpretive themes, and evaluative
components of the FWB construct aligned with the study's framework.
For example, stress was initially interpreted as personal strain but was
subsequently reinterpreted as a relational mechanism linking gover-
nance structures to balance perceptions after repeated comparisons
showed that participants associated stress with managerial practices and
work design. This process reflects the abductive movement between
empirical observation and theoretical interpretation.
Two researchers independently coded all transcripts and resolved
discrepancies collaboratively (Miles & Huberman, 1994). Reflexive
memoing was employed throughout the analysis to improve trans-
parency and consistency in analytical decision-making (Nowell et al.,
2017). Inter-rater reliability produced a Cohen's Kappa coefficient of
0.67, indicating substantial agreement.
5.1.3. Template analysis for FWB: findings and interpretive integration
Participants described FWB as emerging from the interaction be-
tween managerial structuring and personal life. The qualitative analysis
identified four interpretive themes, Gratifying Experiences, Welfare and
Psychological Sustainability, Job Fulfillment as a Legitimizing Signal,
and Unreachable, through which participants evaluated their experi-
ences of balance. These themes constitute conceptual categories derived
from the template analysis and, in Study 2, were operationalized as the
four reflective dimensions of the higher-order FWB construct.
Throughout the remainder of the manuscript, interpretive themes refers
to the qualitative findings, whereas reflective dimensions refers to their
quantitative operationalization. Consistent with Vocational Fit Theory,
participants evaluated balance according to perceived role alignment
and personal circumstances rather than objective workload alone.
Although template analysis is commonly presented using nested The-
me–Subtheme hierarchies (King, 2004; King & Brooks, 2016), Fig. 2
adopts a conceptually adapted representation to illustrate how lower-
order descriptive codes were integrated into higher-order interpretive
themes that subsequently informed the reflective specification of FWB in
Study 2.
Fig. 2 presents the final hierarchical template structure emerging
from the abductive analysis. It illustrates the progression from lower-
order descriptive codes to higher-order interpretive themes underpin-
ning the higher-order FWB construct. The figure focuses exclusively on
the reflective thematic architecture of perceived FWB; contextual bar-
riers, coping mechanisms, and post-work routines are presented sepa-

responses rather than reflective dimensions of the focal construct.
5.1.3.1. Gratifying experiences. Participants evaluated balance in terms
of experiential coherence across domains. References focused primarily
on the quality of personal time, the enjoyment of personal life, the
amount of stress, and the loss of personal time. Enjoyment of personal
time and psychological presence outside work were viewed as central
indicators of balance, whereas the amount of stress and loss of personal
time disrupted this coherence.
One participant stated (P2): “When I go out, I want to enjoy my time.
Not to have part of my mind locked at work.” This theme captures
whether managerial expectations permit cognitive disengagement from
work. Participants repeatedly associated target pressure, excessive
meetings, and availability norms with diminished experiential quality,
suggesting that this aspect of balance is shaped by governance condi-
tions rather than individual boundary management alone.
This interpretation aligns with boundary and spillover perspectives
emphasizing permeability across role domains and cognitive intrusion
between work and personal life (Ashforth et al., 2000; Clark, 2000).
However, participants explicitly linked boundary permeability to
managerial governance practices rather than solely to individual
preferences.

5.1.4. Barriers and contextual conditions for FWB
Although Study 1 focused on clarifying the reflective evaluative
structure of perceived FWB, participants also described conditions
related to how balance was enabled, constrained, and managed. These
themes were treated as contextual governance conditions surrounding
balance enactment rather than as dimensions of the higher-order FWB
construct.
Participants referred to personal dispositions, ambition, career stage,
workload intensity, and managerial expectations as conditions influ-
encing perceptions of balance. Although these barriers align with
established work–family conflict research, distinguishing between in-
dividual antecedents and structurally generated demands, participants'
accounts suggest that even ostensibly personal barriers are embedded
within governance systems.
Personal barriers were frequently linked to organizational structures
and expectations. For example, ambition became problematic primarily
within systems rewarding constant availability, while workload stress
was associated with staffing shortages and escalating targets. As P5
explained: “The stress is not just because the job is demanding. It's
because there are not enough people, targets keep increasing, and you
feel you have to prove yourself all the time if you want to progress.”
Similarly, ambition became disruptive when performance systems
normalized continuous responsiveness and boundary permeability. Ac-
cording to P11: “Ambition by itself is not the problem. The problem is
that once you show you can deliver, they expect you to always be
available, always responsive. At some point, work stops having clear
boundaries.”
Participants also linked career-stage pressures to organizational ex-
pectations surrounding advancement and sacrifice. As P17 stated: “At
this stage of my career, you feel pressure to prove yourself all the time.
The company may not say it directly, but you quickly understand that
the people who progress are the ones who sacrifice more of their per-
sonal time.”
These findings suggest that barriers emerge through the interaction
between personal orientations and structural performance conditions
rather than as isolated individual failings. Governance mechanisms such
as target escalation, monitoring intensity, and availability expectations
therefore appear to function as boundary-setting devices shaping per-
ceptions of balance.

5.2. Study 2: Quantitative examination
The qualitative findings informed the specification of FWB as a
higher-order evaluative construct and clarified the governance-related
antecedents examined in Study 2, particularly managerial structuring
and perceived vocational fit. Building on these insights, the quantitative
phase validated the proposed FWB structure and examined its associa-
tions with managerial structuring and perceived vocational fit. The
qualitative phase provided the conceptual grounding for construct
specification and item development, whereas the quantitative phase
provided formal empirical testing of the proposed relationships.
5.2.1. Research design
Drawing on the conceptual insights generated in Study 1, the
quantitative phase examined the relationships between managerial
structuring, perceived vocational fit, and employees' perceptions of
FWB. This stage formally assessed the higher-order reflective specifi-
cation of FWB and evaluated its associations with managerial gover-
nance practices and perceived role alignment.
Data were collected through a structured survey administered to
sales professionals in two national contexts. The primary empirical
setting was the United Kingdom, while Greece was included to assess
cross-context robustness. Greece was selected because, although it is a
developed European Union economy, it differs from the UK in institu-
tional and economic conditions, including labor market structure and
employment stability (Kaasa et al., 2014). Including a second context
enabled assessment of whether the measurement structure and struc-
tural associations observed in the primary sample were stable across
distinct yet comparable European environments.
The inclusion of UK and Greek samples was intended to strengthen
the robustness of the proposed FWB specification rather than to support
cross-cultural comparison. The UK represents a mature, performance-
driven sales environment, whereas Greece reflects a context shaped by
prolonged economic instability and different employment conditions.
Examining the stability of the measurement structure and structural
associations across both settings enabled assessment of whether the
relational specification of FWB remained consistent under different
institutional conditions. Following the establishment of measurement
invariance, the samples were pooled for structural analysis, consistent
with the study's emphasis on theory testing and construct robustness
rather than country-level comparison.
The final sample comprised 473 sales professionals, including 314
respondents from the United Kingdom and 159 from Greece. Eligibility
criteria were identical across countries, requiring participants to have at
least two years of continuous experience in sales and a minimum of one
year with their current employer. These conditions ensured that re-
spondents had sufficient exposure to sales demands and managerial
practices to meaningfully evaluate their experience of FWB. None of the

5.2.2. Variables and their measurement
The operationalization of variables in Study Two was informed by
the qualitative insights generated in Study One. The purpose of the
qualitative phase was to ensure conceptual clarity and contextual
sensitivity in measurement rather than to derive causal ordering.
Accordingly, survey items were developed to reflect the evaluative ex-
pressions and surrounding conditions identified during the interviews.
Perceived FWB was operationalized as a higher-order reflective
construct grounded in the thematic structure identified in Study 1.
Specifically, the qualitative analysis identified four interrelated evalu-
ative domains of FWB, which informed the specification of the four di-
mensions modeled in Fig. 1 and subsequently validated quantitatively:
Work–Life Reflection on Personal Life (WL-PL), Personal-Life Conditions
(PLC), Work-Life Conditions (WLC), and the negatively valenced Un-
reachable (UN) dimension. Accordingly, the quantitative specification
translates these interpretive themes into the operationalized reflective
dimensions shown in Fig. 1, and these labels are used consistently
throughout the remainder of the quantitative analysis.
To strengthen conceptual coverage and content validity, the quali-
tative insights were triangulated with established conceptualizations of
balance reported in the pertinent literature. Specifically, prior measures
of work–family conflict (Carlson et al., 2000; Netemeyer et al., 1996),
work–family enrichment (Carlson et al., 2006), and multidimensional
balance conceptualizations (Carlson et al., 2009; Fisher et al., 2009;
Greenhaus et al., 2003) were reviewed to identify core evaluative do-
mains commonly associated with balance perceptions. Items were not
adopted verbatim from prior scales. Instead, the review informed item
refinement and domain specification while preserving the relational and
managerial emphasis emerging from Study One. This procedure sup-
ported content validity by ensuring conceptual alignment with estab-
lished work–family literature while retaining the contextual specificity
of the present framework.
Consistent with the conceptual model presented in Fig. 1, gover-
nance barriers, coping mechanisms, and post-work routines were treated
as contextual conditions or adaptive responses rather than reflective
dimensions of FWB itself. An initial 13-item pool was developed to
operationalize the four reflective dimensions of perceived FWB identi-
fied in Study 1. Indicators were specified reflectively because they
capture evaluative manifestations of an underlying perception of bal-
ance rather than forming the construct causally. Additional details
regarding item refinement, pilot testing, measurement purification, and
psychometric assessment are reported in Appendix B.
Additional variables were operationalized to capture contextual in-
fluences surrounding the focal construct. These included perceived
barriers to FWB (10 items), daily routines (13 items), stress-coping
strategies (10 items), and obstacles to enjoyable work (9 items),
reflecting recurring themes identified during the interviews. These
variables functioned as exploratory contextual indicators intended to
capture governance-related conditions, adaptive responses, and broader
relational dynamics associated with FWB. Because their role in the study
was contextual and descriptive rather than construct-defining, they were

5.2.3. Data analysis
The data analysis followed a structured and sequential logic aligned
with the study's theoretical objectives. First, measurement properties
were assessed to establish the validity and comparability of the con-
structs. Second, structural equation modeling (SEM) was employed as
the primary analytical approach to examine the hypothesized relation-
ships among managerial structuring, perceived vocational fit, and FWB.
Third, complementary canonical correlation analyses (CCA) were con-
ducted to explore whether the associations identified in SEM were
embedded within broader multivariate relational configurations. In the
present study, CCA is employed strictly as a descriptive, set-level
analytical extension rather than as an alternative explanatory or
hypothesis-testing technique.
Throughout the quantitative analysis, the term associations refers to
theoretically specified relational linkages identified within a cross-
sectional design rather than to causal effects. Accordingly, SEM esti-
mates are interpreted as structured relational associations consistent
with the proposed conceptual framework, while the complementary
CCA analyses examine broader multivariate configurations among var-
iable sets. This distinction is important because the study seeks to
explain how managerial structuring, perceived vocational fit, and FWB
are relationally interconnected without inferring temporal or causal
ordering.
Common method variance was assessed using the CFA marker-
variable approach (Richardson et al., 2009). Marker loadings were not
significantly related to substantive constructs in either sample, sug-
gesting CMV was not a concern. Independent-sample t-tests indicated no
statistically significant differences in means between the UK and Greek
samples. Measurement invariance was examined using multi-group
CFA. Configural, metric, and scalar invariance were supported, justi-
fying pooled structural analyses. Additional details regarding invariance
testing and psychometric assessment are reported in Appendix B.
Nomological validity was supported through theoretically consistent
associations with work engagement and turnover intentions (Appendix
B). Predictive validity was assessed by regressing job satisfaction on the
four FWB dimensions within the UK sample. Job satisfaction was
included exclusively as an external criterion variable to assess the
nomological and predictive validity of the newly developed FWB
construct rather than as a focal endogenous component of the concep-
tual model presented in Fig. 1. The model explained 37.5% of the
variance in job satisfaction.

construct validity, CFA results provide the basis for the subsequent
structural modeling analyses reported in Table 7, while the exploratory
factor analysis used during instrument refinement is reported separately
in Appendix B (Table B2) (Churchill, 1979; Hinkin, 1995).

5.2.3.2. Complementary analysis: canonical correlation analysis. While
SEM provides theory-driven examination of the hypothesized relation-
ships, it assumes linear and additive associations among constructs. To
examine whether these relationships were embedded within broader
multivariate configurations, complementary canonical correlation ana-
lyses (CCA) were conducted. CCA was employed as a descriptive set-
level technique to identify broader patterns of association between
variable groups, consistent with the premise that FWB is experienced as
a relational configuration rather than as the outcome of isolated
predictors.
The first CCA examined the associations between the four di-
mensions of FWB and perceived barriers. Unlike regression-based ap-
proaches that estimate the unique contribution of individual predictors,
CCA evaluates the relationship between two variable sets simulta-
neously by deriving linear composites (canonical variates) that are
maximally correlated.
As shown in Table 9, four statistically significant canonical functions
emerged (Rc ranging from 0.595 to 0.261, all p < 0.001). The first
function (Rc = 0.595) represented the strongest shared pattern between
the predictor and criterion sets. The redundancy index of approximately
47% indicated that the barrier configuration accounted for a substantial
proportion of variance in the FWB canonical variates. (See Table 10.)
The canonical loadings suggested that the first function reflected a
configuration in which organizational expectations and employee-level
orientations co-occurred with stronger evaluations of WL-PL and WLC
and lower endorsement of UN. This pattern extends the SEM findings by
indicating that perceived balance is associated with aligned configura-
tions of organizational conditions and individual orientations rather
than with isolated variables examined independently.
The remaining canonical functions reflected weaker patterns of as-
sociation involving stress, career stage, and perceived feasibility of
balance. These findings indicate that different combinations of organi-
zational and individual conditions are associated with variation in
perceived FWB, rather than reflecting a simple bipolar continuum.
Importantly, these patterns should be interpreted as descriptive exten-
sions of the SEM findings rather than as independent explanatory
evidence.
A second CCA examined whether the evaluative dimensions of FWB
were associated with broader patterns involving adaptive responses and
organizational barriers. Whereas the first CCA focused primarily on
governance and structural conditions, the second explored how coping
mechanisms and obstacles to enjoyable work co-occurred with different
expressions of FWB. This analysis therefore examined whether balance
perceptions were associated not only with structural conditions but also
with the adaptive relational practices employees used to manage them.
Four statistically significant canonical functions emerged, with an
overall redundancy of approximately 72%. The strongest function re-
flected a configuration in which perceived organizational fairness,
managerial communication, and personal coping strategies were asso-
ciated with variation in the UN dimension. Subsequent functions reflect
smaller patterns of association linking managerial expectations, stress
levels, and communicative support mechanisms with WL-PL and WLC
evaluations.

6. Discussion
This study examines FWB in sales as a relational, higher-order
evaluative state shaped through the interaction between managerial
structuring and employees' perceived vocational fit rather than as an
individual responsibility alone. Consistent with the conceptual model
presented in Fig. 1, the study focuses on the relationships among
managerial structuring, perceived vocational fit, and perceived FWB,
while variables such as job satisfaction, well-being, engagement, and
turnover are treated as supplementary outcomes rather than as formal
components of the focal model.
The findings support a distinction between antecedent conditions,
evaluative perceptions of FWB, governance pressures, adaptive re-
sponses, and attitudinal outcomes. More specifically, FWB appears to
develop through the interaction between salespeople and the organi-
zational systems within which work is structured and evaluated. While
prior research has primarily emphasized individual coping strategies,
psychological strain, or resource depletion (Casper et al., 2018;
Greenhaus & Allen, 2011), the present findings suggest that balance is
interpreted within managerial and organizational systems that sub-
stantially shape employees' evaluations of compatibility between work
and personal life. Integrating VFT (Cable & DeRue, 2002) with a rela-
tional perspective therefore provides a more contextualized
interpretation of balance as an evaluative perception emerging from the
alignment between managerial demands and employees' life circum-
stances. This interpretation is consistent with research showing that
supervisory practices and governance structures influence salesperson
attitudes and performance outcomes (Guenzi et al., 2014; Habel, Alavi,
& Pick, 2020).
The qualitative findings further clarify how sales professionals
interpret FWB in practice. Participants did not describe balance as a
fixed state or a simple trade-off between work and family demands.
Instead, they described it as a dynamic evaluative perception shaped by
work conditions, perceived attainability, and broader implications for
well-being and life quality. Consistent with contextual perspectives on
the work–family interface (Voydanoff, 2005), participants linked these
evaluations to managerial decisions and organizational practices rather
than exclusively to individual preferences. Recognition, career devel-
opment, and job security emerged as evaluative expressions associated
with the Job Fulfillment dimension, while workload calibration and
flexibility operated as governance-related conditions shaping percep-
tions of balance. Importantly, these evaluative expressions do not
represent generic job satisfaction assessments or broader job-quality
evaluations. Rather, they reflect employees' perceptions of whether
balancing work and personal life is institutionally supported, sustain-
able, and viewed as legitimate within the organizational environment.
In performance-driven environments characterized by goal intensity,
monitoring, and boundary-spanning responsibilities (Jaramillo et al.,
2011), and increasingly mediated through digital performance in-
frastructures (Winter, Habel, & Alavi, 2024), FWB appears to emerge
through employees' ongoing interpretation of everyday organizational
practices rather than solely through individual accommodation
mechanisms.

The quantitative findings support this relational interpretation.
Across two national contexts, perceived FWB demonstrated theoretically
consistent associations with perceived vocational fit and managerial
structuring variables, supporting the construct's nomological validity
and cross-context robustness (Cable & DeRue, 2002; Jaworski et al.,
1993). In addition, the analyses suggest that higher and lower levels
of perceived FWB may be associated with differentiated patterns of
organizational conditions and individual orientations rather than func-
tioning simply as opposite ends of a single bipolar continuum. This
interpretation is consistent with multidimensional conceptualizations of
FWB (Fisher et al., 2009; Greenhaus et al., 2003). The negatively
valenced UN (“Unreachable”) dimension appears to capture a distinct
evaluative perception that balance may be structurally difficult to attain
within certain sales environments. Rather than functioning merely as
the inverse of positive balance expressions, this dimension reflects per-
ceptions shaped by repeated exposure to demanding managerial prac-
tices and persistent performance pressures, echoing research on stress
and withdrawal tendencies in high-pressure sales contexts (Jaramillo
et al., 2011; Mulki et al., 2015).
The findings also clarify how FWB is evaluated within sales envi-
ronments. Participants did not evaluate balance solely in terms of
workload or formal flexibility. Instead, they evaluated whether mana-
gerial expectations, performance demands, and organizational signals
remained compatible with sustainable personal lives. In this respect,
managerial structuring influences FWB not only by shaping objective
work conditions but also by shaping employees' perceptions regarding
the legitimacy and sustainability of balancing work and personal roles
within performance-driven systems.
Conceptually, the findings support the specification of FWB as a
higher-order reflective construct manifested through four interrelated
yet distinct dimensions. Rather than aggregating conflict and enrich-
ment components, the construct captures an overarching evaluative
perception of alignment between managerial structuring and individual
life circumstances. The findings therefore help differentiate the present
specification from prior conflict-based (Netemeyer et al., 1996),
enrichment-based (Carlson et al., 2006), and broader balance concep-
tualizations (Fisher et al., 2009), while extending discussion regarding
how governance mechanisms shape employee-level evaluations within
sales environments (Habel, Alavi, et al., 2020; Piercy et al., 2009).
6.1. Theoretical contribution
This study makes two primary theoretical contributions. First, it
contributes to FWB theory by positioning balance within a relational
framework that explicitly incorporates managerial agency. Established
perspectives such as Role Theory (Carlson et al., 2000; Kahn et al., 1964;
Netemeyer et al., 1996) and Job Demands–Resources Theory (Bakker &
Demerouti, 2007) remain analytically relevant. However, the present
framework situates these perspectives within a structure where mana-
gerial calibration of demands, targets, and boundaries functions as an
important explanatory mechanism shaping employees' evaluative per-
ceptions of balance. By integrating VFT (Cable & DeRue, 2002) into this
relational framing, the study clarifies how balance perceptions are
associated with the alignment between managerial structuring and
employees' perceived needs–supplies and demands–abilities fit. In doing
so, the study responds to calls for greater conceptual precision in dis-
tinguishing balance from adjacent constructs (Allen et al., 2013;
Greenhaus & Allen, 2011) while complementing research emphasizing
the structuring role of supervisory systems in shaping salesperson atti-
tudes and behaviors (Guenzi et al., 2014; Habel, Alavi, & Pick, 2020).
Second, the study contributes to sales management research by of-
fering a contextually grounded examination of FWB within
performance-structured sales environments. Sales roles combine au-
tonomy with goal intensity, accountability pressures, and relational
customer engagement, creating distinctive conditions for the work-
–family interface (Jaramillo et al., 2011; Mulki et al., 2008). The
findings suggest that perceived FWB comprises multiple interrelated
dimensions reflecting both managerial and individual elements. Welfare
and Psychological Sustainability relate to perceptions of psychological
sustainability and reduced personal sacrifice under conditions influ-
enced by workload structuring and supervisory support (Karatepe &
Tekinkus, 2006). Gratifying Experiences relate to discretionary time
and the quality of personal life, which may be undermined by cultures
normalizing excessive availability (Guedes et al., 2023). Job Fulfillment
reflects institutional recognition and signals of career progression,
themes long associated with internal market orientation and supervisory
support (Gounaris, 2006; Piercy et al., 2009). The negatively valenced
UN dimension captures perceptions that balance may be incompatible
with sales success within certain performance systems. In addition, the
stability of the measurement structure across two institutional contexts
supports the broader robustness of the proposed relational specification.
The study also develops and empirically supports a multidimensional
operationalization of perceived FWB composed of four dimensions:
Gratifying Experiences, Welfare and Psychological Sustainability, Job
Fulfillment as a legitimizing signal, and Unreachable (the negative dis-
ruptor). These dimensions emerged from participants' accounts and
were operationalized as reflective manifestations of a higher-order FWB
construct. This operationalization extends prior approaches by incor-
porating governance-sensitive and relationally embedded evaluative
dimensions.
Finally, the findings suggest that variable-centered explanations
alone may not fully capture the relational patterning associated with
perceived FWB. The complementary CCA analyses indicate that per-
ceptions of balance and imbalance may be associated with broader
relational configurations combining organizational conditions and in-
dividual orientations rather than with isolated predictors examined
independently. This interpretation aligns with calls within sales schol-
arship for greater attention to contextual complexity and system-
oriented explanations of salesperson outcomes (Piercy et al., 2009;
Ragin, 2008; Woodside, 2016).
6.2. Managerial contribution
From a managerial standpoint, the findings translate into four
interrelated implications for sales management and role design. More
broadly, the results suggest that FWB in sales is not merely an individual
accommodation issue but a managerial and organizational concern
shaped through daily decisions regarding work design, expectations,
and support systems. When balance is treated as a shared organizational
responsibility, sales leaders become important actors in sustaining per-
formance while supporting employees' longer-term capacity to manage
work and personal demands (Bernuzzi et al., 2022; Habel, Alavi, & Pick,
2020; Winter, Zacharias, et al., 2024).
A first implication concerns managerial expectations and role design.
The findings underscore the importance of calibrating performance
targets, clarifying role boundaries, and aligning goals with the temporal
and emotional demands inherent in sales work (Jaramillo et al., 2011).
Excessive workload or implicit expectations of constant availability
appear to undermine perceived FWB even when formal flexibility pol-
icies are present. Managers, therefore, need to consider not only per-
formance outcomes but also the structural conditions under which those
outcomes are pursued. Transparent prioritization and consistent
signaling that personal boundaries are legitimate may contribute to
more sustainable allocation of effort across work and personal domains.
A second implication concerns psychological well-being and stress
management. The findings suggest that managerial actions promoting
supportive supervisory behaviors (Hammer et al., 2009), encouraging
open discussion of workload pressures, and offering personalized
coaching may help sustain engagement and reduce burnout risk
(Karatepe & Tekinkus, 2006; Mulki et al., 2008). Importantly, these
practices extend beyond procedural flexibility by fostering organiza-
tional environments in which discussing constraints and pressures

aligns with research emphasizing the importance of relational supervi-

Third, the findings suggest that formal policy implementation alone
may be insufficient unless embedded within a supportive organizational
culture. This interpretation aligns with internal market orientation
principles, which conceptualize employees as internal stakeholders

within sales enablement and people-management practices (Gounaris
et al., 2010; Guedes et al., 2023) may help organizations sustain pro-
ductivity while supporting more sustainable evaluations of balance
across work and personal domains.
7. Limitations & directions for future research
As with any research, findings should be viewed with limitations that
suggest further inquiry. The study highlighted FWB's relational nature
and examined its antecedents and associations but did not track how
managerial–employee relationships change over time. Work–family
dynamics are inherently temporal and influenced by career transitions
and life events (Voydanoff, 2005). Perceptions of FWB may shift after
promotion, parenthood, territory redesign, or customer portfolio
changes. The cross-sectional design offers clarity but can't track how
managerial and vocational adjustments evolve. Future research could
use longitudinal, diary, or experience-sampling methods to examine
how supervisory behaviors and performance expectations influence
balance trajectories time (Beal & Weiss, 2003; Ohly et al., 2010). Such
approaches would clarify how managerial agency can be continuously
adapted rather than through static policy interventions.
A further limitation concerns the construct development process.
Although the study followed an abductive mixed-method design that
combined qualitative construct specification with subsequent quantita-
tive validation, it did not fully follow the sequential procedures advo-
cated in classical scale development frameworks (Churchill, 1979).
Rather than generating and refining items through multiple iterative
purification stages, the study prioritized preserving the conceptual
richness emerging from participants' experiences before evaluating the
construct empirically. Future research could extend this work by un-
dertaking additional scale refinement and cross-validation across inde-
pendent samples, thereby providing further evidence of the measure's
robustness and generalizability.
The study focused on the UK and Greece, both Western environ-
ments, enabling comparison and testing measurement invariance.
However, this limits applicability to regions with different authority,
norms, or work values. Cultural factors like power distance and collec-
tivism may influence how managerial structuring impacts FWB
(Hofstede, 2001). Broadening the relational framework to include Asia,
the Middle East, Africa, or Latin America would enable investigation of
how institutional logics and governance systems influence balance
enactment in diverse market systems (Jackson et al., 2013). Such studies
could clarify which dynamics are specific to certain contexts and which
are universally applicable.
Fourth, although the study indicates that perceived FWB is shaped by
managerial and individual factors and is associated with key attitudinal
outcomes, it does not directly examine its impact on broader
organizational outcomes. Sales research routinely links well-being,
stress, and job satisfaction to performance and turnover (Jaramillo
et al., 2011). Future studies could examine if relationally enacted FWB
predicts metrics such as sales, retention, growth, or team effectiveness.
Using multi-level designs combining individual perceptions with team
and organizational results would support including FWB in performance
management and broader debates on relational governance and sus-
tainable performance. (Palmatier et al., 2007; Ulaga & Reinartz, 2011).
Another limitation is the lack of distinction between field sales,
which have more autonomy, and inside sales, which involve closer
oversight, digital tracking, and fixed locations (Rapp et al., 2010;
Verbeke et al., 2011). Future research should examine if FWB dynamics
differ across setups, as relational mechanisms may vary when autonomy
is high, but supervision is remote versus digitally monitored environ-
ments. Exploring these differences can deepen understanding of how
managerial control interacts with role structure.
An important trend involves AI and digital tech in sales management,
transforming automation, analytics, CRM, and performance monitoring
(Davenport et al., 2020; Jarrahi et al., 2021). AI's impact on FWB varies
with how managers integrate these tools into work design, expectations,
and control systems (Raisch & Krakowski, 2021). Automation reduces
routine tasks, but algorithmic oversight may increase pressure and blur
boundaries. The use of AI monitoring, CRM analytics, and digital sys-
tems could alter employees' views on autonomy and control in sales,
affecting how they assess FWB. Future research should examine AI as a
managerial tool in relational governance, aligning with studies on dig-
ital transformation and control. (Paschen et al., 2020; Rapp et al., 2021).
While this study used an abductive qualitative approach to support
construct specification, future longitudinal, process-oriented abductive
research could examine how evolving managerial technologies reshape
FWB evaluations over time. As hybrid work, digital platforms, and
ecosystem selling evolve, concepts of balance, responsibility, and legit-
imacy may change (Gioia et al., 2013; Storbacka et al., 2016). Future
studies extending abductive and interpretive qualitative approaches
could explore how AI supervision, platform coordination, and ecosystem
governance alter relational expectations in sales.
These limitations highlight that FWB is a dynamic phenomenon
within various systems. Applying this perspective across time, cultures,
levels, and digital contexts can help sales organizations create gover-
nance systems that maintain performance and employee well-being in
complex markets.
