Abstract

This article reviews how AI -generated marketing videos and related synthetic social media
advertising formats influence consumer trust and perceived deception. The review is important
because generative AI allows brands to create humanlike voices, virtual endorsers, simulated
product scenes and personalized video content at low cost. A PRISMA-guided systematic literature
review was conducted using Scopus, Google Scholar, OpenAlex, Crossref  and publisher pages.
Search terms covered AI -generated advertising, deepfakes, virtual influencers, disclosure,
persuasion knowledge, trust, authenticity and deception. After duplicate removal and screening,
58 core studies were synthesized thematically. The literature indicates that consumers do not
automatically reject AI -generated marketing videos. However, trust decreases when AI creates
ambiguity about source identity, product evidence, commercial intent or data -driven
personalization. Disclosure can support trust, but only when it is visible, specific and meaningful.
The article integrates AI advertising, virtual influencer, deepfake, disclosure and consumer trust
research into a framework for responsible synthetic video marketing.

Keywords: Generative AI, Social Media Marketing, Consumer Trust, Perceived Deception,
Virtual Influencers, Disclosure.

Introduction

Generative artificial intelligence has changed the way marketing content is produced, edited and
distributed. In the past, a marketing video normally required human actors, physical product
scenes, voice talents and professional editing. At present, brands can use AI tools to create scripts,
synthetic voices, avatars, virtual influencers, product demonstrations and different versions of the
same video for different consumer groups. This shows that AI is no longer only a back -end
analytics tool. It has become part of the visible communication that consumers see on social media
platforms (Davenport et al., 2020; Huang & Rust, 2021; Mariani & Dwivedi, 2024).

The issue is important because social media videos are processed in a fast and informal
environment. Consumers watch marketing content between entertainment clips, influencer posts,
reviews, personal stories and user -generated content. Therefore, they may not always know
whether the source is a real person, a paid influencer, a virtual influencer, a brand-controlled avatar
or an AI -generated customer. Social media advertising already creates difficulty because
commercial content can be embedded into personal and entertaining formats (Boerman et al., 2017;
De Veirman et al., 2017; Evans et al., 2017). AI -generated video adds another layer because the
visible human cues may also be synthetic.

Marketing videos are persuasive because they use faces, voices, emotions, movement and
social proof. When these cues are generated or modified by AI, consumers may still interpret them
as signs of real experience. For instance, a synthetic customer can loo k like a satisfied buyer, a
virtual influencer can speak like an independent user, and an AI-generated product scene can look
like actual product evidence. Hence, perceived deception may occur even when the advertisement
does not contain an obviously false  sentence. The total impression of the video can still lead
consumers to believe that real human experience or real product performance is being shown
(Campbell et al., 2022; Han et al., 2018; Mustak et al., 2023).

Consumer trust is therefore central to this topic. Trust is commonly linked to expectations
of ability, benevolence and integrity, and it is also an important condition for digital exchange and
online persuasion (Hasan et al., 2021; Mayer et al., 1995; McKnight et al., 2002). In social media
marketing, trust is shaped by source credibility, authenticity, disclosure, relationship cues and
consumers' ability to recognize persuasive intention (Friestad & Wright, 1994; Ohanian, 1990;
Reinikainen et al., 2020; S ingh et al., 2020). AI -generated videos complicate these judgments
because they allow marketers to reproduce the signs of trust without necessarily providing real
human presence or real experience.

Existing studies provide useful insight, but the evidence is fragmented. AI marketing
research explains how AI changes marketing practice and customer experience (Ameen et al.,
2022; Davenport et al., 2020; Mariani et al., 2022; Puntoni et al., 2021). Virtual influencer research
examines authenticity, humanlikeness and trust in synthetic endorsers (Looi & Kahlor , 2024;
Mouritzen et al., 2024; Sands et al., 2022). Disclosure research studies whether consumers
recognize sponsored or AI-created content (Boerman, 2020; Karagür et al., 2022; van der Bend et
al., 2023). Deepfake research discusses the risk that synthetic media can mislead consumers about
identity, endorsement or evidence (Campbell et al., 2022; Mustak et al., 2023). These streams are
related, but they are often discussed separately.

Therefore, this article conducts a PRISMA -guided systematic literature review on AI -

Objectives of the Study

The objectives of this review are:
1. To identify the dominant themes in the literature on AI -generated marketing videos and
related synthetic social media advertising.
2. To examine how these formats influence consumer trust and perceived deception.
3. To evaluate the role of disclosure in reducing or increasing trust concerns.
4. To develop a future research agenda for responsible AI-generated video marketing, especially
in Asian business contexts.

The review asks four research questions. First, what themes dominate the literature on AI-
generated marketing videos and related synthetic social media advertising? Second, how do these
formats influence consumer trust and perceived deception? Third, what role does disclosure play
in reducing or increasing trust concerns? Fourth, what future research agenda can support
responsible AI -generated video marketing, especially in Asian business contexts where social
media commerce, influencer marketing and mobile  video consumption are growing rapidly
(Dwivedi et al., 2021; Joshi et al., 2025; Page et al., 2021).

Literature Review

The first concept supporting this review is consumer trust. Trust can be understood as the
willingness to accept vulnerability based on positive expectations about another party's ability,
benevolence and integrity (Mayer et al., 1995). In digital markets,  trust also depends on the
reliability of technology, privacy expectation, institutional protection and confidence in
information quality (McKnight et al., 2002). In social media advertising, consumers evaluate not
only the brand, but also the source, platform, comments, visual realism and commercial intention
behind the message. This is why trust in AI-generated marketing videos cannot be studied only as
trust in technology. It also involves trust in the brand's representation of people, evidence and
persuasion.

The second concept is perceived deception. In marketing, deception does not only mean
that a direct product claim is false. Deception can also occur when the overall impression of an
advertisement creates a misleading belief about source identity, product evidence, endorsement or
commercial intent. Native advertising research shows that persuasive messages can be more
influential when consumers do not recognize them as advertising (Han et al., 2018). Influencer
marketing studies similarly show that sponsore d content becomes ethically sensitive when
commercial persuasion is hidden inside personal recommendation or entertainment (Evans et al.,
2017; Jhawar et al., 2024). AI -generated video extends this issue because consumers may also
need to know whether the human-looking source or product scene is synthetic.

The persuasion knowledge model explains why disclosure and recognition matter.
Consumers develop knowledge about marketers' motives, tactics and persuasion attempts, and they
use this knowledge to cope with marketing messages (Friestad & Wright, 1994). When persuasion
knowledge is activated, consumers may become more careful and skeptical. Recent work extends
this logic to algorithmic advertising, where consumers differ in their awareness of personalization

and platform influence (Voorveld  et al., 2024; Willemsen et al., 2025). In AI -generated video,
persuasion knowledge may be activated by labels, unnatural voice, synthetic faces or platform
warnings. However, if these cues are hidden, consumers may process the content as human -
generated and later feel deceived when the synthetic nature is known.

The third concept is source credibility. Classic endorsement research identifies expertise,
trustworthiness and attractiveness as important dimensions of source evaluation (Ohanian, 1990).
In social media influencer marketing, credibility is also influence d by authenticity, perceived
similarity, parasocial relationships and engagement with followers (Gross & von Wangenheim,
2022; Hughes et al., 2019; Leung et al., 2022; Reinikainen et al., 2020). Virtual influencers create
a special case. They can be visually consistent, controllable and novel, but consumers may question
whether they can honestly recommend products when they have no real human experience (Batista
da Silva Oliveira & Chimenti, 2021; Gerlich, 2023; Kim et al., 2023; Mouritzen et al., 2024; Mrad
et al., 2025; Sands et al., 2022).

The fourth concept is disclosure. Studies on sponsored content show that disclosure can
increase advertising recognition and persuasion knowledge, but its effect depends on wording,
placement, timing and consumer understanding (Amazeen & Wojdynski, 2020; B oerman et al.,
2017; Evans et al., 2017; Ikonen et al., 2017; van Reijmersdal & van Dam, 2020). Some disclosures
are too vague or too late to help consumers. Other disclosures may create resistance if consumers
interpret the label as a signal of manipulation. This means that AI disclosure should not be treated
as a small technical label. It should be designed as part of the communication because it affects
how consumers interpret the whole video.

The fifth concept is authenticity. Social media marketing often depends on the feeling that
the source is genuine, relatable and personally involved. Studies show that authenticity can support
credibility and purchase intention, while perceived commerciali sm can weaken influencer
effectiveness (Balaban et al., 2022; Balaban & Szambolics , 2022; Pradhan et al., 2023). Virtual
influencer studies suggest that authenticity can be constructed through consistent narrative,
transparency and social presence, but it remains fragile because consumers know that the source
is designed and controlled (Ju et al., 2024; Koles et al., 2024). Therefore, AI-generated marketing
videos may be accepted when they are clearly framed as creative or fictional, but rejected when
they pretend to show spontaneous human experience.

From these concepts, the ethical issue is not simply whether AI is used. AI can support
creative experimentation, translation, editing and personalization. The concern emerges when AI
use changes the beliefs that consumers form about the source, evidence or intention of the message.
For example, a synthetic fashion model may be acceptable when the campaign is openly stylized,
while a synthetic health testimonial may be problematic because it simulates lived experience.
Therefore, responsible AI-generated video marketing requires both creative freedom and careful
transparency (Campbell et al., 2022; Cillo & Rubera, 2025; Hermann, 2022).

Methodology

This study used a PRISMA -guided systematic literature review design. PRISMA was selected
because it requires researchers to explain how records were identified, screened, excluded and
included, rather than presenting the literature as a general narrative (Page et al., 2021). The review
is qualitative rather than meta -analytic. A meta -analysis was not suitable because the included

studies use different stimuli, platforms, product categories, theoretical models and outcome
variables. Therefore, the purpose is to synthesize themes and mechanisms rather than to calculate
a pooled effect size.

The search was conducted on 9 June 2026. The main search sources were Scopus, Google
Scholar, OpenAlex, Crossref and publisher pages. Scopus and Google Scholar were used to check
discoverability and relevance, while OpenAlex and Crossref  were used to verify bibliographic
metadata, DOI information, publication year and journal title. Publisher pages were checked when
article details needed confirmation. This approach was used because the topic is still emerging and
the relevant literature is spread across marketing, advertising, communication, consumer
behaviour and information systems journals.

The search strings combined technology terms, marketing terms, trust/deception terms and
platform terms. Examples included 'AI -generated advertising consumer trust deception social
media', 'deepfake advertising disclosure consumer trust', 'virtual influenc er authenticity trust
disclosure', 'synthetic media advertising ethics', 'AI disclosure advertising consumer responses',
and 'influencer marketing disclosure persuasion knowledge trust'. Studies were included when they
addressed AI -generated advertising, s ynthetic or deepfake advertising, virtual influencers, AI
disclosure, influencer disclosure, consumer trust, perceived deception, authenticity or persuasion
knowledge in digital advertising.

Studies were excluded when they were purely technical deepfake detection papers, general
AI papers without marketing relevance, non -scholarly commentary, records without clear
bibliographic metadata, or papers that discussed trust in unrelated contexts wit hout a transferable
advertising connection. Earlier foundational work was retained when it supported the conceptual
base of the review, especially trust, source credibility, persuasion knowledge and PRISMA
reporting. The initial search produced 700 records . After duplicate removal, 443 records were
screened, 123 reports were assessed for eligibility, and 58 studies were included in the thematic
synthesis.

Results

The reviewed literature shows strong growth after 2020, especially after generative AI became
visible to consumers and marketers. Earlier studies focused mainly on influencer sponsorship
disclosure, native advertising recognition and social media persuasio n. More recent studies

connect these issues with AI -generated ads, virtual influencers, deepfake advertising and
algorithmic persuasion. This indicates that the ethical concern in AI -generated marketing videos
is not entirely new. It extends older problems of hidden persuasion, commercial transparency and
source credibility into a synthetic media environment (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Mariani & Dwivedi,
2024; Waltenrath, 2024).

The first theme is AI-generated advertising as an innovative but ambiguous marketing tool.
AI marketing research argues that AI can transform targeting, customer experience, creativity and
service interaction (Davenport et al., 2020; Huang & Rust, 2021). G enerative AI further allows
marketers to produce many versions of text, image and video content quickly (Banh & Strobel,
2023; Cillo & Rubera, 2025). However, advertising studies show that consumer response depends
on whether AI use is visible, relevant and appropriate. AI-generated content may be accepted when
it improves creativity or usefulness, but it may also reduce trust when consumers feel that the brand
is hiding the production process (Arango et al., 2023; Baek et al., 2026; Jung et al., 2025).

The second theme is virtual influencers and synthetic endorsers. Studies show that virtual
influencers are not simply weaker versions of human influencers. They can be novel, visually
consistent and controllable for brands (Gerlich, 2023; Looi & Kahlor, 20 24; Sands et al., 2022).
Some evidence suggests that virtual influencers can work in prosocial or sustainability contexts
when warmth, expertise and message fit are managed carefully (Ferraro et al., 2024; Gerrath et al.,
2024; Igarashi et al., 2024). However, the same literature shows that consumers question
authenticity, humanlikeness and lived experience (Ju et al., 2024; Koles et al., 2024; Mouritzen et
al., 2024). This shows that synthetic sources can attract attention, but attention does not
automatically become trust.

The third theme is deepfake and synthetic deception. Deepfake research shows that AI can
change who appears to speak, endorse or experience a product (Campbell et al., 2022; Mustak  et
al., 2023). In marketing videos, this is sensitive because voice, face, movement and emotional
expression are strong credibility cues. If a synthetic person looks like a real customer or expert,
consumers may infer real experience. Therefore, deception  risk is not limited to stolen celebrity
identity. It also includes simulated testimonials, artificial product outcomes and fabricated social
proof. This issue is important because short -form social media videos can make synthetic scenes
appear ordinary and believable.

The fourth theme is disclosure. Disclosure research shows that labels can increase
advertising recognition, but the effect is not uniform (Boerman, 2020; Boerman et al., 2017; Evans
et al., 2017). Consumers respond differently depending on whether the disc losure is clear, early,
specific and easy to understand. For AI -generated video, the label 'created with AI' may not be
enough because it does not explain which part of the video is synthetic. Consumers may want to
know whether the person, voice, background, testimonial, product result or recommendation was
generated. Hence, disclosure should be material and specific, especially when AI affects the
consumer's interpretation of evidence or source credibility (van der Bend et al., 2023; van
Reijmersdal & van Dam, 2020).

The fifth theme is consumer trust and resistance. Trust can be supported when AI use is
transparent, useful and consistent with the product context. However, resistance may occur when
consumers perceive manipulation, hidden commercial intent or false intim acy. Studies on
influencer avoidance and deceptive influencer practices show that consumers can respond

negatively when they believe the source is overly commercial or misleading (Ekinci et al., 2025;
Pradhan et al., 2023). In AI -generated videos, this resistance may be stronger if consumers feel
that a brand has used synthetic human cues to imitate friendship, expertise or personal experience
without giving enough information.

A further pattern is that consumers do not evaluate all synthetic sources in the same way.
Studies on virtual influencers show that perceived similarity, social presence, privacy concern,
diversity cues and humanlikeness can change consumer response (Ameen  et al., 2024;
Davlembayeva et al., 2025; Ferraro et al., 2024; Kim & Wang, 2023; Stein et al., 2024). This
indicates that AI -generated video should not be judged only by whether the source is human or
non-human. Consumers also consider whether the source fits the product, whether t he campaign
is transparent, and whether the synthetic character creates a believable but honest communication
setting.

Overall, the findings show a trust -deception tension. AI -generated marketing videos can
support creative efficiency and personalized communication, but these benefits create ethical risk
when consumers cannot determine what is real, who is speaking or whether the experience shown
actually occurred. Therefore, the literature does not support the simple claim that AI -generated
content is always unethical. Rather, ethicality depends on transparency, claim accuracy, source
representation, product risk and respect for consumer autonomy (Campbell et al., 2022; Hermann,
2022; Mustak et al., 2023).

Conclusion and Recommendation

The synthesis suggests that consumer trust in AI -generated marketing videos is shaped by two
judgments. First, consumers judge whether the content is useful, entertaining and relevant. Second,
they judge whether the content is honest about its source and evidence. These judgments can move
in different directions. A video can be creative and enjoyable, but still be distrusted if consumers
believe that the human source or product experience has been fabricated. This explains why AI -
generated marketing video s hould be evaluated through both innovation value and ethical
communication (Friestad & Wright, 1994; Mayer et al., 1995; Ohanian, 1990).

Perceived deception is useful because it captures more than factual accuracy. In synthetic
video marketing, deception can be representational. A video may mislead consumers by making a
virtual person appear to be a real customer, by making a simulated scen e appear to be observed
evidence, or by hiding the commercial purpose behind an entertainment format. This is consistent
with native advertising and influencer disclosure research, where persuasion becomes more
sensitive when consumers cannot recognize the advertisement or the sponsor (Evans et al., 2017;
Han et al., 2018; Jhawar et al., 2024).

Disclosure should therefore not be treated as a simple yes -or-no variable. If disclosure
reduces ambiguity, it can support trust. However, if disclosure only states that AI was used without
explaining the material AI element, it may create more uncertainty. For instance, a label that says
'AI-generated' may make consumers wonder whether the person, voice, product demonstration or
testimonial is synthetic. This suggests that AI disclosure should be specific enough to answer the
consumer's likely question. Th e more humanlike or evidence -like the synthetic element is, the
stronger the disclosure should be (Boerman et al., 2017; Karagür et al., 2022; Qiu et al., 2025).

The proposed framework in Figure 2 summarizes the main pathway. AI -generated video
cues can create four types of ambiguity: source ambiguity, evidence ambiguity, commercial intent
ambiguity and data/algorithm ambiguity. These ambiguities influence consumer  interpretation
through persuasion knowledge, perceived authenticity, source credibility and perceived risk. When
source, evidence and intent are clear, trust is more likely. When synthetic cues imply false human
experience or hidden persuasion, perceived deception is more likely. Boundary conditions such as
product risk, disclosure quality, humanlikeness, AI literacy and platform context shape the strength
of these outcomes (Ameen et al., 2024; Liyanaarachchi et al., 2024; Voorveld et al., 2024).

The framework also shows why product category matters. AI-generated video may be more
acceptable in fashion, entertainment, gaming and low -risk consumer goods because consumers
may interpret the content as stylized creativity. However, the same practice ma y be more
problematic in health, finance, education, charity or sustainability marketing because consumers
rely more heavily on trust and evidence. A synthetic patient, investor, student or charity beneficiary
can create a stronger deception risk than a synthetic fashion model. Hence, firms should use a risk-
based approach rather than applying the same AI policy to all marketing videos ( Gerrath et al.,
2024; Hermann, 2022).

The findings also extend source credibility theory. In traditional endorsement, source
credibility is attached to the perceived expertise, trustworthiness and attractiveness of a person
(Ohanian, 1990). In AI -generated marketing videos, credibility also de pends on the brand's
governance of synthetic representation. Consumers may ask whether the brand has used AI
honestly, whether the synthetic source is clearly identified, and whether the message implies
experience that cannot exist. Therefore, credibility is not only a characteristic of the endorser. It is
also a characteristic of the brand's disclosure and accountability.

The findings further extend persuasion knowledge. Consumers are not only asking whether
a message is trying to persuade them. They are also asking how the message was produced and
whether the visible evidence is real. This additional layer changes the cons umer's coping task. If
consumers do not know that a video is synthetic, their persuasion knowledge may not be activated.
If disclosure activates suspicion without enough explanation, the advertisement may be evaluated
negatively even if the product claim i s accurate. Therefore, future research should examine
disclosure meaning, not only disclosure presence (Rahmani, 2023; Voorveld et al., 2024).

Future research should test realistic short -form videos rather than relying only on written
scenarios or static images. AI-generated video includes movement, voice, music, facial expression
and editing style. These features may influence trust differently from static content. Experiments
can manipulate source type, disclosure wording, disclosure timing, product risk and
humanlikeness. Qualitative studies can also examine how consumers explain their trust, suspicion
or discomfort after watching synthetic video advertisements. This will help the field move closer
to real platform experience (Baek et al., 2026; Barta et al., 2023; Jung et al., 2025).

Future research should also consider Asia more directly. Many Asian markets have high
social media use, mobile commerce growth and strong influencer economies. At the same time,
Asian consumers are not homogeneous. Cultural norms around authority, celebrit y, technology,
privacy and advertising disclosure may shape how consumers interpret AI -generated marketing
videos. Comparative studies across Asian countries can therefore explain whether trust and
deception judgments differ by market, platform and product  category. This is important because
global AI marketing tools may be deployed locally without enough understanding of consumer
interpretation in specific Asian contexts (Dwivedi et al., 2021; Joshi et al., 2025).

The findings have several implications for business practice. First, firms should treat AI -
generated marketing videos as a trust-sensitive communication tool, not only as a low-cost content
production tool. Many Asian markets have active social media comme rce, livestream selling,
influencer marketing and mobile -first consumer behaviour. In this environment, consumers may
make fast judgments based on short videos, creator cues and peer signals. If brands use AI -
generated people, voices or product scenes with out clear explanation, consumers may later feel
that the brand has hidden important information. Therefore, transparency should be designed
before the campaign is published, not added only after complaints arise.

Second, Asian businesses should use risk-based disclosure. For low-risk creative uses such
as background editing, language translation or visual enhancement, a simple label may be
sufficient depending on platform rules. For higher -risk uses such as virtual  experts, synthetic
testimonials, cloned voices, simulated product results or AI -generated customer stories, stronger
disclosure is needed. The disclosure should explain what is synthetic and why it matters. A label
such as 'AI-generated video' may be too general. A clearer label may state that the spokesperson
is virtual, the product scene is simulated, or the voice is AI -generated. This helps consumers
understand the message before they rely on it.

Finally, businesses should invest in AI literacy for marketing teams and consumers.
Marketing teams need clear policies on disclosure, consent, claim substantiation and synthetic
representation. Consumers also need understandable platform labels and brand-level explanations.
In the long run, responsible AI-generated video marketing may become a competitive advantage.
Brands that are transparent and accountable can still enjoy creative efficiency, but they are less
likely to damage consumer trust. Hence, the  best use of AI in Asian business is not simply to
produce more content, but to produce persuasive content that consumers can understand, evaluate
and trust.  For managers, the implication is also operational. AI video governance should be
included in campaign briefing, agency contracts and approval checklists. The checklist can ask
whether the video uses synthetic people, whether consent is required, whether th e product result
is simulated, whether the disclosure appears on screen, and whether the message may mislead
vulnerable consumers. This turns ethical AI use from a general principle into a practical marketing
control that Asian firms can apply across campaigns.
