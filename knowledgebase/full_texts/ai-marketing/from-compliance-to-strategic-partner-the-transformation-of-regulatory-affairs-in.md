Abstract
The role of Regulatory Affairs (RA) within local pharmaceutical affiliates is evolving from a predominantly compliance-
driven support function to a strategic partner in drug development and patient access. Traditionally, local RA focused on
dossier preparation, regulatory compliance, liaison with national authorities, and maintenance of marketing authorisations,
largely executing global plans downstream of clinical development. The increasing complexity of novel therapies (including
advanced therapy medicinal products), evolving EU regulatory frameworks, and the broader Medical Affairs (MA) 2030
vision now require RA to be integrated earlier and to operate as a more proactive and strategic partner across functions.
This article describes the transformation of local Marketing Companies Regulatory Affairs (MCRA) teams at AstraZeneca
(AZ) in Europe, structured around three pillars: (1) Launch Excellence, embedding local regulatory insight into early-asset,
launch, and brand planning to support evidence generation, risk anticipation and implementation-ready launches; (2) Exter-
nal Engagement and Advocacy, strengthening strategic dialogue with national Health Authorities and contributing local
perspectives to policy discussions on key EU initiatives such as the Health Technology Assessment (HTA) Regulation, New
Pharmaceutical Legislation, Clinical Trials Regulation, Biotech Act, Critical Medicines Act, digital labelling, accelerated
pathways and adaptive licensing; and (3) Digitalisation and Process Optimisation, leveraging standardised tools, RA platforms
and emerging artificial intelligence (AI) solutions to simplify ways of working, improve alignment, and support more efficient
submission and lifecycle management. Central to this transformation is “One-Team” Local–Global RA culture of AZ, opera-
tionalised through the MCRA Operating Model. Local MCRA professionals are fully embedded in affiliate cross-functional
teams while maintaining bi-directional collaboration with Global RA, enabling local insights to inform global strategy and
helping ensure that global decisions are implementable at the country level. To monitor progress, a contribution-based
measurement framework is proposed, combining quantitative and qualitative indicators aligned with the three transformation
pillars and the “One-Team” model. The framework focuses on predictability, launch readiness, environment-shaping activities
and organisational effectiveness, recognising that regulatory outcomes result from cross-functional decision making across
the product lifecycle. Overall, early and strategic RA involvement, structured around the three pillars and underpinned by a
“One-Team” culture, helps accelerate patient access to innovative medicines across Europe.

broader Medical Affairs (MA) discipline has undergone a

that drives medical innovation, evidence generation, value
demonstration, and patient-centric solutions [3 , 4]. In
parallel, RA is evolving beyond traditional compliance and
registration tasks toward a more strategic and integrative role
within this new MA ecosystem.
The EU regulatory environment for life science innovators
is increasingly complex, with recent and upcoming
regulatory reforms across the medicines lifecycle that
introduce new layers of legislation and compliance demands
with growing emphasis on sustainability and digital health
frameworks [5, 6]. The MA 2030 paradigm in an evolving
healthcare regulatory and payer landscape demands strategic
planning from early development stages rather than routine,
late-stage dossier submission [7 –9]. This transformation to
adapt to the new healthcare ecosystem scenario is crucial
for the development of any medicine, but unique regulatory
challenges are encountered for complex medicines, such
as advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs), or
combination products and companion diagnostics [10].
Under these conditions, regulatory strategies must address
the needs of regulators, payers, HTA agencies, and broader
market-access stakeholders [11]. Close co-ordination within
cross-functional teams is therefore essential to align on evi-
dence generation, regulatory strategy, and value proposi-
tions. Particular attention should be directed to real-world
outcomes that underpin the viability of innovative products.
Initiatives such as DARWIN EU facilitate the integration of
real-world evidence (RWE) into decision making and create
opportunities for RA to proactively inform health-outcomes
assessment and real-time benefit–risk evaluations [16]. In
addition, implementation of Regulation (EU) 2021/2282
on HTA is reshaping the evidence-generation landscape
across Europe. The introduction of Joint Clinical Assess-
ments (JCAs) requires earlier planning and increased co-
ordination of evidence packages intended to support both
regulatory and HTA decision making. Although regulatory
and HTA assessments remain distinct processes with dif-
ferent objectives and decision criteria, the increasingly co-
ordinated timelines surrounding market authorisation and
HTA activities reinforce the need for closer collaboration
among RA, HTA, Market Access, and other cross-functional
stakeholders.
As portfolios pivot to biologics, advanced modalities, and
targeted therapies, approvals increasingly run through the
European Medicines Agency (EMA) centralised procedure,
shifting focus from national filings for well-established small
molecules to co-ordinating complex, centralised approvals.
This evolution demands stronger cross-functional engage-
ment and proactive alignment with regulatory frameworks
from the earliest stages of development [12]. In parallel,
digitalisation and artificial intelligence (AI) are transforming
RA workflows [13]. Structured content authoring, advanced
data-management systems, cloud-based submissions, and
AI-driven document generation can streamline processes,
but require expertise in digital governance, validation, and
compliance, particularly under new EU legislation on AI and
data use in healthcare [14, 15].
Against this backdrop, RA departments in AstraZeneca
(AZ) affiliates across Europe have undergone a strategic
transformation: from predominantly reactive, dossier-
focused units to strategic business partners embedded in
cross-functional teams from early clinical development,
and closely collaborating with global RA teams to support
local product launch readiness while enabling consistent
implementation of regulatory strategies across increasingly
complex and evolving regulatory frameworks. This trans-
formation has been driven not only by external changes,
including HTA reform, increasing regulatory complexity and
digitalisation, but also by internal business needs, such as
reducing organisational silos, improving launch predictabil-
ity, strengthening evidence planning, and ensuring greater

2  The Journey of Regulatory Affairs (RA)
Transformation
In AZ, the evolution was mediated through the European
Marketing Companies Regulatory Affairs (MCRA)
framework, which embeds local RA teams earlier in
development, strengthens external shaping, and facilitates
alignment of local and global strategies [16]. As a
result, today’s RA function plays a central role in our
multidisciplinary teams, contributing to high-level decision
making while ensuring that regulatory frameworks remain
fit-for-purpose in an increasingly complex ecosystem
[17]. This evolution reflects a shift from a predominantly
sequential operating model, in which local affiliates
primarily implemented global regulatory strategies, to
a more integrated model in which local and global RA
teams contribute complementary insights throughout
the development and product lifecycle. In the traditional
model, interactions between RA, HTA, Market Access,
and other functions frequently occurred at different stages
of development, creating a risk of fragmented evidence
planning and delayed identification of local implementation
challenges. By contrast, the current model promotes earlier
alignment across functions and geographies, enabling local
considerations to be incorporated proactively into global
planning.
From a business perspective, this transformation is not
only a response to external regulatory change but also an
enabler of launch readiness, evidence alignment, and organi-
sational efficiency. Earlier involvement of local RA helps
anticipate regulatory risks, identify implementation barri-
ers, and facilitate co-ordinated planning across functions,
thereby reducing avoidable surprises during key develop-
ment and launch milestones.
To ensure this transformation is sustained, communica-
tion between local and global RA teams has been reinforced,
enabling the translation of local insights into global strategy
and vice versa. For the orchestration of true pharmaceuti-
cal success, it is crucial that global and local MCRA teams
operate with strategic partnership, sharing insights and feed-
back in a bi-directional way (Fig.  1). Building this align-
ment bridge enhances regulatory agility, supports a broader
evidence-based dialogue with authorities, and increases the
likelihood that innovative therapies are approved efficiently
and reimbursed sustainably. The One-Team model seeks to
create a structured framework for bi-directional alignment,
ensuring that strategic decisions benefit from both global

2.1  Launch Excellence
MCRA teams contribute to launch excellence by embedding
local regulatory strategy into early development and pre-
launch planning. From the early-asset stage, they collaborate
with Global RA and local cross-functional teams to share
key regulatory insights, such as regulatory strategies,
indication scenarios, and associated risks. MCRA teams
conduct horizon scanning and regulatory landscape
mapping, providing decision-grade input to local Launch
and Brand teams. Such decision-grade input may include
assessments of regulatory precedents, potential indication
scenarios, anticipated health authority expectations, key
regulatory risks, probability-of-approval considerations,
implementation challenges, and potential implications
for evidence-generation strategies. These insights support
informed cross-functional decision making and help ensure
that launch planning incorporates both global development
objectives and local market realities. To de-risk the path to
launch, MCRA performs continuous regulatory intelligence
and precedent tracking, evaluates regulatory feasibility of
accelerated or expedited tools and early-access pathways,
and informs global regulatory strategies and health authority
(HA) interactions. In parallel, MCRA works closely with
local HTA, Market Access, and Medical Affairs colleagues
to provide a regulatory perspective on future evidence
requirements and value-related considerations. This
collaboration supports the integration of HTA perspectives,
including Population, Intervention, Comparator, and
Outcomes (PICO) requirements, into evidence-planning
discussions, and helps identify potential local evidence needs
relevant for future reimbursement and HTA assessments.
Leveraging deep knowledge of local HAs, including areas
of expertise and interest, precedents, and process nuances,
MCRA helps shape national Scientific Advice plans with
local competent authorities, acts as a liaison in these early
HA interactions, and contributes local considerations,
providing local context, anticipating questions and
interpreting local nuances, reducing uncertainty, and
aligning expectations from the outset. Where applicable, and
in particular in countries where the competent regulatory
authority also has HTA responsibilities, a similar approach
supports national joint scientific advice or consultation
involving regulatory and HTA perspectives.
As dossiers progress through the EU centralised
procedure, MCRA ensures local embedding of global
strategy, liaising with Global RA to provide local cross-
functional teams with key regulatory highlights. Particular
focus is placed on questions of strategic relevance for the
business, such as uncertainties around indication wording
or target populations, response strategies and their
associated risks, probability-of-approval assessments,
revised best/base/worst-case timelines, and, where
applicable, orphan drug status and potential outcomes.
This strategic dialogue allows MCRA to identify
regulatory strengths and gaps that must be reflected in
local launch plans, both to create market opportunities and
to mitigate risks, always keeping patients at the centre of
decision making.
Strategic thinking is also applied to the more operational
dimensions of launch. MCRA plays a critical role in planning
and overseeing product-information translations, Quality
Review of Documents (QRD)-compliant localisation of
Module 1, and the management of labelling-related activities
such as artworks, mock-ups, and blue-box requirements.
These tasks, although operational in nature, are tightly linked
to regulatory strategy and timelines. By co-ordinating them in
an integrated way with Global RA and local cross-functional
teams, MCRA helps ensure that the approved product
information can be implemented smoothly and consistently
across markets, supporting timely and high-quality launch
execution.
Post-approval, MCRA co-ordinates the national phase
activities (e.g., national code application and approval,
national regulatory notifications, product information
publication, adapted Summary of Product Characteristics
(SmPC) for promo materials), orchestrates HA interactions,
and leads issue management to keep timelines intact. In
parallel, MCRA collaborates with HTA and Market Access
functions to ensure that regulatory positions, approved
labelling, and evidence packages remain coherent with local
HTA and price and reimbursement (P&R) expectations. In
this context, ‘HTA-relevant evidence’ refers to evidence
components that may influence future HTA assessments,
such as patient populations, comparators, endpoints, labelling
considerations, and evidence-generation strategies, rather than
activities related to pricing decisions themselves. Finally, by
partnering with Quality and Supply, MCRA secures launch
readiness, ensuring compliant product information and the
timely implementation of post-approval variations in each
market.

Building strategic alignment bridges across global and local
perspectives enhances regulatory agility. Local MCRA
professionals must operate as true strategic partners within
the local healthcare ecosystem, strengthen engagement with
national HAs and Trade Associations, and help shape the

regulatory environment, aligned with global strategic goals
and patient-centric principles. Beyond supporting timely
access, patient centricity in this context refers to ensuring
that regulatory strategies appropriately consider unmet
medical needs, accessibility of innovation, and the practical
implications of regulatory decisions for patients throughout
the product lifecycle. Patient centricity has become a
defining pillar of the RA transformation [18], with MCRA
teams orienting regulatory strategy toward timely, equitable

This external-engagement model differs from more
traditional approaches in which interactions with HAs
were predominantly focused on product-specific regulatory
procedures. While global regulatory functions have long
contributed to policy discussions through organisations
such as EFPIA and other international forums, the evolving
role of local MCRA expands participation in environment
shaping, enabling affiliates to contribute local insights
regarding implementation challenges, healthcare-system
needs, and patient access considerations.
In this model, MCRA leads strategic engagement with
local HAs to both de-risk development and accelerate
access in four ways: (1) strengthening relationships with
local regulators based on trust and scientific dialogue; (2)
sharing pipeline visibility and milestones with HAs through
horizon scanning, so that workload can be anticipated and
resources planned efficiently; (3) conducting structured
advocacy to address emerging regulatory challenges
(e.g., innovative endpoints vs. classical surrogates where
clinically meaningful) and co-create practical solutions
with local HAs, articulating the real-world regulatory
impact of legislative changes from the sponsor perspective
to inform HA decision making [19]; and (4) advocating
to shape the design and implementation of legislative
reforms. In this latter role, MCRA works hand in hand
with Global Regulatory Policy and Legal and Government
Affairs to contribute to public consultations, position
papers, and multi-stakeholder forums, highlighting where
proposed rules or guidance could unintentionally delay
access, increase administrative burden or undermine EU
competitiveness. Examples of such activities may include
contributions to discussions related to the implementation
of the Clinical Trials Regulation, the EU HTA Regulation,
New Pharmaceutical Legislation reform, electronic
product information (ePI), digital labelling initiatives, and
specialised product frameworks. Through this co-ordinated
approach, MCRA helps ensure that evolving regulatory
frameworks remain patient centred, science based, and
compatible with sustainable, timely access to innovation.
Ultimately, this patient-first, external engagement
approach, built on continuous, transparent interaction with
HAs, across horizon scanning, advice, and advocacy, helps
shape a regulatory environment that is both fit-for-purpose
and responsive to the urgency and complexity of modern
therapeutic innovation, and complements internal launch
excellence by enabling earlier, more predictable availabil-
ity of innovation for patients with high unmet needs [20].
2.3  Digital Transformation
Digitalisation is further accelerating this evolution [21].
Routine tasks such as formatting, document management,
and process tracking are increasingly automated through
our structured content-management systems and cloud-
based platforms. This shift, which is aimed to drive
improvements on systems and tools for the benefit of the
MCRA community, frees up regulatory talent to focus on
strategic high-impact work. The MCRA model reinforces
this transition, with an explicit emphasis on agility, speed,
and efficiency driven by AI, electronic product information
(ePI), and next-generation submissions [16]. AI is reshaping
RA by expediting authoring, technical quality checks,
regulatory intelligence, and response management across
the dossier lifecycle. Format modernisation and cloud
collaboration is beginning to demonstrate the amplified AI
impact on end-to-end digital submissions at scale.
At local level, this shift is increasingly supported by
automation and AI embedded in day-to-day operational
work, including AI-enabled translation workflows to
streamline document management and structured support
for artwork review and implementation activities. Process
transformation underpinned by technology provides a
robust framework to accelerate high-quality regulatory
submissions, while the move toward end-to-end labelling
automation improves efficiency, reduces the potential
for avoidable errors, and enables faster decision making
for labelling implementation. In parallel, digitalisation
supports better regulatory intelligence and governance (e.g.,
consolidated views of EU vs. national requirements, early
alerts on changes) and more integrated access to information
through searchable repositories and connected systems.
Evidence ecosystems are also modernising through
DARWIN EU by providing a federated RWE capability
to support regulatory decision making across the product
lifecycle. In parallel, implementation of the EU HTA
Regulation and its JCAs is driving earlier consideration of
evidence requirements across regulatory and HTA pathways.
While regulatory and HTA assessments remain distinct
and independent processes, increasingly co-ordinated
development timelines encourage earlier cross-functional
planning to ensure that evidence packages can appropriately
address the needs of both decision-making frameworks. In
this context, AI may support literature review, evidence
synthesis, and RWD curation activities, helping teams
manage increasingly complex evidence ecosystems without
replacing scientific or regulatory judgement [22– 24].

Regulators themselves are adopting AI to improve
review throughput and consistency [25]. In June 2025,
the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) launched
‘Elsa’, a secure, agency-wide generative-AI assistant that
helps scientific reviewers summarise protocols and adverse
events, compare labels, and prioritise work, signalling
institutional acceptance of AI as a force multiplier for
regulatory science while maintaining expert oversight. A
prior announcement documented completion of the FDA’s
first AI-assisted scientific review pilot [26]. Across the
Atlantic, EU regulators are moving in the same direction.
In March 2024, EMA introduced Scientific Explorer, an
AI-enabled knowledge-mining tool for the EU regulators
[27]. Scientific Explorer allows focused, precise search
across network sources to support decision making and
streamline processes, with initial releases centred on
scientific advice procedures.
EMA has also published guiding principles for the safe,
responsible, and effective use of large language models by
European medicines regulatory network staff, recognizing
their potential to help regulators process extensive
documentation, automate data mining, and optimise routine
administrative tasks, while maintaining human oversight
and accountability [ 28]. In Spain, the national medicines
agency is piloting an internal AI tool (IPTMaker) to support
drafting therapeutic positioning reports that inform HTA
and reimbursement decisions, further illustrating how AI
is starting to permeate not only company-side regulatory
work but also the assessment processes of authorities and
payers [29].
Use of digital technologies is also creating an opportunity
to improve the delivery of patient care and the patient
experience, as well as support greater health literacy through
ePI and leaflets. The transition to ePI enables faster access
to the latest regulatory-approved and scientifically validated
product information, with improved accessibility in multiple
languages and searchable formats for patients, carers, and
healthcare professionals. Beyond improved information
access, ePI also accelerates bringing new medicines to
market following approval by reducing reliance on printed
leaflets and is a tangible step toward more sustainable
healthcare. Taken together, these developments translate
into tangible, near-term benefits for sponsors, and MCRA
aligns goals and defines digitalisation strategies to maximise
contribution to this pillar of transformation.
3  One‑Team Culture and Capability Building
The transformation of MCRA into a strategic function
depends on a genuine One-Team culture. This unifying
culture has been cultivated to align MCRA teams across
geographies around shared objectives and patient-centric
values. Since global regulatory strategies drive develop-
ment programs, while local market realities (including
P&R processes, clinical use patterns, and healthcare sys-
tem nuances) demand locally tailored approaches, local
affiliates must interpret global dossiers in the context
of national regulatory environments, HTAs, and payer
evidence needs, and make them work for their markets,
thereby contributing to launch excellence without com-
promising alignment to global timelines and strategy
[30]. In addition to supporting implementation activi-
ties, local MCRA teams increasingly contribute strategic
insights regarding how regulatory decisions may influence
future HTA assessments and reimbursement discussions.
Although MCRA does not perform HTA evaluations or
define pricing strategies, close collaboration with HTA
and Market Access colleagues enables the regulatory
implications of comparator selection, target populations,
endpoints, evidence-generation plans, and labelling out-
comes to be considered proactively throughout develop-
ment and launch planning. This collaborative approach
helps reduce the risk of fragmented decision making and
ensures that evidence-generation activities are informed
by both regulatory and downstream access considerations
from an early stage. MCRA informs local P&R interfaces
through HTA-relevant evidence and labelling predict-
ability. In this context, HTA-relevant evidence refers to
evidence elements that may influence future HTA assess-
ments—such as patient populations, comparators, end-
points, regulatory precedents, and anticipated labelling
outcomes—rather than activities related to pricing nego-
tiations themselves. This underscores the critical transi-
tion in RA from executing global directives to providing
strategic insight and direction, bridging global strategy
and local implementation [7 ].
In this context, collaboration means that local MCRA is
systematically integrated into cross-functional governance
(e.g., early-asset, launch and brand forums), and that
Global RA and MCRA work with shared priorities,
common tools, and aligned timelines. Information flows
in both directions: global strategies, scientific positions,
and procedural updates are translated into clear, actionable
guidance for affiliates, while local intelligence on HA
expectations, HTA practice, and implementation hurdles
is channelled back to global teams to refine development
and submission plans (Fig.  1). This represents a significant
evolution from traditional operating models, where
communication between global and local functions was
often centred on procedural execution. The One-Team
approach promotes continuous bi-directional exchange
throughout development and commercialisation, enabling
local experience and implementation considerations to
influence strategic decisions earlier in the process. This
ongoing dialogue underpins the ‘One Local–Global

New capabilities have been implemented specifically
to operationalise our One-Team culture, focusing on
building a robust portfolio. Key skills include digital
savviness (data, automation, AI governance), regulatory
intelligence and horizon scanning, strategic and HTA-
informed thinking, evidence translation (RWE, endpoints,
comparators), advanced stakeholder engagement with HAs
and HTA bodies, as well as communication, cross-functional
networking, agility, change leadership, and disciplined
program and risk management. New collaborative ways of
working among MCRA teams have been introduced, notably
through the strategic project and product CHAMPION
roles, which foster the RA global–local relationships and
strengthen horizontal collaboration across affiliates.
The BRAVO role acts as a product-specific ambassador
between the Global Regulatory and EU MCRA teams
from early development up to EU approval. BRAVO
ensures a streamlined information flow between Global
and Local RA, builds comprehensive knowledge about
the assigned project (including the global regulatory
strategy), and shares this expertise with MCRA teams,
establishes and leads an MCRA network to discuss local
launch excellence topics related to the project, and supports
Global RA with in-depth knowledge of MC needs for
local launches in EU markets. In doing so, BRAVO helps
align expectations, reduce information asymmetry, and
keep local teams equipped to support cross-functional
launch readiness. Beyond information exchange, BRAVO
also contributes to reinforcing alignment across the three
pillars of transformation—launch excellence, external
engagement, and digital transformation—ensuring that
local implementation perspectives are consistently reflected
throughout the product lifecycle. Complementing BRAVO,
a series of MCRA project CHAMPION roles (e.g., for
HTA/JCA, chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T)
therapies, genetically modified organisms (GMOs), and
radioconjugates) provide cross-market expertise in high-
complexity areas. These Champions interpret evolving
guidelines, map country-specific requirements, consolidate
regulatory intelligence into shared dashboards, lead
training and best-practice exchange, and identify risks and
opportunities from an MCRA perspective. They also act
as strategic partners to Global RA and Global Regulatory
Policy, feeding local regulatory insights into global process
guides and toolkits, and contributing to consultations on
JCAs and specialised product legislation.
Together, the One-Team culture catalysed by BRAVO
and CHAMPION roles creates a structured collaboration
framework that keeps local and global RA closely con-
nected, ensuring that regulatory strategies are both globally
coherent and locally implementable, and that the collective
RA function can safeguard compliance while enabling faster,
more predictable access to medicines for patients across
Europe.
4  Monitoring the Evolution: Measuring
Success
In the context of transforming RA from a predominantly
support-oriented function to a strategic partner, measuring
success is essential. However, assessing the impact of
RA transformation presents an important methodological
challenge. Regulatory approvals, successful launches,
reimbursement outcomes, and patient access are inherently
the result of cross-functional collaboration. Consequently,
direct attribution of these outcomes to RA alone would not
adequately reflect the collective nature of decision making
across the product lifecycle.
For this reason, a contribution-based measurement
approach is proposed, focusing on how RA supports strategic
decision making, predictability, launch readiness, external
engagement, and organisational effectiveness, rather than
attempting to establish direct causality between regulatory
activities and final business outcomes.
The proposed framework is structured around the three
pillars of transformation described throughout this article—
launch excellence, external engagement and advocacy, and
digital transformation and process optimization—together
with a specific set of indicators designed to assess the
effectiveness of the One-Team Local–Global operating
model (Table 1).
Launch excellence indicators evaluate the extent to
which MCRA is successfully embedded into development
and launch planning activities. Measurement focuses on the
timing and quality of regulatory input throughout the product
lifecycle, including participation in early asset discussions,
delivery of decision-grade regulatory assessments,
integration of local regulatory and HTA perspectives into
evidence planning and contribution to Health Authority
engagement strategies.
Given the variability of regulatory procedures across
products and therapeutic areas, success is evaluated primar-
ily through predictability rather than speed alone. Accord-
ingly, indicators include the alignment between anticipated
and final regulatory outcomes, the identification and man-
agement of potential regulatory risks, and the accuracy
of milestone forecasting. Together with launch-readiness
measures and stakeholder feedback, these indicators help
assess whether regulatory input is enabling more informed

set of indicators is used to evaluate its effectiveness. These
measures assess the quality of bi-directional communica-

qualitative evidence that complements quantitative

the organisation is successfully translating local knowledge
into global strategy while ensuring that global decisions
remain implementable at the affiliate level.

a holistic and contribution-based approach. By aligning
measurement with the three pillars of transformation and
the One-Team operating model, AstraZeneca seeks to
evaluate not only what RA does, but also how effectively
it contributes to predictability, launch excellence, external
engagement, digital innovation, and cross-functional
collaboration. Together, these indicators provide a realistic
assessment of the ongoing transformation of RA into
a strategic partner supporting timely patient access to
innovation.
5  Discussion: Future Challenges
and Opportunities
As RA matures into a strategic function, anticipating and
adapting to legislative and market shifts is essential. A major
inflection point is the EU HTA Regulation 2021/2282 [23],
which mandates JCAs for key therapeutic areas: oncology,
ATMPs from January 2025, expanding to orphan drugs
in 2028 and all centrally authorised medicines by 2030.
This paradigm shift requires earlier and more co-ordinated
planning of evidence-generation activities and closer
alignment between regulatory and market access functions
at both local and global levels, particularly as Member States
contribute to the JCA conducted in parallel with the EMA
evaluation process, while recognising that regulatory and
HTA assessments remain distinct frameworks with different
objectives and decision criteria.
In parallel, the ongoing reform of the EU pharmaceutical
legislation [31] will reshape development, authorisation,
and lifecycle obligations, with implications for data-
protection periods, unmet-need definitions, environmental
requirements, supply continuity, and post-authorisation
evidence. This is happening in an environment where
the European Commission continues to create new, often
horizontal, regulations (including those governing data
and AI), further increasing the pace and complexity of
change. Here, MCRA, working hand in hand with Global
Regulatory Policy, and in close co-ordination with Legal
and Government Affairs, plays a critical advocacy role
engaging HAs and policymakers to clarify the real-
world impact of proposed rules not only on sponsors but
also on EU competitiveness and, crucially, on patient
access. This structured policy engagement helps surface
practical solutions (e.g., workable evidence expectations,
proportionality of post-authorisation obligations,
interoperable digital labelling/ePI), ensuring that reforms
are both scientifically rigorous and implementation
feasible. This role reflects the increasing contribution of
local affiliates to the interpretation and implementation
of regulatory reforms, complementing the policy-shaping
activities traditionally led through global regulatory
organisations and industry associations.
Beyond Europe, evolving global policies (including
accelerated and reliance pathways) present opportunities
and risks. Whether aligning to changing HTA expecta -
tions or adapting to supply-related frameworks, RA must
maintain a global perspective while addressing regional
nuances [32]. To navigate this landscape, teams need robust
regulatory intelligence to anticipate change rather than react:

continuous monitoring of guidance, competitor moves, and
ecosystem signals. Internally, self-assessment frameworks
that measure capability maturity (e.g., regulatory intelli-
gence, payer-evidence planning, RWE, patient-centric evi -
dence) enable iterative refinement of strategy and alignment
with the MA 2030 vision.
Local-global collaboration will be a critical enabler of
agility and responsiveness. Strengthening ties between
national affiliates and global regulatory peers ensures
that local insights are factored into global strategy,
while maintaining alignment with overarching corporate
ambitions. The One-Team cultural ethos (shared priorities,
performance indicators, mutual accountability, and shared
governance mechanisms) is central to enable rapid adaptation
as regulatory structures and healthcare expectations evolve.
This is key to transforming management capabilities and
accelerating organisational response to new legislation or
policy demands. By embedding flexibility in governance,
mindsets, and ways of working, and co-ordination into
the global-local collaboration organisational design, RA
can transition more seamlessly as regulatory frameworks
advance.
Finally, this transformation requires capability
development: upskilling in innovative regulatory science,
digital tools and data/AI governance, HTA literacy, and
multi-stakeholder communication. This includes practical
capability to work with AI-based tools in areas such
as regulatory intelligence, document authoring, signal
detection, and RWE analysis, while ensuring compliance
with emerging AI-specific regulations. Persistent challenges
such as medicine availability and supply chain disruptions
further underscore the need for co-ordinated RA–Supply
actions (early notification, emergency authorisations,
contingency labelling) to mitigate patient impact [33].
6  Conclusion
The transformation of RA from a traditionally compliance-
driven function to a strategic enabler marks a pivotal shift in
the pharmaceutical industry. This evolution has been driven
by both external factors—including increasing regulatory
complexity, HTA reform and digitalisation—and internal
needs for greater cross-functional alignment, launch readi-
ness, and organisational agility. The evolving landscape,
shaped by new HTA frameworks, digitalisation, AI, and con-
tinuous regulatory reform, demands that RA teams remain
agile, collaborative, strategic, and patient focused. By pro-
actively aligning with global strategies while responding to
local healthcare needs, regulatory professionals help ensure
that innovation reaches patients effectively and sustainably.
Today, regulatory teams in AZ are essential contributors to
product development, working increasingly through three core
pillars: launch excellence, external engagement, and digital/
process optimisation. Through the One-Team Local–Global
RA model, these pillars are connected through structured col-
laboration, shared governance and bi-directional exchange of
insights across functions and geographies. The transformation
described in this article highlights the potential value of fos-
tering cross-functional integration and investing in strategic
capabilities within a One-Team Local–Global RA culture to
support predictability, launch readiness, and organisational
effectiveness across the product lifecycle.
Looking forward, the success of RA will depend on its
ability to anticipate change, shape policy and implementation,
and continue building trust with authorities and stakeholders.
The contribution-based framework proposed in this article
provides a structured approach to monitor the evolution of the
three pillars of transformation and the effectiveness of the One-
Team Local–Global operating model over time. Continued
monitoring of both operational and strategic indicators will
be important to assess the maturity and effectiveness of this
evolving operating model over time. In doing so, RA will
remain a cornerstone in delivering meaningful innovation to
patients across diverse and dynamic healthcare systems.
