ABSTRACT:
Family-owned agricultural small businesses have a vital role in rural economies by providing
employment, ensuring food security, and promoting community stability. However, these enterprises
frequently face significant challenges, including limited resourc es, restricted market access, and
concerns regarding long -term financial sustainability. In this context, innovation, specifically digital
innovation, emerges as a pivotal mechanism for enhancing economic resilience within small -scale
agricultural family enterprises. This study investigates the role of youth, particularly students enrolled
in entrepreneurial education programs within an agricultural technological high school, as catalysts of
digital innovation in family-owned agricultural micro and small enterprises. It also focuses on low-cost
and accessible digital solutions, including social media marketing, online sales channels, and basic
digital management tools, which are particularly suited for small agricultural businesses with limited
financial resources. Employing a quantitative, cross-sectional pilot design, data were gathered through
a structured, self -administered questionnaire from nine adult students (N=9, aged 18 and above)
enrolled in entrepreneurial education classes at a dually funded (Min istry of Education and Ministry
of Agriculture and Rural Development) technological agricultural high school in a rural area of Ilfov
County, Romania, who are actively involved in their family-owned agricultural businesses. The analysis
explores the degree of youth engagement in digital innovation activities and examines the perceived
impact of these innovations on financial sustainability, market visibility, cost-effectiveness, and overall
business resilience. Given the small pilot sample, findings are preliminary and exploratory rather than
confirmatory. Results suggest that young people may have an important influence in initiating and
implementing digital innovations within their families' businesses, serving as a potential link between
conventional agricultural practices and contemporary market requirements. Furthermore, this research
points to a possible role of agricultural education in developing innovation capabilities that could
promote sustainable business models in rural contexts. By focusing on the intersection of innovation,
education, and economic sustainability, the study aims to provide preliminary insights and a validated
instrument for a larger -scale investigation into the long -term viability of family -owned agricultural
enterprises.

Keywords: family businesses, digital innovation, youth, economic sustainability, agricultural education, rural
development

1. Introduction

Family-owned agricultural small businesses play an essential role in rural
economies, serving as crucial contributors to employment, food security, and community
resilience. However, these businesses often grapple with significant challenges, including
restricted access to resources, limited market presence, and issues related to long -term
financial sustainability. The rise of digital innovation offers a transformative mechanism
through which these challenges can be mitigated, enabling family farms to strengthen their

economic resilience and competitiveness in an increasingly digital market (Tirdasari et al.,
2019; Cheng et al., 2021). At the same time, as education evolves, the digital transformation
of micro family businesses has become one of the most important areas  of research,
reflecting the growing recognition that innovative learning approaches are essential for
fostering adaptability and long-term sustainability within these enterprises (Atanase Murar
& Bi țu, 2025). Moreover, as the dynamics of the global workforce undergo significant
transformation, educational systems must cultivate leadership competencies capable of
addressing emerging sustainability and ethical challenges (Enăchescu, 2025).
One noteworthy trend in this context is the involvement of young individuals,
particularly those studying in agricultural high schools, who are emerging as vital instigators
of digital innovation within their family -run farms. Their educational background equips
them with the skills needed to deploy modern digital tools that can bridge traditional
agricultural practices with contemporary market demands. Innovations such as social
media marketing, e-commerce platforms, and digital management applications are not only
cost-effective but also particularly suited for small -scale agricultural operations that may
lack substantial financial resources. These tools empower youth to enhance their family's
business visibility and operational efficiency (Paunović & Vučković, 2024; Zhang & Fan,
2022).
Agricultural education plays a pivotal role in developing these digital skills among
young people, fostering a generation capable of driving change within family businesses.
Programs tailored to instill digital literacy and entrepreneurial competencies facilitate the
application of innovative practices in traditional farming settings. As these students engage
in digital initiatives, they not only contribute to the sustenance and growth of their family's
enterprises but also acquire valuable professional skills that prepare them for future careers
in the agricultural sector (Johnston & Humphries, 2019; Sandhu et al., 2012).
Empirical research underscores the transformative impact of youth involvement
in digital innovation within family-owned agricultural businesses. Studies have shown that
engaging younger generations in innovation -driven activities is associated with
improvements in financial viability, market visibility, and overall resilience (Tiwasing et al.,
2022; Jack et al., 2020). This research explores these dynamics through an exploratory pilot
study, seeking to highlight the role of youth and educational structures in fostering a
culture of innovation that is vital for sustaining family-owned agricultural enterprises in a
rapidly changing economic landscape. In summary, the integration of digital tools and
innovative practices within family -owned agricultural businesses, alongside educational
frameworks promoting digital skills, is understood in the literature as important for
enhancing economic sustainability. This study aims to provide preliminary insights into
the intersection of youth, education, and innovation as components in driving the
sustainability of agricultural family businesses.

Research on family -run agricultural small enterprises can be organized around
three interconnected themes that together form the conceptual framework guiding this
study: (a) the structural challenges these businesses face and the adaptive strategies
proposed to address them; (b) digital transformation as a mechanism for overcoming these

2.1 Structural challenges and adaptive strategies
The literature on family -run agricultural small enterprises underscores their
significant contributions to rural economies, primarily through job creation, food
provision, and enhancing community stability. Nonetheless, these businesses encounter
persistent challenges that may jeopardize their long -term viability, including restricted
access to financial resources, limited market opportunities, and difficulties with succession
planning (Bordean et al., 2025; Ceptureanu  et al., 2020). Effective succession planning
paired with robust internal governance has been identified as critical for sustaining
performance in family -owned small and medium enterprises (SMEs), facilitating the
transfer of knowledge and leadership to yo unger generations and minimizing
organizational disruptions. Diversifying agricultural activities has been widely
recommended as a complementary strategy to mitigate market volatility: farmers engaging
in diversification strategies can bolster their resili ence against economic fluctuations and
decrease their vulnerability to the risks associated with mono-cropping practices (Stephens
et al., 2019).
This need for adaptive strategies aligns with Ceptureanu (2016), who suggests that
family involvement in change management allows controlling families to influence
organizational behavior effectively, positioning smaller firms to remain innovative in the
face of evolving market demands. Furthermore, the comprehensive research by Cheng et
al. (2022) emphasizes the necessity for new agricultural business models to enhance
employment decisions among farmers, elucidating how such changes can lead to better
economic outcomes for rural communities (Alex, 2024). This kind of business -model
adaptation within family -owned agricultural enterprises is not a purely contemporary
phenomenon: historical analysis of family-run wine farms documents a comparable shift
toward more businesslike, market -oriented operating styles in response to changing
economic conditions (Geraci, 2000), while more recent work on the dairy sector shows
that the success of change  and innovation processes in family - and community -based
farming is itself shaped by the underlying values held by those involved ( Knook et al.,
2023).

2.2 Digital transformation as an adaptive mechanism
The digital revolution has become a transformative influence in agriculture,
reshaping operational strategies and stakeholder engagement (Macrì  & Orsini, 2024).
Digital tools such as social media marketing and e-commerce platforms enable family-run
farms to improve visibility and customer interaction while optimizing internal operations
(Nikulina et al., 2022). These low-cost, accessible tools are particularly suited to small-scale
agricultural operations with limited financial resources (Paunović  & Vučković, 2024;
Zhang & Fan, 2022), and their adoption has been associated with improved farmer welfare
and employment outcomes more broadly (Cheng et al., 2021; Tirdasari et al., 2019).
This pattern is consistent with broader family-business research: survey evidence
from German family firms shows that non-family managers and transgenerational control

rather than a one-time switch (Ohashi et al., 2024).

2.3 Youth, agricultural education, and digital innovation
Youth engagement, particularly from agricultural high school students, is
positioned in the literature as a vital driver of these digital transitions. By bridging
generational divides, young individuals introduce contemporary skills and competencies
that align with current market demands. Salanță et al. (2024) argue that developing digital
competencies is crucial for organizations seeking to implement and benefit from
innovative technologies, reinforcing the premise that targeted skill acquisition can provide
a competitive advantage (Bordean et al ., 2025). Uzhva (2022) further discusses how
increasing employment opportunities in rural regions can enhance overall economic
growth, emphasizing the value of integrating youth into these transformative processes.
Educational frameworks that provide structured training in agricultural and
entrepreneurial practices contribute significantly to building the innovative capabilities of
young individuals; investing in human capital, especially through such educational
initiatives, has been shown to strengthen the adaptive capacity of agricultural businesses
(Nikulina et al., 2022). This is consistent with evidence from vocational education more
broadly, where digital literacy and entrepreneurial agility jointly predict students' readiness
for the digital economy ( Swaramarinda et al., 2025), and with curriculum -development
research identifying the specific competencies (e.g., evaluating business opportunities,
market assessment, and agricultural value -chain knowledge) that youth agricultural
entrepreneurship programs should target (Zickafoose et al., 2023).

2.4 Outcomes: economic performance and resilience
Empirical research links youth involvement in digital innovation to improvements
in financial viability, market visibility, and overall resilience (Tiwasing  et al., 2022; Jack et
al., 2020). Community-level financial mechanisms have been shown to complement these
micro-level dynamics: community-invested loans have stimulated agricultural growth by
addressing the financial needs of rural producers, thereby enh ancing economic
sustainability (Stephens et al., 2019). Such community-driven approaches not only address
the financial barriers faced by farmers but also help build resilient local economies.

For the purposes of this study, economic sustainability is understood specifically
through the lens of intergenerational transfer: a family -owned agricultural business is

competitiveness of family-run agricultural businesses. However, the mechanisms link ing
youth digital competencies, agricultural/entrepreneurial education, and firm -level
economic outcomes have rarely been examined jointly, and, to the author's knowledge,
have not previously been examined among students enrolled in a dually-funded (Ministry
of Education and Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development) technological
agricultural high school. This study addresses that gap through an exploratory pilot
investigation, the design of which is detailed in the following section.

3. Research Methodology

3.1 Research Purpose and Framework
This research investigates how young individuals, particularly students enrolled in
agricultural high schools, contribute to digital innovation within small, family -run
agricultural enterprises, as well as the extent to which their participation influences
economic sustainability and the overall resilience of these businesses. The chosen
methodological framework corresponds closely with the study's aims, guiding questions,
and proposed hypotheses, ensuring coherence throughout the research design.

3.2 Research Objectives
The research is guided by the following objectives:
O1. Identify the forms of youth engagement in digital innovation processes within
family-owned agricultural businesses.
O2. Analyze the types of low -cost digital tools adopted or proposed by young
participants.
O3. Evaluate the perceived impact of digital innovation on economic
performance, including sales, customer acquisition, and costs.
O4. Investigate the relationship between agricultural education and the ability of
young people to contribute to business innovation.
O5. Highlight the role of youth involvement in enhancing the economic resilience
of small family-owned agricultural enterprises.

3.3 Research Questions
The study addresses the following questions:
RQ1. To what extent are young people involved in digital innovation within
family-owned agricultural businesses?
RQ2. What types of low-cost digital innovations are initiated or implemented by
youth?
RQ3. How does digital innovation influence perceived financial sustainability?
RQ4. How does agricultural education contribute to the development of young
people's digital innovation skills?
RQ5. In what ways does youth involvement impact the economic resilience of
small family-owned agricultural businesses?

3.4 Research Hypotheses
The study tests the following hypotheses:
H1. Youth involvement in digital innovation positively influences financial
sustainability.
H2. Low-cost digital tools enhance business visibility and attract new customers.
H3. Digital competencies of young people are positively associated with the level
of digital innovation.
H4. Youth participation in digital innovation strengthens business resilience.
H5. Agricultural education facilitates the transfer of digital competencies to
family-owned agricultural businesses.
Linkages to the research instrument:
H1, H4 → correlations and regression analysis
H2 → items measuring costs and visibility
H3 → digital competencies ↔ innovation level
H5 → agricultural education ↔ innovation

3.5 Research Design
A quantitative, cross-sectional design was employed to measure and analyze  the
relationships among key variables: digital involvement, skills, tool adoption, economic
impact, performance, and resilience. Given the exploratory nature of this stage of the
research, a single pilot administration was used both to assess the psychometric properties
of the instrument and to generate preliminary evidence regarding the proposed
hypotheses; results are therefore reported as exploratory rather than confirmatory
throughout this article. Commonly cited benchmarks for pilot studies recommend at least
12 participants per group as a pragmatic minimum for assessing feasibility and instrument
properties (Julious, 2005), with 10 -40 participants generally needed depending on the
specific aim being assessed (Hertzog, 2008) and a well -defined framework of objectives
recommended to avoid pilot studies being under-sized for their stated purpose (Lancaster,
Dodd, & Williamson, 2004). The present pilot (N=9) falls slightly below these
benchmarks; this is acknowledged explicitly as a limitation in the Concl usions, and the
planned full-scale study will target a sample consistent with these recommendations.

3.6 Participants and Sampling
Participants were recruited exclusively from this single technological agricultural
high school, located in a rural area of Ilfov County, Romania; no other schools or regions
were sampled at this pilot stage. This school operates under the dual coordinatio n of the
Ministry of Education and the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development. Students
were recruited through purposive sampling from among those enrolled in entrepreneurial
education classes. Eligible participants were students aged 18 years or ol der who were
actively involved in their family's agricultural business. Among this one school's adult
(18+) students enrolled in these classes, only nine met this eligibility criterion of active
involvement in a family agricultural business; all nine completed the questionnaire (N=9),
yielding a full response rate within the eligible group at this single institution. Because the
sample was drawn from this single school through purposive/convenience sampling, the
findings are not statistically generalizable and should be interpreted as exploratory; this
limitation is discussed further in the Conclusions.

3.7 Instrument
The questionnaire was structured around the five research objectives and
corresponding hypotheses, combining categorical items (age bracket, education level, area
of residence, family business ownership, and the types, complexity, and cost of digital tools
used) with thirteen attitudinal items rated on a five -point Likert scale (1 = “Total
dezacord”/strongly disagree to 5 = “Total de acord”/strongly agree), and one open-ended
question inviting participants to describe a concrete example of their contribution to the
family business. Item wording was aligned with the study's five hypotheses (H1 -H5), as
summarized in the Linkages to the Research Instrument above.

3.8 Ethical Procedures
Given that all participants were adults, informed consent was obtained directly
through the online questionnaire: before accessing the substantive items, participants were
presented with a mandatory consent statement describing the study's purpose, its voluntary
nature, and data confidentiality, and could proceed only after indicating their agreement.
Parental consent was not required, as no participant was a minor. A separate institutional
ethics committee review was not sought for this exploratory pilot p hase; this is
acknowledged as a limitation, and formal institutional and ethics committee clearance will
be sought prior to the planned large-scale extension of this study.

3.9 Pilot Data Collection
The questionnaire was administered to nine young adults (N=9) actively engaged
in family -owned agricultural businesses. Participants' responses, including the closing
open-ended item, allowed an assessment of item clarity, relevance, comprehensibility, and
the appropriateness of the response scales, alongside the substantive pilot results reported
below. Internal consistency of the three multi-item composite scales was acceptable given
the small sample: Cronbach's α = .86 for Digital Involvement (4 items), α = .89 for
Economic Impact (5 items), and α = .77 for Resilience (3 items); these estimates should
be interpreted cautiously given N=9 and will be re-assessed in the full-scale study.

These pilot results are broadly consistent with prior evidence linking digital social
capital to family -owned SME performance (Tiwasing  et al., 2022) and linking farm
diversification and adaptive capacity to business resilience (Jack et al., 2020). Unlike these
earlier studies, which examined established business owners, the present pilot specifically
targets adolescent/young-adult family members enrolled in a dually-funded technological
agricultural high school in a rural Romanian context — a population and setting not
previously examined jointly in this literature. Whether the associations observed here
extend, confirm, or diverge from prior findings cannot be determined from a single pilot
administration and remains an open question for the larger-scale study.
The pilot study provides preliminary support for the proposed research model
and for the validity of the questionnaire instrument. Young people appear to act as agents
of digital innovation, and their involvement, alongside agricultural education, is associated
with market visibility and business resilience in this small sample. These findings justify
extending the study to a larger, more representative sample rather than confirming the
proposed relationships conclusively.

5. Discussion

This exploratory pilot study offers a preliminary understanding of how young
people, especially students enrolled in agricultural high schools, may contribute to digital
innovation within small, family-run agricultural enterprises. The results show that although
direct involvement in routine digital operations is moderate, respondents' input at a
conceptual level is rated highly. Young individuals are described as introducing fresh ideas,
suggesting digital improvements, and offering strategic perspectives.  This gap between
hands-on participation and perceived creative potential is consistent with their possible
role as catalysts of digital transformation on family farms, complementing the long -
standing knowledge of older generations. The study also indicate s that affordable digital
tools, such as social media channels and basic online management platforms, are perceived
as particularly effective for increasing visibility and attracting a wider customer base, while
not meaningfully reducing operational expenses in the short term.
Formal agricultural education emerges from this pilot as another factor associated
with digital innovation. Students with structured training in agricultural high schools

6. Implications and further research

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its
findings. First, the pilot sample was small (N=9) and drawn through
purposive/convenience sampling from a single technological agricultural high school; this
falls below commonly cited pilot-study benchmarks (e.g., Julious's, 2005, rule of thumb of
at least 12 participants), which limits statistical power and precludes generalization to the
broader population of young people involved in family -owned agricultural businesses.
Second, all outcome measures were self -reported perceptions rather than objective
business indicators (e.g., audited sales figures, verified customer counts), which may be
subject to social desirability or recall bias. Third, the cross-sectional design does not allow
causal inference regarding the direction of the observed associations. Fourth, informed
consent for this pilot phase was obtained through the questionnaire itself, without separate
institutional ethics committee review; future phases will seek formal ethical clearance and
institutional approval in advance of data collection. Building on these limitations, future
research should pursue a representative, larger -scale sample across multiple schools and
regions, consistent with established pilot/full-study sample-size guidance (Hertzog, 2008;
Lancaster et al., 2004); a longitudinal design capable of tracking changes in digital adoption
and business performance over time; comparative analysis across regions and school types;
and the use of objective, v erifiable business indicators alongside perceptual measures, in
order to test more rigorously whether the perceived benefits identified here translate into
measurable economic outcomes.
With these limitations in mind, engaging youth in digital innovation appears to be
a promising, low -cost strategy for small family farms confronting market volatility,
technological change, and shifting consumer expectations, and one that merits
confirmation through a larger, more rigorous follow-up study.
Returning to the question of economic sustainability that motivates this study: the
pilot findings tentatively suggest that sustainability in these family -owned agricultural
businesses is not produced by digital tools alone, nor by youth involvement alone, but by
the intergenerational transfer through which younger members' digital competencies are
combined with older members'  accumulated agricultural and business experience. The
highest-rated pilot indicators were precisely those describing this transfer —
intergenerational collaboration, adaptability to economic change, and perceived chances
of long -term development (Table 4)  — which is consistent with the strong pilot
association between agricultural education and digital involvement (H3) reported above.
