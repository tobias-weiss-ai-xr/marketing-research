ABSTRACT
Nudge Theory, introduced by Thaler and Sunstein in their seminal book, inspired the creation of human
choice architects who applied behavioural insights into influence decisions across various domains. In the
online retail industry, however, the evolution of Artificial Intelligence, predictive analytics, and real time
data personalization has transformed traditional nudging into algorithmic choice architecture powered by
data-driven insights. This paper conceptually examines how AI driven Algorithmic nudges operate through
cross- platform behavioural tracking, recommender systems, push notifications, and default designs to
influence consumer decisions. The epistemological orientation of this paper is grounded in qualitative and
interpretative approach based on critical realism to explore how consumers conform to stacked and
multichannel nudges(multiple, sequential interventions across apps, websites and social platforms) that
leverage cognitive biases and bounded rationality. Findings suggest that AI enabled nudges outperform
human- designed ones in precision, scalability, and contextual adaptab ility, allowing retailers to subtly
guide consumer behaviour under the guise of personalisation. However, the opacity of these algorithmic
mechanisms raises serious ethical questions regarding informed consent, privacy, and autonomy,
especially for vulnerable consumer groups. The study underscores the need for transparent AI governance,
ethical design principles, and regulatory frameworks to prevent behavioural manipulation in digital
marketplaces. It further argues that the evolution from libertarian pater nalism to algorithmic paternalism
represents a critical shift that challenges the foundational ethics of Nudge Theory and calls for cross -
disciplinary collaboration between marketing, technology, human- computer interaction and law to ensure
responsible AI-driven persuasion in online retail.

1. Introduction
The integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) into digital platforms has
significantly reshaped the landscape of online retail, transforming how consumers encounter, interact with,
assess, and ultimately choose products in the r apidly changing world of online retail. Research shows that
the consumer journey in digital retail unfolds in multiple stages, each influenced by digital touchpoints. It
begins with awareness, where consumers first encounter products through targeted ads, social media
content, and search-engine results, establishing initial brand salience (Colman, 2023). Consumers evaluate
alternatives using product pages, comparison tools, recommendations, and reviews that guide decision -
making. During the decision or purc hase stage, interface elements such as scarcity badges, countdown
timers, default selections, and one-click checkout nudge users toward conversion (Colman, 2023). After the
transaction, the post -purchase or retention phase continues to influence through de livery notifications,
feedback requests, loyalty rewards, and personalized reminders (Odjick & Guide, 2024). Ultimately,
repeated positive engagement may lead to loyalty or advocacy, reflected through repurchases, referrals,
and user -generated reviews (Col man, 2023). Crucially, studies highlight that the digital journey is not
linear: AI-driven personalization enables consumers to transition fluidly across stages, as touchpoints and
nudges are sequenced and adapted in real time to sustain engagement and acc elerate decision making
(Joosten, 2024).
Online retail now operates on three aspects with the rise of e -commerce: ubiquity, speed and hyper -
personalisation, reaching consumers seamlessly across devices and contexts through targeted
advertisements, tailored recommendations, dynamic interface adjus tments, and persuasive engagement
loops. Within this paradigm, AI-driven personalization continuously structures the consumer journey, with
recommender systems, predictive analytics, and real -time profiling modifying default options, triggering
countdown timers, sending push notifications, ranking product visibility, and sequencing discount prompts
to maximise conversion (Hassan et al., 2025). As a result, the digital marketplace is not a neutral
information channel with plain nudges but as an active behavi oural influence system in which algorithmic

cues operate across multiple touchpoints with nudges that adapt according to each customer’s tastes and
preferences and online purchase behaviour (Nanta et al., 2025). Artificial Intelligence (AI), defined broadly
as the ability of machines to execute tasks and optimize performance through autonomous learning (Geisel,
2018), has become a core infrastructural force in online retail, providing automated solutions to complex
decision and prediction problems (Crittenden et al., 2019).
Central to this evolution is the emergence of algorithmic choice architecture, wherein AI systems
operationalise nudge techniques originally theorised by Thaler and Sunstein. Nudges were conceptualised
in the book “Nudge: Improving Health, Wealth and Happiness” as “any aspect of the choice architecture that
alters people’s behaviour in a predictable way without forbidding options or significantly changing
economic incentives” (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008). What began as subtle, non -coercive interventions to
compensate for systematic biases in human judgment (Thaler & Sunstein, 2003) has now been
computationally scaled and automated thus challenging the theoretical understanding of nudges on
purchase influence. Digital nudging, defined as the strategic deployment  of interface elements and
algorithmically curated content to influence user decisions online (Weinmann, Schneider & vom Brocke,
2016), operates through programmably adaptive UI/UX elements such as personalised recommendations,
one-click defaults, scarcity badges, countdown timers, push notifications, tab pre -selections, social proof
widgets, and time -sensitive promotional prompts. These interventions do not act as in dividual nudges;
rather, they form a layered and synchronised persuasion ecosystem through multichannel and stacked
nudges in which real-time profiling and predictive modelling continuously reshape choice environments to
maximise conversion and retention.
Recent advances have deepened studies and findings in this paradigm further by demonstrating how  AI
systems construct and optimise behavioural influence. In contrast to traditional human choice architects,
algorithms now function as autonomous designers of influence (Mills & Sætra, 2022). These AI Models now
model consumers as probabilistic subjects, an alysing behavioural histories, psychographic patterns,
contextual cues, and individual biases to identify the optimal moment, message, and media touchpoint for
influence. This operationalizes the 5Ms of marketing - mission, money, message, media and measure ment
for maximum persuasive impact. Algorithms can infer motivational states, cognitive susceptibilities, and
purchase intent across multiple platforms, enabling multichannel and staged nudging that spans the entire
digital customer journey, from awareness (feed rankings, product visibility), to consideration (social proof,
personalised reviews), to decision (time pressure badges, discount sequencing), and to post -purchase
loyalty (reward retention loops). Recommendation engines themselves act as nudges: th ey suggest the
products that users are most likely to purchase based on personal histories and behavioural similarity to
other consumers (Zhao & Keikhosrokiani, 2022; Bathla, Singh & Sharma, 2017). At this scale, online retail
functions as an active behavioural influence system, deploying computational persuasion strategies across
touchpoints to accelerate decision making.
These mechanisms extend not only to conscious reasoning but also to subliminal and affect -triggered
persuasion which involve cognitive and conative components affecting the behaviour of a consumer.
Research shows that AI -personalised messaging aligned with  inferred psychological traits significantly
amplifies behavioural influence (Matz et al., 2017), while staged digital cues across platforms increase
compliance probability beyond single -channel exposure (Caraban  et al., 2019). In a demonstration of
persuasion efficiency, Dezfouli  et al. (2020) trained an AI agent to strategically steer decision -makers
toward a target choice, achieving a statistically significant shift from a 50% baseline to 70% compliance
thus illustrating the potency of autonomous, learning -based nudges at scale. The increasing sophistication
of such systems, however, also obscures the origin and intent of influence, raising questions of
transparency, accountability, and consumer self-agency (Mills & Sætra, 2022).
To interrogate these dynamics beyond surface-level behavioural effects, this paper adopts a critical realist
perspective. Critical realism conceptualises reality across three ontological layers: the real – underlying
mechanisms and structures; the actual – events and activities produced by those mechanisms; and the
empirical – observable outcomes and lived experiences (Bhaskar, 2008). Applied to online retail, this
analytical stance reveals how deep algorithmic and commercial mechanisms such as behavioural analytics
pipelines, recommender systems, affect-prediction models, and profit-maximising optimisation objectives
generate nudging events across diverse digital touchpoints, ultimately shaping consumer experiences such
as heightened convenience, reduced ex ploration, impulsive purchasing, and perceived loss of autonomy
(Kosinski, Stillwell & Graepel, 2013; Kramer, Guillory & Hancock, 2014). The stratified ontology enables an
explanation of how and why behavioural outcomes occur, while simultaneously enabling  critique of the
power asymmetries in AI -mediated markets. The strength of this perspective lies in its ability to trace
deeper mechanisms to address socio- technical issues and concerns.
Despite the pervasive adoption of algorithmic nudging in online retail, its ethical implications remain
under-theorised within marketing and consumer research. While personalisation has been shown to
increase consumer trust, satisfaction, and loyalty when perceived as beneficial (Hassan et al., 2025),
longstanding concerns persist regarding opacity, informed consent, manipulation, vulnerability
exploitation, and digital fairness (Yeung, 2017). Importantly, majority of the existing literature examines

isolated interface nudges and A/B testings rather than the cumulative behavioural consequences of
adaptive, sequenced, and cross-platform influence systems. The shift from libertarian paternalism (nudges
that support welfare -enhancing decisions) to algorit hmic paternalism (nudges driven by commercial
optimisation rather than welfare) represents a pivotal tension that remains under -theorised within

This paper conceptually maps AI-driven nudging in online retail from underlying algorithmic mechanisms

autonomy, transparency, and fairness. By applying a c ritical realist analytical lens to digital touchpoints,
psychological triggers, and commercial optimisation logics, this research provides a mechanism -based
foundation for future empirical investigation and contributes to ongoing debates on ethical AI and
responsible personalisation in consumer contexts. In doing so, it underscores both the commercial
advantages and the normative risks of AI -mediated persuasion, advocating for a deeper theoretical
understanding of how influence is produced, distributed, and  experienced within algorithmic
marketplaces.

2. Research Objectives
This conceptual paper aims to critically examine how AI-driven nudging shapes consumer decision-making
in online retail by analysing the underlying algorithmic mechanisms, cross-channel digital touchpoints, and
behavioural outcomes through a critical reali st lens. To achieve this, the study aims to study certain
objectives. Firstly, to identify and explain the underlying algorithmic and behavioural mechanisms( real
layer) that enable AI -powered nudging in online retail, including recommender systems, predictive
analytics, interface-level design cues, behavioural profiling, and commercial optimisation logics. Secondly,
to analyse how these mechanisms materialise as observable nudging events across digital touchpoints
(actual layer) such as feed ranking, default settings, scarcity badges, countdown timers, dynamic pricing,
multi-platform reminders, and staged recommendation sequences across the consumer journey. Th irdly,
to examine the empirical outcomes of AI -driven nudging on consumer experience and decision -making
(empirical layer), including convenience, satisfaction, impulsive purchasing, perceived inevitability,
constrained autonomy, and vulnerability across d emographic and psychographic groups. Based on these
reviews, we aim to identify and theorise the ethical tensions emerging from opaque algorithmic choice
architectures in retail, particularly concerning autonomy, transparency, fairness, manipulation, and
surveillance capitalism and propose a mechanism -based conceptual model for responsible and ethical AI
nudging, outlining how structural interventions (transparent algorithms, accountable design, and
explainable choice architectures) can mitigate the risks o f algorithmic paternalism while preserving
personalisation benefits in analysis and findings.

3. Literature Review
The literature review is structured using a critical realist ontology, providing a multilayered explanation of
how AI - mediated nudging operates in online retail. The Real layer concerns the deep generative
mechanisms that make algorithmic nudging possible: behavioural data pipelines, psychographic inference
systems, predictive modelling architectures, reinforcement -learning optimisation, and commercial
incentive structures embedded in platform economics. These mechanisms construct the algorithmic choice
architecture, setting the conditions under which digital influence can take place. The Actual layer captures
how these mechanisms materialise as observable nudging events across digital touchpoints, such as
personalised recommendations, scarcity badges, dyna mic pricing prompts, countdown timers, staged
reminder notifications, feed ranking manipulation, and trait -congruent persuasive messages. Finally, the
Empirical layer examines how consumers perceive and experience these nudges, including feelings of
convenience and satisfaction, the narrowing of perceived choice, over-reliance on recommendations, mixed
reactions to transparency, and concerns over privacy, autonomy, and manipulation.
Positioning the literature review in this manner is necessary for explaining why algorithmic nudging
unfolds systematically as it maps the progression from latent mechanisms(Real), to manifest
activities(Actual) to lived outcomes(Empirical) revealing how ethical risks originate upstream in structural
and algorithmic incentives rather than only on downstream user behaviours. It first establishes the Real
domain, then equips to show digital touchpoints become behavioural cues (Actual ) and how nudging
sequences produces both desirable and undesirable consumer experiences (Empirical).
3.1 Real Layer: Underlying Structures and Deep Mechanisms
3.1.1 Evolution of Nudging to Algorithmic Choice Architecture
Classical nudge theory established that subtle adjustments in choice architecture such as defaults,
framing,salience and sequencing can sequentially steer decisions without restricting options or altering
economic incentives. This behavioural influence ori ginally relied on predictable cognitive tendencies,
particularly the dominance of fast, intuitive judgement over slow, reflective reasoning when cognitive

resources are limited (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). When this paradigm shifted to  digital  environments,
nudges ceased to be static design choices and became progressively embedded in interface logic enabling
it to adapt in real- time and nudge customers based on customer profiles and behaviour. Online retail began
integrating behavioural design features such as personalised product suggestions, scarcity badges, limited-
time flash offers, countdown timers, and frictionless one -click checkout search which intend to shorten
deliberation time and heighten perceived urgency without appearing coerciv e (Johnson et al., 2012). The
important conceptual shift is that the influence of the choice architect became computationally adaptive:
rather than a designer specifying a single choice structure, systems now learn which structures maximise
persuasion for each user (Weinmann et al., 2016; Caraban et al., 2019). In this transition, nudging evolved
from an intervention designed once into a capability that optimises itself continuously, marking the rise of
algorithmic choice architecture.
3.1.2 AI Mechanisms and Infrastructures as Generative Structures of Digital Influence
In online retail, the mechanisms used are rooted in back -end infrastructures that collect, infer and
operationalise behavioural data. The deep mechanisms of algorithmic nudging are powered by data
aggregation, machine -learning pipelines, and behavioural an alytics. Big data infrastructures in
contemporary platforms integrate cross -device tracking, clickstream logs, browsing histories,
transactional footprints, CRM records, and third -party data to construct persistent behavioural profiles
(Gavrila & de Lucas Ancillo, 2021). Machine -learning methods ranging from collaborative filtering, deep
learning, clustering to reinforcement learning predict preferences and purchase probabilities by modelling
users as probabilistic subjects defined by histories of behaviour , psychographics, contextual cues, and
biases (Wang & Zhang, 2013)
These systems  perform three causal functions:
1. Profiling (inferring traits and intent)
2. Prediction (estimating the most likely behavioural trajectory)
3. Optimisation (selecting interventions that most likely trigger action, maximise conversion, retention,
and customer lifetime value)
Digital nudging becomes operationalised directly at the interface layer through button placement, feedback
signals, navigation flows, pre -selected options, and ranking systems engineered to influence decisions
(Weinmann et al., 2016; Caraban  et al., 2019). The economic intent while making these infrastructures is
to ensure optimisation that targets commercial metrics such as conversion, cart value and retention rather
than consumer autonomy. The architecture of persuasion through these digital channels is the downstream
expression of a deeply optimised influence system.
3.1.3 Behavioural Micro-Mechanisms and Psychometric Persuasion
The effectiveness of digital nudging also depends on psychological mechanisms that shape how consumers
process information. Under bounded rationality, individuals rely on heuristics to simplify decisions in
environments characterised by high information lo ad, time pressure and abundant alternatives (Simon,
1955; Mertens et al., 2021) These heuristics- anchoring, scarcity sensitivity, loss aversion, social proof and
default acceptance accelerates judgements such as anchoring on initial prices and discounts, responding
impulsively to scarcity signals, and deferring to social proof via ratings and reviews, but simultaneously
opens pathways for systematic bias (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). Digital platforms intensify these
conditions by providing constant stimuli and instant feedback, which encourage rapid, intuitive responses
rather than deliberative reasoning. Emotional states further amplify susceptibility, with high -arousal
emotions increasing compliance with persuasive cues, as demonstrated by targeted adverti sing during
periods of emotional vulnerability (Hamelin et al., 2017). Digital ecosystems intensify these tendencies:
constant stimuli, abundant choice, and rapid feedback loops push users toward fast, intuitive (System 1)
processing rather than slow, deli berative reasoning. Emotional susceptibility amplifies this effect; high -
arousal states increase compliance with persuasive cues, as demonstrated by Facebook’s targeting capacity
based on detected emotional distress (Hamelin et al., 2017). Psychometric tar geting extends beyond
emotion by inferring cognitive styles and motivational drivers to personalise nudges at the level of
psychological traits (Hauser et al., 2009; Weinmann et al., 2016). These behavioural mechanisms interlock
with AI infrastructures to create a deep structural influence system that operates below conscious
awareness and across multiple touchpoints. Consequently, algorithmic nudging must be treated not as
incidental persuasion but as a systemic outcome of technological and psychological causation embedded in
this layer which is engineered around how people predictably think, feel and decide.
3.2 Actual Layer – Events, Digital Touchpoints & AI-Powered Nudging
The Actual Layer explains how the deep causal structures identified in the Real Layer which consists of AI
infrastructures, behavioural micro -mechanisms, and commercial optimisation logics materialise as
observable events within digital consumer journeys. While the Real Layer concerns how nudging becomes
technically and behaviourally possible, the Actual Layer examines how nudging manifests in practice across
digital interfaces, customer touchpoints, and adaptive persuasion sequences. In this layer, AI syst ems
translate behavioural predictions into concrete UI/UX interventions, generating measurable decision cues
that shape consumer behaviour throughout the awareness -to-purchase cycle.

3.2.1 AI-Powered Nudges in Digital Interfaces
AI-driven personalization has transformed digital retail from a passive information environment into an
adaptive choice architecture that guides consumer decisions in real time. These include behavioural
prompts such as one -click checkouts, urgency badges,  limited-time discounts, pre -selected add -ons, and
recommendation widgets strategically positioned to streamline choice and reduce friction( Johnson et al.,
2012). These are powered by recommender systems that are driven by machine learning models trained
on cross-device tracking, clickstream logs, and transaction histories to produce dynamic feed ranking and
personalized banners that adapt in real time to browsing history and cart abandonment (Zhao et al., 2017).
As users browse, hesitate, or abandon carts, banners and suggestions recalibrate dynamically, and
persuasive cues often reappear across platforms via retargeted notifications and prom otional emails
raising conversion rates by 20–30% in many campaigns (Matz et al., 2017).These events exemplify Yeung’s
(2016) theory of hypernudging, where interface elements  function as fluid micro -interventions that are
constantly reconfigured based on behavioural feedback.
3.2.2 Psychometric Targeting, Dynamic Pricing & Ethical Concerns
The persuasive intensity of these cues increases when psychological inference and affective profiling is
layered into the system. AI models increasingly infer personality traits, emotional states and cognitive
tendencies such as extraversion and openness o r dejection and fatigue from digital footprints to tailor
persuasive cues, as demonstrated in Matz et al.’s (2017) the landmark Facebook study where personality -
targeted ads doubled engagement relative to demographic targeting alone. Cross -channel stacked nudges
in sequencing amplifies this effect: for instance, a mobile advertisement framed as a limited gain may be
followed by an email that defaults to purchase, leveraging loss aversion and default bias to drive conversion
(Mirbabaie et al., 2023). Dynamic pricing algorithms add another behavioural layer by leveraging clustering
and deep learning by modifying costs based on session duration, location, and device type creating urgency
and anchoring effects that accelerate impu lsive purchase behaviour (Saulītis et al., 2024). These stacked
nudges across the awareness -consideration-purchase journey prioritise optimization metrics such as
lifetime value, repeat purchases  and  basket size over autonomy, mirroring critiques of surveillance
capitalism where commercial incentives in place of consumer welfare shape the temporal sequencing of
influence (Hamelin et al., 2017; Yeung, 2016)
3.2.3 Multichannel Touchpoints & Adaptive Nudge Modalities
The persuasive system becomes most visible when viewed across the full spectrum of touchpoints that
form a modern retail journey. Web listings, social-media feeds, email reminders, push notifications, chatbot
prompts, in -app recommendations, and mobile adv ertisements serve as behavioural cues reinforcing
earlier interventions. Generative AI chatbots now supplement recommendations with social proof
messaging (e.g., “X others bought this”) paired with progress indicators and salience -enhanced buttons to
activate social-proof and scarcity heuristics during high -stakes decisions such as checkout ( Caraban et al.,
2019; Hauser et al., 2009). Interface -level nudges become especially potent in abandonment contexts,
where defaults (automatic enrolment in reward programs), framing cues (“₹99 instead of ₹120”), and
countdown timers can recover 15–20% of lost sales through retargeting cycles (Bergram et al., 2022; Valta
et al., 2022). Systematic reviews confirm that defaults, scarcity cues, and social proof consistently generate
the strongest behavioural lift, especially when paired with AI -driven “morphing,” wherein layouts evolve
to match inferred cognitive styles and decision tendencies (Zimmermann et al., 2023; Tamilarasi, 2025).  In
effect, the retail interface becomes a persuasion ecosystem distributed across multiple channels,
dynamically responsive to micro -behavioural cues, and structurally or iented toward commercial
optimisation rather than neutral decision support.
3.3 Empirical Layer: Observed Experiences and Behavioural Outcomes of Algorithmic Nudging
While computational and psychological mechanisms explain how digital nudging is designed and deployed,
the empirical literature focuses on what users actually do, perceive, and experience in real -world retail
environments. This body of work documents behav ioural effects at scale such as increased conversions,
narrowed exploration, heightened impulsivity, and evolving perceptions of autonomy, privacy, and fairness
across experiments, field studies, HCI research, surveys, and regulatory audits. The empirical layer reflects
the outcomes of algorithmic nudging for consumers, designers and online marketplaces.
3.3.1 Behavioural Amplification Through Psychometric and Trait -Aligned Targeting
A major part of empirical evidence examines how psychometrically personalised nudges enhance
algorithmic nudges. In a landmark large-scale online experiment, Matz, Kosinski, Nave and Stillwell( 2017)
demonstrated that psychologically personalised advertising messages significantly increased click-through
and purchase rates compared to non -tailored alternatives, confirming that alignment with inferred
personality traits amplifies persuasion. Retail field studies show that recommender-system personalisation
increases click -through by up to 50% and conversions by 30%, with stronger effects when messages fit
motivational profiles rather than past purchase behaviour alone. Meta-analyses covering 231 digital nudge
experiments highlight robust behavioural effects for default settings and social proof in online retail,
reporting a pooled Cohen’s d = 0.346, and substantially higher effects in food retail (d = 0.72) where urgency
signals and defaults accelerate checkout decisions (Mertens et al., 2021; Matz et al., 2017).

Across studies, persuasion is shown to grow stronger when nudges appear repeatedly and sequentially
across touchpoints. Advanced generative AI nudges reduce cognitive effort and boost goal desirability, but
also encourage habituation, whereby users reduce exploratory behavior  and develop narrowed
preferences over time (Valta et al., 2022; Zimmermann et al., 2023). Dynamic pricing prompts and scarcity
badges increase impulsive purchases; cross -device retargeting campaigns recover 15 –20% of abandoned
carts; and countdown timers accelerate last -mile checkout decisions, increasing conversion speed by
approximately 25%. Longitudinal UX data reveals a cumulative pattern: repeated exposure to personalised
cues narrows product exploration, increases over -reliance on system recommendati ons, and gradually
shifts preferences toward what platforms highlight most frequently. Empirically, therefore, algorithmic
nudging produces not isolated moments of persuasion but durable behavioural trajectories shaped through
repeated, psychologically aligned interventions across channels.
3.3.2 Autonomy, Opacity, Fairness and Perceived Vulnerability in Consumer Experience
Empirical work also documents how users interpret and emotionally respond to AI mediated nudging.
Calboli (Calboli, 2025), Sætra (Sætra, 2020) and Sunstein (2024) report that personalisation can generate
feelings of convenience and cognitive relief, yet users frequently perceive a reduction of self -agency when
interface cues become excessively directive or unavoidable . Surveys find that approximately two-thirds of
users appreciate the convenience of personalized banners and one -click defaults, while a majority express
concerns about privacy, perceived manipulation, and opaque personalization algorithms (Caraban  et al.,
2019; Valta & Maier, 2025; Zimmermann et al., 2023).
HCI Large Scale Survey -based research finds that 68% of online shoppers appreciate convenient defaults
and personalised banners, while 62% simultaneously report privacy concerns and discomfort with being
“steered,” with vulnerable groups (e.g., low digital  literacy or emotional instability) showing 40% higher
reports of reduced autonomy while interacting with personalised prompts.
Opacity emerges as one of the most frequently documented experiential themes. Zuboff (Zuboff, 2019) and
Arrieta (2020) show that proprietary black-box architectures limit user awareness of how personal data is
collected and how interface elements are perso nalised, resulting in uncertainty about where decisions
originate from the self or the system. Transparency does not fully resolve this tension. Michelson (2020)
identifies a “transparency paradox”: users appreciate disclosure about personalisation and fee l more
positively toward platforms that offer it, yet transparency does not significantly decrease behavioural
compliance with personalised nudges.
Qualitative human-computer interaction studies highlight cognitive dissonance caused by nudges: social
proof widgets strengthen trust and perceptions of credibility for 85% of users, whereas subliminal scarcity
cues evoke feelings of manipulation and mistr ust, with 55% demanding greater transparency in choice
architectures (Caraban et al., 2019; Valta et al., 2022). Regulatory and ethical concerns have led to active
calls for oversight, including algorithmic audits and "right to explanation" mandates, especially given
documented dark patterns found in 70% of retail applications audi ted in the EU (Yeung, 2017; Saulītis,
2025). Empirical data further link emotional susceptibility with adverse reactions to distress -targeted
nudges, contributing to policy interventions aimed at protecting consumer autonomy in digital
marketplaces ( Zimmermann et al., 2023).
Together, these empirical findings show that consumers experience algorithmic nudging simultaneously as
assistance and as intrusion benefiting from reduced effort and personalised relevance while sensing that
their autonomy and privacy may be diminished. T he behavioural effectiveness of nudging, supported by
large-scale evidence, thus coexists with nuanced emotional and ethical responses that shape how
consumers judge the legitimacy of influence in online retail environments.

conditioning. By mapping nudges across these layers, the table highlights how deep algorithmic strategies
translate into concrete interf ace choices and ultimately influence user behaviour, agency, and perceived
autonomy.

4. Theoretical Framework
The theoretical framework applies critical realism, behavioural science to construct a multilayered account
of AI-mediated nudging in retail interfaces.
4.1 Stratified Ontology
The framework adopts CR’s stratified understanding of social causation, in which digital nudging is shaped
by three stratas. The first stratum consists of generative mechanisms and structures (e.g., data
infrastructures, recommender systems, big data pipel ines, reinforcement - learning optimisation
loops,commercial incentive systems). The second stratum consists of observable events and activities that
arise from those structures (e.g., feed ranking, countdown timers, flash sale prompts, multichannel
retargeting, psychometrically aligned recommendati ons). The third consists of lived experiences and
perceptions (e.g., convenience, privacy concerns,decision relief, perceived loss of autonomy).
This structure prevents analytical flattening by distinguishing between what is observed and what causes
the observation, ensuring that surface -level behaviours such as clicks and conversions are interpreted as
the outcome and the mechanism are treated as the origin of this influence.

4.2 Retroduction as a Logic of Explanation
Retroduction is used to move from patterned observations to explanatory mechanisms. For example, the
recurring pattern of users receiving sequential nudges across devices and platforms logically implies the
existence of cross-channel persuasion architectures. The observation that personalised nudges outperform
generic ones aligns with the inference of psychometric modelling and motivational segmentation. The
consumer tension between satisfaction and high convenience and perceived autonomy loss suggests dual -
causal driv ers, where optimisation for friction reduction coexists with commercial incentives for
behavioural acceleration. Through repeated testing against interdisciplinary research, the framework
generates a coherent explanation of how algorithmic nudges function in online retail.
4.3 Core Theoretical Constructs
Four constructs integrate the literature into a coherent explanatory model:
1. AI-Driven Choice Architectures: systems of algorithmic decision design that structure user pathways
and optimise behaviour.
2. Consumer Cognitive Biases and Affective Responses: psychological tendencies that shape susceptibility
to influence decisions.
3. Digital Touchpoints and Interface Cues: visible surfaces through which deeper algorithmic structures
express influence.
4. Ethical Concerns: autonomy, fairness, and transparency, which reinstate normative implications of
aligning commercial optimisation with psychological vulnerability.
The framework is justified as it accounts for how AI works through the interaction of cognitive biases,
affective triggers and algorithmic optimisation and highlights the concerns about agency, fairness and
asymmetries of power. Grounded in critical realis m, the study ensures philosophical rigour by aligning
stratified ontology in a socio-technical and socio-ethical environments.

5. Methodology
This study adopts an interpretative conceptual method grounded in critical realism to examine how AI
systems have emerged as choice architects in online retail. It synthesizes interdisciplinary knowledge
spanning behavioral science, digital marketing, HCI (Human Computer Interaction), ethics and information
systems to construct an explanatory account of how AI -mediated nudging shapes consumer behaviour in
online retail.
Critical realism is appropriate because it conceptualises reality as stratified ontology that explains -
1. Underlying causal mechanisms and structures
2. Events that occur when these mechanisms are deployed in specific domains
3. Observed experiences and perceptions of these events (Bhaskar, 2008).
This stance avoids the epistemic error of confusing what is observed  with  that is causing the observation
which is a recurrent limitation in existing digital nudging research that focuses on outcomes (e.g., clicks,
conversions, satisfaction) without examining the infrastructures and incentives that produce them.
Methodologically, this study applies retroduction , a logic in Critical realism analysis which begins with
observed behavioural regularities and works backward to infer the conditions that must exist for those
regularities to occur. In the context of digital nudging, such patterns include sequential cross -channel
prompts, hyper-personalised recommendations, and the simultaneous experience of convenience and loss
of autonomy. Through retroduction , these observations are traced to plausible underlying mechanisms
such as cross -device identity resolution, collaborative filtering and reinforcement -learning pipelines,
psychometric inference engines, and commercial optimisation incentives.
The methodological process used involved an iterative triangulation movement between theory and
evidence. It included the following steps:
(a) identifying behavioral outcomes found in research
(b) linking these outcomes to visible retail interface events
(c) figuring out the structures and incentives that logically lead to those events. This involved constantly
checking these ideas against research from different fields until we found the most understandable
explanation.
This methodology can be justified as it ensures logical consistency and ontological depth while other
methods focus on descriptive aggregation. In this respect, the study follows Bhaskar’s stance that social
phenomena should be understood not merely throug h the concepts applied to them, but through the real
consequences they produce just as war is not solely a concept but also fighting, and homelessness is not
solely a definition but the material absence of shelter (Bhaskar, 2008). Parallelly, consumer agen cy is not
treated as an abstract theme in marketing, but a concrete freedom that can be expanded or constrained by
algorithmic design.
Accordingly, the methodological objective is to explain the structural conditions under which AI systems
become de facto choice architects. The literature is therefore analysed mechanistically, situating
behavioural effects within the technological and eco nomic structures that drive them. This enables the
paper to offer an explanatory account of why digital nudges are effective and why they raise ethical

6. Analysis and Findings
6.1 Data Foundations and Predictive Preference Modelling
Recommender systems in online retail are powered by expansive behavioural datasets that capture
transactions and micro -behaviours with granular precision: clickstreams, search paths, device IDs,
scrolling patterns, hesitations, dwell time, and rating or re view behaviour that serves as inputs for AI
models (Zuboff, 2019). Zuboff’s ( 2019) surveillance capitalism details how human experience is used for
behavioral prediction, with Google exemplifying early accumulation of excess data for resale through
transaction brokers. Modern engines apply clustering, regression scoring, and latent -trait modelling to
compute the optimal persuasion path for each user rather than  merely matching products to tastes (Yang
et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024). A pivotal contribution comes from Matz et al. (2017), demonstrating that
digital exhaust can be converted into psychometric profiles that infer cognitive styles and motivational
triggers. Sætra (Sætra, 2020) terms this the “sweet spot of manipulation,” where AI identifies which
message, in what tone, and at what moment will maximise behavioural compliance. As a result, nudges
become dynamic and adaptive when recommendations, urgency  cues, and defaults evolve in real time
based on how users respond, rather than remaining fixed design artefacts.
6.2 Commercial Optimisation and Behavioural Steering Pipelines
Nudging online is driven by the commercial logic embedded in machine-learning systems that optimise for
conversions, retention, engagement time, and lifetime value. Reinforcement -learning agents continuously
experiment with intervention types and intensity, scaling the variants that produce the highest behavioural
yield for the lowest friction (Nofshin et al., 2024). Zuboff’s (2019) account of surveillance capitalism shows
how accumulated behavioural data is repurposed to predict actions, preferences and funnel users toward
profitable outcomes. Real -time personalisation powered by clickstream evolution,  inferred motivations,
and affective states allows platforms to steer users across the journey from initial attention to purchase
consolidation, regardless of whether users recognise this shaping or consent to it. In this configuration,
nudges are evolved deeper predictive and profit -optimisation systems that deploy influence tactically,
continuously, and with increasing psychological precision.
6.3 Stacked and Multichannel Nudge Events Across the Consumer Journey
The mechanisms described in the real layer like data infrastructures and opaque algorithms produce
observable events through stacked, multichannel nudges across mobile apps, desktop websites, email, push
notifications and social media which retarget ads based on persona feeds. A typical sequence begins in the
browser where product pages dynamically reorder based on inferred preferences, scarcity labels like "Only
3 left” are displayed along with product catalog  backlinks while countdown timers increase arousal and
default delivery or add -on selections subtly accelerate commitment. Once the browsing session is
interrupted, retargeting pipelines activate  sending email reminders like “Still thinking about this?”,  mobile
push notifications with personalised coupon drops, Google AdSense placements embedding recently
viewed products, and social media ads tuned to persona -level emotional trigger which creates a seamless
sequence that recovers 20-30% of abandoned carts by exploiting bounded attention( Arrieta, 2020). These
events manifest as browse abandonment prompts (e.g., "Continue shopping?"), remind sequences (e.g., 24-
hour email follow -ups), coupon drops timed to session data, Google Adsense  banners with hyper -
personalized headlines, and scarcity badges ("Limited stock") that evolve across channels, boosting open
rates by 40% and conversions by 25% in omnichannel campaigns (Yeung, 2016). Omnichannel behavioural
sequencing with “browse → remind → coupon → retarget → scarcity escalation” loop recovers abandoned
carts, suppresses deliberation windows, and strategically reduces opportunities for reflective decision -
making.
6.4 Behavioural Outcomes: Impulsivity, Reduced Exploration and Preference –Action Mismatch
The observable consumer outcomes of these nudge cascades include increased impulsive purchases,
narrowing of exploration, and systematic preference –action mismatch( Calboli, 2025). Cal Poly’s RecSys
study (2025) demonstrates that individuals frequently buy items promoted through sequential
recommendation cues despite rating alternative items higher while leveraging exposure bias and moment-
specific susceptibility. Habit formation compounds this effec t wherein users begin to over -rely on AI
recommendations as cognitive shortcuts, reducing their independent search by up to 40% and adopting
platform preferences as if they were their own (SSRN, 2025). FOMO -driven interventions (e.g., flash deals
and varia ble pricing) elevate emotional arousal, increasing checkout speed on one hand but also
heightening post -purchase regret for 15 –18% of consumers on the other, particularly in discretionary
spending categories. Users often perceive autonomy during these sequ ences which are recommended for
them to be intentional yet cannot detect manipulation or control the underlying data flows, creating the
paradox of felt agency without actual choice latitude (Matz et al., 2017).

6.5 Convenience and Autonomy Trade-off in User Perceptions
Empirical research consistently indicates that users experience AI-mediated nudging as both beneficial and
intrusive at the same time. Judith Irene Maria de Groot (de Groot, 2022) found that while personalised
recommendations and streamlined checkouts redu ced decision effort for 72% of users thereby making
consumers value convenience, 62% simultaneously expressed concerns about privacy intrusion, opaque
targeting and loss of agency when algorithmic nudges guide their decisions. This paradox becomes
particularly visible in emotionally sensitive contexts. Matz  ( Matz et al., 2017) documents that platforms
sometimes infer user distress through sentiment analysis and deploy emotionally targeted nudges to
increase engagement. Although this approach results in a 68 percent improvement in task completion, 55
percent of users report a lasting feeling of discomfort because the system appears to take advantage of
vulnerable psychological states (Chun & Rha, 2025). Attempts to mitigate concerns through transparency
do not consistently reduce uneasiness. Carella (Carella & Rebitschek, 2025) demonstrates that even when
users are told that personalisation is occurring, compliance with nudges continues at approximately 40
percent, while Michelson (2020) finds that transparency can actually decrease the perceived fairness of a
nudge when users become aware of the depth of behavioural profiling that underpins it. The cumulative
effect of these findings shows that the empirical lived experience of nudging is characterised by a trade-off
between perceived convenience and perceived loss of autonomy.
6.6 Behavioural Convergence, Preference Shaping and Post -Decision Dissonance
Empirical studies also reveal a consistent pattern of behavioural convergence in which consumers
increasingly substitute independent exploration with algorithmic recommendations. Users exposed to
sequential nudges view fewer alternative options over time, and exploration has been observed to decrease
by 25 to 40 percent per session on average (Cal Poly RecSys , 2025; Jesse, 2021). This over-reliance shapes
not only short-term choices but potentially user preferences themselves. Experimental evidence shows that
individuals frequently purchase items highlighted by recommendation engines despite rating other
products more highly in non -nudged conditions, which supports the presence of systematic preference –
action mismatch. Habituation to persuasive cues is also observed. Urgency prompts increase impulsive
purchases by 35 percent during early exposures but decline to 12 percent after repeated cycles, while post-
purchase regret increases to between 15 and 18 percent in discretionary spending categories
(Zimmermann et al., 2023; Matz et al., 2017). Vulnerable users, particularly those with lower levels of
digital liter acy, experience the most pronounced negative effects and report 45 percent higher rates of
regret after scarcity -based nudges. Empirical research further indicates that users often maintain a
subjective sense of control during purchase decisions, even when  their actions are strongly influenced by
algorithmic sequencing. This sense of autonomy persists despite the fact that the underlying mechanisms
remain inaccessible, non -interpretable and non -modifiable to the user. Across multiple studies, the
empirical evidence converges on a central observation: AI -driven nudging consistently improves
commercial performance by increasing conversions, recovering abandoned carts and reducing cognitive
effort, yet these outcomes occur alongside heightened ambivalence, redu ced reflective decision -making
and growing calls for ethical safeguards against excessively persuasive AI.

7. Discussion
7.1 Shift from Libertarian Paternalism to Algorithmic Paternalism
The findings of this study demonstrate a paradigmatic transition in online retail from Thaler and Sunstein’s
(2008) notion of libertarian paternalism where nudges were designed to improve welfare without
restricting choice to algorithmic paternalism, in which AI optimises behavioural influence based primarily
on commercial objectives rather than on user benefit amplifying power asymmetries via opaque real-layer
structures (Yeung, 2016). The introduction of machine learning, psychometric profiling and real -time
personalisation has transformed nudging from a light-touch behavioural prompt into a hyper-personalised
influence system that anticipates cognitive vulnerabilities and continuously adapts to maximise
conversions, engagement and revenue. This shift impl ies that ethical concerns cannot be adequately
addressed at the level of the interface alone. Instead, mechanisms embedded in the real layer including data
infrastructures, commercial optimisation targets, clustering algorithms and black -box inference mode ls
must be subject to transparency, oversight and accountability thus making the retroduction method
proposed by Bhaskar(2008) to be of paramount value. The discussion therefore synthesises the ethical and
managerial implications of algorithmic nudging in digital retail and outlines future research directions that
support responsible deployment of persuasive AI.
7.2 Ethical Implications
Ongoing ethical debates surrounding AI -mediated nudging have intensified as scholars recognise that the
field has moved beyond welfare -enhancing nudges to computational optimisation strategies that operate
at scale and below the threshold of consciousness (Yeung, 2017). Users are increasingly exposed to
influence patterns that exploit heuristic tendencies and behavioural biases without their awareness, raising
concerns about manipulation, autonomy erosion and violations of dignity ( Schmauder, 2023).This sh ift
necessitates explainable AI (XAI) targeting the real layer's data infrastructures and black -box models to
expose generative mechanisms, enabling transparency mandates that reveal how behavioral data fuels
hypernudging and psychometric targeting (Susser et al., 2019; Li, 2020). The ethical tension is heightened
because individuals exhibit bounded rationality and rarely engage in the full deliberative cost –benefit
evaluations assumed by the rational agent model (Mertens et al., 2021). These vulnerabilities are
intensified by AI’s capacity to identify and target the “sweet spot” of manipulation with high precision, as
Sætra (2019) explains, and by the possibility that behaviours repeatedly shaped by AI may obliterate the
behaviour-action loop making decisions powered by nudges into durable preferences over time (Ariely and
Norton, 2008). Hauser et al. (2009) provide a constructive alternative, showing that matching website
structure, information density and navigation to co gnitive styles can support user autonomy rather than
exploit it. Similarly, research in NeuroIS highlights the potential for neuro -adaptive systems to enhance
usability without reducing agency when designed with ethical intent (Dimoka et al., 2010; vom  Brocke &
Liang, 2014). Yet meaningful ethical governance remains difficult because algorithmic persuasion is often
structurally opaque. Arrieta et al. (2020) note that explainable AI is essential for accountability, but
transparency features can reduce model performance, creating a tension between persuasion efficacy and
ethical responsibility. For these reasons, ethical evaluation must shift focus from visible UI cues to real -
layer decision architectures, including objective functions, data provenance and model evolution dynamics.
This structural orientation is vital to prevent the conversion of freedom into programmable behaviour and
to preserve the conditions for authentic choice in digital marketplaces and and long -term effects like
preference shaping, demanding shared preference justifications for legitimacy (Clavien, 2018; Kuyer et al.,
2023; Tamilarasi, 2025).
7.3 Managerial Implications
Managerial responsibility now extends far beyond UX design into the governance of underlying
optimisation structures. Organisations require access to proprietary models and audit trails at the
mechanism level to detect discriminatory patterns, excessive fr equency of exposure, dark patterns and
cross-channel coercion (Yeung, 2016). However, measuring autonomy degradation is inherently difficult
because susceptibility thresholds differ across individuals and contexts, and the subliminal threshold at
which persuasion becomes coercion is neither universal nor static and different from customer to customer
based on geographic, economic, psychological and cultural factors. Vulnerable users, such as those with low
digital literacy, show disproportionately higher impulsivity and regret responses to scarcity nudges, which

compliance (Bacalja, Beavis, & O’Brien, 2022). Managers therefore need implementation strategies that are
both commercially effective and ethically oriented, including win-win nudges that explicitly align economic
incentives with user welfare, real -time disclosure dashboards, opt -out pathways and cr oss-channel
sequencing visibility. While 62 percent of users express privacy and manipulation concerns, most still value

advantage will depend on the integration of persuasive efficiency with governance mechanisms that
deliberately protect consumer agency, rather than relying solely on optimisation -driven behavioural
engineering.

8. Conclusion and Future Research
This research shows that AI nudging is most accurately conceptualised as a stratified phenomenon in which
real-layer mechanisms generate actual -layer events and empirical -layer effects on user experience and
autonomy. The progression from static digital nu dging to hypernudging  by AI choice architects that
outperform human choice architects raises a societal question: how can markets preserve the economic
benefits of personalisation without undermining the cognitive and emotional conditions for self -
determined choice? Future pro gress requires rigorous interdisciplinary research bridging AI ethics,
marketing, behavioural science and HCI to formulate ontologies and metrics that reliably evaluate long -
term autonomy outcomes (Bhaskar, 2008) .  Promising directions include experimental testing of
explainable AI interventions in retail interfaces, longitudinal mapping of habituation and dependence
across demographics, and regulatory prototypes such as enforced transparency on feed -ranking logic and
EU-style “right to explanation” impact audits. There is also a need for cross -cultural and emerging-market
studies to assess how nudging effectiveness and ethical boundaries vary across contexts (Zimmermann et
al., 2023). Only through such integration of marketing ethics, persuasive technology and accountable AI can
digital marketplaces sustain trust, protect agency and ensure that intelligent persuasion advances
consumer welfare rather than gradually displacing it.
