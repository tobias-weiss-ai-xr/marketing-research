Generative AI chatbots are increasingly used as interactive formative feedback
tools in higher education, yet it remains unclear whether positive feedback
experiences translate into students’ intentions for continued academic use.
This study examines an instructor-designed generative AI chatbot embedded
in a project-based undergraduate digital marketing course. Data were
collected from 100 students who interacted with a course-embedded chatbot
that provided criteria-based feedback on campaign presentations. Survey
measures assessed students’ cognitive perceptions of feedback quality,
emotional perceptions of support, and continued-use intention. Multiple and
simple regression analyses showed that students evaluated the chatbot
feedback positively in terms of clarity, usefulness, guidance, support, respect,
and empathy. The findings suggest a possible experience–intention
disconnect, defined here not as low continued-use intention, but as a lack of
statistically reliable association between positive feedback evaluations and
variation in students’ future-use intentions. This pattern should be interpreted
cautiously given the marginal reliability of the continued-use intention
measure and the limited dispersion of the study variables.

Introduction
Generative artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots, powered by large language models, are
rapidly transforming feedback practices in higher education. Their ability to engage in
natural language dialogue, provide instant explanations, and deliver personalized,
formative feedback on complex assignments positions them as potentially powerful
pedagogical tools (Chang et al., 2023; Kohnke et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2025). Early
evidence suggests that AI-generated feedback can support reflective learning, enhance
content mastery, and reduce instructor workload, particularly in large-enrollment
courses in which it is difficult for instructors to provide timely, individualized feedback
(Gregorac et al., 2025; Smutny and Schreiberova, 2020).
However, positive evaluations of AI tools do not always provide a sufficient
explanation for students’ future academic use. In formal higher-education contexts,
students’ decisions to continue using generative AI tools may be shaped not only by
perceived feedback quality, but also by institutional expectations, academic-integrity

emotional perceptions of chatbot feedback predict students’
intentions to continue using chatbots in future academic
contexts and by considering which contextual factors may help
explain this disconnect in formal educational settings.
Cognitive and emotional perceptions of AI
feedback
Studies on human–AI interactions tend to distinguish between
two complementary dimensions of user experience: the cognitive
pathway and the emotional pathway (Jin and Eastin, 2022; Liu
and Sundar, 2018). The cognitive pathway encompasses
perceptions of information quality, clarity, organization, and
instrumental value. In the context of AI-generated feedback, this
includes judgments about whether the feedback is
understandable, actionable, aligned with learning objectives, and
effective in improving performance. These perceptions align
with constructs such as Perceived Usefulness and Performance
Expectancy in technology adoption models [i.e., technology
acceptance model (TAM), Davis, 1989; and the unified theory of
acceptance and use of technology model (UTAUT), Venkatesh
et al., 2003]. Studies have demonstrated that high-quality,
metacognitive feedback from AI chatbots—feedback that
prompts learners to reflect on their reasoning, identify gaps, and
strategize improvements—can enhance learning outcomes,
promote knowledge transfer, and strengthen learners’ sense of
control over their learning processes (Chang et al., 2023;
Chukwuere, 2024).
The emotional pathway refers to affective responses elicited
during interaction with AI systems, including feelings of
support, empathy, respect, and social presence. According to the
computers-as-social-actors paradigm, users often unconsciously
attribute human-like qualities to machines that employ social
cues, such as empathetic language, personalized address, or
conversational tone (Liu and Sundar, 2018; Nass and Moon,
2000). When chatbots express empathy or warmth, users report
greater satisfaction, reduced anxiety, and greater engagement,
even when they are aware that they are interacting with an
algorithm (Juquelier et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2025). Emotional
support has been identified as a critical factor in sustained
technology use, particularly in educational contexts in which
learners may feel vulnerable when receiving evaluative feedback
(Behforouz and Al Ghaithi, 2024).
Together, these pathways constitute what Behforouz and Al
Ghaithi (2024) termed perceived support, a higher-order
construct integrating both cognitive utility and emotional
reassurance. Previous research has suggested that both pathways
contribute independently and interactively to user satisfaction
and technology acceptance (Rahman et al., 2025). However, few
studies have empirically tested whether these pathways predict
continued-use intentions in formal academic contexts,
particularly for generative AI tools designed to provide
formative feedback.
From feedback quality to continued-Use
intention
Technology-adoption models have long posited a linear
relationship between quality perceptions and behavioral
intentions. The technology acceptance model (TAM; Davis,
1989) proposes that perceived usefulness directly influences
intention to use a particular technology, which, in turn,
predicts the actual adoption of that technology. Similarly, the
unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT;
Venkatesh et al., 2003) positions expected utility and ease of
use as primary determinants of intention to use a particular
tool or system.
Extensions of these models have incorporated affective
dimensions. For instance, perceived enjoyment and perceived
emotional support have been shown to mediate relationships
between system quality and use intentions, particularly in the
context of hedonic or learning-oriented technologies (Behforouz
and Al Ghaithi, 2024; Rahman et al., 2025). Collectively, these
frameworks share a core assumption: If users perceive a tool as
both cognitively useful and emotionally supportive, they will
express stronger intentions to adopt it.
However, this assumption may not hold uniformly across
contexts. Emerging research in educational AI suggests a
disconnect between perceived experience quality and adoption
intentions. For instance, students may evaluate AI tools as useful
yet refrain from adopting them due to concerns about academic
integrity and legitimacy (Sullivan et al., 2023).
In addition, positive experiences may be overshadowed by
social stigma (“I don’t want to seem lazy”) or identity threat
(“I want to develop my own skills”; Juquelier et al., 2025).
Finally, even when tools perform well in scaffolded contexts,
students may perceive institutional ambiguity about
appropriate use, leading them to be hesitant about using those
tools despite their satisfaction with their performance (Elkot
et al., 2025).
These findings suggest that perceived quality may be a
necessary, but insufficient condition for adoption, particularly in
formal educational settings in which use carries implications for
academic legitimacy, autonomy, and identity. Despite the
theoretical and practical importance of this issue, few studies
have directly tested whether cognitive and emotional pathways
predict continued-use intentions for generative AI chatbots
providing academic feedback. Moreover, existing research has
largely assumed, but not empirically verified, that positive
quality perceptions translate into behavioral commitment. This
study addressed this gap by examining the predictive power of
both pathways, while remaining open to the possibility that they
may fail to predict intentions, a pattern we refer to as the
experience–intention disconnect.

Research questions
Given the theoretical framework outlined above, this study
examined a central question about the adoption of Generative
AI chatbots for academic feedback: To what extent do cognitive
and emotional perceptions of AI chatbot feedback predict
students’ continued-use intentions in future academic contexts?
Specifically, this study tested:
1. Whether cognitive-pathway perceptions (i.e., clarity,
usefulness, contribution to learning) significantly predict
continued-use intention.
2. Whether emotional-pathway perceptions (i.e., support,
respect, empathy) significantly predict continued-
use intention.
3. Whether these pathways jointly explain meaningful
variance in continued-use intention when entered into a
multiple regression model.
Given the limited empirical evidence on generative AI feedback
tools in formal higher-education contexts, this study was framed
as exploratory rather than strictly confirmatory. TAM, UTAUT,
and prior chatbot research suggest that cognitive and emotional
evaluations may be positively associated with continued-use
intention. However, the present study examined whether such
associations would emerge in a course-embedded, instructor-
supported generative AI feedback context, without treating null
findings as evidence against these broader models.
This study contributes to educational technology research by
examining whether students’ cognitive and emotional
evaluations of GPT-4-based formative feedback are associated
with continued-use intention in a formal course context. Rather
than presenting the experience–intention disconnect as an
established phenomenon, the study offers it as a cautious
interpretive lens for describing a pattern in which positive
feedback evaluations do not reliably predict variation in future-
use intention.
Materials and methods
Study context and participants
This study employed a quantitative cross-sectional survey
design to examine whether cognitive and emotional perceptions
of AI-generated feedback predict students’ continued-use
intentions. The study was conducted within an undergraduate
Digital Marketing course, a required third-year course for
Marketing majors at Jerusalem Multidisciplinary College,
Jerusalem, Israel. The course employs a project-based learning
approach in which students develop and present marketing
campaign proposals. As part of the formative feedback process
for their campaign presentations, students interacted with a
generative AI chatbot. A total of 100 students completed the
survey and constituted the final analytical sample. After
completing their interactions with the chatbot, students were
invited to complete an anonymous online questionnaire
assessing their perceptions of feedback quality (in terms of
cognitive and emotional pathways) and their intentions to use
the chatbot in future academic contexts. Participation was
voluntary and did not affect course grades.
Chatbot design and implementation
The chatbot was implemented using a GPT-4-based
conversational AI environment configured by the instructor and
embedded in the Digital Marketing course as a formative
feedback tool for students’ campaign presentations. It was
designed to provide criteria-based formative feedback aligned
with the course assessment requirements. The feedback criteria
focused on the clarity of the value proposition, audience
targeting, creative execution, strategic alignment, visual
communication, and practical recommendations for improvement.
Students uploaded their presentation slides to the chatbot and
received written feedback before completing the study
questionnaire. The feedback included three main components:
identification of strengths, identification of areas requiring
improvement, and actionable suggestions for revision. The
chatbot was not used for grading and did not replace instructor
assessment. Rather, it was introduced as a low-stakes learning-
support tool intended to help students improve their campaign
presentations before final submission or presentation.
The chatbot was configured by the instructor to provide
feedback aligned with the course rubric and the learning
objectives of the assignment. The prompt instructed the chatbot
to evaluate the uploaded presentation according to the course
criteria, avoid assigning grades, provide constructive and
pedagogically appropriate comments, and formulate specific
suggestions for improvement. The chatbot was also instructed to
use supportive and respectful language and to distinguish
between strengths and areas for revision.
Prior to student use, the chatbot was pilot-tested by the
instructor using sample presentations. The pilot-testing process
focused on feedback clarity, alignment with the course criteria,
pedagogical appropriateness, and avoidance of overly generic or
unsupported comments. Following this pilot testing, the
feedback instructions were refined to improve consistency
and relevance.
Feedback procedure and student
interaction
Students used the chatbot as part of the formative preparation
process for their digital marketing campaign presentations. After
preparing draft presentation slides, students uploaded their
slides to the GPT-4-based chatbot and received written
formative feedback. The feedback was generated immediately
during the interaction and was intended to support revision
before the final presentation or submission. Students were
encouraged to review the chatbot feedback and revise their
slides accordingly, but the extent of revision was not
systematically recorded.
All students followed the same general procedure: they
prepared campaign presentation slides, interacted with the
chatbot at least twice, reviewed the feedback, and were then
invited to complete the anonymous online questionnaire. The

Instructor support and context of use
Instructor support was an important part of the
implementation context. The instructor explained the chatbot’s
educational purpose, demonstrated its use in class, and framed
it as a legitimate learning-support tool aligned with the course
goals. However, beyond the required presentation assignment,
continued chatbot use was not mandated, incentivized, or
formally integrated into subsequent coursework. This distinction
is important for interpreting the experience–intention
disconnect: students experienced the tool within a supported
and structured activity, but future use depended on their own
willingness to adopt it independently.
Participants and data collection
All 125 students enrolled in a Digital Marketing course
(Spring 2025 semester) were invited to participate. The course is
a required third-year undergraduate course for Marketing
majors at the college.
After completing the campaign presentation assignment and
interacting with the chatbot at least twice (M = 2.7 interactions,
SD = 1.1), students received an email invitation to complete an
anonymous online survey via Qualtrics. Data were collected
during the final two weeks of the semester in May 2025, after
institutional ethics approval had been obtained. No incentives
were provided, but students were informed that their feedback
would inform future course design. Participation was voluntary
and did not affect course grades.
Response rate and nonresponse analysis
One hundred students completed the survey, yielding an 80%
response rate. This rate exceeds recommended thresholds for
educational research (Baruch and Holtom, 2008). To assess
potential self-selection bias, we compared respondents and non-
respondents in terms of demographic variables available in
enrollment records (i.e., gender, age group). Chi-square tests
revealed no significant differences (all χ² tests, p > .30),
suggesting that non-responses did not systematically bias
the sample.
Measures:
Three composite measures were used to assess students’
perceptions of the chatbot feedback and their continued-use
intention. The items were developed for the present study and
aligned with the pedagogical purpose of the GPT-4-based
formative feedback intervention. Because the chatbot was
embedded in a digital marketing course and provided feedback
on campaign presentations, the items were designed to reflect
students’ experience of feedback clarity, usefulness, emotional
support, and willingness to use the tool in future academic tasks.
All items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from
1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. Composite scores
were calculated as the mean of the relevant items, with higher
scores indicating more positive perceptions or stronger
continued-use intention. The questionnaire was administered in
Hebrew. The items were reviewed for clarity, relevance to the
course context, and alignment with the chatbot feedback
activity. Because the items were adapted to the specific
instructional context rather than taken directly from a
previously validated scale, the findings should be interpreted
with appropriate caution regarding construct validity.
Cognitive pathway
The cognitive pathway measure included nine items assessing
students’ perceptions of the informational and instructional
quality of the chatbot feedback, including clarity, usefulness,
organization, contribution to understanding, identification of
strengths and weaknesses, and guidance for improvement.
Sample items included: “The chatbot feedback was clear and
understandable,” “The chatbot feedback helped me understand
how to improve my presentation,” and “The chatbot feedback
identified strengths and weaknesses in my work.” The scale
showed excellent internal consistency (α = .95).
Emotional pathway
The emotional pathway measure included four items assessing
students’ affective experience of the chatbot interaction, including
perceptions of support, personalization, respect, and empathic
concern for learning progress. Sample items included: “The
chatbot feedback made me feel supported,” “The chatbot
responded in a respectful way,” and “The chatbot feedback gave
me the feeling that my learning progress was taken into
account.” The scale showed good internal consistency (α = .85).
Continued-use intention
The continued-use intention measure included five items
assessing students’ willingness to use the chatbot in future
assignments, recommend it to peers, and integrate it into their
study routines. Sample items included: “I would use this chatbot
again for future academic assignments,” “I would recommend
this chatbot to other students,” and “I would consider
integrating this chatbot into my regular study routines.” The
scale showed marginal internal consistency (α = .67).
Given this marginal reliability, the continued-use intention
measure should be interpreted cautiously. The items may reflect
related but not fully identical aspects of future use, including

Data analysis
All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Version
29.0. Prior to hypothesis testing, data were screened for missing
values, outliers, and distributional assumptions.
Data screening
No missing values were observed (100% item completion
rate). Mahalanobis distance analysis identified three multivariate
outliers (p < .001). These cases were retained as they represented
valid, non-erroneous responses within the plausible range.
Skewness and kurtosis values for all variables fell within
acceptable ranges (|skew|<2.0, |kurtosis|<7.0; Curran et al.,
1996), indicating no severe violations of normality.
Regression diagnostics
To ensure the validity of regression analyses, several
assumptions were tested. Scatterplots of predictors against the
outcome variable confirmed linear relationships. Examination of
residual plots revealed no evidence of heteroscedasticity
(constant error variance). To check for the independence of
errors, the Durbin-Watson statistic was calculated and found to
be 1.94, which falls within the acceptable range (1.5–2.5). In
addition, variance inflation factors were below 2.0 for all
predictors, indicating no problematic collinearity.
Power analysis
A post hoc power analysis using G*Power 3.1.9.7 (Faul et al.,
2009) indicated that with N = 100, α = .05, and two predictors,
the study had approximately 80% power to detect a medium
effect size (Cohen’s f² = .15). However, the observed effect size
in the multiple regression model was much smaller (f² = .022).
Therefore, the study was not sufficiently powered to rule out
small effects. The null findings should consequently be
interpreted as indicating that no medium-sized predictive
relationship was detected in this sample, rather than as
definitive evidence that no small association exists.
Analytical strategy
To address the research questions, we conducted descriptive
analyses, calculating means, standard deviations, 95% confidence
intervals, and Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for all composite
measures. We also conducted both multiple and simple
regression analyses. In the multiple-regression model, cognitive
and emotional pathways were entered simultaneously as
predictors of continued-use intention, to assess their unique
contributions while controlling for shared variance. To rule out
suppression effects or multicollinearity, each pathway was also
tested separately in a simple regression model. Statistical
significance was evaluated at α = .05 using two-tailed t-tests. In
addition to p-values, we report effect sizes (Cohen’s f² for
models; standardized β coefficients for predictors) and 95%
confidence intervals, to provide a comprehensive assessment of
effect magnitude and precision.
Results
Participant characteristics
Out of the 125 students enrolled in the Digital Marketing
course, 100 completed the survey, yielding a response rate of
80%. Examination of course enrollment records indicated no
systematic differences between respondents and nonrespondents
in observable characteristics (i.e., gender and age group),
suggesting minimal self-selection bias. The demographic
composition of the final sample across three key variables—
gender, age group, and native languages—is presented in Table 1.
As shown in Table 1, the sample was predominantly female

institution and the broader regional context. All participants
were enrolled in a third-year undergraduate course in digital
marketing and had prior exposure to digital tools and online
learning platforms, though this was their first structured
experience with an AI-powered chatbot designed to provide
academic feedback.

Correlation analysis
To further examine the relationships among the study
variables, we added a correlation matrix including the cognitive

interpreted cautiously and may reflect shared variance between
predictors, limited dispersion, and measurement constraints
rather than a clear absence of association.
Multiple regression analysis
To examine the extent to which cognitive and emotional
pathways predict students’ continued-use intention, a standard
multiple regression analysis was conducted. Both pathways were
entered simultaneously as predictors, allowing us to assess their
unique contributions while controlling for shared variance. Prior
to the analysis, we verified that assumptions of linearity, normality
of residuals, homoscedasticity, and independence of errors were
met. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors,
with both predictors showing values for variance inflation factors
that were below 2.0, indicating acceptable levels of
multicollinearity. In addition to statistical significance testing, we
report effect sizes (Cohen’s f²) and 95% confidence intervals to
provide a comprehensive assessment of the magnitude and
precision of observed effects. The full regression results are
presented in Table 4.
As shown in Table 4, the overall model was not statistically
significant [F(2, 97) = 1.09, p = .34] and explained only 2.2% of
the variance in continued-use intention [R² = .022, 95% CI
(.000,.082)]. Cohen’s f² = .022 indicates a small observed effect
size (Cohen, 1988). Given the confidence interval around R² and
the study’s power to detect medium rather than small effects, this
estimate should be interpreted cautiously. Neither the cognitive
pathway (b = −0.13, β = −.15, p = .41, f² = .01) nor the emotional
pathway (b = 0.01, β = .01, p = .97, f² < .001) emerged as a
significant predictor of continued-use intention. The 95%
confidence intervals for both regression coefficients included zero,
indicating substantial uncertainty around these point estimates.
These findings suggest a possible disconnect between students’
positive evaluations of the chatbot feedback and their continued-
use intentions. However, this pattern should be interpreted
cautiously because the continued-use intention scale showed
marginal reliability and the study variables displayed limited
dispersion. Thus, the null findings may reflect, at least in part,
measurement limitations and restricted variability rather than a
definitive absence of association.

(Model 2). This approach allowed us to examine whether either
pathway demonstrated predictive utility when evaluated in
isolation, without competition from the other predictor. The

continued-use intention measure, the limited dispersion of the
variables, and the small observed effect sizes, these findings
should be interpreted cautiously. Rather than demonstrating the
absence of any relationship, the results suggest that no medium-
sized predictive association was detected in the present study.
The change in the emotional-pathway coefficient from the
simple to the multiple regression model should not be interpreted
substantively because the coefficient was not statistically significant
in either model. Rather, this change may reflect shared variance
between the cognitive and emotional pathways, as indicated by
their strong correlation, as well as conceptual overlap between the
two perception measures. Similarly, the negative cognitive-pathway
coefficient was not statistically significant and should not be
interpreted as evidence of a negative theoretical relationship.
Discussion
Main findings and relation to prior research
This study examined whether students’ cognitive and
emotional perceptions of generative AI chatbot feedback were
associated with their intentions for continued academic use.
Students generally evaluated the feedback favorably in terms of
clarity, usefulness, support, respect, and empathy. However,
individual differences in these evaluations were not reliably
associated with individual differences in continued-use
intention. This pattern should be interpreted cautiously given
the marginal reliability of the continued-use intention measure,
limited variability in the study variables, and the study’s limited
power to detect small effects.
These findings differ from prior research reporting stronger
associations between perceived usefulness or support and
intentions to use educational chatbots (Behforouz and Al
Ghaithi, 2024; Rahman et al., 2025). One possible reason
concerns differences in implementation context. In the present
study, the chatbot was introduced and legitimized by the
instructor within a specific course assignment, but continued
use was not required or systematically integrated into
subsequent coursework. Students may therefore have evaluated
the immediate feedback positively without necessarily viewing
the chatbot as part of their broader academic routines. Because
implementation conditions were not directly compared across
studies, this interpretation remains tentative and requires
future comparative research.
Interpreting the possible experience–
intention disconnect
The experience–intention disconnect is used here as a tentative
interpretive lens rather than as a confirmed empirical phenomenon.
In this study, positive perceptions of feedback quality did not
reliably explain variation in continued-use intention. This finding
does not challenge TAM or UTAUT directly. Rather, it suggests
that the cognitive and emotional feedback perceptions measured
here were insufficient to account for future-use intention in this
particular course-embedded context.
Several contextual factors may plausibly influence the
transition from a positive feedback experience to future use.
These include institutional ambiguity regarding appropria te
AI use, academic-integrity concerns, social norms, identity-
related concerns, effort expectancy, and lack of sustained
normativ e support ( Juquelier et al., 2025; Rahman et al., 2025;
Sullivan et al., 2023; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Because none of
these factors was directly measured, they should be regarded
as theoretically informed possibilities for future investigation
rather than explanations demonstr ated by the present study.
Figure 1 summarizes this interpretation by distinguishing
between evaluation of AI feedback within a structured course
activity and intention to use the tool in future academic
contexts. The figure represents a conceptual interpretation and
not a tested mediation or moderation model.

The findings carry practical implications for instructors,
institutions, and chatbot designers. For instructors, the results

Limitations
Several limitations should be considered. First, the single-
course, single-institution design limits generalizability. Second, the
cross-sectional self-report design prevents causal inference, and
behavioral usage data were not available. In addition, the study
relied on self-report measures collected through the same
questionnaire at a single time point. This design introduces the
possibility of common method bias and may have influenced the
observed associations among variables. Although the anonymous
survey format may have reduced social desirability concerns, the
absence of behavioral usage data and temporally separated
measurements limits the ability to rule out common method bias.

Future research directions
Future research should examine the experience–intention
disconnect across different disciplines, institutions, and cultural
contexts. Longitudinal and behavioral studies are needed to test
whether positive AI feedback experiences translate into actual
use over time. Experimental and comparative studies could
examine whether policy clarity, instructor endorsement, and
pedagogical framing strengthen the link between perceived
feedback quality and continued-use intention. Qualitative
interviews may also help explain why students value AI feedback
yet remain hesitant to adopt it independently.
Conclusion
This study contributes to the growing literature on generative
AI in education by identifying a possible disconnect between
positive AI feedback experiences and continued-use intentions.
Students perceived the chatbot feedback as cognitively useful
and emotionally supportive, yet these positive perceptions did
not predict their intention to use the tool in future academic
work. This finding raises questions about the assumption,
common in technology-adoption models such as TAM and
UTAUT, that perceived usefulness and support necessarily
translate into stronger behavioral intention. The findings suggest
that, in formal higher-education contexts, continued-use
intention may depend not only on feedback quality but also on
broader pedagogical, institutional, and social conditions that
should be examined directly in future research.
For educators and institutions, this possible experience–intention
disconnect suggests that high-quality AI tools require contextual
support. Sustained use may require clear policies, pedagogical
framing, normative endorsement, and transparency about how AI
feedback aligns with learning objectives and assessment criteria.
Without such support, even useful tools may remain underused.
