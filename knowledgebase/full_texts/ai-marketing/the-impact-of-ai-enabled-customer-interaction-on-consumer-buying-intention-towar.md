ABSTRACT:
AI-enabled customer interaction, delivered through recommendation
systems, chatbots and virtual assistants, is increasingly used by housing and
real-estate platforms to engage prospective buyers. Housing  is a high-value,
high-involvement purchase in which trust and accurate information matter,
yet evidence on how AI-enabled interaction relates to buying intention in an
Indian metropolitan market such  as Bangalore remains limited. This study
examines the adoption and usage of AI-enabled customer interaction tools
among prospective housing buyers, their impact on and relationship with
buying intention, and the challenges buyers report. A quantitative, cross -
sectional survey was conducted with a structured five -point Likert
questionnaire, and 214 valid responses collected between 19 and 25 August
2026 were analysed with IBM SPSS Statistics and jamovi using descriptive
statistics, Cronbach's  alpha, one-sample t tests, multiple linear regression,
and Pearson and Spearman correlation. Adoption was moderate -to-high
(item means 3.26 –3.48; composite = 3.373; α = .849). AI -enabled
interaction was significantly and positively correlated with buying intention
(Pearson r = .337; Spearman ρ = .349; p < .001). A regression of the five AI
items on the outcome was significant but explained a modest share of
variance (R² = .056; F(5, 208) = 2.490; p = .032), and no individual item
was a significant unique predictor. The most prominent challenges were a
preference for human interaction at final negotiation (M = 3.36) and
technical issues (M = 3.27); the challenge scale was reliable (α = .792).
Three of four hypotheses were supported and one was partly supported. The
study extends AI -marketing research to the housing context and supports a hybrid AI -plus-human approach: AI for
search and first enquiries, human contact for negotiation, and reliable technology throughout. Because the design is
cross-sectional, the findings describe association rather than causation.

virtual assistants guide users through search, comparison and enquiry.

In retail, banking, hospitality and travel these tools are now routine touchpoints 1, and real estate is following the same
path: housing platforms and developers increasingly use AI to answer buyer queries, shortlist properties and personalise
the search experience.
Housing, however, is not an ordinary purchase. It is a high -value, high-involvement and largely irreversible decision
in which trust, accurate information and timely responses weigh heavily on the buyer.  The market is characterised by
information asymmetry, high transaction values and long decision cycles, and  regulatory developments such as the
Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 have raised the emphasis on transparent project information 2.
Whether an AI -enabled interaction genuinely  builds a buyer's intention to purchase, or is offset by concerns such as
impersonal responses, technical faults and a preference for dealing with a human negotiator, is therefore an open
empirical question. Research on AI touchpoints shows that personalisation aids purchase evaluation while privacy and
transparency concerns can undermine trust 3, and research on virtual shopping assistants indicates that  purchase
decisions are strongest at a medium level of algorithmic autonomy4. AI interaction is thus neither uniformly beneficial
nor uniformly resisted; its effect depends on how it is designed and where in the decision journey it is used.
Three streams of theory frame the question. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) explains adoption through
perceived usefulness and ease of use 5; the Theory of Planned Behaviour treats intention  as the immediate antecedent
of behaviour6; and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) adds performance expectancy,
effort expectancy, social influence and facilitating  conditions7. Together they suggest that a buyer who finds an AI
tool useful  and easy to use is more likely to keep using it, and that a favourable experience can strengthen the
intention to act on what it recommends, while slow, inaccurate or unresponsive tools weaken it.
Despite the growth of AI-marketing research, the existing literature has important shortcomings. First, most studies
concentrate on retail, banking, hospitality, fashion and e -commerce rather than housing. Second, empirical evidence
from Bangalore, widely regarded as India's technology capital with an active  residential property market, or from
Indian housing buyers in general is limited. Third, adoption, buying intention and AI -related challenges are rarely
examined together in one respondent group. Against this background, the objectives of the present study are: (i)  to
assess the level of adoption and usage  of AI-enabled customer interaction tools among prospective housing buyers in
Bangalore; (ii) to examine  the impact of AI-enabled customer interaction on consumers' buying intention towards
housing properties; (iii) to determine the relationship between AI -enabled customer interaction and buying intention;
and (iv) to identify the challenges or limitations consumers face when interacting with AI-enabled tools in the housing
purchase process.
LITERATURE REVIEW:
This section reviews fourteen recent studies (2020–2026) on AI in marketing and consumer behaviour, summarised in
Table 1, together with the foundational technology-acceptance theories. The studies are grouped by theme rather than
by country because their sectors and contexts differ widely. Where the findings of a study were available they are
reported; for the remaining studies only the focus and its relevance to the present research are described.
AI Positioning, Trust and Technology Adoption
Zaman and colleagues studied AI -driven brand positioning and smart -product adoption and  found that perceived
usefulness mediates the path from AI positioning to adoption, while digital  trust strengthens the effect 8. Trust and
perceived usefulness are therefore plausible drivers of housing buyers' use of AI tools. Chiu and Chen examined
travellers' accommodation intention towards smart hotels using SEM and  fsQCA and reported that novelty, efficacy
and compatibility lead to perceived value, which in turn leads to accommodation intention9. Accommodation is the
closest reviewed context to housing, and the study shows how technology attributes translate into intention through
perceived value.
Chatbots, Voice Assistants and Virtual Shopping Assistants
Mahajan reported a TAM-based chain from anthropomorphism to perceived usefulness and ease of use, then to
acceptance and purchase intention, for voice assistants 10, a chain directly relevant to virtual assistants on housing
platforms. Misra and colleagues found that purchase decisions are strongest at a medium level of algorithmic decision

Theoretical Foundations
TAM proposes that perceived usefulness and ease of use determine attitudes and intention to use a technology5; in this
study, ease of use (AI2) and the use of chatbots and recommendation tools (AI1, AI3) map onto this logic. The Theory
of Planned Behaviour holds that intention, shaped by attitude, subjective  norms and perceived control, is the proximal
predictor of behaviour6, which justifies buying intention as the outcome. UTAUT allows technical issues (CL2) to be
read as a  shortfall in facilitating conditions and accuracy problems (CL3) as a shortfall in performance expectancy7.
Across the reviewed literature  the common themes are perceived usefulness,  ease of use, personalisation, trust,
satisfaction, human interaction, purchase intention and AI transparency, with effects moderated by privacy concerns
and a persistent preference for human contact at key decision points.

RESEARCH GAPS:
• Contextual gap: Most prior studies on AI-enabled customer interaction focus on retail, banking, hospitality,
fashion and e-commerce; housing and real estate are comparatively under-researched3,9,12.
• Geographic gap: Limited empirical evidence exists from Bangalore or from Indian housing buyers
specifically; India-anchored AI work is concentrated in banking and consumer products1,18.
• Integration gap: Few studies combine AI adoption and interaction, buying intention and AI -related
challenges within a single housing-purchase context.
• Balance gap: Studies tend to report either the benefits of AI or its concerns3,10; fewer place them side by side
in the same respondent group.
PROBLEM STATEMENT:
AI-enabled customer interaction tools are being adopted by housing and real -estate platforms in Bangalore, but the
extent of buyer adoption and its actual relationship with buying intention are not well understood. It is unclear how
strongly AI -enabled interaction relates to buying intention once challenges such as technical issues, difficulty
obtaining accurate answers and a preference for human interaction are taken into account. The study therefore
addresses the following research questions:
• RQ1: What is the level of adoption and usage of AI -enabled customer interaction tools among prospective
housing buyers in Bangalore?
• RQ2: Does AI-enabled customer interaction significantly impact consumer buying intention towards
housing properties?
• RQ3: What is the relationship between AI-enabled customer interaction and consumers' buying intention
towards housing properties?
• RQ4: What challenges or limitations do consumers face when interacting with AI-enabled tools in the housing
property buying process?

outcome. Adoption and usage (Objective 1) describes the level of the focal construct. Challenges and limitations are
treated as a separate dimension assessed under Objective 4. Perceived trust and consumer satisfaction are shown as

H1 follows technology-acceptance logic and the adoption evidence, in which perceived usefulness and ease  of use
drive uptake5,8, together with the technology literacy of Bangalore's buyers. H2 and H3 draw on the links from AI
interaction to purchase intention through personalisation and perceived usefulness 10,12. H4 reflects the reported
concerns about privacy, transparency, accuracy and human contact 3,4. Accordingly, the following hypotheses were
formulated:
• H1: AI-enabled customer interaction tools are adopted and used at a moderate -to-high level among
prospective housing buyers in Bangalore.
• H2: AI-enabled customer interaction has a significant positive impact on consumer buying intention towards
housing properties.
• H3: There is a significant positive relationship between AI -enabled customer interaction and consumers'
buying intention towards housing properties.
• H4: Consumers face significant challenges or limitations (for example, technical issues and preference  for
human interaction) when interacting with AI-enabled tools in the housing property buying process.
RESEARCH METHODOLOGY:
Research Design
The study adopts a quantitative, cross -sectional survey design. A structured questionnaire was administered once to
each respondent and the responses were coded numerically. The design suits objectives  that involve measuring levels
(Objectives 1 and 4) and testing associations (Objectives 2 and 3), but because measurement occurs at a single point in
time it supports statements about association, not causation19.
Population, Sampling and Sample Size
The target population comprised prospective housing buyers and consumers evaluating housing properties in
Bangalore. Responses were collected through an online structured questionnaire, a non -probability approach 20,
between 19 and 25 August 2026. After screening, 214 valid responses were retained with no missing values on the
analysed variables. Most responses arrived towards the end of the seven-day window (timestamp skewness = −2.297).
With 214 cases, the five -predictor regression has 208 residual degrees of freedom, and the sample can detect a
correlation of about 0.19 at the 5% level with 80% power, below the observed correlation of 0.337.

the SPSS regression. Both blocks are treated as measures of the buying-intention outcome.

Tools of Analysis
IBM SPSS Statistics21 was used for descriptive statistics, reliability analysis, the item-level correlation matrix and
multiple linear regression (Enter method); jamovi22 was used for the construct-level Pearson and Spearman correlation
and for the descriptive and reliability analysis of the challenge items. Internal consistency was judged against the
conventional minimum of 0.70 for Cronbach's alpha 23,24. Supplementary computations (one-sample t tests against the
scale midpoint of 3.0, confidence intervals, variance inflation factors and Cohen's d25) were calculated from the
reported summary statistics and correlation  matrix and are labelled as supplementary. The decision rule was to reject
the null hypothesis when p < .05. Construct validity was not tested with factor analysis.
Ethical Considerations
Participation was voluntary and responses were collected for academic purposes only. No information that identifies
respondents was retained in the analysed dataset, results are reported in aggregate, and the study follows the
university's research-ethics expectations.

Objective 2: Impact of AI-Enabled Customer Interaction on Buying Intention
A multiple linear regression (Enter method) was estimated with AI1 –AI5 as predictors and TS1 as the dependent
variable. The four -item scale to which TS1 belongs is reliable (α = .816). TS1 correlates positively with all five
predictors ( r = .130 –.222). The model is statistically significant, F(5, 208) = 2.490,  p = .032, but the five items
together account for only 5.6% of the variance (R = .238; R² = .056; adjusted R² = .034; Cohen's f² ≈ 0.06, a small
effect), and the standard error of the estimate (1.336) is large on a five -point scale (Table 5). No individual predictor
reaches the 5% level. The closest is AI4, preference for AI over a human agent for initial enquiries (B = 0.212; β =
0.173; p = .062), while AI1 and AI5 carry small negative partial coefficients close to zero (Figure 3). Collinearity does
not explain this pattern: variance inflation factors range from 1.84 to 2.25 (tolerance .445–.543), so the items simply
overlap in what they explain (mean inter-item r = .53).

DISCUSSION:
Moderate-to-high adoption, led by convenience. All five adoption items sit between neutral and agree,  and the
highest-rated statements concern preferring AI for initial enquiries (3.48) and ease of use (3.33). Ease of use scoring
above neutral is consistent with the acceptance logic in which ease of use and  usefulness drive adoption5,10, and the
preference for AI at the enquiry stage suggests that buyers see it as a convenient first point of contact, in line with the
usefulness-and-trust drivers of adoption 8. Recommendation-system use had the lowest mean and the widest spread
(SD = 1.310), indicating uneven adoption: some buyers use these tools heavily and others rarely.
A real but modest link with buying intention. AI-enabled interaction is positively associated with intention  (r =
.337), which agrees with studies reporting positive links between AI-enabled experiences and purchase or behavioural
intention10,12–14. Yet the regression explains only 5.6% of the variance in the single -item outcome, and no item is a
unique predictor. This is consistent with the literature's emphasis on mediating and boundary factors such as trust,
personalisation, perceived value and algorithmic autonomy 4,9,12: a direct model containing only usage and preference
items should be expected to explain a limited share of variance. The larger R² for the composite outcome (.160) indicates
that measurement noise in single-item outcomes understates the relationship, and that chatbot use (AI3) and preference
for AI at enquiry (AI4) carry the clearest positive links. The negative coefficient for AI1 in the composite model should
be read cautiously, as it emerges in a model whose predictors overlap substantially.
Human contact remains valued at the decision point. The leading challenge, a preference for a human  at final
negotiation, echoes the reported persistence of human contact at key decision points and the boundary conditions on
algorithmic autonomy 3,4. Housing involves large sums and contractual commitment, and buyers appear to want an
accountable person for price discussion. Technical reliability ranks second, consistent with the facilitating -conditions
element of UTAUT7. The positive correlations between AI use and the challenge items suggest that heavier users are
more aware of limitations, and the positive link between CL4 and willingness to recommend AI platforms shows that
the two attitudes coexist rather than compete: preferring a human negotiator complements, rather than replaces, use of
AI in the early stages.
Convergence across analyses. The regression, correlation and challenge results point the same way: AI helps at
discovery and first enquiry, is associated with stronger intention, but is one contributor among many, and it works best
alongside human contact and reliable technology.
IMPLICATIONS:
Managerial Implications
• Deploy chatbots and virtual assistants where they are preferred, at initial enquiries, for availability, amenity,
price-range and location queries and for shortlisting (preference for AI at enquiry scored 3.48).
• Keep a visible human hand-off: with 43.5% preferring a human at final negotiation, provide a one -click route
from the assistant to a sales executive, with the conversation history carried over.
• Invest in reliability, since technical issues rank second (3.27): monitor response times and errors and set
service-level targets for the AI layer.
• Improve handling of complex and specific requirements. Although CL1 and CL3 are near neutral, their wide
spread shows that a sizeable group is dissatisfied; review failed conversations and extend coverage of financing
and legal-clarity questions.
• Position AI as support to the buyer journey, not a substitute for trust built through people, site visits and
transparent information, because its direct effect on intention is modest.

Academic Implications
• The study extends AI-marketing research, largely concentrated in retail, banking, hospitality and fashion, to
housing and real estate in an Indian metro.
• It provides within-sample evidence on adoption, buying intention and challenges together, so that benefits and
concerns can be read side by side.
• It shows that a modest direct effect (R² = .056 on a single item; .160 on a composite) is the realistic expectation,
pointing future models towards mediators such as trust and satisfaction and moderators such as property
complexity.
Practical Implications for AI-Tool Designers
• Design for graceful escalation when the tool cannot answer, make recommendation logic understandable
given the emphasis on transparency and trust 3, and test tools with heavier users, who are most likely to detect
gaps.
CONCLUSION:
This study examined how AI-enabled customer interaction relates to consumer buying intention towards housing
properties in Bangalore, using 214 valid responses from prospective buyers. Adoption of recommendation  systems,
chatbots and virtual assistants is moderate-to-high. AI-enabled interaction is significantly  and positively associated
with buying intention (r = .337, p < .001), although a regression of the five AI items explains only a small share of the
outcome (R² = .056, p = .032). Buyers report moderate  challenges, chiefly a preference for human interaction at final
negotiation (3.36) and technical issues (3.27). Taken together, the evidence supports a hybrid approach for housing: AI
for discovery and  first enquiry, people for negotiation and commitment, and reliable technology throughout. Because
the design is cross-sectional, the findings describe association rather than proven causation.
LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDY:
• The cross-sectional design captures perceptions at one point in time and cannot establish causality; the
regression and correlation results describe association.
• Findings are specific to Bangalore's housing context and to the 19–25 August 2026 survey window, and
the online non-probability sample may favour digitally comfortable respondents20.
• The sample is skewed towards men (74.3%) and ages 25–44 (87.9%); older buyers are under-represented (n
= 2 aged 55 or above).
• Self-reported Likert data may be affected by social-desirability and common-method bias26,27.
• Only AI interaction and challenges were modelled; price, location, income, trust and brand may also influence
buying intention, and the direct model has limited explanatory power.
• Construct validity was not tested with factor  analysis, the challenge scale has four items, buying intention is
captured by two four-item blocks (the regression uses the single item TS1), and several supplementary statistics
were computed from summary statistics and therefore carry rounding error.
FUTURE SCOPE:
• Test perceived trust and consumer satisfaction as mediating or moderating variables in the AI-to-intention link.
• Examine property complexity and price band as moderators of the AI–intention relationship.
• Study demographic differences among housing buyers, including older buyers and first-time versus repeat
buyers.
• Investigate the human–AI hand-off at the negotiation stage through qualitative or experimental designs.
• Extend the sample to multiple cities and use longitudinal designs to test causal direction, applying factor
analysis and structural equation modelling to validate the constructs.
