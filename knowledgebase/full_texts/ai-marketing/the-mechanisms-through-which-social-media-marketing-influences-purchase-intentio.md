Abstract. As the Z-generation (born between 1997 and 2012) is gradually reaching
adulthood, this group has become the main driving force in the current and future global
consumer market. Understanding how social media marketing influences their purchasing
decisions is a key market direction that the industry is focusing on. This article
systematically examines the mechanism by which social media marketing affects the
purchasing intentions of the Z-generation, with a particular focus on the roles and pathways
of key opinion leaders (KOLs), key opinion consumers (KOCs), and live-streaming sales.
The study reveals that social interaction and trust building are the core psychological
mechanisms driving purchasing intentions - the professionalism and social appeal of the
merchants' agents stimulate the audience's emotional attachment and imitative behavior,
which in turn positively influence the brand purchase intention. The high-traffic content
produced by these agents aligns with the values of the Z-generation, providing the
emotional compensation that this group finds difficult to obtain in school and at home,
thereby inducing impulsive consumption. At the same time, the trust relationship driven by
social interaction is inherently fragile. Account scandals or trust breakdowns can quickly
erode the accumulated consumer credit and company marketing assets. Based on this, this
article proposes references from three aspects: content design, long-term trust relationship
building, and values-driven marketing, to construct a research report on the consumption
tendencies of the Z-generation.
Keywords: Social media marketing, Generation Z, purchase intention, parasocial interaction,

1. Introduction
Generation Z—the first digital-native consumer cohort—have developed distinct consumption
behavior shaped by lifelong immersion in social media. Brand awareness now often begins not with
traditional ads or in-store exposure, but through algorithmic recommendations or peer-driven
interactions on platforms. A 2025 Retail Economics and Unbox report projects UK non-food social
commerce to reach £9.1 billion, growing at 17% annually, nearly five times faster than general e-

The rise of short-form video has shifted marketing from one-way brand messaging to influencer-

using both parasocial interaction theory and trust transfer as dual theoretical lenses. Practically, it
delivers an evidence-based model to help brands design effective digital campaigns for younger
customers.
2. Major forms of social media marketing and the characteristics of Generation Z audiences
2.1. KOLs, KOCs, and livestream commerce: distinct roles and contextual fit
Social media marketing drives conversion through three key formats: KOLs, KOCs, and livestream
commerce, each building trust differently amd excelling in distinct consumption contexts.
KOLs leverage professional authority. In beauty, for example, Li Jiaqi's background ass a
cosmetic advisor enabled him to build "professional trust" around lipstick—driving purchases based
on followers' confidence in his expertise. Research confirms that influencer expertise and social
attractiveness foster parasocial interaction, which boosts follower engagement and retention [2].
KOCs build trust through authenticity. Unlike highly curated KOLs, they mirror everyday
experience, eg., an unpolished Xiaohongshu review from an ordinary user is often perceived by Gen
Z as more honest than branded content. Literature identifies authenticity, expertise, and
responsibility as core KOC traits, when present, they strengthen Gen Z's purchase intent via trust-
mediated pathways [3]. Their value lies not in ordinariness, but in the credibility of their voice and
content.
Livestream commerce generates situational trust. Real-time interaction—between host and
audience or among viewers—creates immediacy and urgency, often overriding rational deliberation.
Studies show influencer appeal and platform interactivity jointly fuel impulse buying, with pleasure
as a key emotional driver and decision-making styles acting as a moderator [4].
Product category determines optimal format: (1) High-involvement items (e.g., electronics, cars)
benefit most from KOL endorsements—buyers seek authoritative, information-rich guidance, (2)
Low-involvement, fast-moving goods (e.g., snacks, household items) align better with KOC-led
experience-sharing or of short-term livestream promotions, (3) Luxury goods face a tension: KOL
aesthetics reinforce brand prestige, but overexposure risks diluting scarcity and exclusivity.
2.2. Gen Z consumer psychology: authenticity and value-driven consumption
Gen Z is not a passive audience but an active, selective consumer cohort—capable of evaluating or
ignoring marketing messages on their own terms. In China alone, Gen Z digital consumers number
261 million, 27.2% of the national digital consumer base, making them central drivers of emotional
and digital consumption [5]. Not forward-looking brand can afford to overlook them.

Two defining traits shape their behavior: a strong pursuit of authenticity and value-driven
consumption. SAP Emarsys (2025) data shows 64% of Gen Z prioritize specific products over
brands, 31% trust trend-focused social media content more than traditional ads, and 18% buy
directly based on influencer recommendations [6]. Collectively, these figures signal a shift from
brand loyalty to product loyalty and creator loyalty.
Value-driven consumption for Gen Z extends beyond "value for money." The 2025 Generation Z
Emotional Consumption Report (China Youth Daily) identifies five top emotional-spending
categories: (1) tactile items (e.g., plush toys, scented goods), (2) emotionally evocative experiences
(concerts, comedy shows), (3) IP- or brand-linked products, (4) socially embedded consumption
(e.g., group purchases, shared rituals), and (5) digital assets (avatars, virtual pets) [7]. This pattern
reveals a preference for goods and services that elicit immediate or sustained emotional responses—
not just functional utility.
Emotional and rational price comparison coexist. A 2026 DT Finance survey identifies
"survivalism" as Gen Z's top emerging trend: increased spending on essentials (healthcare,
insurance) alongside reduced discretionary social spending [8]. This reflects calibrated rationality,
not irrationality. Gen Z manages overall budgets tightly while allocating funds emotionally:
comparing prices across platforms to save several dozen yuan, yet spending several thousand yuan
on a concert. These seemingly contradictory behaviors are, in fact, strategic, aimed at maximizing
emotional utility within a fixed budget.
3. Key psychological mechanisms linking social media marketing to purchase intention
3.1. A three-dimensional framework of parasocial interaction: cognition, emotion, and action
The notion of parasocial interaction was first brought up by Horton and Wohl in connection with
one-sided, intimate relationships—of a sort—between television audiences and characters on the
screen [9]. With today's social media, this idea is no longer limited but has grown considerably
thanks to digital interactivity. On an operational level, parasocial interaction may be considered as
proceeding—step by step—from cognitive involvement to feeling attached and, finally, to action.
Cognitive processing represents the starting point. As consumers repeatedly encounter a creator's
videos or text-and-image posts, they gradually become familiar with the creator's values and aspects
of their personal life. Research by Chen et al. indicates that empathy and content attractiveness on
the part of the creator are important drivers at this stage [2]. Emotional attachment constitutes the
intermediate stage. Consumers begin to perceive the creator as either "someone like me" or
"someone I want to become," thereby forming a one-sided emotional connection. Research by
Sokolova and Kefi on beauty and fashion influencers on Instagram and YouTube found that
parasocial interaction and perceived credibility are two key predictors of purchase intention [10].
Behavioral engagement represents the final stage. Consumers participate in the creator's social
interactions by liking and commenting on content and may ultimately complete purchases based on
the creator's recommendations. Each type of content encourages parasocial interaction—though not
necessarily in the same way. Text-based or image-based formats (as used on Xiaohongshu) rest on
narratives and unchanging visuals for the building of trust. Short-form video content—on platforms
such as Douyin or TikTok—establishes familiarity quickly with dense emotion and full immersion
in the material. Livestreaming adds a dimension of immediacy and of mutual response, it is thus
helpful for engagement and for triggering actual purchases.

and Copeland conducted an experiment on TikTok in which 280 Generation Z participants watched

3.2. The trust transfer mechanism: from trust in influencers to trust in brands
Generation Z consumers' purchasing decisions often involve a process in which trust is transferred
from an influencer to a brand. This process can be divided into several key psychological stages: the
first step is the formation of a parasocial relationship. In consequence of repeated contact with an
influencer's material, people come to know him or her and to feel positively about him or her. The
second stage is trust formation. Djafarova and Rushworth's study of different categories of online
celebrities on Instagram found that, in influencing the purchasing decisions of young female users,
non-traditional celebrities such as bloggers and YouTube creators were more influential than
traditional celebrities because audiences perceived them as more credible and easier to identify with
[12]. The third stage is trust transfer. Consumers transfer their trust in the influencer to the products
and brands recommended by that influencer, completing the psychological transition from "I like
this blogger" to "The products recommended by this blogger are probably good."
Through a review of literature published between 2015 and 2025, Salimah identified five major
psychological mechanisms—credibility, authenticity, parasocial relationships, attractiveness, and
value congruence—that jointly influence consumer purchasing decisions at cognitive, emotional,
and conative levels [13]. The risk of trust breakdown is one that should not be ignored. Generation
Z's feelings about brands or about influencers do not settle easily: trust is something to be built
slowly, while its opposite may come about in a day's time.
The 2023 "eyebrow pencil incident " involving Li Jiaqi is a good example of this kind of
situation. During one of his livestreams, Li Jiaqi gave an ill-advised reply to a question about a 79-
yuan eyebrow pencil, which led to many of his followers feeling betrayed and posting about it
online. Florasis—his collaborator for the product—was also affected by the fallout. Among the facts
cited by Lanjinger is a sharp fall in sales at Florasis's own flagship store following this episode [14].
Such cases help to explain the idea of contagious trust loss: influencers and their brand partners may
be linked in an interdependent fashion (both benefiting or suffering together) and so are vulnerable
to wide-scale damage from one bad event.
3.3. The moderating effects of content characteristics and platform interactivity:
entertainment, immediacy, and impulse buying
The effectiveness of social media marketing depends not only on the communicator but also on the
characteristics of the content and the platform environment.
The mechanisms underlying livestream commerce are particularly important when examining
impulse buying. Among the scarcity-oriented promotional tactics used in livestream commerce
(such as time-bound offers or inventory countdowns or live updates of how many items have been
sold), there is reliance on the psychological idea of fear of missing out (FOMO). If the stream gives
a message such as "Only 100 units remaining, "then the customer's available time for decision-
making could shrink to just seconds, and reasoning might give way to purely emotional choice.

mechanisms of impulse stimulation remain unchecked.
4. Challenges and optimization of marketing strategies
4.1. Challenges: trust vulnerability and advertising fatigue
Influencer scandals pose serious spillover risks to partner brands, yet these are often underestimated.
Li Jiaqi's 2023 eyebrow pencil incident shows clearly how an ill-advised remark by an influencer
may evolve, in just a few hours, into a major problem for a brand. Risk management is essential
even before an actual collaboration is formed. Brands must vet influencers' past conduct and overall
reputation. Contracts should include enforceable morality clauses permitting termination for clause.
Diversifying influencer partnerships across persona, platforms, and audience segments, also
mitigates overreliance on any single voice or image.
Advertising fatigue is equally critical. As social media marketing saturates feeds, Gen Z has
become more adapt at identifying ads and more reluctant to accept them. Crucially, they6 distinguish
sharply between native and overt advertising: Native ads blend seamlessly into creators' authentic
content flow, lowering psychological resistance. Overt ads interrupt browsing, triggering avoidance.
A 2026 influencer marketing trends survey confirms Gen Z's declining tolerance for blatant
promotion. Brands must more beyond insertion—they need to design commercial messages that
deliver genuine utility: solving problems, saving time, or enriching experience [6].
4.2. Strategic optimization
4.2.1. Precise content design and community-based dissemination
With rising advertising fatigue, brands must shift from chasing maximum reach to cultivatinga
meaningful engagement. The core idea is simple: attracting attracting 100 million passive viewers
matters less than resonating with 1 million who will actively discuss and share the product. This
requires brands to develop differentiated influencer collaboration strategies for different subcultural
communities, select creators whose styles and values closely match those of the brand, and design
content with strong potential for social dissemination.

4.2.2. Long-term trust building and brand value co-creation
One-off KOL promotions may boost short-term sales, but lasting competitive advantage rests on
accumulated trust. Achieving this demands dual focus: With regard to influencers, it is good to form
long-term relations that will help produce genuine, lasting endorsements for a brand. With regard to
consumers, it is helpful to keep talking about one's values and to cultivate emotional identification
with them.
Value co-creation means moving beyond "selling products" to championing ideas. Patagonia's
"Don't Buy This Jacket" Black Friday campaign exemplifies this: by urging reduced consumption, it
reinforced environmental credibility, proving that brands must first stand for something. In the social
media era, shared values, not just influencer reputation, form the most durable foundation of trust.
4.2.3. Full-scenario integration and impulse management in livestream commerce
Livestream commerce is now a mainstream shopping channel for Gen Z. Live demonstrations lower
decision friction: watching a product in real use delivers faster, clearer insight than specs or static
reviews. Integrated checkout further collapses the gap between desire and purchase. Yet 62% of
consumers report regretting impulse buys, highlighting a critical tension. Sustainable livestream
strategy must balance conversion with consumer welfare [15].
Overusing scarcity tactics or aritificial time pressure may lift short-term sales but erodes trust
over time. A more sustainable approach would be to provide sufficient time for product information
during livestreams and proactively explain both the appropriate and inappropriate use scenarios of a
product. In this way, transparency can be used to build long-term trust.
5. Conclusion
This paper integrates parasocial interaction theory with trust transfer to explain—psychologically—
how social media marketing shape Gen Z's purchase deicisions. It answers the central question
posed at the outset: social media marketing influences Gen Z's purchase intentions through two
interlinked psychological pathways. First, the parasocial interaction pathway: users progress from
cognitive processing, to emotional involvement and thence to behavioral action. An influencer's
authenticity and content appeal are critical triggers. Second, the trust transfer pathway: consumers
develop parasocial trust in an influencer and then extend that trust to endorsed brands. These
pathways are not sequential or independent—they co-occur and reinforce each other. Parasocial
interaction forms the emotional foundation of trust: trust, in turn, transforms imagined connections
into real purchases.
Theoretically, this study bridges parasocial interaction and trust transfer—enabling a more
precise analysis of influencer marketing psychology (distinct from traditional celebrity endorsement)
and highlighting generational specificity, especially Gen Z's heightened emotional investment
influencers, . This emotional intensity also heightens vulnerability: rapid information spread on
social media can trigger swift reputational crises for both influencers and associated brands. The
Florasis case illustrates their mutual exposure: reputational damage to either party cascades to the
other.
Practically, brands should (1) Shift content strategy from traffic-driven tactics to building
authentic, lasting customer relationships, (2) Prioritize long-term influencer partnerships grounded
in shared values—whether aesthetic, ethical, or social, and (3) use livestream commerce and
impulse-driven tools responsibly—balancing short-term conversion with long-term brand equity.
