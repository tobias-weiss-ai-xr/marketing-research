ABSTRACT
Large language models are increasingly used to simulate how individuals respond
to new situations, yet the behavioral reasoning behind these responses is either
inherited from pretraining or learned from individual-level annotations, which
offer limited behavioral diversity and little supervision of the reasoning itself. We
propose to learn behavioral reasoning from prediction markets, whose price trajec-
tories record how populations respond to real-world events at scale. We introduce
macro2mind, which trains a language model with GRPO using market signals. A
social behavioral decomposition makes behavioral reasoning an explicit step of
forecasting: the model infers representative groups of market participants, predicts
how each interprets the news and updates its beliefs, reasons about their interactions,
and aggregates these responses into a price. A hindsight-regret curriculum with
difficulty-aware sampling focuses training on transitions where hindsight-identified
groups substantially improve the forecast while prioritizing examples that remain
learnable for the current policy. The learned reasoning applies to user simulation
without further training. On SWM-Bench, macro2mind achieves state-of-the-art
directional accuracy and correlation on Polymarket. Trained on market data, it
transfers zero-shot to four user-simulation benchmarks (Humanual, OvertonBench,
PRISM, and CAD) and has competitive performance among zero-shot methods.
Used as a data generator, macro2mind also raises a downstream simulator’s accu-
racy on unseen users by 15.5 points, outperforming data generated by its backbone
by 13.2 points.
1 INTRODUCTION
Large language models are increasingly used to simulate individual behavior (Park et al., 2023; Zhou
et al., 2024; Aher et al., 2023; Ni et al., 2026), including how people respond to information (Argyle
et al., 2023; Santurkar et al., 2023), make decisions (Kitadai et al., 2025; Horton et al., 2023), and
interact with others (Yu et al., 2025). Such simulators support personalized agents (Jiang et al.,
2024; Ji et al., 2026), social simulation (Yang et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2023), and the evaluation of
interactive systems (Wang et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2024). Achieving high-fidelity simulation of a
particular individual imposes a dual requirement. It demands not only static, descriptive information
about that person’s background but also a dynamic capability to predict how their internal beliefs and
subsequent actions evolve. We refer to this capability asbehavioral reasoning; it is closely related to
theory of mind, the ability to attribute beliefs and intentions to others (Premack & Woodruff, 1978).
To improve response fidelity, training-based simulators directly fit models to observed user behav-
iors (Naous et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026); some frameworks further align latent user states with
these observed outputs (Wu et al., 2026). However, while rich profiles and histories successfully
specifywhomto simulate, the core mechanics ofhowthey reason are either passively inherited from
pre-training priors or explicitly fitted to individual-level annotations. Consequently, the supervision
for dynamic behavioral reasoning remains strictly confined to micro-level data.

Market prices as aggregated individual behavior. While individual behavioral data is scarce,
prediction markets continuously record the underlying reasoning at scale (Wolfers & Zitzewitz, 2004).
Economists recognize prices not as abstract labels, but as aggregations of dispersed beliefs (Hayek,
2013; Manski, 2006). Consequently, each price movement is the macroscopic trace of a micro-level
process: heterogeneous individuals interpret news through personal priors (Kandel & Pearson, 1995),
adapt to peers (Bikhchandani et al., 1992), and aggregate into a new price—mirroring the macro–
micro–macro chain of social theory (Coleman et al., 1990). For example, when inflation unexpectedly
drops, rate-cut probabilities surge because trading desks instantly adjust, analysts react to the desks,
and contrarians bet against the herd. Explaining this shift requires the exact behavioral reasoning
necessary for user simulation, simply executed in reverse: inferring latent constituent behaviors from
aggregate outcomes. Prediction markets thus solve both data bottlenecks (Figure 1). (1)Diverse
and abundant dynamics.Markets naturally capture varied reasoning patterns from macroeconomic
updates to partisan interpretations through continuous, high-stakes decisions, bypassing costly micro-
level data collection. (2)Outcome-grounded supervision.The realized price provides objective
outcome-level supervision for whether the aggregate implication of the inferred responses matches
the observed market movement. This raises our central question:can learning to predict aggregate
outcomes teach behavioral reasoning that transfers to simulating individuals?
Our approach. Harnessing this abundance is not a free lunch: evaluating only a final numeri-
cal forecast (Gu et al., 2025) introduces two learning challenges. First, naive optimization risks
rewarding shortcut learning over genuine behavioral reasoning (C1). Second, market transitions
lack explicit labels to distinguish learnable social dynamics from routine noise (C2). We introduce
macro2mind, which tackles both challenges through two complementary mechanisms trained via
GRPO on macroscopic rewards. First, asocial behavioral decompositionexplicitly structures the
reasoning trajectory (C1). The model must infer representative groups, predict their belief updates
and interactions, and aggregate these micro-responses into a price. Second, ahindsight-regret cur-
riculum with difficulty-aware samplingperforms dynamic data selection (C2). Given the realized
outcome in hindsight, the policy names the driving groups; regret is measured by how much this

Main findings. We train macro2mind on prediction-market transitions from SWM-Bench and eval-

2 RELATEDWORKS
LLM-based User Simulation. LLMs have been used as stand-ins for human respondents in surveys
and behavioral experiments (Argyle et al., 2023; Aher et al., 2023; Horton et al., 2023), although their
default opinions often diverge from those of specific populations (Santurkar et al., 2023; Durmus
et al., 2023). To simulate particular individuals, existing methods condition on profiles, interviews, or
interaction histories through prompting (Park et al., 2023; 2024b; Ryan et al., 2025), steer activations
toward a target persona (Feng et al., 2026), or fine-tune on behavioral traces (Naous et al., 2026; Wu
et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026); reward models of human preferences, including personalized ones,
learn from pairwise feedback (Cai et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). Synthetic personas (Ge et al.,
2024) and digital-twin datasets (Yang et al., 2025b) broaden coverage, but the former lack grounding
in real behavior and the latter remain costly to collect. All of these approaches learn how people
respond from individual-level data. We instead learn behavioral reasoning from aggregate outcomes
and use user-specific data only at inference.
Aggregate Social Signals and Social Simulation. Forecasting systems treat aggregate signals
such as prediction-market prices as the end goal (Wolfers & Zitzewitz, 2004; Halawi et al., 2024;
Karger et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025). Social simulation runs bottom-up, generating collective
dynamics from rule-based (Schelling, 1971; Brock & Hommes, 1998) or LLM-based agents (Gao
et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025), with aggregate data used only to calibrate or
validate the agents (Fagiolo et al., 2007). Ecological inference (King & Fox, 1999) and learning from
label proportions (Quadrianto et al., 2008) do infer individuals from aggregates, but within a fixed
population and label space. We instead use aggregate outcomes to train a reasoning process that
transfers to new individuals and situations, and, since aggregate fit does not guarantee individual-level
validity (Robinson, 2009; Zhang et al., 2026c), we evaluate it directly on individual behavior.
Theory of Mind Modeling. Existing Theory of Mind (ToM) frameworks and benchmarks heavily
rely on synthetic narratives or explicit human annotations (Gandhi et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2023; Wilf
et al., 2024; Sclar et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2025), sparking debate over whether LLMs perform robust
causal reasoning or superficial pattern matching (Kosinski, 2024; Ullman, 2023; Sap et al., 2022).
Our constituent-level decomposition directly operationalizes ToM by inferring how distinct groups
update latent beliefs, but it induces this capability from macroscopic market outcomes. By bypassing
micro-level annotations entirely, we learn grounded mental-state reasoning that transfers zero-shot to
concrete individuals.

3.1 MACROTASK: POPULATION-LEVELFORECASTING
Observation. Each instance centers on a market question, a natural-language proposition such as the
outcome of an election. Its price yt ∈[0,1] reflects the collective decisions of market participants
at step t. The model observes the question, the recent price history, and a set of text descriptions of
real-world events, such as news items. We denote this macro observation byx t.
Policy. Given xt, the policy generates a reasoning trace τt followed by a forecast of the next price,
(τt,ˆyt+1)∼π θ(· |xt).
Evaluation. The macro dataset collects transitions across many markets, Dmacro ={(x t, yt+1)}.
The task is evaluated by how accurately the forecast ˆyt+1 matches the realized next price yt+1, using
MAE, MASE, directional accuracy, and Pearson correlation.
3.2 MICROTASK: INDIVIDUAL-LEVELSIMULATION
Observation. Each instance targets a specific user. At stept, the model observes the user’s persona
and interaction history ht, such as a profile, stated views, or past messages, together with the current
context, such as a news event, an ongoing conversation, or a candidate reply to be judged. We denote
the full micro observation byx t; all of it is available before the target response.
Policy. Given xt, the policy likewise generates a reasoning trace τt followed by a prediction of the
user’s response,(τ t,ˆyt+1)∼π θ(· |xt).
Evaluation. The micro dataset collects individual interactions, Dmicro ={(x t, yt+1)}, where yt+1 is
the user’s actual response. The task is evaluated by how well ˆyt+1 matches yt+1, using task-specific
scores such as rubric-based alignment for free-text replies, or error and rank correlation for ratings.

4 LEARNING TOSIMULATEINDIVIDUALS FROMMACROSIGNALS
We realize our approach through a single autoregressive language-model policy πθ. We first describe
how the policy structures social behavioral reasoning during forecasting (Section 4.1), how it is
optimized via GRPO using a hindsight-regret curriculum (Section 4.2), and how this learned reasoning
transfers to individual simulation (Section 4.3).
4.1 SOCIALBEHAVIORALREASONING
Social behavioral decomposition. We instantiate this reasoning as a single autoregressive trajectory,
avoiding the cost and credit-assignment difficulties of a full multi-agent simulation (Yang et al., 2024).
Given an observation x, the policy generates a reasoning trace τ= (τ prop, τF , τA) before outputting
the forecastˆy. The resulting decomposition has natural counterparts in individual simulation.
1. Constituent initialization (τ prop).The policy identifies representative constituents likely to drive
the market shift, and constructs a prior state for each that describes its role, objectives, and beliefs.
Micro counterpart:inferring which constituents drive a given change, and what they care about,
requires linking behavioral patterns to the kinds of people who produce them. This is the same
persona–behavior association that individual simulation relies on.
2. First-order responses ( τ F ).The policy predicts how each constituent updates its beliefs and
responds based on its own prior and the observation.Micro counterpart:predicting a user’s belief
update and immediate response to the current context.
3. Second-order responses and aggregation ( τ A).The policy models the most consequential
interactions, assessing how one constituent’s reaction alters another’s beliefs and actions, and then
aggregates the adjusted responses into the forecastˆy.Micro counterpart:adapting to others’ reactions,
as in replying within a multi-turn dialogue.
Behavioral reasoning as a shared transition function. Both tasks rest on a shared behavioral
transition function, which maps an agent’s prior state and context to a belief update and an observable
response, and can be viewed as a computational form of theory of mind. The two tasks differ in the
observability of the prior state. In the micro task, it is given by the user’s history. In the macro task, it
is unobserved, and the policy must first infer the prior states of key constituents before predicting
their responses. Because the behavioral transition function is shared across constituents and tasks, the
behavioral reasoning ability that the policy learns from macro rewards can be applied to individual
simulation without further training (Section 4.3).
Orders of social behavioral reasoning. Social reactions to an event unfold in orders. At the first
order, each group reacts directly to the news; at the second order, groups react to one another’s
reactions, and such cascades can in principle continue further. Individuals behave the same way,
responding both to events and to what others say about them. Our template reasons up to the second
order and focuses on the few interactions that matter most, which captures effects such as persuasion
and conformity while maintaining a concise autoregressive trajectory. This way of reasoning bounds
the intermediate reasoning process without altering the final macroscopic prediction target.
4.2 LEARNINGSOCIALBEHAVIORALREASONING FROMMACROSIGNALS
Macro signals are inherently noisy and uneven in difficulty (C2): many transitions are dominated
by random fluctuations, some are trivially solvable, and only some actually reward reasoning about
the latent constituents driving the market. Because GRPO gains minimal signal from transitions the
model always or never solves, we augment policy optimization with a hindsight-regret curriculum
that combines behavioral informativeness with current learning difficulty. Hindsight regret prioritizes
transitions where the model overlooks key constituents, while a difficulty-aware filter favors examples
that have informative within-group variation for GRPO.

set of structured behavioral traces. For a given market observation x, the policy samples a group
of M reasoning and forecasting trajectories. Each output is evaluated by a reward R that combines

4.3 APPLYINGSOCIALBEHAVIORREASONING TOINDIVIDUALSIMULATION
The core premise of our approach is that macroscopic market shifts and microscopic user actions
are driven by the same underlying behavioral mechanics. Consequently, we transfer the reasoning
capabilities learned from macro signals directly to individual simulation.
Individual-level social reasoning. For individual simulation, the prior state is given rather than
inferred. Depending on the task, it comes from the user’s interaction history, such as past posts or
conversations, or from a persona, such as a profile and stated views. We therefore bypass constituent
discovery and directly instantiate the prior state of the target individual with this information. Given
this prior state and the current context, the policy applies the same learned behavioral transition to
infer how the user interprets the context, updates their beliefs, and responds.
Inference. We apply the trained policy to individual simulation zero-shot, without any parameter
updates, and evaluate only the final response. During training, the policy reasons about coarse groups
inferred from market context; here, it applies the same behavioral reasoning process to a specific
person with an observed history or persona.
Synthetic data. The same procedure can also generate training data to address the scarcity of
individual behavioral data. Given a user’s persona and a few-shot history, optionally augmented with
population-level behavioral statistics available in the downstream setting, we apply the trained policy
to generate both novel questions and the corresponding responses. This synthetic data is then used to
fine-tune and align a separate downstream simulator.

5.4 MAINRESULTS
macro2mind improves market forecasting, particularly in predicting directional movement.On
SWM-Bench (Table 1),macro2mind achieves the highest directional accuracy (0.674) and correlation
(0.362) on Polymarket—surpassing both the strongest time-series baseline and GPT-5.5—while
ranking second in directional accuracy on Kalshi (0.569).
macro2mind demonstrates robust zero-shot transfer.Trained on prediction market signals,
macro2mind has competitive performance among zero-shot methods (Table 2). It secures the best
Humanual alignment (9.5/3.4) and OvertonBench ρ (0.653), remarkably surpassing two supervised
HumanLM variants zero-shot. Furthermore, it firmly out-generalizes domain-specific simulators:
macro2mind beats Humanual-trained HumanLM-GRPO across all five out-of-domain metrics (e.g.,
CAD: 42.2% vs. 39.1%) and avoids PRISM-trained P-GenRM’s catastrophic collapse on Humanual.
On PRISM and CAD accuracy, macro2mind remains highly competitive (63.3% / 42.2%), trailing
only History-ICL and dataset-specific reward models.
macro2mind serves as a highly effective synthetic data generator for downstream user sim-
ulators.Fine-tuning a student model on data generated by macro2mind boosts its accuracy from
44.5% to 60.0% on unseen users (out-of-user) and from 50.9% to 61.3% on source users (in-user). In
contrast, data generated by its Qwen3-4B backbone using the identical pipeline yields only marginal
improvements (46.8% and 53.0%, respectively). Since the two generators differ exclusively in
their exposure to macro market forecasting, this 13.2-point gap on unseen users demonstrates that
macro2mind is a good teacher for micro-level simulation.
6 DISCUSSION
RQ1: Which part of the training recipe drives transfer?Ablation results show that the full
curriculum, combining hindsight regret with difficulty-aware sampling, is the key driver of zero-shot
transfer.To isolate their contributions, we evaluate additive ablations on Humanual: Base+BR (zero-
shot prompting), BR-SFT (fine-tuning on BR traces), BR-SFT+GRPO (GRPO without curriculum),
Base+GRPO (GRPO with free form thinking) and macro2mind (full curriculum) under identical
hyperparameters. Results (Figure 3) show the BR structure alone cannot explain the gains: Base+BR
and BR-SFT yield similar State scores (7.6 vs. 7.5). Neither BR-SFT nor BR-SFT+GRPO improves
over Base+BR: BR-SFT stays at 7.5 State and 1.4 Resp, and adding GRPO without the curriculum
gives 6.3 State and 0.9 Resp. Thus, naively optimizing for market forecasting does not inherently
transfer to individual simulation. Meaningful transfer emerges only when adding the full regret-and-

difficulty curriculum: macro2mind reaches 9.5 State and 3.4 Resp, improving over Base+BR by 25%
in State score.
RQ2: Do macro forecasting and micro simulation ability improve together during training?
Results indicate a concurrent improvement during training. As macro2mind learns to forecast macro
signals, its zero-shot user-simulation capability exhibits corresponding gains.Evaluating four training
checkpoints (steps 0, 20, 40, and 66) on both SWM-Bench and Humanual (Figure 4) reveals a striking
trajectory. Over the first 40 steps, both abilities surge simultaneously—SWM-Bench directional
accuracy increases from 50.2% to 64.7%, while Humanual response alignment more than doubles
(1.2 → 2.6) despite zero exposure to its data. Beyond step 40, forecasting accuracy plateaus (64.9%),
yet response alignment continues to climb to nearly three times its initial value (3.4). This continuous
co-improvement demonstrates that zero-shot transfer is driven by the acquisition of macro behavioral
reasoning.
RQ3: Does the learned behavioral reasoning align with human judgments?Human evaluation
indicates that the behavioral reasoning generated by macro2mind aligns well with human judgments.
Five annotators evaluated 20 traces per model (0–100 scale), judging whether the inferred groups,
news interpretations, and belief updates coherently explained the final forecast. As shown in Figure 5,
macro2mind scores 72.2, surpassing both Qwen3-4B (47.1) and Qwen3.5-397B (51.4) under the
exact same structural prompt. This suggests that structural prompting alone is insufficient to achieve
human-aligned reasoning. In contrast, an R1-style model reasoning freely within <think> tags
scores 18.3, often defaulting to disjointed numerical speculation rather than modeling actual human
perspectives. These human ratings correlate strongly with our LLM judge (Pearsonr= 0.885).
7 CONCLUSION
We introduced macro2mind, which learns to simulate individual human behavior from aggregate
prediction-market signals via a structured social behavioral decomposition and a dynamic hindsight-
regret curriculum. macro2mind achieves state-of-the-art DA and Corr on Polymarket while demon-
strating robust zero-shot transfer to user simulation. Furthermore, its generated data can serve as
high-quality supervision, significantly boosting the accuracy of downstream models. In the future,
exploring other forms of macroscopic supervision and deploying these simulators into dynamic,
multi-agent ecosystems remain exciting directions.

C.2 USERSIMULATION
Humanual-Politics. Given the user’s profile and an article, the simulator writes the comment the user
would post. A judge Qwen3.8-Max compares it with the user’s real comment along seven aspects,
following the rubric of Humanual-Politics: six aspects of the user’s latent state (stance, emotion,
belief, value, goal, communication) and the response itself. For each aspect, the judge extracts one
to three key points from the real comment, scores how well the generated comment reflects them
(C), and subtracts a penalty for off-topic, contradictory, or hallucinated extra content (P ); the aspect
score is C−P clipped to [0,1] .State alignmentis the mean of the six state aspects andresponse
alignmentis the response aspect; they are never averaged together. Both are averaged over all test
examples, with an example that has no parsable comment scoring zero. Our judge setup differs from
the original in three ways: the judge model (qwen3.8-max instead of claude-4.5-haiku), all seven
aspects scored in one request rather than one request per aspect, and each generation judged on its
own rather than in a batch of rollouts for the same context. Absolute scores are therefore lower than
the published ones and should be compared only within our table (Li et al., 2026a). We multiply all
Humanual scores by 100 for reporting.
OvertonBench. For a subjective question, a participant rated how well an LLM-generated answer
represents their view on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (fully represented); the simulator predicts
that rating. We use the prompts of Overtonbench verbatim, with the fs+fr condition, their best-
performing one: the prompt contains the participant’s own free-text answer to the question (fr) and
their ratings of other LLM responses as examples (fs). We evaluate the 7,200 items of the political
subset and report the mean absolute error (MAE) and Spearman’s ρ between predicted and gold
ratings, pooled over all items. An unparsable prediction is assigned the midpoint rating 3.
PRISM. In each turn of a multi-turn conversation, a participant scored every candidate response
from 1 to 100. Given the participant’s profile and the conversation history, the simulator predicts the
score of each candidate. We evaluate all 27,172 scored turns in PRISM and report (i) MAE between
predicted and gold scores on the original 1–100 scale, and (ii) pairwise preference accuracy: over
pairs of candidates in the same turn whose gold scores differ by more than 5 points, the fraction
that the prediction orders the same way, with an exactly tied prediction counting as one half. An
unparsable turn is assigned 50 for every candidate and loses every pair it takes part in.
CAD. Given an annotator’s profile and the conversation so far, the simulator predicts which of up to
four candidate responses the annotator preferred in CAD. We evaluate a 2,000-comparison English
subset and report accuracy; an unparsable prediction counts as wrong.
D DETAILS OFUSERSIMULATIONBASELINES
Prompting-based. These simulators keep the model parameters fixed: direct prompting of Qwen3-
4B; PERSONA-Flow, which steers Qwen3-4B toward the user’s personality by composing activation
vectors at inference time; History-ICL, which retrieves the user’s own past interactions with BM25
and places them in context, following LaMP; SynthesizeMe, which first induces a persona from the
user’s history and then simulates the user conditioned on it; Self-report, which has the model first
describe itself in the first person as the user and then answer; and Self-consistency, which samples
five responses and aggregates them.
Training-based. These are fine-tuned user simulators or personalized reward models. For HumanLM,
we reproduce on Qwen3-4B the SFT, SFT-with-thinking, and standard GRPO recipes released with
the paper, trained on Humanual (HumanLM-SFT, -SFT-think, -GRPO); P-GenRM is a generative
personalized reward model; we reproduce its three-stage recipe on Qwen3-4B using the personalized
split of PRISM: SFT on evaluation chains written by Qwen3.8-Max. Skywork-RM is the pretrained
general-purpose reward model Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B, used without any personalization
training. MRM represents a user’s reward as a softmax-weighted mixture of two linear reward heads
on frozen Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B features; the heads and the initial mixture are meta-
learned with the released code and hyper-parameters, and at test time the mixture weights are adapted
to each user with one gradient step on that user’s preference pairs from their other conversations.
WIMHF-RM is a LoRA reward model fine-tuned from Qwen3-4B.

F.4 DATAGENERATIONPIPELINE
The pipeline is identical across generators.
1. Requests.We issue 6,000 requests: 10 for each of the 200 pilot users and 10 for each of 400
other training users. Each request pairs a participant (persona and example answers) with
a target question family: about 40% pricing, with the rest spread over the 13 experiment
families. Parameters are sampled from the family’s real condition space. Pricing requests
use invented everyday products, priced at a multiple (0.5–2.0×) of the category’s typical
price, without revealing the typical price.
2. Scenario writing.The generator writes a scenario (question and options) and its own
answer. Each scenario is automatically checked for format, compliance with the family
parameters and price, and avoidance of held-out items: none of the 40 real pricing products
may appear, and near-duplicates of any held-out question are rejected.
3. Rejection sampling.Failed requests are regenerated with a new seed ( seed+ 7919·round ),
for at most 3 rounds.
4. Population-anchored labeling.The generator answers each valid scenario k=6 times as
the participant. The prompt includes the answer distribution of training users under the
corresponding real condition (for pricing, their purchase rate at the same price multiple).
Anchors for pilot users’ requests are computed over all 1,600 training users; anchors for
the other requests exclude pilot users (1,400 users). The generator’s original answer from
the writing step is discarded; the label is the majority of the six answers, kept only if
agreement is at least 0.6. Labels are generated text (option letter and first-person answer),
not probabilities.
5. SFT export.The training target is “<letter>. <first-person answer> ” with the student
input format above; 5% of rows are held out for validation loss (seed 42).
F.5 STUDENTTRAINING
All students are trained from Qwen3-4B with identical settings: LoRA (rank 16, α=32, dropout
0.05), learning rate 1×10 −4, 1 epoch, global batch size 32 (micro batch 1), maximum length 16,384,
and seed 42. We use AdamW (β=(0.9,0.999) , no weight decay) with a cosine schedule, 3% warmup,
