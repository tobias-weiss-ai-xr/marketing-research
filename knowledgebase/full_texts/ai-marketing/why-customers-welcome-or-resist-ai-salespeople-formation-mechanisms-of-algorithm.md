Abstract. Large language models (LLMs) now answer product questions, recommend items
and help close deals in online shops, banks and telecom services. Customers react to these
AI sales agents in two opposite ways. Some avoid them, a pattern researchers call algorithm
aversion, while others prefer them to human advice, a pattern called algorithm appreciation.
This paper reviews the main empirical studies on both patterns and asks how each one
forms when the "algorithm" is a conversational sales agent. Drawing on research in
judgment and decision making, consumer behavior and human computer interaction, it
proposes a framework with three layers. The first layer contains the cues that an LLM sales
agent gives off. The second layer contains five psychological pathways that operate during
the conversation: competence belief, error sensitivity, empathy and uniqueness concern,
social presence, and perceived control. The third layer contains situational conditions such
as product type, the timing of AI identity disclosure, the customer's emotional state and prior
AI experience. Six propositions are derived. The paper argues that aversion and appreciation
are not fixed traits of customers but outcomes of how these layers combine in a particular
sales encounter, and it discusses implications for firms and for future student research.
Keywords: Algorithm Aversion, Algorithm Appreciation, Large Language Models, AI Sales,
Chatbots
1. Introduction
Buying something has always involved a conversation. A shop assistant asks what the customer
needs, explains the options and tries to clear up doubts before the customer pays. Since generative
AI tools became widely available to the public, many companies have started to hand part of this
conversation to large language models. An LLM sales agent can talk in fluent natural language, keep
track of what the customer said earlier in the chat, compare products in seconds and work at any
hour.
Whether customers accept this new kind of salesperson is far from obvious. Research on AI in
service work shows real gains. In a study of 5,172 customer support agents, access to a generative
AI assistant raised the number of customer issues resolved per hour by 15% on average [1]. Yet a
field experiment with more than 6,200 customers of a financial services company found that when a

Psychologists have names for these two reactions. Algorithm aversion describes the tendency to
avoid an algorithm after seeing it make mistakes, even when it performs better than a human [3].
Algorithm appreciation describes the opposite finding that people often give more weight to advice
when they believe it comes from an algorithm rather than a person [4]. Much of this research,
however, used forecasting tasks with numbers, such as predicting the success of MBA applicants or
estimating a person's weight from a photograph. A sales conversation with an LLM is different. It is
social, emotional and persuasive, and the customer has money at stake.
This paper therefore asks one question: through what mechanisms do algorithm aversion and
algorithm appreciation form when customers deal with LLM sales agents? To answer it, the paper
first defines the key concepts, then reviews the empirical literature, and finally builds a theoretical
framework with testable propositions. The aim is not to report new data but to organise existing
findings in a way that a later survey or experiment could test.
2. Key concepts
2.1. Algorithm aversion
In the study that gave the idea its name, Dietvorst asked participants to make forecasts, for example
about how well MBA applicants would perform [3]. Participants could rely on their own forecasts or
on a statistical model. After watching the model make some errors, many of them chose to rely on
themselves, even though the model was more accurate than they were. The central point is that
people forgave human mistakes more easily than machine mistakes. Later reviews show that
aversion appears in many domains and has several kinds of causes. Burton reviewed 61 articles and
grouped the proposed causes and solutions into five themes: expectations and expertise, decision
autonomy, incentivization, cognitive compatibility, and divergent rationalities [5]. Mahmud
reviewed 80 empirical studies and sorted the influencing factors into four groups: factors of the
algorithm, of the individual, of the task, and of the wider environment [6].
2.2. Algorithm appreciation
Logg challenged the idea that people always distrust algorithms [4]. In a series of experiments,
participants received the same advice labelled either as coming from a person or from an algorithm.
They moved their own estimates further toward the advice when it was labelled as algorithmic. The
effect was weaker when people had to choose between the algorithm and their own judgment, and it
was weaker among experienced professionals. Jussupow later argued that aversion and appreciation
should be treated as two directions of one underlying preference, the preference for a human or an
algorithm in a decision, and that which direction appears depends on what is being compared in the
study [7].
2.3. The LLM sales scenario
In this paper, an LLM sales scenario means a buying situation in which the customer talks, by text or
voice, with an agent powered by a large language model whose job includes informing,
recommending, persuading and helping to complete a purchase. Three features set this scenario

3. Literature review
3.1. Evidence for algorithm aversion
Besides the error effect found by Dietvorst, two lines of consumer research are important for sales
[3]. Longoni found that consumers were reluctant to use medical AI, and one reason was uniqueness
neglect: the belief that an AI cannot take a person's unique characteristics and circumstances into
account [9]. Castelo showed that aversion depends on the task [10]. People trusted and used
algorithms less for tasks they saw as subjective, such as dating advice, than for tasks they saw as
objective, such as financial advice. In a field study with Facebook advertisements, dating advice ads
received more clicks when they were attributed to a human than to an algorithm, while financial
advice ads showed no such difference. Both findings suggest that aversion grows when a decision
feels personal.
3.2. Evidence for algorithm appreciation
Logg provided the clearest evidence of appreciation. Other studies show when appreciation is likely
[4]. Longoni and Cian identified a "word-of-machine" effect: people prefer AI recommenders when
utilitarian attributes, such as function and practical value, are important, and prefer human
recommenders when hedonic attributes, such as sensory pleasure, are important [11]. They trace this
to a lay belief that AI is more competent than humans in the utilitarian realm and less competent in
the hedonic realm. The effect disappeared when the recommendation came from a human and an AI
working together. Aversion can also be reduced by design. Dietvorst found that people were much
more willing to use an imperfect algorithm when they were allowed to modify its forecasts, even by
a small amount [12].
3.3. Evidence from sales and service chatbots
A smaller group of studies looks directly at chatbots that talk to customers. The field experiment by
Luo is the closest to a real sales setting [2]. Customers who were called by an undisclosed chatbot
bought about as often as those called by proficient human workers, and four times as often as those
called by inexperienced workers. When the chatbot disclosed its identity before the conversation,
customers became curt, calls became shorter and purchases fell sharply. The authors found that
customers perceived the disclosed bot as less knowledgeable and less empathetic, although its actual
performance had not changed. The negative effect was smaller when disclosure came later and
among customers with prior AI experience.
Two studies examine humanlike design. Adam ran an online experiment with a customer service
chatbot and found that humanlike verbal cues increased users' compliance with the chatbot's request,
an effect explained by a stronger sense of social presence [13]. Crolic, using data from an
international telecommunications company and four experiments, found that anthropomorphic

4. A theoretical framework
Figure 1 summarises the framework developed in this section. Its main idea is that a customer's
reaction to an LLM sales agent form in three layers. The first layer is the set of cues the agent gives
off: its language and persona, its product knowledge, the risk of hallucination, and the fact that it
serves the seller and may or may not disclose that it is an AI. The second layer is a set of
psychological pathways that turn these cues into trust or distrust during the conversation. The third
layer is the situation, which strengthens or weakens each pathway. In this view, aversion and
appreciation are two outcomes of the same evaluation process, and they differ only in which
pathways dominate.

4.1. Competence belief pathway
Many people hold a lay theory that machines are objective, consistent and well informed. Sundar
called this the machine heuristic: when a message is seen as coming from a machine, people may
judge it as more neutral and accurate [15]. Longoni and Cian found a related belief in consumer
choice, namely that AI is better than humans at judging utilitarian attributes [11]. An LLM sales
agent can retrieve specifications, prices and comparisons quickly, so when a customer is comparing
phone batteries or insurance terms, the agent's apparent knowledge fits this belief well.
P1. When utilitarian attributes of a product are salient, stronger beliefs in machine competence
lead customers to show algorithm appreciation toward an LLM sales agent.
4.2. Error sensitivity pathway
The belief that machines should be accurate also makes their mistakes more costly. Dietvorst
showed that seeing an algorithm err reduced people's use of it more than seeing a human make
errors [3]. Burton link this to expectations: if people expect an algorithm to be nearly perfect, any
error looks like proof that it cannot be trusted, whereas a human error looks like a normal slip [5].
LLMs make a particular kind of error, because they can state false information fluently [8]. In a
sales chat, an invented product feature or a wrong return policy is easy for a customer to discover
later, and because the error came in a confident and polished voice, the gap between what was
expected and what happened may be large.
P2. A visible factual error by an LLM sales agent reduces customers' willingness to rely on it
more than a comparable error by a human salesperson.

4.3. Empathy and uniqueness pathway
Selling is not only about facts; customers also want to feel understood. Luo found that once
customers knew the caller was a chatbot, they judged it as less knowledgeable and less empathetic,
even though nothing about its performance had changed [2]. Longoni described the related worry of
uniqueness neglect, and Castelo showed that aversion is stronger when a task feels subjective [9,
10]. Choosing a gift, a wedding outfit or a holiday involves personal taste and emotion, so these
concerns are more likely to be triggered in such purchases than in the purchase of a charging cable.
P3. When hedonic attributes or the customer's personal uniqueness are salient, perceived lack of
empathy and uniqueness neglect lead to algorithm aversion toward an LLM sales agent.
4.4. Social presence pathway
LLMs make it easy to give a sales agent a name, a friendly tone and a personality. Research on
computers as social actors shows that people respond to computers socially, applying norms such as
politeness and reciprocity, even though they know the computer is not a person [16]. Epley explain
that people are more likely to see human qualities in an agent when humanlike cues are present and
when they want to understand or connect with it [17]. Humanlike design can therefore raise social
presence and cooperation, as Adam found [13]. Crolic show the other side: for angry customers, the
same design raised expectations that the chatbot could not meet. Anthropomorphism therefore cuts
both ways [14].
P4. Humanlike design of an LLM sales agent increases algorithm appreciation among customers
in a neutral emotional state but increases algorithm aversion among customers who are angry.
4.5. Control and transparency pathway
People accept algorithms more readily when they keep some control over the final decision. This is
shown by the modification effect in Dietvorst, by the disappearance of the word-of-machine effect
under human and AI collaboration in Longoni and Cian, and by the setting studied by Brynjolfsson,
in which human agents could accept, edit or ignore the AI's suggestions [1, 11, 12]. In sales, control
could mean that customers can ask the agent to explain a recommendation, change the filters it uses,
or reach a human at any time. Transparency about AI identity belongs to the same pathway. Luo
found that the penalty for disclosure was smaller when it came later and when customers already had
experience with AI, which suggests that familiarity shapes how disclosure is received [2].
P5. Giving customers control over an LLM sales agent's recommendation, including an easy
route to a human, increases algorithm appreciation.
P6. The negative effect of AI identity disclosure on purchase is weaker among customers with
more prior AI experience and when the conversation has already shown the agent's competence.

5. Discussion
5.1. One evaluation process, two outcomes
The review suggests that customers should not be divided into "AI lovers" and "AI haters." The
same person may be happy to let an LLM agent pick a laptop charger and still refuse its help with an
anniversary gift, or may trust it at the start of a chat and leave after one wrong answer. This is
consistent with the argument of Jussupow that the direction of preference depends on the decision
setting [7]. The framework also adds a sense of time. Before and at the start of a conversation,
competence beliefs and disclosure are likely to matter most. As the conversation continues, errors,
perceived empathy and the feeling of being in control update the customer's judgment. Appreciation
can therefore turn into aversion within a single chat, and the reverse is also possible.
5.2. Implications for firms
Four practical points follow. First, LLM agents are likely to be most welcome in purchases driven by
utilitarian attributes, while hedonic and emotional purchases may benefit from a human and AI
combination, which removed the word-of-machine effect in Longoni and Cian [11]. Second, because
machine errors weigh heavily, answers about prices, specifications and policies should be checked
against verified product data before they reach the customer. Third, the agent's persona could adapt
to the customer's mood; a plainer and less humanlike style may suit complaint situations, given the
findings of Crolic [14]. Fourth, hiding the AI identity is not an acceptable solution on ethical
grounds. A better response to the disclosure penalty found by Luo is to earn trust through visible
competence and to offer customers control, including a clear way to reach a human [2].
6. Limitations and future research
This paper is a theoretical review and does not test its propositions. Several of the studies it relies on
were carried out before modern LLMs became common, so features such as long, flexible
conversations and hallucination need to be studied directly. Most experiments also come from a
small number of countries, and cultural differences in trust toward machines and toward salespeople
may change the strength of each pathway. Future work could test the framework with a simple
online experiment that a student research team could run. For example, participants could read a
sales chat about either a utilitarian product or a hedonic product, in which the AI agent either makes
a factual error or does not. Measuring willingness to buy through the agent, perceived competence
and perceived empathy would test P1, P2 and P3 together. Surveys of real customers who have used
AI shopping assistants could then examine P5 and P6.

7. Conclusion
Large language models are changing who, or what, customers talk to when they buy. Earlier
research found both algorithm aversion and algorithm appreciation, and the evidence from sales and
service chatbots shows that both appear in commercial settings. This paper has argued that the two
reactions come from the same process. Cues from the LLM agent activate five pathways, namely
competence belief, error sensitivity, empathy and uniqueness concern, social presence, and control
and transparency, and situational conditions decide which pathways dominate. Understanding these
mechanisms can help firms design AI sales agents that customers trust for good reasons and help
researchers test when and why that trust is given or withdrawn.
