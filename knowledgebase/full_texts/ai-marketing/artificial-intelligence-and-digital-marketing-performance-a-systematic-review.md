	Background:	The	 evolution	 of	 digital	 technology	 has	 transformed	 how
companies	 market	 products	 and	 interact	 with	 consumers,	 particularly
through	 digital	 platforms.	 Intensifying	 competition	 has	 encouraged
businesses	 to	 use	 technologies	 that	 support	 more	 targeted	 and
measurable	 marketing	 activities.	 Artificial	 intelligence	 (AI)	 is	 one	 of	 the
technologies	increasingly	applied	in	this	area.
Objective:	This	study	synthesizes	how	AI	is	utilized	in	digital	marketing,
identifies	 the	 performance	 indicators	 reported	 in	 the	 literature,	 and
examines	 the	 factors	 that	 enable	 or	 constrain	 its	 effectiveness,	 with
particular	attention	to	its	relevance	to	online	marketplace	contexts.
Method:	A	 systematic	 review	 was	 conducted	 using	 17	 peer-reviewed
articles	retrieved	from	the	Scopus	database.
Results:	The	reviewed	literature	identifies	four	main	forms	of	AI	utilization:
prediction	 and	 targeting,	 personalization	 and	 user	 experience,	 customer
relationship	 management	 and	 data	 integration,	 and	 segmentation	 and
strategic	 capability	 enhancement.	 Digital	 marketing	 performance	 is
assessed	 through	 behavioral	and	 conversion	 indicators,	 experience	 and
relational	indicators,	and	market	and	financial	indicators.	The	literature	also
identifies	 data	 quality,	 organizational	 capabilities,	 and	 strategic	 clarity	 as
enabling	 factors,	 while	 algorithmic	 bias,	 privacy	 conc erns,	 limited
transparency,	and	inadequate	governance	constitute	important	constraints.
Conclusion:	AI	 has	 the	 potential	 to	 support	 improvements	 in	 digital
marketing	 performance,	 but	 the	 reported	 outcomes	 depend	 on	 the
implementation	 context,	 organizational	 capabilities,	 data	 quality,	 and
governance	 practices.	 This	 review	 contributes	 an	 integrated	 conceptual
mapping	 of	 AI	 utilization,	 performance	 indicators,	 and	 enabling	 and
constraining	factors	relevant	to	digital	marketing	and	online	marketplace
research.

INTRODUCTION
The	 evolution	 of	 artificial	 intelligence	 (AI)	 in	 digital	 marketing	 has	 fundamentally
transformed	 how	 organizations	 understand,	 reach,	 and	 engage	 consumers	 in	 the	 digital
environment.	 This	 transformation	 extends	 beyond	 a	 mere	 technological	 shift;	 it	 represents	 a
strategic	evolution	that	integrates	predictive	capabilities,	personalization,	segmentation,	channel
optimization,	customer	analytics,	and	performance	evaluation	into	more	measurable	marketing
practices.	 At	 the	 global	 level,	 the	 rapid	 growth	 of	 online	marketplace	 platforms	such	 as	 e-
commerce	 sites	 where	 digital	 promotions,	 algorithmic	 recommendations,	 product	 rankings,
consumer	reviews,	sponsored	advertisements,	and	purchasing	decisions	converge	has	intensified

competition	 and	 driven	 businesses	 to	 leverage	 advanced	 technologies	 for	 enhancing	 digital
marketing	effectiveness.	Huang	(2022)	demonstrates	that	big	data	and	machine	learning	serve	as
tools	for	improving	marketing	strategies	through	user	segmentation,	content	personalization,	and
channel	 integration.	Choi	and	Choi	 (2023)	show	 that	 machine	 learning	 can	 predict	 direct
marketing	responses,	thereby	making	campaigns	more	targeted	and	efficient.	Chinakidzwa	and
Phiri	(2020)	and	Krisnanto	et	al.	(2023)	emphasize	that	digital	technology	does	not	automatically

(2022)	and	Mrad	and	Hnich	 (2024)	expands	 this	 understanding	 to	 encompass	 areas	 including
literature	 mapping,	 systematic	 reviews,	 customer	 analytics,	 attribution	 modeling,
recommendation	systems,	user	experience,	governance,	and	algorithmic	bias.	Collectively,	these
studies	indicate	that	AI	in	digital	marketing	has	evolved	into	a	sociotechnical	system	operating
through	data,	models,	strategies,	consumer	experiences,	and	ethical	governance.
Despite	the	growing	body	of	literature,	prior	research	reveals	three	significant	gaps	that
necessitate	 a	 more	 focused	 synthesis.	 First,	 studies	 have	 spanned	 diverse	 and	 fragmented
contexts	(ranging	from	education,	traditional	SMEs,	direct	marketing,	food	delivery,	and	online
donations	 to	 social	 media	 marketing	 and	 B2C	 e-commerce	 attribution	 modeling)	resulting	 in	 a
rich	 but	 scattered	 body	 of	 work	 that	 has	 not	 consistently	 centered	 on	 online	 marketplace
consumers	 as	 a	 distinct	 analytical	 subject.	 Second,	 definitions	 and	 measurements	 of	 'digital
marketing	 performance'	 lack	 uniformity	 across	 studies.	 Some	emphasize	 campaign	 response
prediction	accuracy,	others	highlight	customer	satisfaction	and	loyalty,	some	assess	engagement
and	 retention,	 while	 others	 prioritize	 profitability,	 market	 share,	 and	 sales	 growth.	 This
inconsistency	 has	 rendered	 'digital	 marketing	 performance'	 an	 umbrella	 term	 obscuring
fundamentally	 different	 outcome	levels.	 Third,	 there	 is	 insufficient	 integration	 between
performance	 enhancement	 discussions	 and	 critical	 concerns	 such	 as	 ethics,	 privacy,	 fairness,
algorithmic	opacity,	and	bias.	Research	by	Akter	et	al.	(2022),	Kaponis	et	al.	(2025),	and	Volkmar
et	 al.	 (2022)	demonstrates	 that	 AI	 effectiveness	 relies	 heavily	 on	 model	 design,	 governance,
system	 trust,	 and	 bias	 risk	 management,	 confirming	 that	 performance	 improvement	 is	 never
automatic.	These	gaps	underscore	the	urgency	of	this	research:	it	is	no	longer	sufficient	to	merely
reiterate	 that	 AI	 benefits	 digital	 marketing.	 Instead,	 it	 is	 necessary	 to	 explain	 more	 specifically
how	AI	is	utilized,	which	indicators	assess	its	impact,	and	what	factors	act	as	enablers,	barriers,	or
boundary	conditions	for	its	effectiveness.
The	 novelty	 of	 this	 study	 lies	 in	 the	 integrative	 synthesis	 developed	 from	 the	 current
review,	which	connects	three	dimensions:	forms	of	AI	utilization,	digital	marketing	performance
indicators,	and	enabling	or	constraining	factors.	Unlike	previous	studies	that	generally	examine
these	 dimensions	 separately,	 this	 review	 organizes	 the	 scattered	 findings	 into	 a	 unified
conceptual	mapping.	The	first	objective	is	to	identify	the	forms	of	AI	application	reported	in	the
literature,	including	prediction	and	targeting,	personalization	and	user	experience,	CRM	and	data
integration,	and	segmentation	and	strategic	capability	enhancement.	The	second	objective	is	to
classify	 the	 indicators	 used	 to	 assess	 digital	 marketing	 performance	 into	 behavioral	and
conversion,	experience	and	relational,	and	market	and	financial	outcomes.	The	third	objective	is
to	examine	the	conditions	associated	with	AI	effectiveness,	including	data	quality,	analytical	and
digital	 marketing	 capabilities,	 strategic	 clarity,	 customer	 relationship	 orientation,	 privacy,
fairness,	 transparency,	 and	 algorithmic	 bias.	 Although	 this	 framework	 is	 relevant	 to	 online
marketplaces,	 the	 reviewed	 studies	 cover	 multiple	 digital	 marketing	 contexts;	 therefore,	 its
relevance	 to	 online	 marketplace	consumers	 should	 be	 interpreted	 as	 a	 conceptual	 orientation
rather	than	as	marketplace-specific	empirical	evidence.
Based	on	these	gaps,	three	research	questions	were	formulated.	First,	how	is	AI	utilized	to
enhance	digital	marketing	performance	as	described	in	the	scholarly	literature?	Second,	which
digital	marketing	performance	indicators	are	most	frequently	used	in	the	literature	to	assess	AI's
impact?	 Third,	 what	 conceptual	 factors	 are	 identified	 in	 the	 literature	 as	 enablers,	 barriers,	 or

expanded	 significantly	 yet	 remains	 fragmented	 across	 contexts,	 performance	 indicators,	 and
determinants	of	effectiveness.	The	final	sample	comprises	17	studies	selected	through	a	multi-
stage	screening	process.
The	search	was	conducted	on	July	15,	2026,	using	the	Scopus	database.	The	initial	search
used	 the	 following	 Boolean	 query	 in	 the	 title,	 abstract,	 and	 keyword	 fields:	 TITLE -ABS-
KEY(("artificial	intelligence"	OR	"machine	learning"	OR	"deep	learning")	AND	("digital	marketing"
OR	 "online	 marketing"	 OR	 "e-marketing")	 AND	 ("marketplace"	 OR	 "e-commerce"	 OR	 "online
consumer"	 OR	 "digital	 consumer")).	 The	 search	 yielded	 202	 records.	 The	 publication-year
criterion	of	2016–2026	was	subsequently	applied	during	pre-screening,	resulting	in	the	exclusion
of	 two	 records	 published	 before	 2016.	 The	 search	 terms	 represented	 three	 main	 conceptual
clusters:	 online	 marketplace	 users,	 machine	 learning	 in	 marketing,	 and	 digital	 marketing
performance.
The	inclusion	criteria	were:	(1)	peer-reviewed	journal	articles	or	conference	proceedings
indexed	in	Scopus;	(2)	publications	from	2016	to	2026;	(3)	articles	written	in	English;	(4)	studies
addressing	 AI	 or	 machine	 learning	 in	 digital	 marketing	 contexts;	 and	 (5)	 empirical	 studies,
systematic	reviews,	or	conceptual	studies	with	an	identifiable	methodology.	The	exclusion	criteria
were:	 (1)	 duplicate	 records;	 (2)	 records	 without	 accessible	 abstracts;	 (3)	 studies	 outside	 the
specified	publication	period;	(4)	studies	focusing	exclusively	on	non-marketing	AI	applications;
and	(5)	reports	for	which	the	full	text	could	not	be	retrieved.	During	pre-screening,	no	duplicate
records	were	identified,	two	records	were	excluded	based	on	the	publication-year	criterion,	and
30	records	were	excluded	because	their	journal	sources	did	not	meet	the	stated	Scopus	Q1–Q4
criterion.	Of	the	170	records	screened	by	title	and	abstract,	132	were	excluded	because	they	did
not	align	sufficiently	with	the	research	focus.	Full	texts	were	sought	for	38	reports,	of	which	21
could	 not	 be	 retrieved.	 Consequently,	 17	 studies	 proceeded	 to	 eligibility	 assessment	 and	 were
included	in	the	final	synthesis.	The	unavailability	of	21	full-text	reports	is	recognized	as	a	potential
source	of	retrieval	bias.
The	data	collection	followed	the	PRISMA	flow:	identification	(202	records),	pre-screening
(170	records	after	removing	2	via	timeframe	filter	and	30	via	journal	quality	criteria),	screening
(38	 records	 after	 excluding	 132	 based	 on	 title-abstract	 review),	 retrieval	 (17	 reports	 obtained
from	38	sought;	21	inaccessible),	eligibility	(17	reports	assessed	with	no	further	exclusions),	and
inclusion	(17	studies	in	the	final	corpus).	The	research	instruments	consist	of	an	article	selection
protocol	 verifying	 relevance	 to	 AI	 utilization	 in	 digital	 marketing	 performance,	 and	 a	 data
extraction	sheet	recording	article	details,	publication	year,	study	context,	forms	of	AI	utilization,
performance	indicators,	facilitating	and	hindering	factors,	and	key	findings	aligned	with	the	three
research	questions.
The	 data	 analysis	 employed	 qualitative	 content	 analysis	 and	 thematic	 synthesis.	 Each
article	was	read	in	full,	and	information	relevant	to	the	three	research	questions	was	extracted
using	 the	 data	 extraction	 sheet.	 The	 extracted	 information	 was	 grouped	 according	 to	 semantic
similarity	to	establish	thematic	categories.	For	RQ1,	the	analysis	identified	four	categories	of	AI
utilization:	 prediction	 and	 targeting,	 personalization	 and	 user	 experience,	 CRM	 and	 data
integration,	 and	 segmentation	 and	 strategic	 capabilities.	 For	 RQ2,	 the	 performance	 indicators
were	 organized	 into	behavioral	and	 conversion,	 experience	 and	 relational,	 and	 market	 and
financial	outcomes.	For	RQ3,	the	extracted	factors	were	classified	into	data	quality,	analytical	and
digital	 marketing	 capabilities,	 customer	 relationship	 orientation,	 strategic	 clarity,	 governance,
privacy,	 fairness,	 and	 algorithmic	 bias.	 Coding	 consistency	 was	 maintained	 through	 iterative
comparison	 between	 the	 initial	 codes	 and	 the	 original	 article	 texts.	 Codes	 were	 revised	 when
categories	 overlapped	 or	 did	 not	 adequately	 represent	 the	 extracted	evidence.	 A	 cross-study
comparison	 was	 subsequently	 conducted	 to	 identify	 dominant,	 minor,	 consistent,	 and
contradictory	 findings,	 followed	 by	 a	 narrative	 synthesis	 integrating	 the	 results	 across	 the
reviewed	studies.

Discussion
The	 findings	 of	 this	 study	 indicate	 that	 the	 use	 of	 AI	 in	 digital	 marketing	 cannot	 be
understood	 as	 a	 single	 phenomenon	 but	 rather	 as	 a	 series	 of	 interconnected	 mechanisms
operating	 through	 data,	 prediction,	 personalization,	 strategy,	 and	 customer	 relation ship
management.	 The	 reviewed	 literature	 consistently	 demonstrates	 a	 shift	 from	 intuition-based
marketing	 toward	 data-driven	 approaches	 leveraging	 big	 data,	 machine	 learning,	 and	 digital
analytics.	 This	 shift	 has	 significant	 theoretical	 implications,	 as	 it	positions	 AI	 not	 merely	 as	 a
technological	innovation	but	as	a	probabilistic	mechanism	that	can	improve	promotion	allocation,
targeting,	and	estimation	of	consumer	response	opportunities.	However,	this	effectiveness	always
emerges	 through	 specific	 organizational	 conditions,	 consumer	 behavioral	 dynamics,	 and	 the
quality	of	system	design,	rather	than	automatically	from	the	technology	itself.
Regarding	 RQ1,	 the	 dominant	 form	 of	 AI	 utilization	 identified	 in	 this	 synthesis	 is
prediction,	targeting,	and	optimization	of	marketing	decisions.	This	finding	is	consistent	with	the
broader	 trajectory	 of	 marketing	 automation	 literature,	 where	 the	 primary	value	 of	 AI	 lies	 in
reducing	 decision	 uncertainty	(Davenport	 et	 al.,	 2020;	Kumar	 et	 al.,	 2019;	Labib,	 2024).	The
present	synthesis	extends	this	understanding	by	demonstrating	that	prediction	capabilities	span
multiple	 functional	 areas	from	 direct	 marketing	 response	 estimation	 to	 real-time	 conversion
probability	 calculation	 and	 multi-touch	 attribution	 modeling.	 Equally	 significant	 are	 the
personalization,	 segmentation,	 and	 customer	 relationship	 management	 applications,	 which
position	AI	as	both	a	targeting	instrument	and	a	relational	infrastructure	that	shapes	consumer
experiences	 and	 maintains	 relationship	 continuity.	 This	 is	 particularly	 relevant	 for	 online
marketplace	 environments,	 where	 algorithmic	 recommendations,	 product	 rankings,	 a nd
personalized	promotions	are	foundational	mechanisms.
Regarding	 RQ2,	 the	 variation	 in	 performance	 indicators	 demonstrates	 that	 digital
marketing	performance	is	represented	at	several	outcome	levels	in	the	reviewed	literature.	At	the
behavioral	and	conversion	level,	the	studies	assess	indicators	such	as	clicks,	campaign	responses,
prediction	 accuracy,	 purchase	 intention,	 and	 conversion	 probability.	 At	 the	 experience	 and
relational	level,	the	reported	indicators	include	user	experience,	engagement,	customer	retention,
satisfaction,	 brand	 admiration,	 and	 relationship	 quality.	 At	 the	 market	 and	 financial	 level,	 the
indicators	 include	 market	 effectiveness,	 profitability,	 sales	 growth,	 market	 share,	 revenue,	 and
sustainable	business	performance.	This	three-layer	classification	provides	conceptual	clarity	by
showing	that	digital	marketing	performance	should	not	be	treated	as	a	single	outcome.	In	practice,
the	 classification	 also	 suggests	 that	 evaluations	 of	 AI-supported	 marketing	 should	 consider
multiple	indicators	because	changes	in	engagement	or	personalization	quality	do	not	necessarily
correspond	directly	to	changes	in	profitability.
Regarding	RQ3,	the	reviewed	literature	indicates	that	the	effectiveness	of	AI-supported
digital	 marketing	 depends	 on	 organizational	 and	 technical	 conditions,	 including	 data	 quality,
analytical	capabilities,	customer	relationship	orientation,	strategic	clarity,	and	leadership.	These
findings	show	that	access	to	AI	technology	alone	does	not	ensure	improved	marketing	outcomes.

The	theoretical	implications	of	this	study	are	fourfold.	First,	AI	in	digital	marketing	should
be	understood	within	a	framework	that	transcends	technological	determinism,	recognizing	that

the	 study	 disentangles	 three	 conceptual	 levels	 often	 conflated	 in	 the	 literature:	 forms	 of	 AI

AI	 in	 digital	 marketing	 should	 integrate	 the	 technical-predictive,	 behavioral-experiential,	 and
ethical-governance	dimensions	simultaneously.	Fourth,	online	marketplaces	warrant	treatment
as	a	distinct	analytical	context	due	to	their	unique	algorithmic	competition	dynamics,	rather	than
as	an	extension	of	general	e-commerce	or	social	media	marketing.	Practically,	these	findings	imply
that	marketplace	operators	should	deploy	AI	not	merely	to	add	features	but	to	formulate	more
precise	data-driven	marketing	decisions.	For	digital	marketers,	AI's	targeting	and	personalization
capabilities	 remain	 beneficial	 only	 when	 supported	 by	 quality	 data,	 clear	 strategy,	 and
organizational	capacity.	For	managers,	the	findings	serve	as	a	caution	that	challenges	often	lie	in
organizational	culture,	governance,	and	the	tendency	to	view	AI	as	a	universal	solution.
Several	limitations	of	this	study	must	be	acknowledged	and	considered	when	interpreting
the	conclusions.	First,	the	reviewed	literature	spans	diverse	contexts	(education,	direct	marketing,
SMEs,	food	delivery,	online	donations,	social	media	marketing,	and	B2C	e-commerce)	and	not	all
studies	 position	 online	 marketplace	 consumers	 as	 their	 primary	 subject.	 The	 synthesis	 is
therefore	 oriented	 toward	 this	 context	 rather	 than	 exclusively	 derived	 from	 it.	 Second,	 'digital
marketing	performance'	is	not	consistently	measured	across	studies,	limiting	direct	comparisons.
Third,	 some	 studies	 emphasize	 technical	 model	 performance	 without	 exploring	 consumer
experiences,	 while	 others	 focus	 on	 behavior	 without	 situating	 findings	 within	 marketplace
environments.	 Fourth,	 methodological	 limitations	 in	 the	 primary	 studies	(including	 cross-
sectional	 designs,	 small	 samples,	 single-context	 settings,	 and	 simulated	 data)	constrain	 the
generalizability	of	the	synthesized	findings.	Fifth,	the	21	articles	that	could	not	be	retrieved	during
the	 full-text	 retrieval	 stage	 represent	 a	 potential	 retrieval	 bias.	 These	 limitations	 are
acknowledged	not	to	diminish	the	study's	contribution	but	to	define	the	boundaries	within	which
its	findings	should	be	interpreted.

CONCLUSION
This	systematic	review	addressed	three	interconnected	questions	regarding	AI	utilization
in	 digital	 marketing.	 First,	 AI	 is	 utilized	 through	 four	 primary	 mechanisms:	 prediction	 and
targeting,	personalization	and	user	experience,	CRM	and	data	integration,	and	segmentation	and
strategic	 capability	 enhancement.	 Second,	 digital	 marketing	 performance	 indicators	 operate
across	 three	 distinct	 outcome	 layers:	 behavioral/conversion	 (response	 rates,	 conversion
probability,	 purchase	 intention),	 experience/relational	 (satisfaction,	 engagement,	 retention,
relationship	 quality),	 and	 market/financial	 (market	 effectiveness,	 profitability,	 sales	 growth).
Third,	AI	effectiveness	is	enabled	by	data	quality,	analytical	capabilities,	customer	orientation,	and
strategic	 clarity,	while	 constrained	 by	 algorithmic	 bias,	 privacy	 concerns,	 lack	 of	 transparency,
and	 inadequate	 governance.	 A	 central	 conclusion	 is	 that	 AI	 has	 the	 potential	 to	 contribute	 to
improving	 digital	 marketing	 performance,	 but	 its	 outcomes	 are	 contingent	 upon	 the
implementation	context	rather	than	automatic.
This	 study	 contributes	 to	 the	 literature	 by	 organizing	 fragmented	 findings	 into	 an
integrated	framework	encompassing	AI	utilization,	digital	marketing	performance	indicators,	and
enabling	and	constraining	conditions.	The	three-layer	classification	of	behavioral	and	conversion,
experience	 and	 relational,	 and	 market	 and	 financial	 outcomes	 provides	 a	 clearer	 basis	 for
interpreting	digital	marketing	performance.	Because	the	reviewed	studies	cover	diverse	contexts
and	 are	 not	 exclusively	 focused	 on	 online	 marketplace	 consumers,	 the	 proposed	 framework
requires	 further	 empirical	 validation	 in	 marketplace	 settings.	 Future	 research	 should	 examine
online	 marketplace	 consumers	 directly,	 test	 the	 relationships	 proposed	 in	the	 framework,
integrate	 technical	 model	 performance	 with	 consumer	 behavioral	 evidence,	 and	 investigate
