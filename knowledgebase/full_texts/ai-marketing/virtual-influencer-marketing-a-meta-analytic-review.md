Abstract
Virtual influencers have become an established part of influencer marketing, yet their effectiveness, the mechanisms
underlying their persuasive impact, and the boundary conditions shaping these effects remain unclear. We address these
questions through a meta-analysis of 210 independent experimental studies and 643 effect sizes. Virtual influencers gen -
erate greater novelty but lower credibility than human influencers, revealing a systematic trade-off between novelty- and
credibility-based persuasion, with the relative importance of these mechanisms shifting over time from novelty toward
credibility. Virtual influencer effectiveness further depends on anthropomorphic design and contextual congruence. Highly
anthropomorphic virtual influencers benefit from artificial intelligence integration, futuristic positioning, and rational,
proximal messaging, whereas low-anthropomorphism virtual influencers perform better with emotional and distal framing.
Meta-analytic structural equation modeling supports a dual-pathway process in which credibility-based indirect effects
are stronger for highly anthropomorphic virtual influencers, whereas novelty-based indirect effects are stronger for low-
anthropomorphism virtual influencers. These results establish anthropomorphism as a structural boundary condition in
influencer marketing.

Despite growing practitioner adoption and scholarly
attention, important gaps remain in understanding (1) the
effects of virtual influencers on consumer responses (i.e.,
credibility, brand and ad attitudes, engagement, and behav -
ioral intentions), (2) the boundary conditions that shape their
effect, and (3) the mechanisms through which they influence
consumer responses. Empirical findings on the effectiveness
of virtual influencers are heterogeneous, particularly regard-
ing persuasion outcomes. Some studies suggest that virtual
influencers can increase consumer engagement (Li et al.,
2023; Sands et al., 2022) or perform comparably to human
influencers (V olles et al., 2024), whereas others report
weaker effects (Xie & Johnson, 2015; Zhao et al., 2024).
These mixed results make it difficult to draw clear conclu -
sions about when and why virtual influencers are effective.
Uncertainty also surrounds the boundary conditions that
shape the effectiveness of virtual influencers. Research
reports conflicting findings across influencer characteris -
tics, product contexts, and message strategies. Some studies
suggest that highly anthropomorphic virtual influencers—
defined as having a higher degree of human likeness in their
appearance and behavior (e.g., human-like virtual influenc-
ers such as @imma.gram)—are more effective than those
with lower levels of anthropomorphism (e.g., cartoon-like
virtual influencers such as @anymalu_real or characters
such as @nobodysausage) (Xie-Carson et al., 2023). Other
studies find no effect (Ma & Li, 2024; Qu & Baek, 2023),
whereas some report the opposite pattern (Wang & Qiu,
2023). These inconsistencies suggest that anthropomor -
phism functions not simply as a direct predictor of effective-
ness but as a structural boundary condition that shapes how
persuasion operates across contexts.
Persuasion in influencer marketing has traditionally been
explained through credibility-based mechanisms, as human
influencers are often perceived as knowledgeable and trust-
worthy sources of information (Barari et al., 2025; Pan et
al., 2024). Because virtual influencers lack lived experience
or expertise in the conventional sense, scholars have pro -
posed novelty as an alternative mechanism explaining their
influence (Franke et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024). However,
it remains unclear whether credibility and novelty operate
differently across levels of anthropomorphism. Moreover,
little is known about how these mechanisms evolve. Novel
phenomena often lose their impact as consumers become
accustomed to them (Bernstein, 1989; Redden, 2008), rais-
ing the question of whether declining novelty may be offset
by increasing credibility as virtual influencers become more
familiar in the marketplace.
To address these questions, our meta-analysis synthe -
sizes results from 210 experimental studies across 110
papers, yielding 643 effect sizes to evaluate the overall and
conditional effectiveness of virtual influencers on engage -
ment, ad and brand attitudes, behavioral intentions, credibil-
ity, attractiveness, and novelty. Overall, we find that virtual
influencers are more novel and just as effective as human
influencers in driving consumer engagement but less effec -
tive in shaping credibility, attitudes, and behavioral inten -
tions. To explain the observed heterogeneity and to draw
on inconsistent findings in the virtual influencer and wider
influencer marketing literature, we include a broad set of
moderators grounded in prior research. These modera -
tors span four dimensions: influencer characteristics (e.g.,
anthropomorphism and autonomy), brand characteristics
(e.g., brand type, positioning, and communication style),
product attributes (e.g., familiarity, self-improvement fram-
ing, and involvement), and message features (e.g., mes -
sage appeal, point of view, and sensory capacity). A key
distinctive feature of virtual influencers, and therefore the
focal moderator in our analysis, is their level of anthro -
pomorphism. We propose different mechanisms within a
dual-pathway framework: highly anthropomorphic virtual
influencers are expected to persuade through credibility,
whereas those with low anthropomorphism are expected to
persuade through novelty. We also incorporated time into
the model to show how credibility and novelty evolve.
Our results provide several new insights. Highly anthro -
pomorphic virtual influencers are more effective when
they are associated with autonomy, aligned with futuristic
brand positioning, endorse new products, use rational mes -
sage framing, and emphasize proximal sensory cues such
as touch, taste, or smell. In contrast, low-anthropomorphic
virtual influencers perform better when they are not associ -
ated with autonomy, are paired with traditional brands and
familiar products, and use emotional messages or distal
sensory cues such as sight or sound. Explaining these pat -
terns, the mediation analysis reveals that both novelty and
credibility act as mechanisms through which virtual influ -
encers shape consumer responses. Credibility emerged as
the dominant pathway for highly anthropomorphic virtual
influencers, whereas novelty was the stronger driver for
low-anthropomorphism virtual influencers. Further, the
analysis shows that the mediating effect of novelty declined
over time as consumers habituated to virtual influencers,
whereas credibility increased as these influencers became
more established.
Together, these findings establish anthropomorphism as
a structural boundary condition in virtual influencer mar -
keting. Rather than viewing virtual influencers as weaker
or stronger substitutes for human endorsers, the results

Conceptual background
Virtual influencer marketing
Influencer marketing is a strategy in which brands collabo -
rate with individuals or entities who can shape consumer
attitudes and behaviors toward a product or brand (Barari
et al., 2025). As shown in Table 1, influencers include
celebrities, social media influencers, and virtual influenc -
ers, each drawing on distinct sources of persuasive power
such as expertise, fame, credibility, or novelty to promote
products, services, or ideas (Wies et al., 2023). While tra -
ditional media once served as the primary platform for
influencer marketing, the landscape has increasingly shifted
toward social media (Leung et al., 2022). However, virtual
influencers differ in a fundamental way. Because they are
computer-generated rather than biological individuals, their
persuasive potential is not inherently grounded in lived
experience, expertise, or authentic self-presentation. This
distinction fundamentally alters the basis on which persua -
sive influence may operate.
Virtual influencers are the latest development in influ -
encer marketing, represented by computer-generated per -
sonas created through graphic design and, more recently,
enhanced by GenAI to engage audiences on social media
platforms (Zhou et al., 2023). Previous research suggests
that virtual influencers may not be as effective as human
influencers because of their lack of authenticity and credibil-
ity compared with opinion leaders or social media influenc-
ers (Di Cioccio et al., 2024), or their lack of fame relative to
celebrities (Knoll & Matthes, 2017); yet, they offer signifi -
cant advantages. Virtual influencers are cost-effective and
highly scalable, allowing brands to engage diverse audi -
ences across multiple markets without the logistical con -
straints of working with human influencers (Laszkiewicz
& Kalinska-Kula, 2023). Recent advancements in GenAI
further enhance its creativity, enabling creators to generate
engaging, visually compelling content at lower costs (Feng
et al., 2024).
While human influencers vary in their source of fame
(Knoll & Matthes, 2017) and audience size (Tian et al.,
2024), virtual influencers can be differentiated by their
degree of anthropomorphism (Laszkiewicz & Kalinska-
Kula, 2023). Anthropomorphism refers to the attribution
of human-like characteristics—such as traits, intentions,
emotions, and psychological states—to nonhuman entities
(Epley et al., 2007; Laszkiewicz & Kalinska-Kula, 2023).
This process is also referred to as humanization (Feng et al.,
2024). For the purposes of this research, we define anthro -
pomorphism as the degree of human likeness expressed
through a virtual influencer’s appearance and behavior,
shaping how human the influencer is perceived to be. Highly
anthropomorphic virtual influencers, such as @lilmiquela,
closely resemble human influencers in their expressions and
interactions (Aggarwal & McGill, 2012). Conversely, low-
anthropomorphic virtual influencers, such as the cartoon-
like @noonoouri or the character-based @nobodysausage,
maintain distinct nonhuman identities.
This variation introduces a central theoretical tension. If
persuasive influence is decoupled from biological human -
ness, does increasing human likeness restore evaluations

Virtual influencers are not the first type of nonhuman
endorser used in marketing. Firms have long relied on
spokes-characters, defined as nonhuman anthropomor -
phized characters created to represent and promote a brand
(Garretson & Burton, 2005; Garretson & Niedrich, 2004).
Classic examples include the Jolly Green Giant, Mr. Peanut,
and the Energizer Bunny, which symbolically communicate
brand attributes and strengthen brand associations (Folse
et al., 2012). Although both virtual influencers and spokes-
characters are nonhuman endorsers, they differ in structur -
ally important ways. To better situate virtual influencers
relative to spokes-characters, Table 2 presents a framework
distinguishing these nonhuman endorsers along two dimen-
sions: brand control (independent vs. brand-owned) and
anthropomorphism (high vs. low).
Virtual influencers are typically independent endorsers,
created and managed by studios or talent agencies, and who
collaborate with multiple brands across campaigns (Thomas
et al., 2025). This independence aligns them with influencer
marketing, in which perceived autonomy can enhance
credibility but reduce direct brand control (Sorosrungru -
ang et al., 2024). In contrast, spokes-characters are usually
brand-owned assets that serve as long-term brand symbols
fully controlled by firms (Folse et al., 2012). Both forms
of nonhuman endorsers also vary in their degree of anthro -
pomorphism, defined as the attribution of human-like char -
acteristics to nonhuman entities (Epley et al., 2007). Some
virtual influencers closely resemble humans (e.g., Imma),
while others adopt stylized or character-like forms (e.g.,
Nobody Sausage) (Laszkiewicz & Kalinska-Kula, 2023).
A similar distinction applies to spokes-characters, ranging
from human-like mascots such as Mr. Clean to whimsical
figures such as the Energizer Bunny.

human influencers, especially regarding behavioral inten -

behavioral intentions (Igarashi et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023).

Zhou et al., 2023). Similarly, previous research on consumer

Moderators and interaction effects
The mixed findings on moderator effects in Table 3 sug -
gest that rather than treating these moderators as indepen -
dent contingencies, we integrate them through a common
theoretical lens. Central to our model is virtual influencer
anthropomorphism (the extent to which a virtual influencer
resembles a human in appearance and behavior). Drawing
on fit theory (Kamins, 1990), which posits that endorsement
effectiveness depends on the perceived match between
source characteristics and the endorsed brand, product, or
message (Barari et al., 2025; Che et al., 2025), we argue that
anthropomorphism shapes how this congruence is evalu -
ated. Therefore, anthropomorphism functions as a structural
boundary condition that determines how virtual influenc -
ers are evaluated across contexts. The sections that follow
therefore specify the contextual conditions under which
anthropomorphic design strengthens or weakens source–
context fit, thereby shaping virtual influencer effectiveness.
Virtual influencer  Virtual influencers differ in the extent
to which their actions are perceived as autonomous. In
AI-driven influencer contexts, autonomy refers to whether
the influencer is seen as independently generating content,
interacting with audiences, and making promotional deci -
sions through AI capabilities rather than being directly con-
trolled by a human team (Sands et al., 2022). Prior research
suggests that autonomy matters particularly for artificial
endorsers: autonomy changes evaluations of AI influencers
but not human influencers (Sands et al., 2022), and highly
autonomous virtual influencers can elicit less favorable brand
attitudes and purchase intentions than managed virtual influ-
encers (Zheng et al., 2026). From a fit perspective (Kamins,
1990), the effectiveness of autonomy should depend on its
congruence with anthropomorphic design. Anthropomor -
phism increases the extent to which a virtual influencer is
interpreted as a human-like agent capable of intentional
action (Aggarwal & McGill, 2012; Epley et al., 2007).
When anthropomorphism is high, AI-enabled autonomy
aligns with expectations of human agency and self-directed
behavior. When anthropomorphism is low, autonomy attrib-
uted to a clearly artificial entity may appear less congruent,
weakening persuasive effect. Therefore, autonomy should

Brand Brand-related factors shape whether the anthro -
pomorphic design of a virtual influencer fits the endorsed
brand. We focus on three brand-level characteristics: brand
type, brand positioning, and brand communication style.
Brand type reflects whether a brand emphasizes functional
performance or experiential benefits (Park et al., 1986).
Functional brands highlight reliability and performance,
whereas experiential brands emphasize sensory and sym -
bolic consumption experiences (Brakus et al., 2009). Prior
research suggests that virtual influencers may be more
persuasive when their perceived characteristics align with
the brand’s dominant benefits (Liu et al., 2025; Pittman &
Kim, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Extending this logic, high-
anthropomorphic virtual influencers should fit functional
brands because their human-like design signals credibility
and expertise. In contrast, low-anthropomorphic virtual
influencers may fit experiential brands because their styl -
ized and artificial design supports symbolic and novelty-
based brand meanings. Thus, high-anthropomorphic virtual
influencers should be more effective for functional brands,
whereas low-anthropomorphic virtual influencers should be
more effective for experiential brands.
Brand positioning reflects the meanings and associations
a brand seeks to establish in consumers’ minds (Sujan &
Bettman, 1989). Futuristic positioning emphasizes innova -
tion and technological advancement, whereas traditional
positioning emphasizes continuity, familiarity, and heri -
tage (Park et al., 1986). Because highly anthropomorphic
virtual influencers showcase the technological sophisti -
cation of realistic digital humans, they should fit brands
positioned around innovation and modernity. In contrast,
low-anthropomorphic virtual influencers resemble stylized
spokescharacters, making them more congruent with brands
positioned around familiarity and tradition (Garretson &
Niedrich, 2004). Thus, high-anthropomorphic virtual influ -
encers should be more effective for futuristically positioned
brands, whereas low-anthropomorphic virtual influencers
should be more effective for traditionally positioned brands.
Brand communication style refers to whether a brand
communicates in an informative or entertaining manner.
Informative communication conveys brand values, prod -
uct attributes, and functional benefits, whereas entertaining
communication relies on humor, storytelling, and amuse -
ment (Kapoor et al., 2025; Ren et al., 2023). Human-like
virtual influencers are often perceived as more trustwor -
thy, whereas cartoon-like virtual influencers tend to evoke
stronger novelty responses (Franke & Groeppel-Klein,
2024). Therefore, high-anthropomorphic virtual influencers
should fit informative brand communication because their
trustworthiness supports information-based persuasion.
Low-anthropomorphic virtual influencers should fit enter -
taining communication because their novelty supports
affective engagement. Thus, high-anthropomorphic virtual
influencers should be more effective with informative brand
communication, whereas low-anthropomorphic virtual
influencers should be more effective with entertaining brand
communication.
Product Product-related factors shape whether the anthro -
pomorphic design of a virtual influencer fits the endorsed
offering. We focus on three product-level characteristics:
product familiarity, self-improvement relevance, and prod -
uct involvement.
Product or brand familiarity reflects the extent to which
consumers possess prior knowledge and experience with an
offering (Alba & Hutchinson, 1987). As familiarity increases,
consumers rely more on existing knowledge structures,
whereas low familiarity increases reliance on cues present
in the advertisement (Alba & Hutchinson, 1987; Campbell
& Keller, 2003). Prior research suggests that endorsement
effectiveness depends on the fit between influencer type and
product familiarity or newness. Zhu et al. ( 2022) show that
social media influencers generate stronger purchase inten -
tions for familiar brands, whereas Yao et al. ( 2025) show
that virtual influencers are more effective for really new
products because they promote abstract processing and
reduce perceived risk. Extending this logic, low-anthropo -
morphic virtual influencers should fit new or low-familiarity
products because their stylized and artificial design supports
abstract processing. In contrast, high-anthropomorphic vir -
tual influencers should fit familiar products because their
human-like realism supports more concrete and credible
interpretations. Thus, low-anthropomorphic virtual influ -
encers should be more effective for new or low-familiarity
products, whereas high-anthropomorphic virtual influencers
should be more effective for familiar products.
Self-improvement products help consumers improve a
task, capability, or aspect of the self (Allard & White, 2015;
Grewal et al., 2022). These products are future-oriented
because they promise movement from the current self to a
more capable future self. Meng et al. ( 2024) show that vir-
tual influencers are more effective than human influencers
for self-improvement products because they evoke a realis-
tic threat related to future capabilities and competitiveness.
From a fit perspective (Kamins, 1990), this effect should be
stronger when the influencer’s design reinforces meanings
of advancement and future readiness. Highly anthropomor-
phic virtual influencers make technological sophistication
and human-like realism more salient, aligning with products

positioned around improvement and future capability. In
contrast, low-anthropomorphic virtual influencers appear
more stylized and less technologically advanced, weaken -
ing this fit. Thus, high-anthropomorphic virtual influencers
should be more effective for self-improvement products
than low-anthropomorphic virtual influencers.
Product involvement refers to the personal relevance and
importance consumers attach to a product category (Richins
& Bloch, 1986). High-involvement products require greater
cognitive processing and are often associated with higher
perceived risk, whereas low-involvement products are pro -
cessed more peripherally and evaluated through affective or
symbolic cues (Warrington & Shim, 2000). Prior research
suggests that high-involvement products benefit from cred -
ible and competence-based signals, while low-involvement
products are more influenced by novelty and playfulness
(Park et al., 2023). Extending this logic, high-anthropomor-
phic virtual influencers should fit high-involvement prod -
ucts because their human-like design signals credibility,
realism, and competence. In contrast, low-anthropomorphic
virtual influencers should fit low-involvement products
because their stylized design enhances novelty and sym -
bolic appeal. Thus, high-anthropomorphic virtual influenc -
ers should be more effective for high-involvement products,
whereas low-anthropomorphic virtual influencers should be
more effective for low-involvement products.
Message Message-related factors shape whether the
anthropomorphic design of a virtual influencer fits the way
the endorsement is communicated. We focus on three mes -
sage-level characteristics: message appeal, point of view,
and sensory cues.
Message appeal refers to whether persuasive content
emphasizes emotional responses or functional informa -
tion. Emotional appeals highlight feelings and affective
reactions, whereas rational appeals emphasize product
attributes and functional benefits (Zhang et al., 2023; Zhu
et al., 2022). Prior research shows that virtual influencers
are often evaluated less favorably than human influencers
when emotional language is used, whereas this disadvan -
tage is reduced when rational language is used (Ozdemir
et al. 2023a). However, emotional appeals may also ben -
efit artificial agents when emotional expression signals
experiential capacity and increases perceived mind (Mo &
Zhou, 2024). Extending this logic, high-anthropomorphic
virtual influencers should fit rational appeals because their
human-like design supports credibility and information-
based persuasion. In contrast, low-anthropomorphic virtual
influencers should fit emotional appeals because emotional
expression may compensate for their artificiality by signal -
ing experiential capacity and increasing engagement. Thus,
high-anthropomorphic virtual influencers should be more
effective with rational messages, whereas low-anthropo -
morphic virtual influencers should be more effective with
emotional messages.
Point of view refers to whether a message is delivered
using a first-person voice (e.g., “I,” “my”) or a third-person
voice (e.g., “he,” “she”). First-person narration increases
psychological immersion and perceived authenticity
because the message appears to originate from the commu -
nicator’s own experience (Chung et al., 2023; Kim, 2022).
From a fit perspective (Kamins, 1990), this style should
align more strongly with highly anthropomorphic virtual
influencers because their human-like design makes first-
person self-expression appear more natural and credible.
In contrast, when anthropomorphism is low and the influ -
encer appears more clearly artificial, third-person narration
may be more congruent because it presents the entity as an
observed character rather than a human-like narrator. Thus,
high-anthropomorphic virtual influencers should be more
effective with first-person framing, whereas low-anthropo -
morphic virtual influencers should be more effective with
third-person framing.
Sensory cues refer to whether endorsements emphasize
proximal senses, such as touch, taste, and smell, or distal
senses, such as sight and hearing (Krishna & Schwarz, 2014;
Zhou et al., 2023). Consumers perceive virtual influencers
as having sensory limitations, particularly for proximal sen-
sory experiences, because they question whether artificial
agents can genuinely touch, taste, or smell products (Zhou
et al., 2023). However, greater form realism can increase
perceived proximal sensory capability and improve brand
attitudes when bodily experience is relevant (Ozdemir et
al., 2025). From a fit perspective, high-anthropomorphic
virtual influencers should be more effective when messages
emphasize proximal sensory cues because their human-like
design signals stronger experiential capability. In contrast,
when messages emphasize distal sensory cues, sensory lim-
itations are less salient, and the advantage of high anthropo-
morphism should be weaker. Thus, high-anthropomorphic
virtual influencers should be more effective with proximal
sensory cues, whereas differences between high- and low-
anthropomorphic virtual influencers should attenuate under
distal sensory framing.
Mediating effects
To explain how virtual influencers shape consumer
responses, we propose a dual-pathway framework in which
persuasion operates through two mechanisms: credibility
and novelty. Credibility has long been recognized as a cen -
tral driver of endorsement effectiveness, reflecting percep -
tions of a communicator’s trustworthiness and expertise

1 3
1218
Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science (2026) 54:1209–1233
(Choi & Rifon, 2007; Friedman & Friedman, 1979). In
endorsement contexts, consumers evaluate advertising mes-
sages partly by assessing these source attributes.
Novelty captures the degree to which a stimulus is per -
ceived as distinctive or unusual relative to what consumers
typically encounter (Moldovan et al., 2011; Pape & Topo -
rowski, 2023). Virtual influencers represent a relatively
new form of endorser, and their artificial nature can create
perceptions of distinctiveness compared with conventional
human influencers. Empirical evidence supports this inter -
pretation. Consumers perceive advertising featuring virtual
influencers as more novel than advertising featuring human
endorsers (Franke et al., 2023). Research comparing dif -
ferent types of virtual influencers also shows that stylized,
anime-like designs are perceived as more novel than highly
human-like designs (Kim et al., 2024).
We argue that anthropomorphism shapes which of these
evaluative mechanisms becomes more influential. As
defined earlier, anthropomorphism is the extent to which
a nonhuman entity resembles a human in appearance or
behavior (Epley et al., 2007). When a virtual influencer
is highly anthropomorphic, consumers may evaluate the
source in ways similar to human endorsers. Consistent
with this reasoning, human-like virtual influencers generate
stronger perceptions of trustworthiness than less human-like
designs (Kim et al., 2024). Under these conditions, persua -
sion is more likely to operate through credibility. In con -
trast, low-anthropomorphic virtual influencers emphasize
their artificiality. Less human-like designs generate stronger
perceptions of novelty than human-like virtual influencers,
and virtual influencers more broadly increase perceived
advertising novelty relative to human endorsers (Franke
et al., 2023). In these cases, persuasion may operate more
strongly through novelty.
This reasoning suggests that anthropomorphism does
not simply increase or decrease the effectiveness of virtual
influencers. Instead, it shapes the mechanism through which
influence occurs by shifting the relative importance of cred-
ibility-based and novelty-based evaluations.
H1 The indirect effect of virtual influencers on consumer
responses through credibility is stronger for high-
anthropomorphism influencers, whereas the indirect
effect through novelty is stronger for low-anthropomor-
phism influencers.
In addition, the relative importance of novelty and credibil-
ity may vary over time as exposure to virtual influencers
increases. Research in psychology shows that novel stimuli
attract attention and stimulate interest because they devi -
ate from existing expectations (Berlyne, 1970). However,
the influence of novelty declines with repeated exposure as
individuals habituate to stimuli that were initially perceived
as distinctive (Redden, 2008). Therefore, as virtual influenc-
ers become more prevalent in social media and advertising
environments, the distinctiveness of this endorser type may
become less salient, reducing the explanatory role of nov -
elty in shaping consumer responses. Recent research on vir-
tual influencers similarly highlights that their effectiveness
partly stems from their perceived distinctiveness relative to
human influencers, suggesting that novelty is a key mecha -
nism underlying early consumer responses to this emerg -
ing endorser form (Feng et al., 2024). Meanwhile, research
on endorsement effectiveness indicates that evaluations of
persuasive sources are strongly influenced by credibility-
related attributes such as expertise and trustworthiness
(Friedman & Friedman, 1979; Schimmelpfennig & Hunt,
2020). These credibility dimensions have long been identi -
fied as core drivers of source effectiveness in endorsement
contexts. As the novelty of virtual influencers diminishes
with increased exposure, consumers may rely more heavily
on these credibility-related attributes when forming judg -
ments about endorsed messages.
Therefore, applied to virtual influencers, increasing mar-
ketplace exposure may produce a relative shift in the mech-
anisms underlying persuasion. Novelty may account for a
larger share of persuasive influence in earlier observations,
whereas credibility may account for a larger share in more
recent observations:
H2 The strength of novelty-mediated effects decreases over
time, whereas credibility-mediated effects increase over
time.
Method
Data collection
We identified all published and unpublished papers related to
virtual influencer effects by searching major databases, includ-
ing Business Source Complete, Scopus, SpringerLink, Emer-
ald, Sage, ProQuest Digital Dissertations, SSRN, and Google
Scholar. Our search terms included “virtual influencer,”
“digital influencer,” “AI influencer,” “artificial influencer,”
and “unreal influencer,” and were searched across the title,
abstract, and keywords (see the PRISMA flow diagram in Web
Appendix A). Additionally, we conducted a snowball search
by reviewing the reference lists of identified studies, particu-
larly those with high Google Scholar citations and those pub-
lished in the Financial Times Top 50 journals. The search was
completed by the end of 2025, yielding 1,289 papers.
We included all studies that experimentally compared
a virtual influencer endorsement condition with a control

1 3
1219
Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science (2026) 54:1209–1233
group to allow causal inference. The results indicate that
almost all studies compared virtual influencers with human
influencers, except for one study that compared a virtual
influencer with advertising as a control group. To enhance
data consistency and therefore the analysis and results, we
excluded this study from our database. Also, studies that
examined virtual influencer characteristics (e.g., credibility)
rather than their direct effects on consumer responses were
excluded. Studies that solely compared different types of
virtual influencers (e.g., human-like vs. cartoon-like) were
also excluded because they assess the differences between
subtypes rather than the overall effectiveness of virtual
influencers. Papers were included only if they provided suf-
ficient data to compute an effect size. After applying these
criteria, we identified 110 papers, comprising 210 indepen -
dent studies including 643 effect sizes with a total sample
size of 91,058 consumers. The list of these papers is pro -
vided in Web Appendix B.
Data coding
To ensure coding accuracy and consistency, a detailed cod-
ing manual was developed and implemented. First, con -
sumer response variables were coded using the definitions
and examples provided in Table 4.
Next, moderator variables were coded according to a
separate manual (see summary in Table 5). To enhance reli-
ability, two coders independently coded all moderator vari-
ables, one author and an external coder who was blind to the
research objectives and not involved in the study. The inter-
coder agreement rate across all moderators was 91%; any
discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Detailed
agreement rates for each moderator are reported in Table 5.
To account for potential methodological and publication-
related variations, two control variables were included in the
analysis. Publication status distinguishes between studies
published in peer-reviewed journals and those not published
in peer-reviewed journals. This variable helps control pos -
sible publication bias or differences in study rigor. Journal
quality captures the quality of publication outlets by distin -
guishing high-ranked journals from lower-ranked or unlisted
journals. These controls ensure that observed effects are not
driven by differences in study quality or publication type but
rather by substantive factors relevant to virtual influencer
effectiveness. Additionally, publication period was included
as a moderator in the MASEM analyses to capture temporal
shifts in the underlying persuasion mechanisms.
Effect size preparation
All effect sizes were transformed into correlations for ease
of interpretation. Reliability and variance weighted cor -
relations were used to integrate effect sizes and to form a
correlation matrix (Table 3). Correlations were adjusted
for measurement errors in the dependent variables to
enhance accuracy. Because most studies reported effect
sizes for multiple consumer response variables, we used
hierarchical linear modeling and the metafor package in
R to aggregate effect sizes. We specified the following
model:
rij = γ00 + µ0j + eij (1)
where r ij represents the i th effect size from the j th study,
γ00 is the average effect size across all studies, µ 0j is the
deviation of the average effect size in the jth study from γ00,
capturing between-study variability, and eij is the deviation
of the i th effect size within the j th study from γ 00, captur-
ing within-study variability. Both µ 0j and e ij are assumed
to follow a normal distribution and are uncorrelated. To
evaluate the homogeneity of effect sizes, we employed the
Q-statistic, which tests whether the observed heterogeneity
exceeds what would be expected by chance alone (Higgins
& Thompson, 2002).

Moderator analysis
Our moderator model included all effect sizes across all
dependent variables, except for novelty, which showed an
effect that differed from those of the other variables (Fre -
quency distribution of the effect sizes is included in Web
Appendix E). Given the hierarchical nature of the data—
multiple effect sizes were nested within studies, and stud -
ies were nested within papers—we employed a three-level
hierarchical linear model to examine the role of moderators.
At Level 1, individual effect sizes were modeled, capturing
within-study variation. Level 2 accounted for the clustering
of effect sizes within studies. Level 3 modeled variability
across papers (see Web Appendix F).
To evaluate the robustness of our moderator analysis,
we first examined correlations among moderators (see Web
Appendix G), which indicated high correlations between
some variables. Then, we used variance inflation factors to
identify multicollinearity in our model. The results showed
that the maximum variance inflation factor value was below
three, suggesting that multicollinearity was not a significant
concern (see Web Appendix G).
Meta-analytic structural equation model
The correlation matrices (see Table 6) served as input for
meta-analytic structural equation modeling (MASEM) in R.
Correlations among variables were extracted directly from
primary studies, either from reported correlation matrices
or computed using available means, standard deviations,
and sample sizes, to form pooled meta-analytic correlation
matrices. Within the model, behavioral outcomes represent
a composite construct combining behavioral intention and
engagement, whereas attitude captures both ad attitude and
brand attitude. To examine the moderating roles of anthro -
pomorphism and time within our conceptual model, we
conducted multi-group structural equation model (SEM)
analyses and Wald tests. Time was coded as a binary vari -
able, with studies published in 2022 or earlier classified as
the “earlier period” and those published after 2022 classi -
fied as the “recent period.” Harmonic mean sample sizes
were applied, yielding 612 for high-anthropomorphic vir -
tual influencers, 605 for low-anthropomorphic virtual influ-
encers, 640 for recent studies, and 622 for earlier studies.
Model fit was assessed using multiple goodness-of-fit indi -
ces: standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), com -
parative fit index (CFI), and goodness-of-fit index (GFI).

Meta-analytic structural equation model
As indicated in the previous sections, our findings reveal
a difference in effectiveness between high- and low-
anthropomorphic virtual influencers. To explore the
mechanisms underlying these effects, we developed and
tested the model presented in Fig. 1. We propose that
two distinct processes, credibility and novelty, explain
the persuasive effect of virtual influencers. Importantly,
the relative influence of these processes varies with the
level of virtual influencer anthropomorphism and changes
over time (recent vs. earlier studies), consistent with H1
and H2.
To test this conceptual model, we conducted a MASEM
analysis, estimating the structural paths separately for high-
and low-anthropomorphic virtual influencer conditions. The
results (see Table 9) yield several key insights. For both
levels of anthropomorphism, we find significant sequential
paths from virtual influencers to credibility and novelty,
from credibility and novelty to attitude, and from attitude
to behavioral outcomes. Importantly, the model fit indices
demonstrate a strong fit for both models (SRMR < 0.09;
CFI > 0.90; GFI > 0.90), providing support for the proposed
mechanism across conditions.

Discussion
Theoretical implications
This meta-analysis advances endorsement and influencer
marketing theory by resolving key inconsistencies and
extending existing frameworks in several important ways.
First, this study advances anthropomorphism theory by
demonstrating that anthropomorphic design operates as a
structural boundary condition that shifts how virtual influ -
encers are evaluated. Anthropomorphism, defined as the
attribution of human-like traits to nonhuman entities (Epley
et al., 2007), has typically been treated as a direct predic -
tor in prior research, leading to inconsistent findings (Ma
& Li, 2024; Wang & Qiu, 2023; Xie-Carson et al., 2023).
Moving beyond this “more human-like is better” view, our
findings show that anthropomorphism matters because it
changes the evaluative pathway through which influence
occurs. The MASEM results indicate that credibility-based
effects are stronger for highly anthropomorphic virtual
influencers, whereas novelty-based effects are stronger for
low-anthropomorphic ones. This dual-pathway perspective
integrates traditional credibility-based persuasion (Choi &
Rifon, 2007; Friedman & Friedman, 1979; Schimmelpfen-
nig & Hunt, 2020) with emerging evidence on novelty as a
key driver in virtual influencer contexts (Franke et al., 2023;
Kim et al., 2024). The key implication is that anthropomor-
phism does not simply increase or decrease effectiveness;
rather, it determines whether consumers rely on credibility-
or novelty-based evaluations, offering a mechanism-based
explanation for previously conflicting findings.
Second, the findings develop a more theory-consistent
account of moderators by reframing them as contextual
conditions that determine when anthropomorphic design
translates into effectiveness through perceived congruence.
Endorsement fit and match-up perspectives suggest that
effectiveness depends on alignment between the source and
the endorsed brand, product, or message (Kamins, 1990;
Kamins & Gupta, 1994), and influencer research similarly
emphasizes the configuration of source and message cues
(Barari et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2024). However, in virtual
influencer research, many contextual features are embed -
ded within experimental stimuli and examined in isolation,
limiting theoretical clarity (Zhou et al., 2023). Our meta-
regression identifies which of these congruence conditions
systematically interact with anthropomorphic design, trans-
lating fragmented evidence into a clearer and more coherent
theoretical framework.
At the virtual influencer level, anthropomorphism inter -
acts with autonomy. Prior work shows that perceptions of
control and agency differ for AI versus human influencers
(Sands et al., 2022), and that high autonomy can reduce
effectiveness (Zheng et al., 2023). Our results extend this
by showing that autonomy cues are not uniformly benefi -
cial: they enhance effectiveness for highly anthropomorphic
virtual influencers but undermine it for low-anthropomor -
phic ones, suggesting that agency is evaluated differently
depending on human likeness. At the brand level, anthro -
pomorphism interacts with positioning, such that anthropo-
morphic realism aligns more strongly with futuristic brands,
whereas stylized designs fit better with traditional position-
ing, consistent with character-based endorsement logic
(Garretson & Niedrich, 2004). At the product and execution
level, anthropomorphism interacts with newness and sen -
sory framing. High anthropomorphism aligns more strongly
with new products and proximal sensory cues—contexts
where perceived experiential capability matters—whereas
low anthropomorphism aligns better with familiar products
and distal framing.
Importantly, not all interactions are significant. The pat -
tern of results suggests that anthropomorphism conditions
effectiveness primarily when contextual cues shape percep-
tions of agency, realism, or experiential capability (e.g.,
autonomy, positioning, product newness, sensory cues).
In contrast, it does not systematically interact with broader
characteristics such as product type, message tone, involve-
ment, or narrative perspective. This distinction indicates
that anthropomorphic realism functions as a contingent cue
that becomes influential when consumers evaluate whether
a virtual influencer can act, experience, or plausibly exist
within the context, but is less relevant for general communi-
cation features. Identifying these boundaries helps organize
fragmented findings and clarifies when anthropomorphic
design meaningfully shapes virtual influencer effectiveness.
Finally, this study contributes by showing that the mech-
anisms underlying virtual influencer effectiveness evolve
over time. Novel stimuli initially attract attention and drive
favorable responses (Berlyne, 1970), but these effects
weaken with repeated exposure (Redden, 2008). Consistent
with this, our results show that novelty-based effects are
stronger in earlier studies, whereas credibility-based effects
are more prominent in recent work. This suggests that as
virtual influencers become more familiar, novelty becomes
less central and credibility plays a greater role in shaping
responses (Friedman & Friedman, 1979; Schimmelpfennig

mechanisms through which nonhuman endorsers influence
consumers are dynamic rather than fixed, shifting from
novelty- to credibility-based evaluations as the category
matures.
Practitioner insights
To complement the meta-analytic findings, we conducted
a survey of marketing and communication professionals
(n = 204) to provide practitioner perspectives on the use of
virtual influencers (see Web Appendices K and L for the
questionnaire and descriptive statistics). While the meta-
analysis identifies the mechanisms and boundary conditions
underlying virtual influencer effectiveness, the survey offers
an additional practitioner-oriented perspective on how mar-
keting professionals interpret these findings and their strate-
gic relevance. These results provide a complementary layer
of evidence beyond the theory-testing component of this
study.
Practitioner perceptions of virtual influencer effective -
ness Consistent with the meta-analytic results, marketing
professionals perceive novelty as the dimension on which
virtual influencers outperform human influencers most
strongly. The results indicate that novelty received the high-
est evaluation among the measured response dimensions,
followed by attractiveness and engagement; credibility and
behavioral intention received the lowest evaluations. Pair -
wise comparisons further indicate that the largest perceived
differences occur between novelty and persuasion-oriented
outcomes such as credibility, brand attitudes, and behavioral
intentions. Although the survey was not designed to test the
meta-analytic relationships directly, this pattern provides
practitioner-based confirmation that managers perceive vir-
tual influencers primarily as novelty-generating communi -
cation tools rather than as inherently credible endorsers.
Practitioner evaluation of the meta-analytic findings  The
second part of the survey asked marketing professionals
to evaluate the usefulness and novelty of the meta-analytic
insights. Respondents rated the findings regarding anthropo-
morphism and campaign context as particularly valuable for
informing influencer marketing strategy. Specifically, inter-
action effects related to message execution, representing the
highest level of managerial control, received the highest
ratings for offering new perspectives and challenging exist-
ing assumptions. In contrast, the comparative effectiveness
of virtual versus human influencers and the relationship
between anthropomorphism and autonomy, representing
relatively lower levels of managerial control because they
are tied to virtual influencer design, were rated as especially
useful for guiding strategic decisions. These results sug -
gest that practitioners recognize the strategic importance of
distinguishing among different forms of virtual influencer
design and tailoring their use to specific campaign contexts,
while also recognizing that different moderator domains
provide managers with different levels of control over cam-
paign effectiveness.
Managerial implications
This meta-analysis also offers several implications for man-
agers considering the use of virtual influencers in market -
ing communications. The central managerial implication
emerging from our findings is that virtual influencers should
not be treated as a uniform category of endorsers. Instead,
their effectiveness depends on how their anthropomorphic
design aligns with the campaign’s objectives, the brand and
product’s characteristics, and the execution of the marketing
message. Importantly, these elements differ in the degree of
managerial control they offer, ranging from relatively fixed
virtual influencer characteristics to increasingly controllable
brand, product, and message decisions. Influencer marketing
research consistently shows that the effectiveness of endors-
ers depends on the congruence between the influencer and
the broader marketing context (Barari et al., 2025; Kamins,
1990; Pan et al., 2024). Our findings extend this logic to
virtual influencers, demonstrating that the level of anthro -
pomorphism shapes the mechanism by which these endors-
ers generate influence. Highly anthropomorphic virtual
influencers are evaluated more strongly through credibility-
related considerations, whereas low-anthropomorphic vir -
tual influencers generate stronger novelty-based responses.
Therefore, managers should begin by clarifying whether a
campaign goal is primarily to build trust and expertise (i.e.,
credibility) or to capture attention and stimulate interest
(i.e., novelty) and then select the level of anthropomorphism
accordingly. Importantly, anthropomorphic design appears
to matter most when campaign elements require consumers
to evaluate whether the influencer can plausibly act, experi-
ence, or authentically represent the product. In contrast, for
more general communication choices such as message tone
or narrative perspective, anthropomorphism plays a less
central role.
When the objective is to strengthen credibility or support
persuasion for new or unfamiliar offerings, highly anthro -
pomorphic virtual influencers are more appropriate. Their
human-like appearance allows audiences to evaluate them
using criteria like those applied to human endorsers, thereby
strengthening credibility perceptions and supporting down-
stream persuasion outcomes. Prior research emphasizes that
credibility and expertise are central drivers of endorsement

with autonomy, when aligned with futuristic brand position-

Although anthropomorphic design is largely determined
once a virtual influencer has been selected, managers can

emphasize rational arguments or proximal sensory cues,

In contrast, when a campaign’s primary objective is to
attract attention, lower anthropomorphic virtual influenc -

character-like designs strengthen novelty perceptions and in
turn, effectiveness outcomes. This aligns with prior research

responses to virtual influencers (Franke et al., 2023; Kim
et al., 2024; Sands et al., 2022). Our results indicate that
these influencers tend to perform better when paired with
more familiar products and when communication strategies
emphasize emotional content. Because message execution
represents the highest level of managerial control, manag -
ers can readily adapt message appeals and sensory framing
to maximize the effectiveness of different virtual influencer
designs without changing the influencer itself. Message
executions that rely on distal sensory cues, such as visual or
auditory stimulation, may also align well with these designs
because such cues do not require the influencer to be per -
ceived as physically capable of experiencing the product
(Zhou et al., 2023). Meanwhile, the absence of moderation
for several broader communication characteristics suggests
that managers retain flexibility in many executional deci -
sions without needing to optimize anthropomorphic realism
in every aspect of campaign design.
Importantly, these findings suggest that firms should
think about virtual influencer selection as a strategic design
decision that shapes the psychological basis of consumer
evaluation. Rather than asking whether to use a virtual
influencer or a human influencer, managers should consider
how the influencer’s anthropomorphic design interacts with
other elements of the marketing program to create congruent
signals about the brand and product. Accordingly, manag -
ers should view virtual influencer campaigns as a hierarchy
of decision levers, where virtual influencer characteristics
represent relatively fixed strategic decisions, while brand,
product, and especially message characteristics provide
progressively greater opportunities to optimize campaign
effectiveness. Therefore, aligning anthropomorphic design
with campaign objectives, product characteristics, and mes-
sage strategy can help firms realize the distinctive advan -
tages virtual influencers offer while mitigating potential
credibility limitations.

Future research directions
The areas outlined in Table 11 reflect the future research
priorities identified by industry experts after they reviewed
the key findings and managerial implications of this study,
indicating where additional evidence is needed to better
inform and refine social media marketing strategies involv-

highlight several important directions for future research,
identifying areas where practitioners seek a deeper under -

These priorities extend beyond short-term performance out-

dimensions that influence the effectiveness and acceptance
of virtual influencers.
Future research on virtual influencers can be broadly
organized around performance, audience heterogeneity,
and effectiveness across contexts. A key priority is to estab-
lish robust performance metrics that link virtual influencer
activity to financial outcomes (e.g., sales, conversion, cus -
tomer lifetime value) and long-term brand equity, while
determining how these outcomes vary across different lev -
els of anthropomorphism. At the same time, responses are
likely to differ across demographic and cultural segments,
suggesting the need to examine generational, cross-cultural,
and digital literacy differences in acceptance, parasocial
relationships, and engagement. Research should also inves-
tigate how trust, authenticity, and credibility develop over
time, particularly how signals of AI autonomy or human
oversight shape these perceptions, and how different social
media platforms and stages of the marketing funnel influ -
ence credibility- versus novelty-based responses.

agency, identity design, and human, virtual collaboration in
shaping consumer responses. This includes examining how
