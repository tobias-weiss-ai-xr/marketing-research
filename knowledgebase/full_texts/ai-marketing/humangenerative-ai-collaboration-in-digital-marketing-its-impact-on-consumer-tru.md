The increasing integration of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) into digital
marketing is transforming how consumers interact with information, evaluate
alternatives, and make behavioural and financial decisions. However, the
effectiveness of GenA I may depend on its integration with human judgment,
contextual interpretation, and oversight. This study examines the influence of
Human–Generative AI Collaboration (HGAIC) on Consumer Trust (CT), Purchase
Intention (PI), and Financial Decision -Making (FD M). A quantitative cross -
sectional design was adopted, using data from 438 consumers with prior
experience of AI-enabled digital marketing interactions. The proposed model was
evaluated using partial least squares structural equation modelling (PLS -SEM) to
assess measurement quality, direct structural relationships, mediation effects, and
predictive relevance. The results indicate that HGAIC has a strong positive effect
on Consumer Trust and significant direct effects on Purchase Intention and
Financial Decision-Making. Consumer Trust positively influences both decision
outcomes and partially mediates the relationships between HGAIC and Purchase
Intention and between HGAIC and Financial Decision -Making. The model
demonstrates satisfactory explanatory and predictive capability, explaining 64.2%
of the variance in Purchase Intention and 55.3% in Financial Decision -Making.
The findings establish trust as a key behavioural mechanism through which
human–GenAI collaboration contributes to consumer decision outcomes . The
study extends collaborative intelligence research into consumer -facing digital
marketing and highlights the importance of combining GenAI -enabled
personalization and decision assistance with transparent human oversight,
validation, and accountability.

1. Introduction
1.1 Background and Research Context
Artificial intelligence (AI) has transformed digital marketing through automated customer analytics,
personalization, recommendation systems, conversational agents, and predictive decision support
(Davenport et al., 2020; Huang & Rust, 2021; De Bruyn et al ., 2020). The emergence of generative
artificial intelligence (GenAI) further extends these capabilities by generating personalized content,
interactive responses, product explanations, and context -specific recommendations in real time
(Dwivedi et al., 2023; Feuerriegel et al., 2024). Consequently, consumers increasingly encounter AI -
supported information while searching for products, evaluating alternatives, assessing financial
consequences, and making purchase decisions.
Despite its potential, autonomous AI interaction can create concerns regarding transparency,
authenticity, privacy, accuracy, and accountability. Consumer responses to AI depend not only on
technological performance but also on perceived competence, transp arency, task characteristics,
and trust (Castelo et al., 2019; Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Puntoni et al., 2021). Human –GenAI
collaboration therefore provides an alternative to complete automation by combining AI capabilities
in data processing, personalizati on, and content generation with human judgment, creativity,
empathy, and ethical oversight (Dellermann et al., 2019; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021; Seeber et al.,
2020). Such collaboration may be particularly relevant in digital marketing environments where
recommendations influence both purchase behaviour and financially consequential consumer
choices.
1.2 Human–Generative AI Collaboration in Digital Marketing
Human–GenAI collaboration represents the coordinated use of human expertise and generative
intelligence to perform tasks, generate recommendations, and support decisions. The underlying
principle is complementarity: AI provides analytical scalability, patt ern recognition, personalisation,
and rapid content generation, whereas humans contribute contextual interpretation, emotional
intelligence, creativity, and accountability (Jarrahi, 2018; Dellermann et al., 2019; Raisch & Krakowski,
2021). In marketing, th is collaboration can support content creation, conversational engagement,
personalised recommendations, customer service, and consumer decision assistance.
The effectiveness of such systems depends on how consumers perceive AI involvement and human
oversight. Although algorithmic recommendations can improve consistency and decision efficiency,
consumers may resist AI when tasks require subjective judgment or when algorithmic processes are
perceived as opaque (Dietvorst et al., 2015; Castelo et al., 2019; Puntoni et al., 2021). Conversely,
effective human oversight and transparent AI assistance may strengthen credibility and acceptance.
Thus, human–GenAI collaboration can potentially combine the efficiency of intelligent automation
with the trust and contextual sensitivity associated with human participation.

collaboration in digital marketing. Specifically, it seeks to:

2. Related Work
2.1 Generative AI in Digital Marketing
Artificial intelligence has progressively transformed marketing through customer analytics,
recommendation systems, predictive modelling, conversational agents, and personalized
communication (Davenport et al., 2020; De Bruyn et al., 2020; Huang & Rust, 20 21). Generative AI
extends these capabilities by producing context -sensitive text, images, recommendations,
explanations, and conversational responses from learned data patterns (Dwivedi et al., 2023;
Feuerriegel et al., 2024). In digital marketing, these capabilities support content generation,
campaign personalization, customer interaction, product discovery, and real -time decision
assistance.
Unlike conventional predictive AI, GenAI enables interactive and adaptive exchanges in which
consumers can refine queries, compare alternatives, and obtain personalized explanations. Recent
research indicates that GenAI is reshaping consumer decision proce sses through personalization,
information synthesis, and interactive assistance (Panda et al., 2025). However, GenAI effectiveness
remains dependent on output accuracy, contextual relevance, disclosure, privacy protection, and
appropriate human control. Co nsequently, the emerging marketing challenge concerns not merely

AI adoption but the effective integration of generative capabilities into trustworthy consumer

2.2 Human–AI Collaboration and Augmented Intelligence
Human–AI collaboration is based on the complementary allocation of cognitive capabilities between
human actors and intelligent systems. Humans contribute contextual reasoning, creativity, empathy,
ethical judgment, and accountability, whereas AI contribute s computational capacity, pattern
recognition, consistency, and scalability (Jarrahi, 2018; Dellermann et al., 2019; Raisch & Krakowski,
2021). This complementarity forms the basis of augmented intelligence, in which AI supports rather
than completely substitutes human decision-making.
In marketing applications, collaborative intelligence can combine AI -generated content,
recommendations, and predictive insights with human validation and contextual interpretation. Such
configurations are important because AI performance varies across tas ks and consumers may
respond negatively when algorithms are used for subjective or identity-relevant decisions (Castelo et
al., 2019; Puntoni et al., 2021). Human involvement can therefore provide verification and
accountability, while GenAI improves proce ssing speed and personalization. The effectiveness of
collaboration consequently depends on appropriate task allocation, transparency, and calibrated
reliance rather than maximum automation (Seeber et al., 2020; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021).
2.3 Consumer Trust in AI-Mediated Marketing
Trust is a critical mechanism determining whether consumers accept, rely upon, and act on AI -
generated information. Human trust in AI is influenced by perceived competence, reliability,
transparency, predictability, and the characteristics of the interacti on context (Glikson & Woolley,
2020). In digital marketing, trust becomes particularly important because AI systems use consumer
data to personalize messages, generate recommendations, and influence product evaluations.
Algorithmic errors, opacity, privacy concerns, and uncertainty regarding responsibility can reduce
trust and produce algorithm aversion (Dietvorst et al., 2015; Castelo et al., 2019). Conversely,
relevant personalisation, perceived usefulness, transparency , and credible interaction can
strengthen consumer confidence. Recent empirical evidence on AI-powered personalized advertising
also identifies perceived trust as an important mechanism connecting personalization with purchase
intention. Human oversight ma y therefore function as an assurance mechanism that reduces
perceived uncertainty while preserving the efficiency benefits of GenAI.
2.4 GenAI and Purchase Intention
Purchase intention represents a consumer's willingness or likelihood to purchase a product or service
following evaluation of available information and alternatives. AI -enabled marketing can influence
this process by improving information relevance, reduci ng search costs, personalizing
recommendations, and supporting product comparison (Davenport et al., 2020; Huang & Rust, 2021).
Recommendation quality and conversational responsiveness can simplify the consumer journey and
strengthen behavioural intention when the system is perceived as useful and credible.

However, personalization alone may not directly generate favourable purchase responses. Consumer
acceptance depends on whether AI -generated recommendations are perceived as relevant,
authentic, and trustworthy (Puntoni et al., 2021). Evidence from AI-personalized advertising suggests
that perceived relevance, usefulness, and trust transmit personalization effects to purchase
intention. Therefore, in collaborative marketing environments, purchase intention is likely to reflect
both the functional value of Ge nAI assistance and the confidence created through appropriate
human involvement.
2.5 GenAI and Financial Decision-Making
Consumer financial decision -making involves evaluating monetary consequences, comparing
alternatives, assessing affordability, and allocating limited financial resources. Digital decision
environments increasingly use AI for information aggregation, compar ison, recommendation, and
personalized assistance. GenAI can further support these processes by summarizing complex
information, generating scenario -based explanations, clarifying product differences, and providing
interactive responses to consumer queries (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Feuerriegel et al., 2024).
Nevertheless, financial decisions involve greater perceived risk than routine consumption decisions.
Inaccurate recommendations, hallucinated information, automation bias, and excessive reliance can
adversely affect decision quality. Accordingly, human ver ification and accountability become
important when GenAI contributes to financially consequential choices. A collaborative model can
potentially combine AI -based analytical support with human contextual judgment, enabling
consumers to obtain efficient info rmation assistance while retaining appropriate validation and
decision control.
2.6 Research Gap and Synthesis
The related literature establishes substantial progress in AI -enabled personalisation, algorithmic
recommendations, consumer trust, and human–AI collaboration. However, three limitations remain.
First, digital marketing studies predominantly examine AI too ls as independent technological
applications, while limited attention has been given to human–GenAI collaboration as an integrated
consumer-facing capability. Second, consumer trust is widely recognized as important for AI
acceptance, but its role as a tra nsmission mechanism between collaborative intelligence and
consumer outcomes requires further empirical examination. Third, prior research has concentrated
primarily on purchase intention and engagement, whereas the influence of human –GenAI
collaboration on financial decision-making remains comparatively underdeveloped.
Accordingly, this study integrates human –GenAI collaboration, consumer trust, purchase intention,
and financial decision -making within a unified model. The framework examines both direct
relationships and the mediating role of consumer trust, thereby connecting collaborative intelligence
with behavioural and financially relevant consumer outcomes.

3. Conceptual Framework and Hypothesis Development
3.1 Conceptual Foundation
The proposed framework is grounded in the principle of human–AI complementarity, which suggests
that superior decision support can emerge when human cognitive and contextual capabilities are
combined with the analytical and generative capabilities of AI (Jarrahi, 2018; Dellermann et al., 2019;
Raisch & Krakowski, 2021). In digital ma rketing, GenAI can provide rapid information processing,
personalized content, interactive recommendations, and alternative evaluation, while human
involvement contributes contextual judgment, empathy, verification, and accountability.
The framework further considers consumer trust as the central behavioural mechanism connecting
collaborative intelligence with consumer decisions. Trust reduces uncertainty and increases
willingness to rely on technology -mediated information and recommendations (Glikson & Woolley,
2020; Puntoni et al. , 2021). Accordingly, the proposed model examines whether human –GenAI
collaboration directly influences purchase intention and financial decision-making and whether these
effects are transmitted through consumer trust.
3.2 Hypothesis Development
Human–GenAI collaboration combines technological efficiency with human judgment and oversight.
Appropriate human involvement can improve the perceived reliability and accountability of AI -
supported marketing interactions, while GenAI contributes personaliz ation, responsiveness, and
information-processing efficiency. Such complementarity is expected to strengthen consumer
confidence in AI -mediated interactions (Dellermann et al., 2019; Glikson & Woolley, 2020).
Therefore:
H1. Human–GenAI collaboration has a positive effect on consumer trust.
AI-supported marketing can reduce information -search costs, personalize recommendations, and
facilitate product evaluation. When combined with human validation and contextual interpretation,
such assistance may improve consumers' willingness to act on mark eting information and
recommendations (Davenport et al., 2020; Huang & Rust, 2021; Puntoni et al., 2021). Hence:
H2. Human–GenAI collaboration has a positive effect on purchase intention.
GenAI can also assist consumers in comparing prices, evaluating alternatives, understanding financial
consequences, and processing complex information. Human oversight may improve the contextual
appropriateness and reliability of such support, thereby cont ributing to more confident financial
decisions (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Feuerriegel et al., 2024). Thus:
H3. Human–GenAI collaboration has a positive effect on financial decision-making.
Trust increases consumer willingness to accept and act upon technology -mediated
recommendations. When AI-supported information is perceived as reliable and credible, consumers
are more likely to develop favourable behavioural intentions and use such inform ation in financially
relevant decisions (Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Puntoni et al., 2021). Accordingly:
H4. Consumer trust has a positive effect on purchase intention.

H5. Consumer trust has a positive effect on financial decision-making.

4. Research Methodology
4.1 Research Design
The study adopted a quantitative, cross -sectional, and explanatory research design to examine the
relationships among Human –Generative AI Collaboration (HGAIC), Consumer Trust (CT), Purchase
Intention (PI), and Financial Decision-Making (FDM). A deductive approach was employed to test the
hypothesized direct and mediating relationships specified in the conceptual framework. The unit of
analysis was the individual consumer with prior exposure to AI -assisted digital marketing
interactions, including AI -generated recommendations, conversational assistants, personalized
promotional content, product comparison tools, or AI-supported decision assistance.
4.2 Population and Sampling Design
The target population comprised adult consumers who had experience with AI -enabled digital
marketing services. Since a complete sampling frame of GenAI-exposed consumers was unavailable,
a purposive sampling approach combined with respondent screening was adopted. Respondents
were included only when they confirmed prior interaction with at least one AI -enabled marketing
application.
The minimum sample requirement was determined using the statistical power principle and
structural model complexity considerations (Cohen, 1992; Hair et al., 2022). A target of
approximately 450 responses was considered appropriate to provide sufficient statistical power and
compensate for incomplete or invalid questionnaires. After data screening, the final valid sample can
be reported based on the actual usable responses retained for analysis.
4.3 Measurement Instrument
Data were collected using a structured questionnaire comprising two sections. The first section
captured demographic characteristics and respondents' experience with AI-enabled digital marketing
applications. The second section measured the four study constructs using multi-item scales adapted
from established literature and modified to suit the GenAI-enabled marketing context.
All construct items were measured using a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = strongly disagree
to 5 = strongly agree. Human –GenAI Collaboration was operationalized through perceived
complementarity, quality of assistance, human oversight, and collaborative decision support (Jarrahi,
2018; Dellermann et al., 2019). Consumer Trust measurement reflected reliability, competence,
confidence, and credibility of AI -supported interactions (McKnight et al., 2002; Glikson & Woolley,
2020). Purchase Intention w as assessed through willingness, likelihood, and readiness to purchase
following AI-supported marketing interaction (Dodds et al., 1991; Spears & Singh, 2004). Financial
Decision-Making captured information evaluation, alternative comparison, decision confidence, and
perceived financial decision quality.

4.4 Data Collection and Screening
The questionnaire was administered electronically to consumers with prior exposure to AI -enabled
digital marketing interactions. A screening question was included at the beginning of the survey to
ensure respondent relevance. Participation was voluntary, a nd respondents were informed of the
academic purpose of the study and the confidentiality of their responses.
Before model estimation, the dataset was examined for missing values, incomplete responses,
response-pattern irregularities, and potential outliers. Descriptive statistics were subsequently used
to summarize the demographic profile and distributional chara cteristics of the study variables.
Common method bias was assessed through procedural controls and appropriate statistical
diagnostics because all constructs were measured through a single survey instrument (Podsakoff et
al., 2003).
4.5 Data Analysis Procedure
The proposed model was evaluated using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS -
SEM). This technique is suitable for prediction-oriented models involving simultaneous estimation of
direct and indirect relationships and does not impose strict multivariate normality assumptions (Hair
et al., 2019; Hair et al., 2022). The analysis was conducted in two stages. First, the measurement
model was evaluated using indicator loadings, Cronbach's alpha, composite reliability (CR), average
variance extracted (AVE), and the heterotrait–monotrait ratio (HTMT). Values of CR above 0.70 and
AVE above 0.50 were used to establish internal consistency and convergent validity, while HTMT
values were examined to assess discriminant validity (Henseler et al., 2015; Hair et al., 2022).
Second, the structural model was evaluated through path coefficients (β), bootstrapped t -values, p-
values, coefficient of determination (R²), effect size (f²), and predictive relevance (Q²). The mediating
role of Consumer Trust was tested using bootstrapped indirect effects and bias-corrected confidence
intervals. An indirect effect was considered statistically significant when its confidence interval did
not include zero (Preacher & Hayes, 2008; Hair et al., 2022).

6. Empirical Results and Analysis
6.1 Structural Relationship Analysis
The structural model results demonstrate that Human –GenAI Collaboration (HGAIC) significantly
influences Consumer Trust (CT), Purchase Intention (PI), and Financial Decision -Making (FDM). The
strongest structural relationship is observed between HGAIC and CT (β = 0.712, p < 0.001), indicating
that the integration of GenAI capabilities with human judgment and oversight substantially
strengthens consumer confidence in AI -supported marketing interactions. This finding is consistent
with the complementarity per spective, which suggests that combining computational capabilities
with human contextual judgment can improve the perceived reliability and effectiveness of
intelligent systems (Jarrahi, 2018; Dellermann et al., 2019; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021).
HGAIC also demonstrates significant direct effects on PI (β = 0.311, p < 0.001) and FDM (β = 0.298, p
< 0.001). These results indicate that collaborative systems can directly support consumer behaviour
by improving information relevance, reducing decision complexity, and facilitating evaluation of
alternatives. The comparatively stronger effect on purchase intention suggests that consumers may
more readily accept GenAI assistance for transactional decisions than for financially consequential
decisions involving greater perceived risk and uncertainty (Castelo et al., 2019; Puntoni et al., 2021).
6.2 Role of Consumer Trust
Consumer Trust has a significant positive influence on both PI (β = 0.510, p < 0.001) and FDM (β =
0.482, p < 0.001). The findings confirm that technological capability alone is insufficient to generate
favourable consumer responses; consumers must perceiv e collaborative AI -supported information
as reliable, credible, and dependable. This result supports prior evidence identifying trust as a central
determinant of reliance on AI systems and algorithm -supported recommendations (Glikson &
Woolley, 2020; Puntoni et al., 2021).
The stronger CT → PI relationship indicates that trust is particularly important in converting
collaborative marketing interactions into behavioural intention. However, the substantial CT → FDM
coefficient also confirms that trust influences consumers' wil lingness to use AI -supported
information when comparing alternatives and evaluating financial consequences. Therefore, trust
functions as a central behavioural bridge between collaborative intelligence and consumer decision
outcomes.

6.4 Explanatory and Predictive Performance
The proposed model demonstrates satisfactory explanatory and predictive capability. HGAIC explains

The positive Q² values for CT (0.371), PI (0.472), and FDM (0.389) further confirm the model's
predictive relevance. Purchase Intention records the highest R² and Q² values, suggesting that the
proposed collaborative intelligence framework has particularly  strong explanatory and predictive
capability for purchase-related behavioural outcomes. Financial Decision-Making also demonstrates
meaningful predictive performance, supporting the relevance of human–GenAI collaboration beyond
conventional promotional and transactional outcomes.
6.5 Overall Interpretation of Findings
Overall, the findings support the proposed Human –GenAI Collaboration and Consumer Decision
Model. Human –GenAI Collaboration emerges as a strong antecedent of trust and a significant
predictor of both purchase and financial decision outcomes. Consumer Trust  further strengthens
these relationships through significant direct and mediating effects. The results suggest that effective
digital marketing systems should not be designed exclusively around maximum automation. Instead,
GenAI capabilities in personaliza tion, content generation, and information processing should be
integrated with appropriate human judgment, validation, and accountability.
The findings also demonstrate that collaborative intelligence has implications beyond purchase
conversion. Its significant relationship with Financial Decision-Making indicates that consumers may
use trusted collaborative systems for information evaluation , alternative comparison, and
assessment of financial consequences. Thus, the proposed model extends the role of GenAI from
marketing communication toward trust-based intelligent consumer decision support.
7. Discussion and Implications
7.1 Discussion of Findings
The findings demonstrate that Human –Generative AI Collaboration improves consumer trust and
positively influences purchase intention and financial decision -making. The results support the
principle of human–AI complementarity, where GenAI contributes personalization, rapid information

processing, and decision assistance, while human involvement provides contextual judgment,
verification, and accountability (Jarrahi, 2018; Dellermann et al., 2019; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021).
Consumer trust emerged as an important mechanism connecting coll aborative intelligence with
decision outcomes, supporting earlier evidence that reliability and confidence are central to
consumer acceptance of AI -mediated interactions (Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Puntoni et al., 2021).
The findings therefore indicate that effective GenAI marketing depends not only on technological
capability but also on trustworthy integration with human oversight.
7.2 Theoretical Implications
The study extends AI-enabled marketing research by conceptualizing Human–GenAI Collaboration as
an integrated antecedent of consumer decision outcomes. Unlike studies that examine AI tools
independently, the proposed model combines collaborative intelligen ce, trust, purchase intention,
and financial decision -making within a single framework. The significant mediation effects further
establish Consumer Trust as a behavioural transmission mechanism through which human –GenAI
collaboration influences consumer d ecisions. The study therefore connects the human –AI
complementarity perspective with consumer trust and decision-making literature.
7.3 Managerial Implications
The findings suggest that organizations should design GenAI -enabled marketing systems around
collaboration rather than complete automation. Marketers should combine AI -generated
recommendations, personalized communication, and decision assistance with appr opriate human
validation, transparent disclosure, and escalation mechanisms for complex interactions. For
financially consequential decisions, stronger verification and accountability mechanisms are
particularly important. Organizations should therefore pr ioritize reliable AI outputs, explainable
recommendations, responsible personalization, and visible human oversight to strengthen consumer
trust and improve decision outcomes.
8. Conclusion and Future Research
This study examined the influence of Human –Generative AI Collaboration on Consumer Trust,
Purchase Intention, and Financial Decision -Making in digital marketing environments. The findings
demonstrate that collaborative integration of human judgment and Gen AI capabilities strengthen
consumer trust and contributes positively to behavioural and financial decision outcomes. Consumer
Trust also partially mediates the effects of Human –GenAI Collaboration on both Purchase Intention
and Financial Decision -Making, c onfirming its role as an important mechanism in AI -supported
consumer decisions. Overall, the study indicates that effective GenAI -enabled marketing should
emphasize human–AI complementarity, transparent interaction, and responsible human oversight
rather than complete automation.  The study is subject to several limitations. The cross -sectional
design restricts causal interpretation and does not capture changes in consumer trust over time. The
use of self -reported survey responses may also introduce common method and response biases .
Furthermore, the model examines Human –GenAI Collaboration as an integrated construct without
distinguishing between different GenAI applications, marketing contexts, or levels of human
involvement. These limitations should be considered when interpreting and generalizing the findings.
