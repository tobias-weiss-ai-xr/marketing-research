Abstract
The integration of advanced technologies has transformed marketing practices, making the roles of artificial intelli-
gence and neuromarketing increasingly relevant to customer experience management. The aim of this study is to sys-
tematically conceptualize the interaction between neuromarketing, artificial intelligence, and customer experience
management by developing a conceptual framework. Methodologically, the research employs a conceptual design
supported by illustrative secondary vignettes, drawing on existing customer experience management and artifi-
cial intelligence models from the literature and extending them with neuromarketing applications. The framework
suggests that neuromarketing data (e.g., EEG, eye-tracking, GSR) processed by artificial intelligence-driven analyt-
ics generate emotional, cognitive, and behavioral insights that enhance firms’ ability to monitor, adapt, and design
touchpoints across the customer journey. Illustrative vignettes drawn from publicly available secondary sources
on three firms (TUI, Zyro, and Liberty Group) operating in distinct sectors show how such integration is associated
with interface optimization, advanced recommendation systems, and platform enhancements, suggesting the poten-
tial for greater personalization, customer satisfaction, and firm efficiency. Beyond big data, neuromarketing offers
unique consumer-level insights that broaden the scope of customer experience management, enabling businesses
to transform B2C strategies through more precise segmentation and highly tailored interactions. However, the study
also underscores ethical dilemmas such as transparency, consumer manipulation, and data privacy, which require
careful management. Taken together, the proposed framework suggests that artificial intelligence–neuromarketing
integration may function not only as a supportive tool but also as a transformative driver in customer experience
management. By balancing technological innovation with ethical responsibility, firms can employ these technologies
to pursue sustainable, trust-based value creation for both customers and businesses.

Introduction
The advent of emerging technologies that autonomously
collect, process, and utilize data has simultaneously
simplified daily life and raised consumer expectations.

fast, seamless digital experiences and instant solutions
[17]. Two key components of these solutions are cus -
tomer experience management and artificial intelligence
applications.

understanding customer data at various touchpoints
throughout the purchasing process, identifying cues per -
ceived by customers, and sending appropriate signals to
create value for them [11, 15, 21, 23]. In this article, we

machines and transformed into vast bodies of informa -

leading scholars in CXM have proposed research ques -

these questions remain largely unanswered in the litera -
ture. The marketing literature often lags behind practical
developments, therefore, it is essential to bridge this gap
by examining the practical implications and validity of
such advancements [36]. We develop a conceptual frame-
work that specifies how AI and neuromarketing inputs
can be integrated into CXM workflows and illustrate the
framework with secondary-case vignettes. Through this
framework, we seek to both inspire practitioners with
innovative and proactive ideas and enrich the academic
literature that has thus far lagged behind practice.
Theoretical basis
Customer experience management refers to the manage -
ment of all experiences that customers have with a prod -
uct or business [28, 29]. These experiences encompass
the entire customer journey, covering the pre-purchase,
purchase, and post-purchase stages. CXM involves sys -
tematically identifying and prioritizing customer cues
obtained throughout the customer journey with analyti -
cal tools, designing and developing interactive processes
to create experiences, and measuring customer responses
using appropriate performance metrics [15]. While
CXM is similar to customer relationship management
(CRM) in its use of data analytics to understand cus -
tomer preferences, it differs in that CRM focuses primar -
ily on improving sales and operational processes, whereas
CXM emphasizes creating holistic and emotional experi -
ences across all touchpoints [11, 21, 33]. Therefore, while
CRM aims to create value for firms, CXM seeks to gener-
ate value for customers by delivering unique and positive
experiences [18, 21].
Leveraging contemporary technologies is crucial for
managing the customer experience, as it has become
almost impossible to do so effectively across the myriad
touchpoints that characterize today’s market environ -
ment. In omnichannel environments, it has become
imperative to integrate information technology, service
operations, logistics, human resources, and external part-
ners [21]. Artificial intelligence is being adapted to mar -
keting strategies, product management, pricing, place
management, and promotion management—in other
words, to every component of the marketing mix—and
gaining insights using large volumes of customer and
transactional data, including text, voice, image, and facial
expression data [8, 25, 34]. Decision-making, customer
segmentation, customer service, tracking touchpoints
along the customer journey, positioning, personalized
recommendations, content creation, pricing and place -
ment management, experiential marketing, branding,
sales forecasting, and market research are among the
areas where AI has become deeply embedded in the mar-
keting field [2, 17]. More recently, as the use of AI tools
has expanded, the concept of generative AI has emerged,
enabling artificial intelligence to produce marketing
content such as text, audio, and images similar to that
created by human marketing professionals, represent -
ing a groundbreaking development for businesses [16].

In marketing and CXM activities, different types of AI
are inevitably utilized in strategic stages such as research,
strategy development, and implementation. Market -
ers can benefit from mechanical, thinking, and feel -
ing AI technologies, which are categorized according to
AI’s ability to perform tasks of increasing cognitive and
emotional complexity [12]. Mechanical AI is used to
automate repetitive and routine tasks, while thinking AI
is designed to process data to reach new conclusions or
decisions. Feeling AI is used to analyze two-way interac -
tions involving human emotions and affective responses
[12]. For example, all three types of AI can be used to
understand the potential impact of a new technological
product planned for market launch, to create an appro -
priate strategy, and to implement it. Mechanical AI can
scrape data from platforms such as social media and
e-commerce sites and perform market segmentation
based on the data collected. Thinking AI can categorize
consumer expectations into themes such as design, per -
formance, technological criteria, and user experience
using natural language processing, and offer product
recommendations to customers based on these criteria.
Feeling AI can understand the emotional tone of data
collected by mechanical AI, such as customer comments
and reviews, and create marketing content based on this
emotional tone. In the conceptual framework developed
in this study, which will be discussed in detail later, AI
tools are considered useful at all levels of CXM, so they
are placed in a horizontal cross section in our conceptual
framework (Fig. 1).

data analytics. This is natural because big data analyt -
ics is recognized as the raw fuel for AI technology [17].
Big data analytics involves the collection, organization,
and utilization of large-scale data, supporting managers
in developing insights into consumer preferences and
behaviors [18]. Holmlund et  al. [11] proposed a strate -
gic framework that integrates CXM and big data ana -
lytics. According to this strategic framework, different

In the customer journey, it is important for CXM to
understand where customers are positioned, which vari -
ables and touchpoints influence them the most, and the
factors that lead them to continue or discontinue their
journey. Understanding the customer, who is the sub -
ject of this journey, has always been the leading subject
of marketing and many methods have been developed to
understand the customer. These methods include tradi -
tional research approaches, big data analytics techniques
that extract information from the vast amount of data left
behind by customers, and as discussed later, neuroscien -
tific methods that aim to generate insights into consum -
ers’ cognitive and physiological responses. In particular,
since customer experiences are triggered by specific stim-
uli [6] and these triggers influence customer attitudes and
behaviors over time, it is important to understand how
consumers respond to different stimuli, and one of the
most effective ways to do so is through the use of neuro -
science tools.
In neuroscience, neural processes that occur during
behavior are measured in real time through methods
that directly assess neural activity (electromagnetic and
metabolic, such as EEG and fMRI), methods that record
peripheral physiological, facial, or behavioral/attentional
activity (such as GSR, facial coding, and eye-tracking),
and methods for manipulating neural activity [22]. The
use of neuromarketing data, which enables the genera -
tion of valuable insights into sophisticated and complex
human behavior, offers significant opportunities for cus -
tomer experience management. Neuromarketing and
AI have a mutually beneficial, bidirectional relationship.
Specifically, neuromarketing generates highly complex
biometric and neural data from consumers’ brains and
bodies (e.g., EEG, eye-tracking, GSR, facial coding). On
their own, these datasets are vast and unstructured, mak -
ing it difficult to derive meaningful insights. At this point,
artificial intelligence and machine learning play a crucial
role. AI processes these data and translates them into
faster, more accurate, and predictive analytical outputs
[10]. Conversely, the most valuable input for AI algo -
rithms is large-scale, rich data grounded in real human
behavior. Neuromarketing provides biometric traces of
consumers’ emotions, attention levels, and preferences,
thereby facilitating AI’s “learning” process. As a result,
AI can perform more precise segmentation, develop per -
sonalized campaigns, and deliver more accurate recom -
mendations to target audiences. A productive balance
between neuroscience, artificial intelligence, and mar -
keting can provide marketers with opportunities to dis -
cover and strategize for niche markets. Neuromarketing
data from diverse neuroscientific sources can be inte -
grated with AI to create valuable applications [36]. The
application of neuromarketing data to AI will expand the
boundaries of neuromarketing, and AI systems fed with
such data will advance efforts to understand the con -
sumer to the next level. Personalization is a key factor for
CXM [16, 20], and neuromarketing data provide a valu -
able input for AI to deliver personalized experiences.
Studies that bridge AI and neuroscience in the lit -
erature generally focus on the imitation of the human
brain by AI [5]. Studies on AI in marketing have gener -
ally focused on big data analytics and their application
to business contexts [11, 27, 30]. However, in contrast to
the academic literature, companies have implemented
methods to enhance customer experience by integrating
neuromarketing data into AI systems. In addition, many
information technology companies collect neuromarket -
ing data, train AI systems with these data, and market
such systems—for instance, Neuro-Insight, Emotiv, Neu -
roFocus, Innerscope Research, and Ipsos. Moreover, it is
widely recognized that corporations such as Procter &
Gamble, Coca-Cola, Nestlé, General Motors, Campbell,
PayPal, Walmart, and IKEA have been actively involved
in neuromarketing research and in the adoption of artifi -
cial intelligence and machine learning technologies [10].
Framework development and illustrative vignettes
In developing the conceptual framework for this study,
we drew upon Lemon and Verhoef’s [21] study on cus -
tomer journey stages and on Schmitt’s [29] and Hol -
mlund et  al. ’s [11] works on the CXM process. These
models were subsequently expanded and positioned
within a neuromarketing framework based on illustrative
secondary vignettes. As a result of the literature review
and the mapping of these vignettes onto the framework,
the conceptual framework presented in Fig.  1 was devel -
oped. This conceptual framework focuses on the neuro -
marketing-to-AI direction of this reciprocal relationship.
To illustrate the proposed framework, three enterprises
were examined: Liberty Group SA (insurance sector,
South Africa), TUI Group (tourism and travel sector,
Germany), and Zyro (software and technology sector,
Lithuania). These companies were selected for their sec -
toral diversity, for the integration of AI and neuromarket-
ing tools in customer experience management, and for
the public availability of data. The vignettes illustrate how
AI–neuromarketing collaboration has been described
across different contexts—Liberty through an AI-based
chatbot supported by neuromarketing analytics, TUI
through emotion recognition for travel personalization,

and Zyro through AI-generated heatmaps informed by
eye-tracking data—and are used to exemplify the catego -
ries of the proposed framework rather than to test it.
It is appropriate to examine this conceptual framework
from two perspectives. The first dimension is the cus -
tomer journey, which begins with the customer’s recog -
nition of a need and continues through their behavior in
post-purchase processes. This stage represents the clas -
sic consumer decision-making process. It consists of
pre-purchase, purchase, and post-purchase stages, each
influenced by numerous stimuli and elements that affect
consumer decisions. There are many marketing research
studies on customer intentions, behaviors, and percep -
tions. In this article, we are concerned with the man -
agement of stimuli at the points where firms come into
contact with customers. Touchpoints are communica -
tion environments where customers and firms intersect.
These may include physical stores or advertisements
encountered while browsing social media. Touchpoints
can be owned by the customer, the firm, the firm’s part -
ners, or the customer’s social and external environment.
Each of these touchpoints is regarded as a communica -
tion channel, and businesses must deliver consistent
messages across them to enhance customer experience.
The second component is what we call the firm jour -
ney. If the customer journey is defined as the process
beginning with the search to satisfy a need and ending
with the post-purchase evaluation of a product, the firm’s
CXM process can be regarded as the firm’s journey—
from employing neuroscientific methods to identify
needs, to developing corresponding solutions, and ulti -
mately to creating value. Firms start this journey with the
insights and knowledge they already possess. To progress
further, they require customer data. At this stage, the use
of neuroscientific methods that measure neural processes
occurring during behavior—whether through direct
neural measures or peripheral/biometric and behavioral
measures—represents an essential first step for CXM to
achieve a deep understanding of customers and deliver
optimal outcomes that meet their needs.
Customer data obtained in this way are processed
through data analytics techniques and transformed into
actionable information. This information enables the
generation of insights into customers—from the moment
they encounter a stimulus, to their use of a product, to
observing others consuming a product of interest. The
critical issue is whether the insight gained can be realized
at a touchpoint where the firm can intervene and whether
a strategy can be formulated accordingly. Because cus -
tomers are influenced by numerous variables across
many touchpoints, CXM aims to ensure a consistent
experience at nearly every one of them. To maintain such
consistency, AI tools provide substantial advantages.
As illustrated in Fig. 1, firms create, design, or configure
experiential platforms to enhance customer experience
and generate value based on the insights they acquire.
The strategies derived from these insights are generally
categorized as touchpoint monitoring, touchpoint prior -
itization, touchpoint adaptation, and touchpoint-journey
design. Insights may reveal the need to monitor customer
behavior across different touchpoints and other interact -
ing variables. Some touchpoints may exert greater influ -
ence than others, or firms may need to develop entirely
new designs that transform customer experience at a par-
ticular point of contact. It may even become evident that
a new touchpoint must be created.
Indeed, these touchpoint alternatives are broad enough
to encompass all marketing strategies. As discussed in
the theoretical section, CXM is an overarching concept
that integrates numerous customer-related constructs,
including customer satisfaction, perception, motiva -
tion, loyalty, engagement, relationship marketing, and
customer centricity. The framework in Fig.  1 serves as
a comprehensive, bird’s-eye-view map encompassing
all these dimensions. What must be emphasized is that
the individual customer strategies implemented at each
touchpoint need to remain coherent and integrated
within the broader picture—a task that is highly chal -
lenging for firms to achieve. If they succeed, the ultimate
outcome is value. Leveraging the possibilities offered by
today’s technologies is therefore vital to accomplishing
this complex endeavor.
This study is a conceptual article that uses illustra -
tive secondary vignettes to map the proposed frame -
work, focusing on CXM, AI, and neuromarketing. This
approach draws inspiration from the case research tra -
dition [9, 35], which recommends grounding conceptual
development in real-world settings, particularly where
prior conceptualization is limited and where the field
is changing rapidly [4]. However, the cases examined in
this study are derived from publicly available secondary
sources—company websites, press releases, and industry
coverage—rather than from primary data collected by the
authors within organizational settings. They are therefore
used strictly as conceptual illustrations of the proposed
framework and should not be interpreted as case studies
in the methodological sense described by Eisenhardt [9]
and Voss et al. [35]. To make this approach transparent,
the vignettes were selected using three criteria: sectoral
diversity, integration of AI and neuromarketing tools,
and the public availability of sufficiently detailed infor -
mation. Information was drawn from company websites
and press coverage. Because the underlying informa -
tion originates from company and public sources rather
than independently verified data, the inferences that
can be drawn from the vignettes are necessarily limited:

The primary contribution of this framework lies in
positioning neuro/biometric signals as a distinct class
of data that can inform customer experience manage -
ment decisions beyond behavioral and transactional data.
The framework enables these signals to be transformed,
through AI-enabled analytical processes, into inferences
regarding customers’ affective, cognitive, and attentional
states, and links these inferences to concrete touchpoint
decisions such as monitoring, prioritizing, adapting,
and designing. In this way, the proposed approach goes
beyond CXM models based solely on behavioral data
by providing a theoretical and practical basis for more
nuanced managerial interventions, including the iden -
tification of affective friction throughout the customer
journey, the analysis of visual attention allocation, and
the matching of content to customers’ emotional states.
Lemon and Verhoef [21] and Holmlund et  al. [11] con -
ceptualize the customer journey and CXM analytics pri -
marily around behavioral and transactional data,Huang
and Rust [12] classify AI capabilities (mechanical, think -
ing, feeling) but do not specify the data inputs that feed
each type. The present framework’s contribution is to
identify neuro/biometric data (EEG, GSR, facial coding,
eye-tracking) as a distinct data class that gives AI-driven
analytics access to customers’ affective, attentional, and
cognitive states not directly observable in transactional
or behavioral records. Two mechanisms follow from this:
First, neuro/biometric signals allow AI systems to infer
affective and attentional states in situ, at the moment of
the customer–firm interaction, rather than retrospec -
tively from purchase or clickstream data,second, these
inferences can be mapped onto specific managerial
actions—touchpoint monitoring, prioritization, adap -
tation, and journey design—creating a more direct link
between psychophysiological signals and firm-level deci -
sions than existing behavioral-data frameworks provide.
Positioning the contribution around these two mecha -
nisms, rather than around the case narratives themselves,
clarifies that the framework’s novelty lies in the data
source and its mapping onto managerial decisions, build -
ing on Lemon and Verhoef [21] and Holmlund et al. [11].

lected and used, and they should be able to opt out with -
out losing access to essential services. Firms should also
explain, as far as possible, how AI systems use these data
to make inferences about customers. Appropriate safe -

Discussion
In this study, the proposed conceptual framework illus -
trates, in a step-by-step manner, how CXM can be rede -
fined, addressed, and implemented in the context of AI
and neuromarketing. The reciprocal interaction between
the customer journey (pre-purchase, purchase, and post-
purchase) and the firm journey (data collection, analyt -
ics, insights, and actions) is explained through different
touchpoints and AI tools.
Figure  1 illustrates how neural (e.g., EEG, fMRI) and
peripheral/biometric (e.g., GSR, ET, facial coding) data
can be processed with AI-supported analytics (descrip -
tive, predictive, diagnostic, and prescriptive) and trans -
formed into emotional, cognitive, and behavioral insights
about consumers. The framework suggests that these
insights may help firms optimize the customer journey
through monitoring, prioritization, and adaptation of
touchpoints. In this way, the framework suggests that
value creation can be achieved both on the customer
side (enhanced experience, personalization, satisfaction)
and on the firm side (continuous innovation). Table  1
shows how the proposed framework is operationalized
through the illustrative vignettes; as noted above, the
reported outcomes are drawn from company and public
sources and are not independently verified. In the Liberty
case, a combination of neural data (EEG) and periph -
eral physiological data (GSR, facial coding, eye-tracking)
was reportedly used to inform emotional and cognitive
responses, and user interface optimization was report -
edly achieved through touchpoint monitoring, with the
firm reporting gains in time and cost efficiency and in
purchasing behavior. In the TUI case, peripheral data
such as facial coding (FACS) were reportedly employed
to infer consumers’ affective reactions—and, more ten -
tatively and with less evidentiary support, cognitive
responses—while advanced recommendation systems
were reportedly implemented through the adaptation
of touchpoints, with the firm associating this with out -
comes such as stimulating customer curiosity. In the
Zyro case, a predictive attention model together with
AI-supported touchpoint journey design were reportedly
applied, which the provider associates with increased
responsiveness of consumers to stimuli. Taken together,
the framework proposes that when neuromarketing and
AI are integrated into customer experience management,
the diversity of data—including both neural and periph -
eral/biometric signals—may provide firms with insights
into customers that transactional and behavioral records
alone do not capture. Within the framework, AI-based
analytical processes translate these data into predictive
and strategic insights, which in turn inform concrete
actions such as user interface optimization, recommen -
dation systems, and platform design. The framework pos-
its that these actions are the mechanism through which
value may be created within the customer experience, on
both the customer side (satisfaction) and the firm side
(efficiency and competitive advantage). The vignettes are
consistent with this logic but do not establish it: They are
illustrative observations drawn from company and pub -
lic sources rather than causal evidence, and testing the
proposed relationships would require primary empirical
research.
Compared to previous studies, the proposed frame -
work both aligns with and extends earlier findings. For
example, similar to Holmlund et al. [11] and Kumar et al.
[17], our framework is consistent with the view that big
data analytics play a fundamental role in CXM. The pro -
posed framework goes further by suggesting that, beyond
big data, neuromarketing may offer unique consumer-
level insights that AI can learn from, thereby broadening
the scope of customer understanding. While Wedel and
Kannan [36] emphasized the potential of neuroscience
for CX research and noted the lack of empirical applica -
tions, the vignettes indicate that firms such as Liberty,
TUI, and Zyro publicly describe initiatives of this kind,
suggesting that such applications are beginning to appear
in practice. In this sense, our study offers a conceptual
response to the unanswered calls for research on the
role of neuroscientific methods in CXM [21, 36], which
future empirical work could test directly. Earlier litera -
ture on AI in marketing has largely emphasized person -
alization based on behavioral or transactional data [2, 8].
In contrast, our study suggests that personalization can
be deepened through emotional and cognitive insights
derived from neuromarketing, offering a richer form of
customer engagement. This aligns with the growing rec -
ognition that AI-enabled customer experience extends
beyond efficiency gains to encompass human-like
engagement, authenticity, and responsible implementa -
tion [28]. More broadly, these developments underscore
that CXM itself is increasingly understood as a strategi -
cally differentiated process rather than a uniform practice
[38]. Furthermore, while Lim [22] described the meth -
odological potential of neuroscientific tools in under -
standing consumer behavior, our vignettes illustrate how
these tools may be practically embedded into AI-driven
systems, pointing to a possible route for overcoming ear -
lier limitations of scalability and application. Further -
more, while it is evident that applying neuromarketing
techniques and artificial intelligence in marketing raises

Page 8 of 11Topcugil and Hiziroglu  Future Business Journal (2026) 12:256
certain ethical concerns, it is equally important to rec -
ognize that similar issues arise in the context of CXM,
necessitating preventive measures. Goncalves et  al. [10]
emphasize that the use of AI and neuromarketing brings
forth fundamental ethical challenges, including a lack of
transparency, manipulation, protection of human sub -
jects, racial bias, and data security.
Taken together, these insights suggest that the conver -
gence of AI and neuromarketing advances CXM beyond
traditional big data analytics toward a more nuanced,
emotion-based understanding of customers. By illustrat -
ing practical applications across three industries, our
study highlights how this integration may enable firms
not only to monitor and adapt touchpoints but also to
design entirely new customer journeys. Thus, while our
findings are consistent with earlier calls to incorporate
new data and analytics into the analysis of customer
experience [21], they also provide concrete illustrations
of how such integration can be achieved, offering both
theoretical and practical contributions to the field.
Conclusions
The rapid development of artificial intelligence is trans -
forming marketing practices and competitive dynamics.
In today’s business environment, it is crucial for compa -
nies to invest in AI technologies to remain competitive.
At the same time, they must explore new opportunities
to identify potential growth areas and remain in the blue
ocean.
This study conceptualizes the integration of neuro -
marketing and AI within the context of CXM through
a conceptual framework and outlines the potentially
transformative role of this integration. It argues that the
convergence of neuromarketing with AI and machine
learning may offer firms more granular insights, stronger
predictive capabilities, and more finely personalized
interaction opportunities. While previous research has
predominantly portrayed AI as a tool for big data analyt -
ics and neuromarketing as a method for understanding
neural mechanisms, publicly available accounts of com -
pany practice suggest that some firms are beginning to
use neuromarketing data as an input to AI systems in a
CXM context. By developing a conceptual framework,
this study seeks to bridge the gap between theory and
practice and to underscore the strategic significance of
neuromarketing–AI integration for both marketing lit -
erature and practice.
From a managerial perspective, the framework points
to the potential of these technologies to transform B2C
strategies by enabling firms to segment consumers more
effectively, design personalized campaigns, and optimize
customer journeys. However, the study also emphasizes
that collaboration between AI and neuromarketing gives
rise to ethical dilemmas that cannot be overlooked.
While these technologies provide firms with unprec -
edented opportunities to deliver personalized and seam -
less experiences, they simultaneously raise concerns
about consumer manipulation, data privacy, and human
rights. Therefore, companies adopting such approaches
must ensure transparency, obtain informed consent, and
establish clear ethical standards to safeguard consumer
trust.
This study has several limitations that follow directly
from its design. First, it is a conceptual article: The pro -
posed framework is not empirically tested, and no claims
are made about the size or direction of any performance
effects. Second, the three vignettes are drawn from pub -
licly available secondary sources—company websites and
press releases—some of which are promotional in nature.
The outcomes reported by the firms have not been inde -
pendently verified, and the vignettes therefore illustrate
how the framework’s categories map onto described
practice rather than demonstrating that the practices
produce the stated results. Third, with three purposively
selected firms, the vignettes do not support statistical or
theoretical generalization, and no causal inference can be
drawn about the effect of AI–neuromarketing integration
on customer or firm outcomes.
The study is also bounded by a deliberate scope deci -
sion. We focus on B2C contexts because the publicly
documented applications of AI–neuromarketing integra -
tion are currently consumer-facing; this reflects the avail-
ability of observable practice rather than any inherent
limitation of neuromarketing or CXM, both of which are
equally relevant to organizational buyers. Future research
could examine how the framework travels to B2B set -
tings, where buying centers, longer sales cycles, profes -
sional risk perceptions, and relational dynamics may alter
the role of neuro/biometric data. Empirical work—exper-
imental studies, primary multiple-case research with
organizational access, or field tests of specific touchpoint
interventions—would allow the framework’s propositions
to be tested directly. Finally, the ethical issues raised here
(data management, consumer control, and the responsi -
ble use of neuroscientific findings), along with the role of
regulatory and governance mechanisms, should remain
a priority for both academic research and practical
applications.

Vignette 1: An Insurance Company — Liberty Group SA
Liberty is an insurance company based in South Africa that previously received short-term insurance applications through its call centre. To reduce
costs and enhance the customer experience, the company developed an AI-based chatbot, deployed on its website and messaging channels,
with a live-chat handover to a call-centre agent where a customer becomes stuck [24]
To understand how users interacted with the chatbot interface, Liberty engaged the neuromarketing consultancy Neural Sense, which was tasked
with optimising the chatbot’s development through iterative consumer neuroscience testing. The stated objectives were to establish what the chat-
bot should look like, what personality it should possess and the tone and register it would use in communicating with clients, and to optimise
the end-to-end user journey so that it would be smooth, effortless and intuitive (Neural Sense [26])
While customers interacted with the chatbot, the consultancy reports using eye-tracking to determine what attracts and holds attention, gal-
vanic skin response and facial coding to measure the type and degree of affective response, and electroencephalography to assess cognitive flow,
motivation and engagement across the user journey (Neural Sense [26],iMotions [14]). The resulting data were used to establish how the interac-
tion between the chatbot and the customer could be optimised without human intervention, and informed decisions on the chatbot’s tasks, tone
of voice and visual representation
At a pre-launch media briefing, the chief executive of Liberty Short-term Insurance stated that the intended quote experience was a quote in five
minutes and a policy purchase in eight minutes, against a call-centre benchmark of thirty to forty-five minutes for a quote [24]. These are objectives
announced before the chatbot’s full commercial release and reported in trade press; they are not independently verified performance measures, and the source
does not attribute them to the neuromarketing work
Vignette 2: A travel retail company: TUI
TUI, one of Europe’s largest tourism companies, partnered with Realeyes, a company specialising in facial coding and emotion measurement, to make
holiday selection more engaging. Together they developed the “Destination U” prototype, unveiled in October 2017 and presented by TUI as a first
of its kind in travel retail (TUI [32])
The prototype presents a rapid series of evocative moving images of destinations and experiences while a camera records the customer’s
facial responses. An algorithm computes each subtle facial response and uses the resulting data to calculate a “perfect holiday” prescription,
with the images shown varying according to the customer’s reactions (TUI [32],Travel Weekly [31]). The technology is described as using sophisticated
facial coding and emotion measurement to tap into responses that are largely subconscious, with the stated aim of helping customers unlock travel
possibilities they might not otherwise have considered (TUI [32])
At the time of the announcement the system was described as a prototype still undergoing testing rather than a fully deployed service (TUI [32]). No
source consulted discloses the composition of the dataset on which the underlying emotion models were trained
Vignette 3: A software company: Zyro
Zyro is a Lithuania-based software company headquartered in Kaunas, founded in 2019 and operating under Hostinger. It offers website building,
e-commerce and AI-based digital tools that enable users to design professional websites without coding skills. Zyro’s tools have since been consoli-
dated into Hostinger Website Builder; this vignette describes the service as documented at the time of data collection
One of its key AI features is the AI Heatmap. The provider describes it as a tool that gives insights into what website visitors will focus on before they
actually visit, so that decisions can be made before money is spent on user acquisition [39]. The tool belongs to the class of visual-attention (sali-
ency) models, which are conventionally trained on gaze data in order to predict where viewers will look. Independent review coverage describes
it as an AI-enabled heatmap showing where a page’s viewers are likely to focus, overlaid on the site design to highlight the content that will stand
out when users land on the page or scroll down it (IT Pro [13])
These AI-generated heatmaps help website owners understand likely user attention, optimise site layout, and place key elements such as calls
to action where users are most likely to notice them. The provider does not disclose the composition of the training data underlying the model. In terms
of the proposed framework, attention-based data therefore enter the firm journey through a pre-trained predictive model rather than through primary data
collection by the focal firm
