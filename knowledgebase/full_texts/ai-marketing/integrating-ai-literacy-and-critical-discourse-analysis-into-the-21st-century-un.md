Abstract: The rapid integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) into academia and industry
necessitates a fundamental transformation of higher education c urricula. Moving beyond purely
technical proficiency, this paper proposes a comprehensive framework for embedding AI Literacy
and Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA) as transdisciplinary core competencies, positioning AI
outputs not as neutral tools but as i deologically laden discourse requiring rigorous scrutiny.
Drawing on a qualitative multiple-case study involving undergraduate Communication (n=45) and
Business (n=38) courses at a Zambian university, this research demonstrates how a pedagogy
synergistically combining CDA with hands -on AI interaction fosters deeper digital citizenship,
enhances critical thinking, and improves employability skills. Over a three -week intervention,
students applied Fairclough’s three -dimensional CDA model to AI -generated press releases and
marketing content. The findings reveal three transformative outcomes: first, students adeptly
identified embedded societal biases such as gendered leadership stereotypes; second, they
reconceptualised AI from an authoritative “oracle” into a fallible, dialogic partner requiring
ongoing interrogation; and third, they developed sophisticated, ethically -informed prompting
strategies that directly corrected identified biases, a synergistic loop between critique and
creation. This approach ensures graduates emerge as critical, innovative, and ethical producers
rather than passive consumers of technology. The paper recommends curriculum design, including
transdisciplinary modules, faculty development, open -access resources, and reformed assessment
strategies that reward critical engagement over mere output generation.

1. Introduction
The contemporary higher education landscape is undergoing a profound
transformation, a paradigm shift driven by the pervasive integration of Artificial
Intelligence (AI), particularly generative AI technologies such as large language
models (LLMs like ChatGPT and Claude) and image generators like DALL-E and
Midjourney. These tools offer unprecedented opportunities to enhance research,
personalize learning, automate administrative tasks, and foster creativity (Bozkurt,
2023; Zawacki -Richter et al., 2019). However, their rapid and often uncritical
adoption poses significant and multifaceted risks. These include the perpetuation
and amplification of societal biases (Bender et al., 2021), the dissemination of
plausible but inaccurate misinformation (a phenomen on known as
"hallucination"), data privacy concerns, and the potential erosion of foundational
academic skills such as critical analysis and original thought (UNESCO, 2023).
The initial institutional response to generative AI has often been reactive,
oscillating between outright prohibition and cautious, tool -focused integration.
This paper contends that a more robust and sustainable response is required, one
that moves beyond merely teaching students the functional mechanics of AI tools.
It necessitates a curriculum that equips them with the critical faculties to
interrogate these technologies, to understand them not as oracles of objective truth
but as cultural artifacts shaped by human data and decisions.
This paper proposes the integration of two complem entary domains to
achieve this end: AI Literacy; the conceptual understanding of AI systems, their
applications, benefits, and limitations (Long & Magerko, 2020) and Critical
Discourse Analysis (CDA), a well -established methodological approach for
analyzing how language use in texts and talk reinforces, challenges, or subverts
power structures, ideologies, and social inequalities (Fairclough, 2013; Wodak &
Meyer, 2016). By fusing these competencies, students can deconstruct the "black
box" of AI, understand ing not just its algorithmic workings but also the cultural,
political, and economic assumptions embedded within its outputs. They learn to
read AI-generated text as a discourse that is never neutral.
The primary objective of this paper is to present and v alidate a practical,
transdisciplinary framework for embedding this combined AI Literacy -CDA
approach across the university curriculum. The central research question is: How

2. Literature Review
The scholarly foundation for this work rests on three converging streams
of literature: the evolution of digital and AI literacy, the principles and
applications of C ritical Discourse Analysis, and the emerging dialogue at their
intersection.

2.1. From Digital Literacy to Critical AI Literacy
The concept of  digital literacy  has been a cornerstone of 21st -century
education discourse. Spante et al. (2018) highlighted it s dual focus on technical
competence and the cognitive and socio -emotional skills required to participate
effectively in a digital society. As technology evolved, so did the specificity of
these literacies, giving rise to concepts like data literacy and new media literacy.
Post-2020, the discourse has sharpened its focus on  AI literacy. Long and
Magerko (2020), in a seminal work, defined it as a set of competencies that enable
individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies, communicate and collaborate
effectively with AI, and use it as a tool online and at home. Their model outlines
key concepts, including knowledge of AI's capabilities (such as perception,
representation, and reasoning) and its potential for bias. Subsequent research has
expanded on thi s. Ng et al. (2023) proposed a multi -dimensional model that
includes not only technical know -how (Knowing and Using AI) but also critical
dimensions (Evaluating AI) and ethical futures (Applying AI). They strongly
argue for integrating ethics and critical thinking as foundational, rather than
ancillary, components of AI education.
A crucial thread in this literature is the recognition of AI's societal impact.
Bender et al. (2021) powerfully illustrated how LLMs, trained on vast, uncurated
corpora of interne t text, inevitably absorb and amplify the biases, racist, sexist,

2.2. Critical Discourse Analysis: Deconstructing Language and Power
Critical Disco urse Analysis represents a well -established theoretical and
methodological tradition that traces its intellectual roots to critical theory and the
work of scholars such as Fairclough, Van Dijk, and Wodak (Fairclough, 2013;
Van Dijk, 2015; Wodak & Meyer, 20 16). This approach to language study is
fundamentally concerned with examining the intricate relationships between
language, power structures, and ideological formations that operate within society.
At its core, CDA posits that discourse functions as a for m of social practice that
simultaneously shapes and is shaped by the societal contexts in which it emerges
and circulates (Fairclough, 2013). This means that language does not merely
reflect pre -existing social realities but actively constitutes and reprod uces those
realities, including social identities, power relations, and institutional
arrangements.
Fairclough's influential three-dimensional framework for conducting CDA
has proven particularly valuable for researchers seeking to systematically analyze
discourse at multiple levels of abstraction (Fairclough, 2013). The first dimension,
text analysis, involves close examination of linguistic features such as vocabulary
choices, grammatical structures, cohesion devices, and rhetorical strategies
employed within a given text. The second dimension, discursive practice, focuses
on understanding the processes through which texts are produced, distributed, and
consumed within specific social settings. The third dimension, social practice,
situates the analysis w ithin broader socio -cultural contexts to illuminate how
discourse participates in maintaining or challenging existing power asymmetries
and ideological formations (Van Dijk, 2015).
Scholars working within the CDA tradition have consistently emphasized
several key principles that distinguish their approach from other forms of
discourse analysis. These include a sustained focus on power imbalances and how
discourse reproduces dominance by particular social groups, an investigation into
how texts promote specific worldviews as natural or common sense, an insistence
on situating analysis within specific historical and cultural conditions, and an

2.3. The Converging Frontier: CDA Meets AI
The re cent literature (2022 -2025) has begun to explicitly connect these
domains. Researchers are recognizing that AI-generated text is a form of discourse
that requires critical unpacking. For instance, studies have begun to use CDA -
inspired methods to analyze g ender bias in ChatGPT's outputs (Kumar, 2024) or
the neoliberal assumptions embedded in AI -generated business advice (Smith &
Jones, 2024).
The gap in the current literature, which this paper addresses, is the lack of
practical, pedagogical models for impl ementing this critical, discourse -analytic
approach to AI literacy across diverse university curricula. Most proposals remain
theoretical or are confined to computer science and media studies departments.
There is a pressing need for a flexible, actionable  framework that can be adapted
by instructors in the humanities, social sciences, and professional fields to equip
all students, regardless of discipline, with the skills to critically engage with the AI
systems that are permeating their future professions and civic lives.

3. Theoretical or Conceptual Framework
The pedagogical framework proposed in this study rests upon the
synergistic integration of two complementary pillars, namely AI Literacy and
Critical Discourse Analysis, which are together underpinn ed by a critical digital
pedagogy perspective (Stommel, 2020). This foundational perspective,
championed by scholars such as Jesse Stommel and Audrey Watters,
conceptualizes education not as the passive transfer of technical skills from
instructor to student but rather as a practice of freedom that actively questions and
interrogates the tools, platforms, and systems we incorporate into learning
environments (Stommel, 2020). The critical digital pedagogy stance insists that

3.1. AI Literacy
The AI Literacy component of the framework provides students with a
foundational understanding of the technology they are learning to engage with
critically. This  pillar is structured around three interconnected domains that
together equip learners with comprehensive knowledge about artificial
intelligence systems and their operations. The first domain, conceptual
knowledge, encompasses understanding of core AI con cepts, including machine
learning processes, neural network architectures, the nature and function of
training data, algorithmic operations, and the fundamental differences between
automation systems and generative AI technologies (Long & Magerko, 2020).
This conceptual grounding serves the essential function of demystifying the
technology, revealing that AI systems are not magical or oracular but rather
technical artifacts created by humans and trained on human-generated data.
The second domain, practical  skills, involves developing the ability to
interact with AI tools effectively and strategically. This includes competencies
such as prompt engineering, iterative refinement of queries, and interpreting AI
outputs within appropriate contexts (Ng et al., 20 23). The focus here moves
beyond basic command-giving to what might be described as strategic co-creation,
where students learn to work collaboratively with AI systems while maintaining
human oversight and direction. The third domain, critical ethics, addr esses the
essential task of recognizing and evaluating the ethical dimensions of AI
deployment. Students learn to consider issues including data privacy, surveillance
implications, accountability for AI -generated content, intellectual property
questions, and most centrally, the identification and mitigation of various forms of
bias, including algorithmic bias, representation bias, and confirmation bias that
permeate AI systems (Bender et al., 2021; UNESCO, 2023).

Students learn to scrutinize lexicon, including wo rd choices, levels of formality,

The second dimension, discursive practice analysis, shifts attention to the
meso-level processes surrounding the production and interpretation of AI -
generated texts. This involves careful analysis of the prompt provided to the AI,
including its specificity, framing assumptions, and underlying presuppositions.
Students also consider the AI's processing mechanisms, understanding that the
system generates responses based on inferences drawn from its training data, and
examine their own interpretive practices as consumers of AI output (Van Dijk,
2015). The guiding question here might be:  how did my prompt influence the AI's
perspective, and what kind of training data might have produced this specific
output? The third dimension, social practice analysis, operates at the macro -level
by connecting AI outputs to wider social practices, power  relations, and
ideological formations. This is where students explore how AI -generated texts
reflect, reinforce, or could potentially challenge dominant narratives about gender,
race, class, nationality, or economic systems (Wodak & Meyer, 2016). The
guiding question might be: how does this AI -generated marketing plan assume a
Global North consumer, and what model of economic growth does it perpetuate
through its recommendations?

4. Methodology
This study employed a  qualitative, multiple-case study desi gn (Yin, 2018)
to investigate the implementation and outcomes of the proposed framework in
authentic educational settings. A case study approach was deemed appropriate as
it allows for an in -depth, contextual exploration of a contemporary phenomenon
within its real-life context.

4.1. Research Context and Participants
The empirical investigation was conducted at Kwame Nkrumah University
during the first semester of the 2024 academic year, with careful attention to
selecting research contexts that would provide meaningful variation in disciplinary
orientation and practical application. Two undergraduate courses were purposively
selected to participate in the study based on their distinct disciplinary perspectives
and the contrasting ways in which they might e ngage with generative AI
technologies (Siame & Banda, 2024). The first case, COM 301 Advanced Public
Communication, enrolled forty -five students and focused on developing
competencies in crafting persuasive messages for diverse audiences across
multiple co mmunication channels. This course provided an ideal context for
examining how students might learn to critically evaluate AI -generated content
intended for public persuasion and organizational communication purposes.
The second case, BUS 250 Principles of  Marketing, enrolled thirty -eight
students and introduced foundational concepts and strategies related to marketing
practice, including audience analysis, message development, and campaign
planning. This course offered opportunities to explore how students  might apply
critical analysis to AI-generated marketing content and develop awareness of how
commercial messages embed particular assumptions about consumers and markets
(Smith & Jones, 2024). A total of eighty -three students participated across both
cases, providing a robust dataset for qualitative analysis. This research did not
require ethical approval from the university's Institutional Review Board before
commencing data collection. Nevertheless, informed consent was obtained from
all participants wit h explicit guarantees of anonymity and confidentiality in all
subsequent reporting of findings, following established ethical guidelines for
educational research (Yin, 2018). Regarding researcher positionality, the author is
a faculty member at the same in stitution, which provided insider access but also
required reflexive bracketing of assumptions. This was addressed through peer

4.2. Pedagogical Intervention
A three -week instructional module titled Critical Engagement with
Generative AI was designed and systematically integrated into both participating
courses following a structured sequence of learning activities. During the first
week, which focused on esta blishing foundational knowledge, students received
direct instruction on core concepts of AI literacy, including explanations of how
large language models function, basics of prompt engineering, and typologies of
bias that commonly appear in AI systems (Lo ng & Magerko, 2020; Ng et al.,
2023). This was accompanied by instruction in Critical Discourse Analysis, with
particular emphasis on Fairclough's three -dimensional model and its potential
applications (Fairclough, 2013). The foundational instruction was s upported
through lectures, assigned readings, and in -class workshops in which students
practiced applying CDA to non -AI media texts, including advertisements and
political speeches (Siame et al. 2025), thereby developing analytical skills that
would later transfer to AI-generated content.
The second week involved a scaffolded assignment that required students
to apply their developing knowledge in hands -on analysis (Yin, 2018). For COM
301 students, the task required prompting ChatGPT to generate a three -hundred-
word press release announcing a groundbreaking new technology from a visionary
tech leader, followed by conducting a comprehensive CDA on the generated text
using a detailed worksheet that guided them through text analysis, discursive
practice analy sis, and social practice analysis. For BUS 250 students, the task
required prompting ChatGPT to create a marketing campaign outline for a new
sustainable fashion brand targeting young, affluent professionals, followed by the
same systematic CDA process. St udents were required to submit both the AI -
generated text, complete with their initial prompt, and their completed CDA
worksheet for assessment.
The third week culminated in a facilitated ninety -minute class discussion
during which students shared their f indings, identified common patterns of bias
and ideological framing across different AI outputs, and reflected on the
implications of their discoveries for their respective professional fields (Braun &
Clarke, 2019).

4.3. Data Collection and Analysis
The study employed a multi -method approach to data collection designed
to capture rich qualitative evidence of student learning and critical development.
The primary data source consisted of the eighty -three completed CDA worksheets
submitted by students, whic h provided direct documentary evidence of their
analytical processes and the critical insights they generated through systematic
application of the CDA framework. Students additionally submitted five -hundred-
word reflective journals after completing the as signment, responding to prompts
about their changing perceptions of artificial intelligence, the challenges they
encountered during analysis, and the personal and professional relevance of the
skills they had developed (Braun & Clarke, 2019).
Two semi -structured focus group discussions were conducted after
completion of the module, with six participants selected from each course through
voluntary sign-up procedures. These discussions, each lasting approximately sixty
minutes, explored themes including trus t in AI systems, perceived skill
development, and the potential for transferring analytical competencies to other
contexts (Yin, 2018). The focus group sessions were audio -recorded and
transcribed verbatim for analysis.
Data analysis followed the systemat ic procedures of thematic analysis as
articulated by Braun and Clarke (2019), involving repeated reading of transcripts
and journals to achieve familiarization, generating initial codes from the data,
searching for potential themes by collating codes, revi ewing themes against coded
extracts and the entire dataset, defining and naming themes by refining their
essential characteristics, and finally producing the analytic narrative presented in
the findings section.
Trustworthiness was ensured through triangu lation of multiple data
sources, peer debriefing with colleagues not involved in the study, and provision
of thick descriptive evidence in the reporting of findings (Yin, 2018).

5.1. Unmasking Implicit Bias and Ideology: From Abstract Concept to
Concrete Discovery
The most pow erful and consistently observed finding across the dataset
was students' demonstrated ability to move from an abstract, theoretical
understanding of AI bias toward concrete identification and critique of bias
operating within actual AI -generated texts (Ben der et al., 2021). The structured
CDA worksheet provided students with a systematic pathway for this
transformative discovery process, guiding them through successive levels of
analysis that progressively deepened their critical engagement. At the level of
textual analysis, students in the COM 301 course discovered that over eighty
percent of the AI -generated press releases about visionary tech leaders employed
male pronouns and descriptors such as charismatic, bold, visionary, and game -
changing. Students m oved from abstract understanding to concrete identification
of bias. The study also shows that over 80% of AI -generated press releases about
“tech leaders” used male pronouns, such as charismatic and visionary, while
prompts about non -profit directors gene rated female pronouns, such as
compassionate and dedicated.
When students conducted follow -up prompts requesting content about
highly effective non -profit directors, the AI tended to generate texts employing
female pronouns and descriptors, including comp assionate, dedicated, and
community-focused. Students were able to pinpoint this lexical patterning through
their systematic textual analysis, identifying how word choices constructed
different leadership personas along gendered lines. This finding aligns with recent
research demonstrating that large language models consistently reproduce and
amplify societal gender stereotypes present in their training data (Bender et al.,
2021; Kumar, 2024).
At the level of discursive practice analysis, students reflecte d critically on
how their own prompts, often formulated simplistically without explicit
specification of gender or leadership style, inadvertently allowed the AI's default
biases to surface in generated content. One student articulated this realization
powerfully in their reflective journal, noting that they had simply typed tech leader
without thinking to specify gender, and that their own unexamined assumption
had been that the leader would be male, an assumption the AI merely confirmed.
This student recognized that the exercise revealed their own biases as much as the
AI's, a phenomenon documented in research on the reciprocal relationship
between user assumptions and AI outputs (Long & Magerko, 2020).

across the dataset as students moved from initial to final understandings of
artificial intelligence systems. Before the intervention, the dominant view of AI

authoritative, qua si-oracular sources whose outputs could be accepted with
minimal questioning or scrutiny (Zawacki -Richter et al., 2019). Following the
intervention, this conception had fundamentally transformed into an understanding
of AI as a fallible, dialogic partner r equiring ongoing critical engagement and
interrogation. The process of systematically deconstructing AI -generated
language, noting its formulaic structures, reliance on clichés and stock phrases,
and internal contradictions, effectively shattered students'  initial illusion of AI
authority.

particular ways. Students began approaching AI outputs with a systematically
developed set of critical questions representing direct application of the CDA
mindset. A focus group participant explained that th ey no longer simply accepted
the first answer generated by AI but instead actively interrogated it by considering
alternative perspectives, populations that might be excluded by the AI's framing,
and the nature of training data that led the system to prese nt particular answers as
correct.
This fundamental shift marks the transition from passive consumption of
technology toward active, critical participation, aligning closely with the goals of
critical digital pedagogy, which seeks to empower learners to qu estion the very
tools they are given rather than accepting them uncritically (Stommel, 2020).
Recent research on student interactions with generative AI confirms that such
metacognitive awareness represents a sophisticated form of engagement that
distinguishes superficial users from critical consumers of AI -generated content
(Ng et al., 2023; UNESCO, 2023).

5.3. The Synergistic Loop: Enhanced Prompting as a Consequence of
Critique
An emergent and practically significant outcome of the integrated
framework was the substantial improvement in students' ability to craft
sophisticated, critically informed prompts for AI interaction. This development
was not initially a primary focus of the instructional design but emerged
organically from the CDA process as stud ents applied their analytical insights to
subsequent AI engagements (Fairclough, 2013).
As students identified specific biases and limitations in initial AI outputs
through systematic discourse analysis, they instinctively developed and refined
strategies to correct for these identified shortcomings in their subsequent
interactions with the technology. This created a virtuous cycle of continuous
improvement in which critical analysis directly informed and enhanced practical
skill development. Students bega n incorporating corrective or constraining
instructions into their prompts, such as specifying that a press release about a
technology CEO should ensure the CEO is portrayed as a woman and describe her

6. Limitations and Future Research
This study h as several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single
institution in Zambia, which may limit generalizability to other national or cultural
contexts where AI access, digital infrastructure, and pedagogical norms differ.
Second, the intervention was l imited to three weeks; longitudinal research is
needed to assess the retention of critical AI literacy skills over time. Third, while
the qualitative design provided rich insights, future studies could employ quasi -
experimental designs with control groups to measure the magnitude of the
framework’s impact. Fourth, all data were self -reports or worksheet-based; future
research could incorporate analysis of actual student -AI interaction logs. Finally,
the study did not explore potential differences in outcome s across student
demographic variables (e.g., prior digital experience, gender, disciplinary
background), which represents an important avenue for further investigation.

7. Conclusion
This paper has argued that the responsible and effective integration of
generative AI into higher education requires a paradigm shift from functional tool-
use to critical, discursive engagement. The proposed framework, which
intertwines the conceptual and practical dimensions of AI Literacy with the
methodological rigor of Cr itical Discourse Analysis, provides a robust,
transdisciplinary pedagogical model for achieving this shift.
The findings from the case studies in Communication and Marketing
courses demonstrate that this approach is not only feasible across disciplines but is
also highly effective in achieving its goals. It successfully empowered students to:
Identify and deconstruct  the implicit biases and ideological assumptions
embedded within AI -generated content; Reconceptualize their relationship with
AI from one of p assive acceptance to active, critical interrogation; Improve their
practical interactions  with AI through more sophisticated, ethically -aware
prompting strategies, demonstrating a synergistic loop between critique and
creation.
The skills cultivated through this process, nuanced critical thinking, ethical
reasoning, advanced digital literacy, and the ability to manage human -AI
collaboration are precisely those identified as crucial for graduate employability
and active citizenship in a world increasingly me diated by AI (World Economic
Forum, 2023). By embedding this Critical AI Literacy across the curriculum,
universities can move beyond a reactive stance on AI. They can proactively fulfill
their mandate to develop discerning, adaptable, and empowered graduates who are
prepared not just to navigate an AI -driven world but to shape it critically,
ethically, and innovatively.
Based on the evidence and conclusions of this study, the following
recommendations are proposed for higher education institutions, curricu lum
designers, and policymakers:

i. Develop and Mandate Transdisciplinary Modules:  University senates and
curriculum committees should champion the development of foundational,
credit-bearing modules on Critical AI Literacy. These modules should be
designed for adaptability and integration into a wide array of
undergraduate programs, from the arts and humanities to the sciences and
professional schools.
