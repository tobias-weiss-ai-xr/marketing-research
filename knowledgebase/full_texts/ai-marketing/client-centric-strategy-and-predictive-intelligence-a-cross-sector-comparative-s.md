Abstract: This research explores the use of client -focused AI by the top global
consulting and financial institutions in terms of personalisation and algorithmic bias
reduction, the impact of data protection law across jurisdictions on the quality of
predictive capability and the potential value of generative AI in text, audio and video.
Research methodology: This research employed a two -stage approach. Firstly, a
bibliometric analysis was conducted on the body of work related to client focused AI
and algorithmic bias. Following this qualitative comparative research was conducted
on global consulting and financial institutions using the Information Systems Success
Model and the Technology Acceptance Model as a theoretical basis. Personalisation
and sentiment analysis research has been conducted at a higher rate tha n bias
reduction research and as a result algorithmic bias has only made a limited
contribution to client focused AI. Institutions which embed data protection and
accountability requirements into the design of their systems can achieve innovation in
prediction whilst also achieving customer trust. Disconnected accountability results
in siloed visibility through marketing and sales and results in disjointed customer
trust. The use of generative AI can provide value to customers and enhance the
customer experience but only if supported by transparency and human in the loop.
Contribution to research: A framework for Client -value based on Predictive,
Responsible, Integrated, Sustainable and Measurable is proposed. This framework
makes the connection between responsible generative and predictive AI and resilience
and trust in volatile environments. It can provide a tool for governance diagnosis for
managers and provide a foundation for proportionate oversight by regulators.
Novelty: This research is novel in its u se of both bibliometric analysis and cross
industry case studies to explore generative engagement, compliance and fairness
within a client focused AI context.

Theme 2: Institutional Logic Conflict in Predictive-Intelligence Delivery
Risk perception depends on the concept of "universalism vs. particularism”, something that
companies need to look out for. On one hand, when firms perceive more danger to adapting to
technology, they are less likely to achieve it, and the negative effect is not constant, it depends
on factors like how the market is doing, where the economy is headed etc. On the other hand,
“trust” has the opposite effect. Trust is built more on cult ural values, and how much control
people feel they have over the situation; both influence how much trust ends up contributing in
someone's decision to adopt (Ladeira et al., 2025). Beyond individual perceptions of risk and
trust, adoption is further const rained by the structural realities of regulation and compliance
governing financial data and model use. Alongside broader regulatory realities, control over
where client data is visible has become non -negotiable. A client journey in today's world
touches multiple channels, and often the data is sitting idle in silos. It is to maintain compliance
under frameworks like GDPR and HIPPA that leads to fragmented visibility across sales and
marketing channels. To tackle this issue predictive frameworks have been p roposed that
integrate data -driven modelling, probabilistic attribution, and advanced analytics to reduce
revenue disparity and maintain compliance. (Sanni, 2026). Financial service models are tied to
two pillars, pillar 1 and pillar 2. Pillar 1 = formula-driven minimum capital requirements, pillar
2 = supervisory review of additional risks. Models in this space touch capital allocation,
compliance (AML/fraud/transaction monitoring), credit decisions, collections, and early -
warning systems. These models can not be deployed freely, they go through rigorous checks,
sometimes even have to go through a signed approval of a supervisor. Because this space has
had regulatory eyes on it the longest, it's become the most standardized/developed part of model
governance. Two forces pushed that: constant supervisory pressure and the need to keep things
efficient enough to stay profitable. (Fritz-Morgenthal et al., 2022). Amidst all the compliances
and regulations, one question remains is about responsibility, who is respo nsible for if things
go south.

A. Research Design
This study uses a multiple case, cross sector comparative study, where each firm is treated as
an in dividual case which is then evaluated by its AI strategy, client product portfolio,
governance structures and disclosure practices. The framework compares two distinct global
sectors; Financial services and Consulting firms. Both industries share an overlap of specialized
advice, client trust and long term relationships, both seeking commercial incentives to
personalize clientele experiences. The difference lies between the strict regulatory laws that
financial firms operate under, and are subjected to prud ential supervision, conduct guidelines,
model risk rules, consumer laws. On the other hand consulting firms face far fewer regulations
outside of their auditing practices. This discourse tested whether strict regulatory oversight
helps discipline and give fairness a direction and transparency in client facing AI, and the
direction of that divergence is an empirical question the design is built to answer

D. Analytical Framework: the Client-Centric Predictive Intelligence Maturity Index
Due to the lack of existing analytical frameworks about  both the client centricity and fairness
postures of public disclosures from firms AI estate. This study therefore introduces the Client -
Centric Predictive Intelligence Maturity Index (CPIMI).The index comprises of six distinct
dimensions across two struct ural blocks. Dimensions D1 -D3 form the client centricity block,
indicating and assessing to what extent generative AI capabilities are deployed in client
interaction. Dimensions D1 -D6 represent governance blocks, that measures the governance
blocks, captures fairness, transparency and safety architecture built around it. Each dimension
is evaluated on a scale of 0-4, which produces a raw cumulative score of 24, that then is adjusted
and normalized to a 0 -100 CPIMI scale. A second derived measure is the Trus t-Risk Balance
Ratio, that is calculated by dividing the total of the client centricity block score by the total
governance block score. An achieved ratio of more than 1 indicates that clients facing AI
ambitions outweigh the disclosed governance safeguard s, a ratio below one indicates the
opposite. This ratio serves as a primary quantitative tool for evaluating balance within the
central question.

E. Coding Procedure and Reliability
Coding was conducted using directed qualitative content analysis, which already had the
codebook in table 2 specified before coding and applying it deductively across the collected
corpus. Every collected document was then read in depth and specific codable statements were
mapped and assigned to one of the six dimensions. Unsupported or exaggerated marketing
language and claims were deliberately downgraded during scoring, so a firm's final score in
any dimension reflects the strongest position backed by the evidence across the whole corpus,
rather than relying on a single document which can be mistaken for its most favorable one.  To
prevent the most likely sources for bias, three coding rules were strictly enforced. 1) Aspiration
Cap: future statements for intention (“we will”, “ we aim to”) were capped at a maximum score
of 2, unless it is accompanied by an active deployed mechanism which provides a spine for the

statement. 2) Scale neutrality: Firm size was completely ruled out to prevent larger institutions
scoring higher on metrics like D2 for simply having a higher volume in client count. To ensure
reliability, a second independent coder was deployed to evaluate a random 33% sample using
the same codebook. The metric used was Cohen's Kappa coefficient (k) which followed the
standard threshold, where values above 0.70 were treated as acceptable for direct content
analysis. The o nes scoring below that were resolved by re -examining the source, where
ambiguous wording defaulted to a more conservative score. Transparency in audit trials that
maintained links to every score to its underlying document was maintained.

F. Statistical Treatment
The dataset contains small sample sizes (n=6 firms per sector) which mandates the use of non-
parametrical tests. The first test used was the Mann -Whitney U test which was applied to
compare financial firm’s vs comparison across individual dimensions and block totals, with

Conclusion

Clients facing AI are expanding rapidly across consulting and financial sectors. But the public
proof of fairness is still missing. The reports show that they have moved past the
experimentation fairly easily, but the AI has to reach clients through human acting as a medium
rather than direct. Current regulations enforce internal governance, but still risk reputation as a
collateral for accountability. Both financial and consulting firms quantify their deployment in
precise numbers and describe their fairness work in general terms. The resulting verific ation
gap means that no external stakeholder can currently confirm that any firm in the sample is
mitigating algorithmic bias effectively. Evidence also shows that the disconnect represents a
reporting gap rather than a tradeoff, as high deployment and ado ption and governance are not
mutually exclusive. Firms must transition from current framework to metric backed
verification, getting AI to transition from a compliance cost to a trusted asset.

The aim of this research was to use artificial intelligence wit hin the global financial and
management consulting firms to deliver client relationships that are secure, safe, fair and
improved. The research used both qualitative case studies across sectors and bibliometric
mapping to reach three key findings which relate to the research questions.

First, research on AI for customers has developed separately from research on fairness in
algorithms. This has led to personalization research far in excess of bias reduction mechanisms,
which presents a strategic risk not just a technical issue.

Second, strictness in regulation (HIPAA, GDPR, etc.) does not automatically limit the
application of predictive technologies. Instead, if compliance is incorporated into the design of
a system, credit, risk, and fraud monitoring will benefit from increased reliability. Additionally,
incorporating compliance up front can avoid the fragmented view resulting from applying
regulation as a post-hoc list

Third, generative AI in video, audio and text can improve the customer experience and provide

This work makes several contributions. Theoretically, it adapts the Information Systems
Success Model and the Technology Acceptance Model to responsible predictive and generative
AI. Further, this work also adapts the two base models to a cross-sector analysis of global firms
from small to multiple enterprises to a single firm. PRISM -C is a model that combines

measurable client value into one model. Practically, this work provides managers with tools to

value will accrue  to companies that implement personalisation in an ethical and fair manner
towards their customers.

Future Scope

The PRISM-C framework can be utilized in further studies involving SMEs, offshore service
organizations, and larger studies involving coope rative and commercial banks. Cross -national
studies can examine the implementation of AI across various regulatory environments.
Technological studies can focus on diffusion of big data analytics and the creation of hybrid
machine learning algorithms that incorporate measures for bias and data -ethics. Lastly,
empirical tests of the framework can be conducted utilizing structural equation modeling

Methodological Limitations

Bibliometric analysis is sensitive to the coverage of databases and the choice of keywords. The
case studies involve a small number of major companies and use information from disclosure
and interviews. This limits the statistical ability to generalise. The study also uses a snapshot
of governance at a time when regulation is changin g quickly. Four limitations bear directly on
interpretation and are revisited in Section 6. This study sees disclosures vs gaps. It leans towards
evaluating firms with more public disclosure, so a well governed but reticent firm is penalized,
while an articulate organization with a weaker governance receives higher evaluations. Scoring
is purely interpretive. Though double coding procedures prevent individual bias, the
subjectivity does not eliminate it.The sample size of twelve firms remains statistically small, it
is analytically rich, and because of the rapid nature of the field, temporary boundaries are
imposed. Platforms deployed or disclosed after 2026 are excluded from the corpus, which
makes its a specific timeframe rather than a base line.
