Abstract

This paper analyses a concrete channel throug h which artificial intelligence is reshaping the
global economy: generative AI in digitally delivered business services. Combining WTO, UNCTAD,
OECD, Eurostat, IMF, ILO and European Commission evidence with recent peer-reviewed
workplace studies, the paper examines software, customer support, marketing, finance support,
legal-administrative work and anal ytics services. The findings show that AI can reduce task time,
raise quality and lower trade costs, but only when firms redesign workflows, protect data and
maintain human verification. The gains are uneven . Economies with cloud access, digital skills,
trusted regulation and service-export capacity ca n expand high-value exports, while countries
dependent on routine clerical outsourcing face price compression. A practical policy agenda
therefore requires digital infrastructure, applied AI skills, SME adoption support, competition policy
and credible governance under emerging AI regulation.

Key words: generative AI, digital services trade, productivity, labour reallocation, competitiveness

1. Introduction

Artificial intelligence is often discussed as a broad technological revolution, but its first practical
global-economic channel is more specific: digitally delivered business services. Generative AI can
draft, translate, summarise, cla ssify, code, search, design wor kflows and support customer
interaction. These activities map directly onto services that a re already traded across borders,
including software, customer support, digital marketing, finance support, legal-administrative work,
consulting and analytics. The res ult is a trade and competitiveness issue, not only a labour-market
issue.
The scale of the channel is large. World Trade Organization (WTO, 2025) evidence suggests that
AI could raise global trade by 34-37% and global GDP by 12-13% by 2040 if technology and policy
gaps are bridged. WTO (2026) also places digitally delivered services at the centre of current global
trade statistics. UNCTAD (2026a; 2026b) shows that global ICT s ervices trade reached USD 1.2
trillion in 2024 and that digitally deliverable products accounted for 56% of world services exports.
Europe is a major participant, accounting for 57% of ICT-services exports in 2024. At the same time,
Eurostat (2025) reports that only  20.0% of EU enterprises with 10 or more employees used AI
technologies in 2025, although this was up from 13.5% in 2024. The opportunity is therefore
substantial, but adoption is still incomplete.
This paper asks how generative AI affects the global economy th rough traded business services.
The central argument is that AI lowers the cost of many cognitive tasks, but the gains are conditional
on complementary assets: digital infrastructure, data access, h uman skills, managerial redesign,
cybersecurity, intellectual-property controls and trustworthy r egulation. Comparative advantage in

2. Literature review

The literature increasingly treats AI as a general-purpose tech nology with potentially wide
productivity effects. Filippucci et al. (2024) describe the mec hanisms through which AI can affect
productivity, distribution and growth, while Cazzaniga et al. ( 2024) show that advanced economies
are more exposed to AI because their employment is more concentrated in cognitive and knowledge-
intensive occupations. This reverses part of the conventional a utomation narrative: the early shock
is not primarily to manual factory tasks, but to office, service and professional workflows.
Workplace evidence is already strong enough to reject both extr eme narratives. Noy and Zhang
(2023) find that ChatGPT reduced completion time and increased quality in professional writing
tasks. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond (2025) show that AI assista nce raised customer-support
productivity, especially for less experienced workers. Dell’Acq ua et al. (2026) add an important
caution: AI can improve performance inside the frontier of mode l competence but worsen
performance outside that frontie r. The implication is that AI a doption is not a simple software
purchase. It is an organisational change requiring task selection, verification and accountability.
The labour literature also distinguishes exposure from replacem ent. International Labour
Organization (Gmyrek et al., 2023) research finds that generative AI is more likely to transform jobs
than fully automate them, while Gmyrek et al. (2025) identify substantial occupational exposure and
country differences in a refined global exposure index. Cazzani ga et al. (2024) similarly emphasise
that exposure is higher in advanced economies but that compleme ntarities determine whether
workers benefit; Lane (2024) show s that white-collar and highly  educated workers are especially
exposed. For global services, this means that firms and countri es able to combine AI with skilled
human supervision may expand, while routine service exporters may face price pressure.
The trade literature now connects AI with cross-border flows. WTO (2025) argues that trade can
enable AI by giving economies access to semiconductors, intermediate inputs and knowledge, while
AI can reduce trade costs through translation, compliance and l ogistics. OECD modelling by
Filippucci et al. (2026) estimates that AI-driven productivity gains may raise per-capita real income
growth by 0.1-0.95 percentage points annually across OECD and G20 countries, but the distribution
depends on adoption and trade linkages.

4. Findings

4.1 Business services are the first scalable AI trade channel

The first finding is that digitally delivered services are the most immediate global channel for
generative AI. Their production process already uses digital ne tworks, documents, code, language
and remote coordination. When AI reduces drafting, translation,  coding, support and analysis time,
it lowers the marginal cost of internationally supplied service s. This can expand trade by making
smaller contracts profitable, enabling multilingual delivery an d allowing firms to compete outside
their domestic market.
The trade base is large and uneven. UNCTAD (2026a) reports that  global ICT services exports
reached USD 1.2 trillion in 2024, with Europe holding 57% and Asia and Oceania 33%, while Africa
and Latin America and the Caribbean together accounted for only  2.5%. UNCTAD (2026b) also
reports that digitally deliverable services reached 56% of glob al services exports in 2024, with
developed economies accounting for around three quarters. This m ean s AI m ay am pl ify ex isting
service-export advantages unless infrastructure and skills gaps narrow.

The second finding is that AI changes what can be traded. A sma ll firm can translate product
documentation, answer foreign-lang uage tickets, prepare marketi ng material, draft code comments
and produce compliance summaries with a smaller team than before. This does not remove the need
for human expertise. It changes the minimum efficient scale and  raises competitive pressure in
routine tasks. The most exposed services are those where client s can  specify o utpu ts clearly and
where verification is cheap. The least exposed are services req uiring deep context, liability-bearing
judgement, complex negotiation or sensitive data governance.
The third finding is that the productivity effect is real but c onditional. Eurostat (2025) enterprise
data show rapid EU adoption growth, from 7.7% in 2021 and 13.5%  in 2024 to 20.0% in 2025.
Evidence from Bick, Blandin and Deming (2026) on rapid generati ve AI adoption reinforces the
point that diffusion can be fast but uneven. The most common 20 25 enterprise use was analysis of
written language, followed by generation of pictures, video or audio, generation of written or spoken
language, and speech conversion. These uses map directly onto b usiness-service tasks. However,
adoption rates differ sharply ac ross countries, from 42.0% in D enmark to 5.2% in Romania,
indicating that the EU itself contains a digital adoption divide.
Workplace studies explain why adoption does not automatically c reate productivity. AI works
best where tasks are well specified, outputs can be checked and  employees understand the domain.
In customer support it can compress learning curves and standar dise responses. In software
development it can accelerate code completion and testing, but it can also generate security or
reliability risks. In legal, financial and compli ance support it can lower document-processing costs,
but errors are costly. The most productive model is therefore supervised augmentation, not
unsupervised substitution.

4.2 Comparative advantage is being rewritten

The fourth finding is that AI changes comparative advantage in services. Low wages still matter,
but they matter less when routine drafting, translation, summarisation and coding assistance become
cheap and widely available. Advantage shifts toward economies and firms with cloud access, reliable
broadband, trusted data governance, cyber resilience, high-skil l labour, domain expertise and
managerial capability. WTO (2025) and Filippucci et al. (2026) both imply that the gains from AI
are largest when economies combine adoption with open and predictable trade.
This creates both risk and opportunity for developing economies. Countries relying on routine
back-office outsourcing may face price compression if buyers ca n automate or rebundle tasks. Yet
AI can also help new exporters move into localisation, software maintenance, regulatory technology,
cybersecurity support, model evaluation, human-review services and sector-specific analytics. The

4.3 Policy requirements for inclusive productivity

The fifth finding is that policy should focus less on resisting AI and more on widening productive
adoption. The Digital Europe Programme (European Commission, 2026a) provides a relevant model
because it funds capacity in supercomputing, AI, cybersecurity,  advanced digital skills and digital
technology deployment. The EU AI Act framework (European Commis sion, 2026b) also matters
because full application from August 2026, with staged obligations for prohibited practices, general-
purpose AI and high-risk systems, creates a framework for trust . For services trade, credible
governance is not a compliance burden alone; it is a market-access asset when clients need assurance
about data, security, IP and liability.
A practical policy agenda has five elements. First, improve broadband, cloud access and compute
affordability for SMEs. Second, make applied AI skills part of vocational, university and adult-
learning systems, with emphasis on prompt design, verification,  data protection, cybersecurity and
domain judgement; this is consistent with Jaumotte et al. (2026 ), who stress the importance of
bridging AI-related skill gaps for new job creation. Third, support organisational adoption in SMEs
through advisory services and testbeds rather than only frontier research. Fourth, use competition
policy and procurement to prevent excessive concentration of AI -enabled service markets. Fifth,
align data governance, IP and liability rules so that cross-border AI-enabled services are trusted and
auditable.

5. Conclusions

Generative AI is already affecting the global economy through a  concrete and practical route:
digitally delivered business services. The channel matters beca use it connects micro-level
productivity effects with cross-border competition, labour real location and national service-export
strategies. AI can reduce task time, improve quality, lower transaction costs and widen market access,
but these gains depend on complementary assets and governance.
The global impact is therefore uneven. Economies with digital i nfrastructure, cloud access,
applied skills, trusted regulation and strong service firms can expand high-value exports. Economies
dependent on routine outsourcing may experience price pressure unless they upgrade toward human-
