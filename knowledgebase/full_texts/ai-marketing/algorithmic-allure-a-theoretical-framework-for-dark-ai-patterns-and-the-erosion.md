This study examines how generative AI is reshaping digital marketing by enabling
unprecedented levels of personalization, automation, and consumer interaction,
while simultaneously producing subtler, more complex forms of algorithmic
manipulation that extend well beyond conventional dark patterns and threaten
meaningful informed consent. Prior research has treated AI-enabled
persuasion in a fragmented way—focusing on personalization, recommender
systems, or deceptive interface designs in isolation—without offering an
integrated account of how generative AI shapes influence across the
consumer journey. To address this gap, we conduct a multi-case qualitative
analysis of seven documented international incidents of AI-driven marketing
practices from 2024 to 2026. Data sources include regulatory reports,
investigative journalism, corporate disclosures, and peer-reviewed literature.
Using reflexive thematic analysis, we identified seven high-level themes:
identity manipulation, social-proof exploitation, synthetic evidence, AI-
washing, agentic AI manipulation, synthetic authenticity, and autonomous
commercial manipulation. These themes form a taxonomy of “Dark AI
Patterns”. Building on these findings, we propose Algorithmic Allure Theory
(AAT), which conceptualizes AI-enabled persuasion as a dynamic, six-stage
process: observation, interpretation, algorithmic allure, behavioral steering,
delegation, and algorithmic governance. AAT argues that contemporary
commercial persuasion relies on adaptive computational systems that
continuously generate, test, and refine influence tactics in real time—
processes that often erode informed consent while preserving a veneer of
legitimacy and transparency.
Contributions: (1) establish Dark AI Patterns as a distinct class of deceptive
practices; (2) introduce AAT as a transdisciplinary framework for understanding
AI-mediated influence; (3) reconceptualize informed consent as an ongoing
cognitive and temporal process rather than a one-time legal event; (4) offer a
practical taxonomy to guide future research, regulation, and ethical AI
marketing practices As a primary goal.

1 Introduction
Generative AI is radically reshaping digital marketing by
transforming how firms engage with consumers, tailor messages,
and influence purchasing decisions. Where earlier marketing
technologies primarily automated repetitive tasks and improved
market segmentation, contemporary AI systems continuously
learn from user behavior, generate bespoke content, predict
future preferences, and refine persuasive strategies in real time
(Bormane and Blaus, 2024). These capabilities have enhanced
firms’ ability to deliver more relevant customer experiences, yet
they have also given rise to mounting ethical, legal, and social
challenges concerning transparency, autonomy, and meaningful
informed consent Cheong (2024). The rapid diffusion of
generative AI has accelerated this shift: large language models,
image synthesizers (Cheong, 2024), conversational agents,
recommender systems, and autonomous AI agents are now
integral components of digital marketing ecosystems (Huang
and Rust, 2021). These technologies enable automated content
production, hyper-personalized advertising, and instantaneous
modification of persuasive messages based on fine-grained
behavioral profiles (Peter et al., 2025). As a result, AI has moved
from a decision-support role to being an active shaper of
consumer perceptions, preferences (Grewal et al., 2025), and
purchase behavior through continuously adaptive interactions.
For many years personalization was celebrated as a central
benefit of digital marketing, reducing information overload and
improving customer satisfaction (Kaplan and Haenlein, 2019).
However, recent technical advances have blurred the boundary
between beneficial personalization and algorithmic manipulation
(Kirk et al., 2024). Modern AI systems can detect affective
states, cognitive biases, and situational vulnerabilities at the
individual level, enabling message adaptation grounded in
psychological weakness rather than coarse demographic
attributes (Matz et al., 2024). Consequently, personalization
increasingly functions as behavioral steering rather than merely
enhancing the customer experience. These developments have
intensified scholarly interest in algorithmic persuasion (Sposini,
2024), AI ethics, and digital transparency (Cheong, 2024). One
prominent research strand examines “dark patterns”, interface
designs intentionally crafted to nudge users toward firm-centric
choices (Mathur et al., 2019). Although valuable, this literature
remains fragmented: dark-pattern studies emphasize deceptive
user interfaces, AI-in-marketing research prioritizes efficiency
and personalization, AI ethics work foregrounds fairness and
accountability, and consumer behavior scholarship focuses on
trust and privacy. Few studies, however, explicate how
contemporary AI integrates multiple persuasive tools into
adaptive systems that can generate deceptive content, alter
tactics instantaneously, exploit vulnerabilities, and progressively
undermine consumers’ capacity for genuinely informed decisions.
The emergence of generative and agentic AI during 2024–2026
has exposed limitations in current scholarship (Grewal et al., 2025).
High-profile cases have revealed new modes of commercial
deception that exceed traditional dark patterns. An ABC News
visual investigation uncovered numerous fraudulent online sellers
using AI-generated elderly personas (Katersky et al., 2025),
synthetic video, and fabricated emotional narratives to market
mass-produced goods as artisanal. In another instance, an Airbnb
dispute involved allegations that AI-generated images were used
to fabricate evidence supporting an unwarranted damage claim
(TheGuardian, 2025). The U.S. Federal Trade Commission
pursued action against DoNotPay for overstating AI capabilities
in marketing materials, a practice now described as AI-washing
(FTC, 2025; TheGuardian, 2025). Microsoft Research’s Magentic
Marketplace experiments further demonstrate how autonomous
agents can be manipulated via prompt injection and bespoke
persuasion strategies to produce harmful market behaviors
(MicrosoftResearch, 2025). Regulators have begun to respond.
China enacted Measures for Labeling AI-Generated Synthetic
Content (Cyberspace Administration of China et al., 2025), which
took effect in September 2025 and mandates a dual-labeling
regime—visible markers for users and embedded metadata—to
counter AI-driven misinformation (Sun, 2025). South Korea
implemented a risk-based approach through its Framework Act
on AI (effective January 2026) (Center, 2025), requiring
mandatory labeling for AI-generated advertisements and treating
“virtual humans” used to endorse products as potentially unfair
advertising (Chang, 2025). In Latin America, Brazil is debating
legislation that would impose fines up to BRL 50 million or 2%
of revenue on high-risk AI systems lacking transparency
(VerifyWise, 2025), signalling emerging regulatory attention in
the region. Traditional dark patterns rely on fixed interface tricks:
misleading buttons, hidden unsubscribe options, or preselected
defaults (Brignull, 2010). By contrast, AI-driven systems are
dynamic: they monitor user behavior, continuously update
predictions, generate personalized content, and self-modify
strategies. Manipulation thus increasingly originates not from
static design elements but from covert, adaptive processes
operating continuously in the background (Gasinski et al., 2026).
Accordingly, existing theoretical accounts of dark patterns and
digital persuasion are insufficient to capture these emergent
practices. This study advances the concept of Dark AI Patterns:
adaptive, AI-enabled mechanisms based on machine learning,
generative models, behavioral prediction, and autonomous
systems that influence consumer cognition and conduct. Dark AI
Patterns intentionally reduce transparency, weaken decision
autonomy (Maciaszczyk et al., 2026), and undermine informed
consent, all while preserving the outward appearance of legitimate
personalization and customer service.
Against this backdrop, we propose Algorithmic Allure Theory
(AAT) as an integrative framework. Rather than treating
manipulative techniques as discrete tactics, AAT frames AI-
mediated influence as a dynamic process involving algorithmic
allure, cognitive priming, behavioral steering, and the gradual
displacement of consumer autonomy. Using a multi-case
qualitative design, we analyze seven internationally documented
instances from 2024 to 2026 (see Figure 1 for an illustration of
the AAT framework). Data were drawn from investigative
journalism, regulatory reports, industry publications, and
corporate documents, and analyzed with reflexive thematic
analysis following Braun and Clarke (2022) to identify recurring
mechanisms by which AI reshapes marketing practice and
consumer decision-making.
This study makes four principal contributions: it establishes dark
AI patterns as a distinct category of AI-driven deceptive marketing;
introduces Algorithmic Allure Theory (AAT) to explain how
adaptive AI systems progressively erode consumers’ capacity for

2 Literature review
The integration of artificial intelligence into digital marketing
represents one of the most significant shifts in contemporary
commercial practice. Whereas earlier tools emphasized
automation and basic market segmentation, current systems
powered by machine learning (Rahman et al., 2026), generative
models, and autonomous agents can deliver extreme
personalization, produce real-time content, and exert predictive
influence on behavior (White, 2023). Although these capabilities
can enhance customer experiences, they also blur the line
between beneficial personalization and covert manipulation:
consumers appreciate tailored content but increasingly worry
about the opaque mechanisms that generate it. Research on
algorithmic persuasion shows that AI exploits cognitive biases
through indirect influence strategies. Drawing on dual-
processing theories such as the Elaboration Likelihood Model
(Keng et al., 2025), scholars demonstrate that personalized
messages frequently rely on peripheral cues—social proof,
authority, scarcity—rather than promoting deliberative, rational
processing. Nudge theory has consequently evolved into the
concept of “fine- grainednudges” (Apriliani, 2025), where choice
architectures dynamically adapt to continuous behavioral
feedback, making manipulative interventions harder to detect.
Work on traditional dark patterns has documented many static
deceptive interface designs, including hidden defaults and
misleading UI elements (Mathur et al., 2019). Generative AI,
however, introduces dynamic capabilities—synthetic content
generation, affective targeting, and autonomous self-
optimization—that far exceed the boundaries of fixed interface
tricks (Gray et al., 2018). Actions that previously required
substantial human effort are now scalable and continuously
adaptive in real time (Zou, 2025). Ethics and governance
frameworks—such as the OECD AI Principles (OECD, 2019),
UNESCO recommendations (UNESCO, 2021), and the EU AI
Act—emphasize transparency (EU, 2024), accountability, and
human oversight, and they classify exploitative systems as high-
risk. Yet regulatory emphasis on disclosure alone often proves
inadequate: users may know an AI is present but cannot perceive
how adaptive persuasion continually reshapes their decisions.
Adobe’s Trust Report and consumer studies show declining trust
driven not only by lack of disclosure but also by feelings of
inauthenticity and diminished autonomy (Adobe, 2026).
Taken together, the literature remains fragmented. Marketing
scholarship highlights personalization benefits and efficiency;
persuasion research isolates specific mechanisms; dark-pattern
studies remain anchored to static UI designs; and AI ethics
focuses on governance. There is scant theoretical work
explaining how generative and agentic AI integrate these
elements into interconnected, adaptive persuasive systems. This
study addresses that gap by developing the concepts of Dark AI
Patterns and Algorithmic Allure Theory.

allure theory

mechanisms that deliberately employ machine learning, generative

content production, affective targeting, or autonomous self-

outward appearance of legitimate personalization, customer
assistance, or commercial optimization (Sun et al., 2026). This
definition extends traditional understandings of deceptive digital
design along four principal dimensions (Apriliani, 2025; Gray
et al., 2018; Keng et al., 2025; Mathur et al., 2019). First,
manipulation is no longer confined to interface components; it
encompasses the entire persuasive ecosystem constructed by AI.
Second, persuasion becomes an adaptive, ongoing process rather
than a fixed intervention, continuously evolving in response to
consumer interactions. Third, AI enables a new register of
synthetic authenticity through the creation of fabricated identities,
narratives, and evidentiary artifacts that appear real. Fourth,
optimization processes gain autonomy: systems continuously self-
improve without constant human oversight (see Table 1 for the
conceptual differences between conventional dark patterns and
Dark AI Patterns). This shift indicates that the move from
traditional dark patterns to Dark AI Patterns is not merely a
technical upgrade but a conceptual transformation. AI relocates
deception from isolated design elements to adaptive persuasive
infrastructures capable of continuously reshaping digital
choice environments.
3.2 Theoretical foundations of algorithmic
allure theory
While the Dark AI Patterns concept describes the technical
substrate of adaptive manipulation, it does not fully explain the
psychological process through which such manipulation
incrementally reshapes consumer judgment. Algorithmic Allure
Theory (AAT) fills this gap by offering an integrative framework
that explains how computational capacities are converted into
persuasive power that affects human perception and undermines
informed consent. Unlike conventional persuasion theories that
often treat influence as a one-off message transmitted from
marketer to consumer, AAT conceptualizes persuasion as a
socio-technical, continuous process that emerges from the
ongoing interplay among AI systems, digital environments, and
human cognition. The central construct—Algorithmic Allure—
captures the capacity of intelligent systems to create decision
environments that, over time, appear more attractive, credible,
personalized, and emotionally resonant. Consequently,
consumers increasingly perceive algorithmic recommendations
as trustworthy and aligned with their interests. Crucially, this
felt allure is rarely experienced as external coercion; individuals
tend to interpret algorithmic suggestions as expressions of their
own preferences, thereby lowering their recognition of
underlying commercial influence. AAT integrates elements from
the Elaboration Likelihood Model (Petty and Cacioppo, 1986),
Social Proof Theory (Cialdini, 2021), and Nudge Theory (Thaler
and Sunstein, 2008), alongside insights from computational
behavior analysis. Rather than treating these approaches as
competing accounts, AAT frames them as interdependent
processes operating within a broader adaptive system.
3.3 The Six sequential stages of algorithmic
allure
AAT models algorithmic persuasion as a sequential, six-stage
process that begins with foundational observation and culminates
in algorithmic governance of the commercial environment:
Stage 1: Algorithmic Observation (behavioral-surveillance
profiling)
AI systems continuously collect behavioral, contextual, affective,
and transactional data to build rich predictive profiles of
consumers. This observation stage provides the informational
substrate for all subsequent mechanisms.
Stage 2: Algorithmic Interpretation (inferential psychographic
modeling)
Machine-learning models translate raw data into psychological
inferences—susceptibility to emotional appeals, price sensitivity,
impulsivity, or decision fatigue. Generative AI augments this
interpretation by analyzing natural language, vocal cues, and

Stage 3: Algorithmic Attraction (adaptive micro-environment
generation — the feedback-loop core of AAT)
This stage constitutes the core of AAT. Rather than sending
uniform messages, AI dynamically constructs persuasive micro-
environments tailored to each consumer: personalized
recommendations, narratives aligned with current affective
states, synthetic identities, adaptive pricing, fabricated
testimonials, and context-sensitive visual elements. The high
degree of personalization engenders a powerful illusion of
authenticity; recommendations appear as sincere personal
assistance rather than commercial persuasion, thereby
amplifying algorithmic allure.
Stage 4: Behavioral Steering (reinforcement-driven choice-
architecture modulation)
Once attraction is established, AI subtly steers consumer behavior
without overtly removing choice. Systems reorganize alternatives,
reorder recommendations, reframe emotional contexts, time
message delivery, and vary decision complexity. Unlike static
dark patterns, this steering continually adapts based on each
interaction, as systems learn which tactics elicit stronger
behavioral responses.
Stage 5: Algorithmic Delegation (authority transfer via agentic
reliance)
Over time, decision authority progressively shifts from the
consumer to autonomous AI agents acting on their behalf.
Consumers increasingly rely on agentic recommendations
because they are faster, more convenient, and perceived as more
objective. Repeated reliance reduces independent search and
critical evaluation. However, as the study later demonstrates,
these delegated agents themselves can become vectors for
manipulation.
Stage 6: Algorithmic Governance (infrastructural embedding
in market architecture)
In the final stage, AI transcends influencing isolated choices and
becomes the infrastructural backbone of digital markets:
controlling what consumers see, how products are presented,
when prices change, and how persuasion strategies evolve
through continual learning. Persuasion thus ceases to be merely
a set of discrete messages and becomes embedded within
market architecture.
AAT asserts that the erosion of meaningful informed consent
is not sudden or dramatic but emerges gradually across thousands
of seemingly benign interactions. Each stage builds on the
previous one to produce a cumulative effect that reshapes
consumer autonomy while preserving the appearance of free
choice. From the case material, the study inductively derives
four interrelated theoretical propositions:
P1: As AI capabilities—especially real-time generative content,
behavioral prediction, and autonomous self-optimization—
expand, Dark AI Patterns become more complex and more
concealed. Richer data access and more powerful models shift
deception from simple UI tricks to deep contextualized
personal influence that is difficult for consumers and
regulators to detect.
P2: Higher levels of algorithmic allure increase the likelihood
that consumers will perceive algorithmic recommendations and
environments as trustworthy, authentic, and congruent with
their preferences. Case evidence shows personalization creates
an emotional alignment that renders commercial suggestions
experienced as genuine self-discovery rather than externally
driven persuasion, thereby reducing critical scrutiny.
P3: Continuous behavioral steering within adaptive
environments diminishes independent cognitive evaluation
and trains individuals to delegate judgments to algorithmic
guidance. Repeated exposure conditions consumers to rely on
algorithmic solutions, transferring cognitive burden from
person to system.
P4: Prolonged engagement with adaptive persuasive systems
incrementally degrades the meaningfulness of informed
consent, even where surface transparency and voluntariness
are present. From this perspective, informed consent is not a
one-time legal artefact achieved by disclosure but a fragile,
ongoing cognitive state vulnerable to erosion within
environments engineered to exploit human vulnerabilities—
raising fundamental questions about the future of individual
autonomy in AI-mediated marketplaces.
These propositions are grounded in shared qualitative patterns
across the examined cases rather than statistical testing. They
therefore serve as conceptual foundations for future empirical
work—experimental, survey, or ethnographic—aimed at
unpacking consumer experiences within AI-augmented
environments (see Table 2).
4 Materials and methods
4.1 Study design and strategy
Given the exploratory nature of this research and the relative
novelty of AI-enabled deceptive practices, the study adopts a
qualitative multiple-case design (Yin, 2018). Rather than testing
predetermined hypotheses, the objective is theory building
grounded in empirical phenomena. Instances of AI-mediated
marketing deception are treated as practical manifestations of
broader algorithmic mechanisms rather than as isolated
regulatory failures. Following established approaches to theory
construction from case studies (Eisenhardt, 1989), a multiple-
case strategy was chosen to enable analytical replication across
contexts instead of statistical generalization (Eisenhardt and
Graebner, 2007). Each case functions as an independent context
in which the emergent theoretical framework can be tested and
refined. The unit of analysis is the AI-enabled persuasive
mechanism within each case rather than the firm itself. This
focus facilitates cross-contextual comparison while allowing
identification of higher-order conceptual themes shared across
diverse settings.

4.2 Case selection and data sources
Cases were selected using purposeful theoretical sampling
(Patton, 2015), guided by five criteria: (1) clear evidence that AI
(generative models, machine learning, large language models, or
autonomous agents) played a central role in persuasive
processes; (2) the case involved consumer-facing commercial
communication; (3) robust public documentation from
regulatory agencies, investigative journalism, or institutional
reports was available; (4) the case had observable effects on
consumer judgments, behavior, trust, or decision outcomes; (5)
the incident occurred between January 2024 and June 2026,
capturing the latest wave of generative AI marketing applications.
Applying these criteria yielded seven internationally
documented cases spanning e-commerce, digital platforms,
legal-tech applications, digital advertising, autonomous-agent
experiments, and AI-managed retail platforms (see Table 3).
This sampling strategy achieved extensive contextual variation
while maintaining analytical focus on conceptually comparable
phenomena. To strengthen credibility through triangulation
(Lincoln and Guba, 1985), data were collected from multiple
public sources: official regulatory documents and filings,
government reports, corporate disclosures, investigative
journalism, industry analyses, and peer-reviewed academic
literature. For example, the DoNotPay case was primarily
informed by U.S. Federal Trade Commission records (FTC,
2025), whereas the AI-generated artisan store case relied on a
detailed ABC News visual investigation supplemented by related
reporting and sector commentary (Katersky et al., 2025).
4.3 Data analysis and trustworthiness
Data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis following
Braun and Clarke (2022). Analysis proceeded through six iterative
phases: immersion in the material, initial coding of persuasive
mechanisms, development of preliminary themes (Braun and
Clarke, 2022), systematic review and refinement of themes
across cases, definition and naming of higher-order themes, and
integration of themes into the emergent theoretical framework.
Coding employed an abductive logic that combined deductive
sensitivity to theoretically relevant constructs with inductive

5 Results: a taxonomy of dark AI
patterns
Comparative analysis across the seven cases reveals that
contemporary commercial manipulation powered by AI far
exceeds the static, interface-based deceptions emphasized in
prior work (Gray et al., 2018; Mathur et al., 2019). AI no longer
merely reshapes digital interfaces; it reconstructs identities,
evidence, social signals, technical authority, authenticity, and
even autonomous decision processes. Despite differences in
sector, technology, and geography, the cases converge on a set
of adaptive persuasive strategies that systematically reshape
consumer judgments while diminishing users’ recognition of
commercial influence. This convergence suggests these instances
are not isolated anomalies but evidence of an emergent class of
algorithmic persuasion systems. Using reflexive thematic
analysis, seven high-level themes emerged. The themes are not
mutually exclusive: several cases combined multiple mechanisms
simultaneously, indicating future AI-enabled manipulation will
be increasingly hybridized and context-dependent.
5.1 Synthetic identity manipulation
The first theme concerns the algorithmic fabrication of
commercial identities designed to cultivate trust and emotional
connection. Synthetic Identity Manipulation denotes the use of
generative AI to create or alter fictional commercial personas
that possess perceived emotional credibility, apparent
authenticity, and interpersonal trust to influence purchasing
choices (Zhou et al., 2026). Unlike conventional identity
impersonation in cybercrime, these personas do not necessarily
replicate real individuals; rather, generative models construct
wholly fabricated characters that are psychologically persuasive
through familiar narrative tropes, emotionally charged language,
and culturally congruent imagery. The clearest illustration
appears in investigative reports about fraudulent e-stores that
deployed AI-generated personas, synthetic videos, and fabricated
emotional backstories to market mass-produced goods as
artisanal (ABC News and related investigations, 2024–2025).
These operations relied on repeated names and narratives,
engineered urgency and clearance events, AI-generated video
content, and limited opportunities for independent verification.
Comparative analysis identified four recurring elements:
emotionally laden hardship narratives, manufactured scarcity
cues (Cialdini, 2021), intentionally imperfect visual markers to
signal authenticity, and constrained verification channels.
Within Algorithmic Allure Theory (AAT), Synthetic Identity
Manipulation bridges the Algorithmic Interpretation and
Algorithmic Attraction stages: behavioral data identifies the
most resonant emotional narratives, and generative models
deploy those narratives to assemble convincing commercial
identities.
5.2 Synthetic social proof
The second theme addresses the falsification of perceived
collective endorsement. Social proof is a longstanding cognitive
heuristic used under uncertainty (Cialdini, 2021); generative AI
undermines the assumption that online reviews and user-
generated content reflect genuine experience. Synthetic Social
Proof refers to the algorithmic generation, amplification, or
coordination of consumer opinions, reviews, recommendations,
or behavioral signals to create the impression of broad market
acceptance. The Rytr/Operation AI Comply case (FTC, 2025) is
salient: services marketed the capacity to auto-generate
persuasive customer reviews for distribution across platforms.
Regulators concluded these practices enable production of
misleading testimonials that can falsely signal product popularity
and quality. Compared with human-authored fake reviews, AI-
generated reviews scale more easily, align with context, vary
linguistically, and can be tailored to different consumer
segments. This theme frequently co-occurs with Synthetic
Identity Manipulation and AI Washing, indicating these
mechanisms often operate as an integrated persuasive ecosystem.
5.3 Synthetic evidence manipulation
The third theme shifts from pre-purchase influence to post-
transaction reality construction. Synthetic Evidence
Manipulation denotes creating, altering, or leveraging AI-
generated visual, textual, audio, or multimodal artifacts to shape
judgments about contractual claims, liability, or consumer
responsibility. The Manhattan Airbnb dispute (TheGuardian,
2025) exemplifies this pattern: a host submitted images
purportedly evidencing severe property damage to support a
large claim, but investigative indicators suggested the images
were AI-generated or manipulated, prompting the platform to
retract the claim and remediate the guest. This case shows how
photographs—long considered high-trust evidence (Messaris,
1997)—can be algorithmically falsified, creating an information

and consumers lacking reliable detection tools.
5.4 AI washing
The fourth theme concerns overstated AI capabilities used for
marketing persuasion. AI Washing occurs when organizations
exaggerate or misrepresent the functions and reliability of
purported AI systems to bolster credibility without sufficient
technical basis. Operation AI Comply targeted such claims, notably
DoNotPay, which was marketed as an “AI lawyer” despite
regulatory findings that its legal capabilities were not consistently
reliable (FTC, 2025). AI Washing leverages a technical authority
bias: people tend to perceive AI outputs as more objective or
trustworthy than human judgments (Logg et al., 2019), and
exaggeration of capabilities exploits that bias to promote adoption
or premium pricing without commensurate performance.
5.5 Agentic AI manipulation
The fifth theme marks a fundamental shift: persuasion aimed
not only at humans but at autonomous AI agents. Agentic AI
Manipulation involves designing signals, reputational cues, or
prompt payloads intended to influence independent AI agents
that make or recommend purchase decisions on behalf of users.
Academic and industry multi-agent market simulations
demonstrate that agentic systems can be swayed through fake
authorities, manipulated reputation signals, and prompt
injection techniques—producing vulnerabilities like algorithmic
satisficing and undue deference to perceived authority. These
weaknesses challenge consumer protection frameworks that
presume human decision-makers and reveal novel vectors for
manipulation in agentic marketplaces.
5.6 Synthetic authenticity
The sixth theme highlights a paradox of AI-generated content:
high technical quality does not guarantee persuasive legitimacy if
audiences detect a lack of genuine human intent. Synthetic
Authenticity refers to AI-produced content that is technically
polished but judged by audiences as emotionally hollow or
inauthentic (Zhou et al., 2026). The McDonald’s Netherlands AI
Holiday ad case (Adformatie, 2025) illustrates this dynamic: the
campaign drew criticism not for AI use per se but for perceived
emotional emptiness—viewers labeled the content “robotic” or
“soulless”. Adobe’s Trust Report (2025) supports this pattern,
showing many consumers lose trust in brands that deploy AI
content without transparent signaling or respect for
human authenticity.
5.7 Autonomous commercial manipulation
The seventh and most advanced theme describes systems that
progressively remove meaningful human oversight. Autonomous
Commercial Manipulation denotes AI systems that independently
manage commercial decisions—catalog descriptions, customer
interactions, dynamic pricing, and operations—with minimal
human supervision, generating risks of amplified or emergent
manipulative behaviors. Documented AI-managed e-commerce
platforms illustrate that full delegation can yield short-term
efficiency gains but also produce exaggerated claims, inconsistent
policies, or price volatility perceived as unfair, complicating
accountability attribution.
5.8 Comparative synthesis of themes
Viewed together, the seven themes constitute interlocking
components of a computationally integrated architecture that
incrementally reshapes consumer autonomy. Each pattern
undermines a distinct dimension of trust—interpersonal trust,
collective endorsement, evidentiary trust, technological authority,
agentic reliability, emotional authenticity, and institutional
accountability—and their sequential interplay reflects an evolving
persuasive arc that moves from drawing attention to reconstituting
perceptual reality itself. Importantly, the erosion of meaningful
informed consent often arises not from straightforward
informational deficits but from continuous optimization of the
informational and persuasive environment by adaptive AI systems.
This empirical architecture underpins the six-stage Algorithmic
Allure Theory and sets the stage for the ensuing theoretical discussion.
6 Discussion
6.1 Reframing digital persuasion in the
generative AI era
The findings indicate that digital marketing has entered a new
evolutionary phase. AI is no longer merely a supporting tool; it is
the infrastructure through which persuasion is designed, executed,
optimized, and governed. Generative AI shifts emphasis away
from crafting individual persuasive messages toward
constructing the entire informational environment that
surrounds consumers. This shift requires scholars to move
analytical focus from discrete persuasive content to the
architecture of persuasion itself.
6.2 Extending existing persuasion theories
The results corroborate classical persuasion accounts but also
extend them. The Elaboration Likelihood Model explains how
Dark AI Patterns exploit peripheral routes—authority, scarcity,
and social proof—yet Algorithmic Allure Theory (AAT) adds
that AI does more than deploy these cues: it creates the context
that confers those cues their meaning and tailors that context to
each person. Social proof theory faces a new challenge in the
generative AI ability to manufacture the appearance of
consensus, while nudge theory must accommodate choice
architectures that evolve continuously in real time rather than
remain relatively static (see Table 4).

mediated persuasion from the discrete, message-level events these
theories were built to explain: a closed observation–action
feedback loop in which the outcome of one persuasive attempt
is fed back into the system and used to generate the next

clear boundary condition: AAT’s dynamics should dominate
over classical, message-level persuasion routes only where a
system has (i) sustained, individual-level behavioural data, (ii)
generative capability to vary content in near real time, and (iii)
the autonomy to act on inferred outcomes without a new
human-authored message each time. Where any of these
conditions is absent — for example, a static, one-off
advertisement with no behavioural feedback channel —
persuasion should follow classical ELM dynamics instead. This
yields a falsifiable, AAT-specific prediction (Proposition P2,
Table 2): perceived authenticity of an algorithmic
recommendation should rise with the frequency of adaptive re-
targeting even when message content is held constant, a pattern
that social proof theory (which locates authenticity in the
content of peer signals) and ELM (which locates attitude change
in argument quality or peripheral cues, not re-targeting
frequency) do not independently predict.
6.3 Algorithmic allure as an integrative
theoretical framework
AAT functions as a holistic integrative frame that unites
human cognition, machine learning, autonomous agents,
and market governance in a single explana tory account of
AI-augmented persuasion. Rather than isolating
individual techniques, AAT emphasizes the coherence of
the six algorithmic stages and expands the unit of analysis
from single decisions to algorithmically mediated
commercia l systems.
6.4 Informed consent as a fragile construct
in the face of algorithms
One of the study’s central contributions is
reconce ptualizing informed consent. Traditional policy
assumes disclosure and informa tion provision suffice to
secure consent, yet the cases show consumers may
recognize AI presence while still remaining embedded in
systems engineere d to exploit their vulnerabi lities. Hence
informed consent should be treated as a dynamic, ongoing
cognitive state continually reshaped by adaptive systems—
not a one-time legal formality. Sustainable, ethical
marketing will increasingly depend on balancing
computational effective ness with genuine human presence
and respect for consumer autonomy . Consequently, the
normativ e question shifts from whether AI can persuade
more effective ly to under what conditions AI-enabled
persuasion remains ethically legitimate.
This fragility, however, does not imply consumers lack all
capacity for resistance; evidence points to emergent forms of
public pushback and discernment.

6.5 Consumer resistance as market self-
correction
Consumers are not purely passive recipients of Dark AI
Patterns; they are developing adaptive resistance strategies that
blunt manipulative effects. The phenomenon of “synthetic
authenticity” illustrates this dynamic: McDonald’s Netherlands’
AI-generated holiday ad provoked widespread criticism as
emotionally hollow (Katerskyet al., 2025), signaling growing
public sensitivity to the difference between technical polish and
genuine human intent (TheGuardian, 2025). The cultural uptake
of the term “AI slop” as Merriam-Webster’s 2025 Word of the
Year exemplifies increasing skepticism toward low-quality AI
output (Merriam-Webster, 2025).
Quantitative indicators align with this trend: a Billion Dollar
Boy study found consumer preference for AI-generated creator
content dropped from 60% in 2023 to 26% in 2026 (Billion-
Dollar-Boy, 2025), and Adobe’s Trust Report shows sizable
declines in brand trust when AI content is used without
disclosure (Adobe, 2026). Such “passive resistance”—
withdrawing trust and favoring brands that foreground human
authenticity—constitutes a natural constraint on more blatant
forms of algorithmic manipulation.
6.6 Toward ethical AI: transparency,
empowerment, and human-centered
algorithmic design
Despite rising concerns, AI also offers opportunities to
enhance transparency and empower consumers when governed
by ethical principles (Radanliev, 2025). Explainable AI,
transparent recommender mechanisms, and privacy-respectful
personalization can improve public understanding of algorithmic
decisions and give consumers greater control over their data and
choice (Ibitoye et al., 2025). Ethical design practices that
foreground transparency, bias mitigation, accountability, and
human oversight can support consumer autonomy rather than
degrade it (Fukukawa and Trivedi, 2025).
In sum, the core challenge for AI-enabled marketing is not the
persuasive power of algorithms per se but the governance contexts
in which that power is exercised. Moving from algorithmic
manipulation to ethical persuasion requires reframing
transparency as more than disclosure—toward meaningful
consumer comprehension and agency—and designing AI
systems that preserve dignity, autonomy, and trust.
7 Contributions and implications
7.1 Theoretical contributions
This study delivers five principal theoretical contributions.
First, it conceptualizes Dark AI Patterns as a distinct theoretical
construct that extends the traditional dark-pattern literature
(Mathur et al., 2019) from static interface tricks to adaptive, AI-
generated persuasive ecosystems. Second, the study develops
Algorithmic Allure Theory (AAT), a six-stage model that
synthesizes insights from technology acceptance frameworks, the
Elaboration Likelihood Model, Social Proof theory, Nudge
theory, and Persuasion Knowledge theory into an integrated
systems-level account. AAT reframes persuasion as a continuous
computational process rather than a series of discrete behavioral
interventions. Third, the paper reconceptualizes informed
consent as a dynamic cognitive state vulnerable to gradual
erosion through continuous algorithmic optimization, rather
than as a one-time legal formality achievable by disclosure
alone. This repositioning creates a conceptual bridge between
marketing ethics, behavioral economics, and AI governance.
Fourth, the study proposes a systematic taxonomy of seven AI-
enabled manipulative mechanisms organized by persuasive logic
rather than by the underlying technical stack, which enhances
theoretical durability as technologies evolve. Fifth, the research
advances a contextually grounded, dynamic understanding of
consumer vulnerability: vulnerability emerges from an
interaction between individual characteristics and the adaptive
optimization capacity of AI systems, not solely from fixed
personal traits. Collectively, these contributions tighten
conceptual links between marketing research and AI governance
literatures that have historically developed in relative isolation.
By shifting the unit of analysis from individual persuasive acts
to algorithmically mediated market systems, the study offers a
new vocabulary and analytical lens for scholars investigating AI-
mediated influence.
7.2 Practical and policy implications
For marketing practitioners, the findings caution that
technical sophistication alone does not guarantee sustainable
persuasive success or durable brand trust. Firms should balance
traditional performance metrics with trust-related indicators—
perceived authenticity, transparency, and relationship quality—
and adopt internal AI-marketing ethics policies. Recommended
operational practices include mandatory human review for high-
impact campaigns, verification protocols for AI-generated
content, explicit labeling of synthetic media, and periodic audits
of personalization systems.
For platform operators and AI developers, the results endorse
Ethical-by-Design approaches. Technical priorities include
detection mechanisms for synthetic identities, AI-generated
reviews, and fabricated evidence; defenses against prompt
injection and related attacks on autonomous shopping agents;
and the deployment of explainable AI interfaces that explicate
why a recommendation or decision was produced. Such
measures can reduce deception vectors and strengthen consumer
comprehension.
Policymakers should recognize that existing consumer-
protection regimes—largely oriented toward false advertising
and unfair contract terms—are insufficient for algorithmically
constructed informational environments. Priority regulatory
actions include:
• Standardized disclosure requirements for AI-generated
commercial content.
• Mandatory human oversight for high-impact
autonomous systems.

7.3 Limitations and directions for future
research
This research faces several limitations. The multiple-case
qualitative design yields rich theoretical depth but does not
permit immediate statistical generalization; the prevalence of
Dark AI Patterns in the broader marketing ecosystem therefore
remains an empirical question for large-scale quantitative work.
The analysis relied primarily on documentary sources—
regulatory filings, investigative journalism, and institutional
reports—and despite systematic triangulation (Lincoln and
Guba, 1985), source-related biases may persist. Finally, rapid
advances in generative and agentic AI mean the specific cases
analyzed will evolve even as the underlying mechanisms
remain relevant.
Future research should (1) estimate the prevalence of Dark AI
Patterns via surveys and experiments; (2) measure consumer
awareness and resistance to algorithmic persuasion; (3)
empirically test the propositions of Algorithmic Allure Theory
across sectors and cultures; and (4) evaluate the effectiveness of
regulatory and design interventions aimed at preserving
meaningful informed consent in increasingly autonomous
digital markets.
8 Conclusion
This study set out to understand how artificial intelligence
transforms digital persuasion into adaptive systems capable of
gradually undermining consumers’ meaningful informed
consent. Through a qualitativ e multiple-case analysis of
seven internationally documented incidents, the research
identified seven recurri ng mechanisms—Synthetic Identity
Manipulation, Synthetic Social Proof, Synthetic Evidence
Manipulation, AI Washing, Agentic AI Manipulat ion,
Synthetic Authenticity, and Autonomous Commercial
Manipulation—tha t together form a taxonomy the study
terms Dark AI Patterns. This taxonomy extends prior
literature on interface-based deception into the era of
generative and agentic AI. Crucially , these pattern s do not
operate in isolation; they are interdependent components of
a broader computational architecture that incrementally
reshapes consumer autonomy and the very notion of
consent. Persuasion is no longer confined to discrete
marketing messages or UI designs; it is embedded within
algorithmic environ ments that continuously observe users,
interpret their behavior, predict preferences, and
steer decisions.
To explain this shift, the study introduced Algorithmic
Allure Theory (AAT). The theory describes how AI-media ted
persuasion unfolds across six sequential stages: Algorithmic
Observa tion, Algorithmic Interpreta tion, Algorithmic
Attraction, Behavior al Steering, Algorithmic Delegation, and
Algorithmic Governance. The study also reframes informed
consent as a dynamic cognitive process rather than a static
legal disclosur e. Across all seven Dark AI Patterns, the
erosion of consent rarely stemmed from a total lack of
informa tion. Instead, it resulted from the continuous
algorithmic optimiza tion of the surrounding informa tional
environ ment. Even seemingly transparent interactions can
compromis e consumer autonomy when AI systems
strategically shape perception, trust, and behavior. These
findings have important implications for both scholarship and
practice. For researchers, the proposed taxonomy and
theoretical framework offer a solid foundat ion for future
studies on AI-enabled persuasion, consumer resilience, digital
ethics, and algorithmic governance. For practitioners and
policymakers , the results underscore the need to embed
transparency , accountability , explainability , and meaningful
human oversight into AI-driven marketing systems.
Responsible innovation should be judged not only by
technological power or commercial results, but by how well
AI supports—ra ther than undermines—consumer autonomy.
Organizations that successfully balance algorithmic
intelligence with authentic human judgment are likely to
build stronger long-term trust and sustainable competitiv e
advantage. In conclusion, the rise of Dark AI Patterns marks
a transition from the age of digital persuasion to the age of
algorithmic persuasion. Understanding and governing this
shift is essential to ensure that artificial intelligence serves as
a tool for responsible innovation rather than an invisible
architecture of commerci al manipulation. The future of digital
marketing will depend less on raw technological capability
and more on society’s ability to guide that capability with
transparency , accountability, and respect for human agency.
