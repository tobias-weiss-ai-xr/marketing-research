 Marketing has shifted from transactional promotion to value -oriented engagement, where
sustainability and environmental responsibility increasingly influence consumer decision-making;
Artificial Intelligence (AI)-driven sustainable marketing leverages informatics, data intelligence,
and ethical analytics to coordinate marketing approaches with green consumer expectations and
long-term engagement. However, the effectiveness of AI -driven sustainable marketing is
constrained by context -specific consumer percep tions and variations in ethical information
governance practices. To observe how AI -driven sustainable marketing mechanisms influence
green consumer engagement through informatics -based insights, eco-oriented personalization,
green information transparency, and responsible data utilization. The data were collected using a
structured questionnaire from environmentally conscious consumers; out of 587 responses, 465
valid samples were gathered. The conceptual framework includes AI -enabled sustainability cues,
green information transparency, ethical data governance, and perceived environmental value as
predictors of green consumer engagement, analyzed using reliability analysis, exploratory factor
analysis (EFA), multiple regression, and structural relationship modelling via IBM SPSS and
AMOS. The outcomes show that all AI -driven sustainable marketing factors, perceived
environmental value, and green trust significantly influence green consumer engagement, with
effects ranging from β = 0.203 –0.319, t = 2.54–3.13, p < 0.011.AI-driven sustainable marketing,
supported by informatics and ethical analytics, significantly enhances green consumer engagement
and provides organizations with a strategic approach to integrate environmental responsibility with
competitive marketing performance.

1. Introduction
The marketing process has evolved towards value -based promotion instead of transactional promotion, whereby a focus on
long-term relations, trust, and mutual values between consumers and brands is emphasized (Sulaiman et al., 2024). Purpose
and authenticity are becoming popular because contemporary marketing is influenced by new demands that organizations are
capable of creating value for society and a meaningful consumer experience (Alam et al., 2024a). Academic perspectives
highlight marketing as a tactic al procedure of value co -creation, where consumer action is shaped by morally ethical
positioning, disclosure, and relationship direction (Pascucci et al., 2023). The notion of artificial intelligence (AI) has been
adopted as an agent of change in sustainable marketing approaches, enabling intelligent information to be applied to facilitate
brand communication that is both environmentally aware and tailored to fit engagement techniques (Salhab et al., 2025). The
topicality of environmental messages can be strengthened with the help of AI-powered sustainable marketing, as the content
is adjusted to consumer values to make green brand messages more credible and resonant (Alam et al., 2024b). To maintain
consumer trust in socially responsible company brands, AI  can preserve sustainability -focused marketing by assisting in
maintaining trust through ethical analytics and open communication (Kanellopoulou et al., 2025).

AI-driven analytics used to expose consumer insights via massive online reviews were investigated (Islam et al., 2024). These
analytics apply sentiment analysis, predictive modelling, and recommendation strategies to display patterns in satisfaction
and engagement over time, noting limitations in terms of data volume, geographic coverage, and computational capability
(Baruno & Indrasari, 2025; More & Unnikrishnan, 2024). AI-driven analytics revolutionize product marketing by improving
better segmentation, p ersonalization, and engagement. They determine superior behavioral and predictive division with a
greater number of conversions, but moral issues associated with privacy and bias require open and responsible AI approaches.
Previous research has significantly depended on large -scale online reviews, which restrict representativeness, geographic
coverage, and ethical disclosure, and focus on segmentation and conversion metrics. The current research mitigates these
shortcomings by proposing ethical AI-based sustainable marketing focused on transparency, responsible analytics, and value-
based green consumer interaction. This research aims to study the impact of AI -driven sustainable marketing on green
consumer engagement through t he application of ethical analytics, green -focused personalization, green information
transparency, informatics -based insights, and the increasing levels of trust, advocacy, and long- term environmentally
responsible brand interactions.

The organization of this research is as follows: the background and introduction are found in Section 1. Section 2 discusses

2. Related Works

This section summarizes previous studies by presenting a description of goals, procedures, main conclusions, and limitations
applicable to AI-based sustainable marketing research situations.  Acatrinei et al. (2025) conducted a quantitative survey of
501 users of social media to investigate the importance of AI in online marketing and promoting sustainable consumer
behavior. The outcomes indicated that social norms, transparency, perceived bene fits, and satisfaction increased trust and
acceptance, whilst risks and negative emotions reduced them, with weaknesses being a cross-sectional design and self-report
measurements. Bashynska (2023) discussed AI in combination with personalized advertising to transform consumer
interactions and create a sustainable lifestyle focused on adhering to societal values through mixed methods and case studies,
proving that AI can be implemented to promote greener behaviors. The constraints pertained to shifting consumer
preferences, ethical data concerns, inclusivity, and circular economy applications.

Magableh et al. (2024) explored how marketing AI is indirectly related to sustainable financial performance through customer
engagement. They showed that AI adoption enhances performance with the help of such mediators but did not show any
direct relations hip with long -term financial performance, accentuating technology and customer concerns for sustainable
growth. Kudapa (2024) investigated AI -enhanced data science that optimizes user involvement in online marketing
campaigns using predictive analytics, causal inference, and data-driven systems based on 312 academic and industry sources.
The findings showed that AI techniques outperform traditional ones in engagement, offering a holistic approach to the theory
and practice of dynamic digital marketing. Majo r threats were the use of secondary data and generalization limited to the
digital marketing environment.  Allil (2024) introduced a pedagogical model of applying AI -based analytics to marketing
education, focusing on practice, learning, and utilizing tools  such as predictive analytics. The model also resolves gaps in
curricula, ethical factors, and resource issues to encourage cross-disciplinary work and constant renewal to equip students for
AI-enhanced marketing jobs. Ratta et al. (2024) introduced the variations between human-created and AI-created advertising
content in terms of consumer engagement and purchasing behavior. Using qualitative interviews of marketers and AI
professionals, they noted that AI -generated ads are more creative, engaging, and eff ective in facilitating sales, brand
communication, and competitive advantage, as well as providing guidance on future brand strategies.

Jankovic and Curovic (2023) incorporated AI into sustainable business models strategically with a view to enhancing data
governance and digital interaction. Manual reporting and fragmented databases were the basis of the conventional model of
sustainability management, which was restricted in responsiveness. Structural modeling, based on firm -level sustainability
data (n=300), showed that engagement is significantly affected (β≈0.28, p<0.01). Nevertheless, implementation complexity
and data confidentiality decreased scalability across industries. Chatterjee et al. (2021) used an integrated TAM- TOE model
to explain the adoption of AI in manufacturing companies. Classical production systems relied on human observations and
solitary automation devices. Results of a survey of 298 firms indicated that perceived usefulness played a major role in
adoption intention (β≈0.41, p<0.001), and organizational readiness also played a role (β≈0.33). However, long- term
sustainability alignment was limited by high investment c osts and technological uncertainty.  Ridwan et al. (2024) used
financial analytics based on AI to assess environmental sustainability performance in the United States. Previous economic
models were not merged in a predictive manner with environmental terms. Regression and time -series estimates revealed
that AI investment has a significantly positive effect on green GDP indicators (β≈0.35, p<0.05), and carbon-efficiency ratios
improved in terms of figures. However, technological inequality and automated power  use posed a trade -off for
environmental and socioeconomic status. Gul et al. (2024) obtained sustainable consumption insights by employing
participatory visual methodologies and AI analytics. Traditional consumer research used surveys and behavioral -based
interviews with limited granularity. Significant clustering patterns in eco -preferences appeared in image- based datasets

3. Research Gap

Previous research was limited to cross -sectional survey designs and self -reported behavioral intentions, limiting the ability
to infer causality and support over time, context -specific case-based evidence, and ambiguous ethical considerations about
AI personalization practices, indirectly operationalized linkages of financial performance, and analytics based on secondary
data constrained to online settings (Acatrinei et al., 2025; Bashynska, 2023; Kudapa, 2024; Magableh et al., 2024).
Educational and qualitative investigations had no empirical verification at the consumer level (Allil, 2024; Ratta et al., 2024).
No individual green engagement mechanism is contained in organizational and macro- level sustainability analyses
(Chatterjee et al., 2021; Gul et al., 2024; Jankovic & Curovic, 2023; Ridwan et al., 2024; Zaman et al., 2024). Studies based
on perception and segmentation were geographically limited and did not adequately incorporate the construct of ethical data
governance (Alam et al., 2024c; Bhatnagar & Sharma, 2024). Personalization research focused on engagement and
satisfaction but did not develop transparency, the establishment of trust, or a continuation of behavioral sustainability
(Alghaswyneh, 2025; Owusu- Mensah et al., 2025; Popa & Chenic, 2025; Sipos, 2025). Sector -specific or hybrid models
limited the generalizability between consumer markets (Acatrinei et al., 2025; Alam, 2023; Chao et al., 2022; Nadeem, 2025;
Panigrahi et al., 2023; Ramesha et al., 2025; Rana et al., 2025; Soomro et al., 202 5). The gaps identified in the research are
filled as the authors use primary data provided by environmentally conscious consumers and combine AI-based sustainability
cues with green information transparency, ethical data governance, perceived environmental value, and green trust into a
single empirical approach. This facilitates the progress of knowledge by means of extensive multivariate evaluation to explain
programmed routes that affect green consumer engagement.

5. Materials and methods

The design is used to consider the collision of AI -based sustainable advertising processes on the engagement of green
consumers. The design allows conducting systematically assessed relationships between AI -enabled sustainability cues,
green information transparency, ethical data governance, perceived environmental value, and consumer engagement results.
The survey tool is chosen to understand consumer perceptions and behavioral reactions in actual marketing conditions where
AI-enhanced sustainability pract ices are becoming embedded. Respondents were selected using a purposive sampling
strategy targeting environmentally conscious consumers.  Fig.  2 shows the methodological framework for AI -driven
sustainable marketing analysis.

5.1 Target Population and Sampling Strategy

The target group is the segment of consumers who are environmentally conscious and know the sustainability practices and
AI-powered digital marketing. Contextual relevance is achieved by purposive sampling. Online distribution resulted in 587
responses, of which 465 were valid samples collected after screening, which is adequate enough to conduct multivariate and
structural analysis.

5.2 Instrument Development and Measurement Scales

A structured questionnaire using 5-point Likert-scale statements (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree) was residential
to measure key constructs influencing Green purchaser Engagement in the context of AI -driven sustainable marketing. All
constructs were operationalized using reflective indicators adapted and contextualized from prior sustainability, AI
marketing, and green consumer behavior literature. The research questionnaires are given below:

AI-Enabled Sustainability Communication (AISC)

• AISC1: AI-driven marketing messages clearly communicate a brand’s environmental responsibility.
• AISC2: AI-based sustainability communication helps me understand a company’s green initiatives.
• AISC3: AI-enabled platforms effectively highlight a brand’s commitment to sustainability.
• AISC4: Sustainability messages delivered through AI feel relevant and timely.

Green Information Transparency (GIT)

• GIT1: Companies provide clear and accessible information about their environmental practices.
• GIT2: Green claims communicated through digital platforms are easy to verify.
• GIT3: Transparency in sustainability information increases my confidence in green brands.
• GIT4: AI systems help present honest and detailed environmental information.

Eco-Oriented Personalization (EOP)

• EOP1: AI-based personalization aligns product or service recommendations with my environmental preferences.
• EOP2: Personalized green content makes sustainability more meaningful to me.
• EOP3: Eco-oriented personalization increases my interest in environmentally responsible brands.
• EOP4: AI-driven recommendations encourage me to adopt greener consumption choices.

Ethical Data Governance (EDG)

• EDG1: I believe companies use my data responsibly when promoting sustainable products.
• EDG2: Ethical handling of consumer data increases my acceptance of AI-driven marketing.

• EDG3: Transparency in data usage strengthens my perception of a company’s sustainability ethics.
• EDG4: Responsible data governance enhances my trust in AI-enabled green marketing.

Perceived Environmental Value (PEV)

• PEV1: AI-driven sustainable marketing enhances the perceived environmental value of products or services.
• PEV2: I believe green brands offer meaningful environmental benefits.
• PEV3: Sustainability-focused marketing increases the value I associate with a brand.
• PEV4: Environmental benefits influence my evaluation of AI-promoted offerings.

Green Trust (GT)

• GT1: I trust brands that use AI to communicate sustainability initiatives.
• GT2: AI-enabled green marketing appears credible and reliable.
• GT3: Ethical and transparent AI practices increase my trust in green brands.
• GT4: I believe sustainability claims communicated through AI are genuine.

Green Consumer Engagement (GCE)

• GCE1: I actively engage with brands that promote sustainability through AI -driven platforms.
• GCE2: I am willing to interact, share, or provide feedback on AI-enabled green marketing content.
• GCE3: AI-driven sustainability initiatives motivate me to support green brands.
• GCE4: I am likely to advocate for brands that effectively use AI to promote environmental responsibility.

5.3 Research Variables

This section describes major constructs used in the analysis, including the AI -driven sustainability behavior, consumer
attitudes towards this behavior, trust development, and the ensuing green engagement behavior.
AISC: It refers to the use of AI to offer plausible and transparent sustainability messaging that would stimulate perceived
environmental value and enhance green consumer response.
GIT: It represents the transparent, right, and available disclosure of environmental statements that make use of computer -
based portals to empower intentional evaluation and consumer confidence and interest.
EOP: It is AI -based dissimilarity of marketing content that adheres to the environmental inclinations of consumers with
greater relevance, green trust, and ongoing engagement.
EDG: It states the responsible use of consumer information in AI -based marketing, focusing on privacy protection,
transparency, and fairness towards the creation of a green trust and engagement.
PEV: It explains how consumers consider the positive effects AI -enhanced sustainable marketing practices have on the
environment, which affects trust, attitude, and engagement intentions.
GT: It discusses the trust that consumers place in the environmental stunts and ethical activities of a brand based on the
transparent communication and careful AI sustainability operations.
GCE: It is defined as the active involvement, promotion, and engagement of consumers with the sustainable activities of the
brands due to their perceived environmental worthiness, trust, and AI-powered tailored marketing.

5.5 Statistical analysis

The analytical procedure highlights the systematic use of statistical and modeling tools to evaluate AI -driven sustainable
marketing constructs and their influence on green consumer engagement. Data processing and preliminary screening were
performed using IBM SPSS (version 26) and AMOS to ensure data accuracy, normality, and reliability. Descriptive statistics
were computed in SPSS to summarize perception patterns across AISC, GIT, EOP, EDG, PEV, GT, and GCE constructs.

Reliability analysis using Composite Reliability and Cronbach’s Alpha was conducted in SPSS and AMOS (version 24) to
confirm internal consistency and construct validity. EFA was performed in SPSS to validate the  latent factor structure in the
AMOS assessed measurement model fitness.

Inferential testing included Multiple Regression and One -Way ANOVA in SPSS to examine predictive relationships and
group differences. Pearson’s Correlation analysis measured linear associations among constructs. Finally, SEM using AMOS
evaluated direct and  mediated effects, providing a comprehensive AI –sustainability engagement framework supported by
robust multivariate tools.

6. Discussion

The analysis has investigated the impact of AI -based sustainable marketing processes on the engagement of the green
consumer with a sense of perceived environmental value and green trust using a holistic empirical framework. Previous
studies indicate a number of limitations. The quantitative social-media-based analyses were based on cross-sectional and self-
reported measures, which restricted the causal inference and depth of behavior ( Baruno & Indrasari, 2025). Mixed-method
and case-based research had difficulties regarding the changing preference of consumers, ethical data issues, inclusivity, and
alignment with the circular economy (Acatrinei et al., 2025). SEM-based AI adoption outcomes focused on indirect impacts
of performance but not on direct results ( Bashynska, 2023) in terms of environmental engagement. The analyses produced
by conceptual and secondary -source research confined the generalizability outside the digital marketing environments
(Magableh et al., 2024). The current research overcomes these shortcomings by adopting validated scales of measurement,
reliability, validity tests, regression, EFA, and SEM into an overall framework that explicitly  reflects ethical governance of
data, transparency, personalization, environmental values as perceived, trust, and green consumer engagement.  The results
reveal that using AI as a driver of sustainability communication, transparency, personalization, and ethical data governance
enhances perceived environmental value and green trust, which together work to enhance green consumer engagement,
confirming the direct and mediating relationships in the suggested framework. The AI -based marketing can increase the
interest of green consumers by ranking ethical data collection, sustainable messaging, and value -based personalization. The
practices foster trusts, enhance the perceived environmental value, and promote sustainable, environmentally responsible
consumer behavior.

7. Conclusion

The impact of AI -based sustainable marketing and its effects on green consumer engagement were examined effectively
through the prism of sustainability communication, transparency, personalization, and ethical data. A systematic
questionnaire was utilized to obtain primary data responses by obtaining 465 usable samples through a structured
questionnaire to environmentally conscious consumers. AI -enabled sustainability communication, green information
transparency, eco -oriented personalization, ethical data governance, perceived environmental value, green trust, and
engagement served as the constructs measured using validated measurement scales. Relationships were measured by
reliability, EFA, multiple regression, and SEM using IBM SPSS and AMOS. Findings wer e supported by high levels of
reliability and validity, significant positive effects along all the paths, standardized coefficients, (b = 0.203 - 0.319), t-values:
(2.54 to 3.13), and all significant positive effects. The results prove that ethical, transparent, and personalized AI practices
improve green engagement outcomes. The limitations are a cross -sectional design and self -reporting whereas the scopes of
future research are longitudinal analysis, cross-cultural validation, and investigation of long-term sustainability effects of AI-
driven marketing.
