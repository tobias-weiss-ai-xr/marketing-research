Abstract
The tourism and hospitality industry faces growing pressure to reconcile marketing-
driven growth with environmental sustainability. Artificial intelligence and related
digital technologies are transforming how destinations are marketed and how visitor
demand is managed. Yet, the field lacks an integrated theoretical account of how
these marketing innovations can systematically advance sustainability goals. This
paper addresses that gap by proposing a conceptual framework that maps AI-
driven marketing mechanisms onto sustainable development outcomes across four
interconnected pathways: smart operations and resource optimisation, environmental
intelligence and impact monitoring, AI-powered demand shaping and immersive
marketing, and governance, ethics, and systemic transformation. Drawing on a
multidisciplinary synthesis of the marketing, digital innovation, and sustainability
literatures, the framework advances eight testable propositions linking specific
AI marketing capabilities, including personalised sustainable recommendations,
predictive analytics for overtourism prevention, smart pricing, and immersive
behaviour nudging, to SDG-aligned outcomes. Three causal mediators (efficiency,
information, and behaviour change) and key moderating conditions (governance
capacity, data quality, and equity) are identified, and each pathway is anchored in an
established theory spanning the resource and capability based reasoning, technology
adoption, planned behaviour, and institutional perspectives. The paper also confronts
rebound effects, algorithmic bias, privacy risks, and digital divides, specifying actor-
specific levers: operators earmarking verified efficiency savings for clean energy,
destination authorities enforcing carbon and visitor caps, regulators mandating
privacy-by-design and bias audits, and development agencies channelling targeted
finance and data access to SMEs. These give managers, firms, and policymakers an
operational roadmap for AI-driven marketing that balances commercial goals with
climate, biodiversity, and social inclusion.

  1 Introduction
The tourism industry faces persistent environmental challenges, including energy-
intensive operations, carbon emissions, water and waste burdens, and biodiversity risks
[1–3]. At the same time, the sector’s marketing landscape is undergoing a fundamental
shift. This transformation has unfolded in successive waves. Early tourism digitalisation
centred on online information and booking, before the rise of social media and user-
generated content reshaped how destinations were discovered and evaluated. The sub -
sequent diffusion of smartphones, sensors, and big data gave rise to the smart tourism
paradigm, and most recently, the maturation of artificial intelligence (AI), the Internet
of Things (IoT), and immersive media has moved the field toward autonomous, data-
driven, and experiential marketing. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated this trajec -
tory by normalising contactless services and remote, virtual engagement. AI, big data
analytics, and immersive technologies such as augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality
(VR) are reconfiguring how destinations attract visitors, how firms communicate value,
and how consumers discover and choose travel experiences [4–8]. These AI-driven mar-
keting capabilities, from personalised recommendations and dynamic pricing to pre -
dictive demand forecasting and immersive pre-trip experiences, carry significant but
underexplored potential to align commercial marketing objectives with sustainability
imperatives.
Despite growing recognition that digital technologies can support greener tourism,
the literature on sustainable tourism and on smart or AI-enabled tourism has developed
along largely separate tracks. The smart-tourism strand documents what AI marketing
tools can do, whereas the sustainable-tourism strand specifies what outcomes are desir -
able; what remains underdeveloped is a theory connecting the two, namely an explicit
account of the mechanisms through which particular AI marketing capabilities translate
into specific sustainability outcomes, and of the conditions under which they fail to do
so [4, 9, 10]. Several critical questions remain unresolved: under what conditions do AI
marketing tools genuinely reduce environmental harm rather than accelerate consump -
tion? How should rebound effects be governed when algorithmic efficiency lowers costs
and stimulates additional demand? What safeguards are needed to address data privacy,
algorithmic bias, and the equitable diffusion of benefits across destinations and firms,
particularly small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) [ 11–14]? Advancing from aspi-
ration to substantiated theory requires investigating the mechanisms, boundary condi -
tions, and trade-offs that determine whether AI-driven marketing serves as a reliable
pathway toward sustainability or merely an accelerant of existing pressures [9, 15].
To address this gap, this paper proposes a comprehensive conceptual framework that
integrates the extant literature on AI-driven marketing and sustainable tourism. It com -
prises four interconnected pathways, detailed in Sect. 4: smart operations and resource
optimisation; environmental intelligence and impact monitoring; AI-powered demand
shaping and immersive marketing; and governance, ethics, and systemic transforma -
tion. Within these pathways, eight testable propositions are advanced that link specific
AI marketing mechanisms to Sustainable Development Goal (SDG)-aligned sustainabil -
ity outcomes while identifying risks and barriers that condition effectiveness. Accord -
ingly, the paper pursues four objectives: (i) to synthesize the dispersed literature on
AI-driven marketing and sustainable tourism into a single, theory-anchored framework;
(ii) to specify the mechanisms, mediators, and moderating conditions through which AI

marketing capabilities translate into sustainability outcomes; (iii) to formulate falsifiable
propositions, mapped to specific SDGs, that can guide future empirical testing; and (iv)
to identify the principal risks and governance safeguards that determine whether these
tools yield net positive outcomes. The corresponding research question is: under what
conditions, and through which mechanisms, can AI-driven marketing advance sustain -
able tourism rather than intensify its environmental pressures? In scope, the framework
is pitched at the destination and enterprise level and is transferable across geographic
scales rather than tied to one country; it addresses tourism broadly rather than a single
segment, while recognising that its mechanisms matter most where resource intensity
and visitor pressure are highest, as in urban, cultural-heritage, and nature-based tour -
ism. Section  5 illustrates the pathways with specific destinations.
Compared with previous studies, this research makes a distinct contribution by inte -
grating opportunities and constraints within a single framework, combining the sustain -
ability potential of AI marketing with governance, ethical, and equity considerations.
Whereas existing reviews of AI in tourism are largely descriptive or bibliometric [ 9, 10,
15], the present framework is explanatory and anchored in four established theories
(Sect. 2.1). It converts their logic into eight falsifiable propositions, each tied to a mecha-
nism and to particular SDGs. Three features distinguish it from prior models: explicit
causal mediators and moderating conditions rather than a list of enablers; the deliber -
ate inclusion of failure modes, such as rebound and algorithmic bias, alongside benefits;
and the integration of supply-side efficiency, demand-side behaviour change, and gover -
nance into a single structure. To be explicit about positioning, this is, at its core, a mar -
keting paper: its unit of analysis is the marketing function and the mechanisms by which
AI reconfigures it, with sustainability as the outcome and governance as the boundary
condition. By delineating mechanisms, contingencies, and trade-offs, it offers a roadmap
for empirical testing and for policymakers, destination managers, and practitioners [ 12,
15–17].
The paper is structured as follows: Sect. 2 reviews the relevant literature; Sect.   3
describes the methodology; Sect. 4 presents the conceptual framework and proposi -
tions; Sect. 5 discusses the theoretical and practical implications; and Sect.  6 concludes
with limitations and directions for future research.
2 Literature review
Digital technologies have fundamentally reshaped the tourism sector, giving rise to intel-
ligent, data-rich environments commonly described as Tourism 4.0 [ 4, 12]. Core tech -
nologies, including AI, IoT, big data analytics, blockchain, and immersive tools such as
augmented reality and virtual reality, have reconfigured the way destinations operate,
communicate, and co-create value with stakeholders [ 5–8]. Within this broader trans -
formation, the marketing function has been particularly affected. Three interrelated
shifts stand out. First, AI enables the curation of personalised experiences, allowing des -
tinations and firms to tailor recommendations, pricing, and content to individual travel -
lers’ preferences in real time [6, 18]. Second, predictive analytics and demand forecasting
tools are moving marketing from reactive promotion toward adaptive, data-informed
strategies that anticipate consumer behaviour and optimise resource allocation [ 15,
19]. Third, immersive marketing through virtual reality and augmented reality is open -
ing new modalities of consumer engagement, enabling pre-trip destination experiences,

interactive storytelling, and virtual access that may reshape both demand patterns and
consumer expectations [20–23]. The acceleration of these trends during the global pan -
demic, which normalised contactless services, remote engagement, and data-driven
operations, further consolidated AI’s role at the centre of tourism marketing strategy
[24, 25]. While these capabilities offer powerful tools for destination promotion and
consumer influence, they also raise the question of whether and how AI marketing can
be directed toward sustainability objectives rather than solely intensifying consumption.
Despite its economic importance, tourism generates substantial environmental pres -
sures, including greenhouse gas emissions, biodiversity loss, and resource depletion,
prompting growing agendas around sustainable and regenerative tourism practices [ 1,
26, 27]. Digital innovations are increasingly being leveraged to address these challenges
on both the supply and demand sides of tourism marketing. On the supply side, IoT-
enabled energy management and sensor-based monitoring of water, energy, and waste
have demonstrated the capacity to improve operational eco-efficiency in hospitality
facilities [ 2, 3, 28]. Blockchain enhances traceability and accountability across tourism
supply chains, countering greenwashing by providing tamper-resistant records of car -
bon and material flows [2, 29, 30]. At the system scale, AI and big data support dynamic
allocation, load balancing, and forecasting that improve destination resilience [ 31, 32].
On the demand side, AI-driven marketing tools are beginning to shape consumer behav-
iour toward lower-impact choices. Gamified applications, social media analytics, and
augmented and virtual reality experiences communicate ecological value and promote
pro-environmental behaviours [ 33–37]. Real-time visitor monitoring and predictive
modelling enable demand redistribution strategies that mitigate overtourism and align
visitor flows with ecological carrying capacities [ 38–41]. Digital twins allow destination
managers to simulate the effects of marketing and pricing policies before implementa -
tion [42].
Research increasingly frames these supply- and demand-side innovations as a twin
transition, in which digitalisation and environmental sustainability reinforce each other
through coordinated, system-level change [ 25, 43]. However, the realisation of this
potential is not automatic. Technology alone is insufficient when factors such as rebound
effects, privacy concerns, algorithmic bias, and capability divides can nullify or distort
intended benefits [ 11, 13, 14, 44]. Multi-stakeholder collaboration, open data gover -
nance, and inclusive practices are necessary to ensure that benefits are equitably distrib -
uted, particularly to SMEs and lower-resource destinations [ 45, 46]. Moreover, policy
instruments such as carbon caps, earmarking of efficiency savings, and access manage -
ment rules must accompany digital tools to lock in absolute environmental gains and
prevent backsliding [47, 48]. In this sense, the twin transition is less a technology rollout
than an institutional project that strategically leverages AI marketing capabilities within
ethical, collaborative governance to achieve environmental and equitable outcomes at
scale [49, 50].
2.1 Theoretical foundations
Rather than building a framework de novo, the paper integrates four established theo -
ries, each governing one segment of the model. The resource and capabilitybased rea -
soning, explains Pathway 1: digital capabilities such as IoT sensing and AI optimisation
serve as valuable, hard-to-imitate resources that reduce resource intensity per unit of

service, creating a defensible basis for credible green marketing claims [ 31]. The Tech-
nology–Organisation–Environment (TOE) framework [ 51] explains adoption across
all four pathways. It links technological attributes, organisational conditions (includ -
ing firm size), and environmental pressures to firms’ decisions to move from intention
to implementation, and is especially relevant to SME asymmetries in Pathway 4. The
Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) [ 52] provides the micro-foundations for Pathway
3: immersive and AI-mediated marketing shapes travellers’ attitudes, subjective norms,
and perceived behavioural control, thereby influencing their pro-environmental inten -
tions. Institutional theory [ 53] underpins Pathway 4, explaining how coercive, norma -
tive, and mimetic pressures steer adoption toward legitimate, sustainability-aligned
configurations, while disciplining rebound and ethical risks. Together, these lenses make
the framework an integrative theory [ 16]: efficiency (resource and capabilites based rea -
soning), adoption (TOE), behaviour change (TPB), and legitimacy (institutional theory)
combine into a single account of when and how AI-driven marketing advances sustain -
ability. The mediators and moderators in Sect. 4 map onto these foundations, and each
proposition is anchored in the lens most relevant to its mechanism.
2.2 Synthesis and research gap
A critical reading also shows that the literature is not uniformly consilient; several cen -
tral claims remain contested. Whether immersive media substitutes for or stimulates
physical travel remains unresolved [ 22–24, 34, 54]; whether AI-driven efficiency lowers
aggregate impact or is offset by rebound effects is disputed [ 13, 32]; and the expectation
that recommender systems disperse demand is contradicted by evidence that they can
concentrate it [55]. These tensions share a flawed premise common in the optimistic lit -
erature, that a beneficial technical capability translates directly into a beneficial sustain -
ability outcome. The synthesis below, therefore, treats each relationship as conditional
rather than guaranteed.
Three persistent gaps follow. Methodologically, the evidence base is dominated by sin -
gle cases and descriptive or bibliometric reviews [ 9, 10, 15], with little theory-grounded,
falsifiable work specifying the mechanism linking a capability to an outcome. Themati -
cally, benefits and risks are studied in separate literatures, so supply-side efficiency,
demand-side behaviour change, and governance remain parallel conversations rather
than parts of a single system [ 11, 13, 14, 44]. Contextually, attention concentrates on
large operators and advanced-economy destinations, leaving SMEs and lower-resource
settings under-examined [45, 46]. The framework addresses each directly: it converts the
synthesised evidence and four theoretical lenses into eight falsifiable propositions with
explicit mediators and moderators; it embeds failure modes and governance within the
same structure as the value-creating mechanisms; and it treats equity and SME inclusion
as a dedicated pathway and an explicit moderating condition.
3 Methodology
This paper employs a conceptual, theory-building research design to examine the poten-
tial of AI-driven marketing to facilitate sustainable tourism development through four
interconnected pathways. A conceptual design suits this pre-paradigmatic stage: empiri-
cal testing presupposes validated constructs and relationships that do not yet exist in
integrated form, and specifying them is the prior task undertaken here. The study

Zeqiri Discover Global Society            (2026) 4:184
follows the integrative variant of conceptual research [ 16], distinguished from the pre -
dominantly descriptive or bibliometric AI-and-tourism reviews [ 9, 10, 15] by deriving
falsifiable, theory-anchored propositions mapped to specific outcomes. It does not col -
lect primary data; instead, it builds propositions and a framework from the literature as
a foundation for subsequent empirical testing [12, 17, 56].
Although conceptual, the framework specifies the elements an estimable model
requires. The independent constructs are the AI marketing capabilities (personalized
recommendation, dynamic pricing, demand forecasting, immersive AR/VR, blockchain
traceability). The dependent constructs are SDG-aligned outcomes (resource and energy
efficiency, emissions reduction, biodiversity protection, equitable access), as mapped in
Fig. 2. The mediators are efficiency, information, and behaviour change. The moderators
are governance capacity, data quality, equity, and, following TOE, organizational capa -
bility and firm size. The eight propositions are the hypotheses: each states a directional
relationship, names its mediating mechanism, and, where relevant, its boundary condi -
tions. The framework is thus directly translatable into structural-equation or mediation-
moderation models, or comparative-case and field-experimental designs; a conceptual
design is not a weaker substitute for such tests but their necessary antecedent, defining
the variables an estimable model would otherwise lack.
The research draws systematically from multiple academic, industry, and policy
sources. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in Scopus, Web of Science,
and Google Scholar, employing targeted keywords such as “ AI and tourism marketing, ”
“digital transformation and sustainable tourism, ” “smart tourism, ” “machine learning and
hospitality, ” “augmented reality and tourism, ” and “green marketing and AI. ” The selec-
tion prioritised works at the intersection of AI-driven marketing, sustainable tourism,
and ethical governance. The search covered the period from 2015 to 2025, with semi -
nal earlier works retained where they provided foundational theoretical grounding (for
example, the parent theories discussed in Sect.   2.1). Inclusion criteria were: (i) peer-
reviewed articles, books, or book chapters; (ii) a substantive focus on at least one AI-
driven or digital marketing mechanism in tourism or hospitality; and (iii) an explicit link
to a sustainability, environmental, or governance outcome. Studies were excluded if they
addressed AI or digitalisation without a marketing or sustainability dimension, were
non-English and lacked an available translation, or were not retrievable in full text. An
initial pool of approximately 180 records was screened by title and abstract; after remov-
ing duplicates and items failing the inclusion criteria, 65 sources were retained for full
synthesis. To complement the database search, backward reference tracing (snowball -
ing) was applied to the reference lists of the most central and most recent articles. The
same inclusion and exclusion criteria limited selection bias. The final corpus integrates
insights from tourism management, sustainability science, digital innovation, informa -
tion systems, and governance studies.
The retained sources were analysed through a structured thematic synthesis compris -
ing four steps. First, in a data-charting step, each source was read in full and coded for
the AI marketing capability examined, the sustainability outcome addressed, the causal
mechanism implied, any reported moderating or boundary conditions, and any docu -
mented risk or failure mode. Second, in an aggregation step, these codes were grouped
inductively into higher-order themes through constant comparison; the recurring clus -
ters were consolidated into the four pathways that organise the framework, while the

mation, behaviour change) and the moderating conditions. Third, in a theory-matching
step, each emergent theme was mapped to the parent theory that best explained it (Sect.

the eight propositions deductively, so that each proposition states a mechanism rather
than merely a correlation. Fourth, in an alignment step, every proposition was mapped

Through systematic thematic synthesis, combining inductive aggregation with deduc -
tive theory matching, four thematic pathways were identified from the literature (Fig. 1):
(1) smart operations and resource optimisation, highlighting how AI and IoT improve
eco-efficiency in tourism operations; (2) environmental intelligence and impact moni -
toring, encompassing predictive analytics, real-time sensing, and digital twins for pro -
active environmental management; (3) AI-powered demand shaping and immersive
marketing, examining how virtual reality, augmented reality, and recommendation sys -
tems influence consumer behaviour toward sustainable choices; and (4) governance,
ethics, and systemic transformation, addressing the institutional frameworks, ethi -
cal safeguards, stakeholder collaboration, and equity conditions necessary for durable
outcomes. Within these domains, eight theoretical propositions were formulated to
articulate hypothesised relationships between AI marketing mechanisms and sustain -

4 Results: the conceptual framework and propositions
The conceptual framework (Fig.  1) organises the relationship between AI-driven mar -
keting and sustainable tourism across four interconnected pathways: (1) smart opera -
tions and resource optimisation, (2) environmental intelligence and impact monitoring,
(3) AI-powered demand shaping and immersive marketing, and (4) governance, ethics,
and systemic transformation. Three causal mediators, namely efficiency, information,
and behaviour change, link AI marketing mechanisms to sustainability outcomes, while
moderating conditions, including governance capacity, data quality, and equity, shape
the net impact. The following eight propositions articulate the hypothesised relation -
ships within each pathway. The alignment of each proposition with specific SDGs is
summarised in Fig. 2.

conjectures derived from theory and evidence, not established findings; verbs such as
“reduce” or “shape” denote hypothesised mechanisms that are to be tested. Second, each
carries an implied competing hypothesis. The contrast is rarely effect versus no effect.

versus rebound (P1, P8), substitution versus stimulation of travel (P4), and dispersion

4.1 Smart operations and resource optimisation
This pathway addresses how AI and related technologies improve the operational eco-
efficiency of tourism businesses, creating the supply-side foundation on which sustain -
able marketing claims can be credibly built. The primary causal mediator in this pathway
is efficiency: digital tools reduce resource inputs per unit of output, lowering environ -
mental intensity.
Proposition 1 AI and IoT systems in tourism operations reduce the energy , water, and
waste intensity of service delivery (consumption per guest-night) by optimising resource
use in real time, thereby strengthening the credibility of green marketing claims. Boundary
condition: whether these per-unit gains yield absolute reductions in total resource use is
not automatic but depends on the governance of rebound effects; where efficiency lowers
costs and stimulates additional capacity or demand , absolute consumption may remain
flat or rise even as intensity falls.
IoT and AI-enabled systems provide occupancy-responsive and condition-responsive
control of building energy, water, and waste streams in tourism facilities. Smart auto -
mation platforms, dynamic lighting, optimised heating, ventilation, and air condition -
ing (HVAC) scheduling, and sensor-based leak detection can reduce avoidable resource
consumption, with measured reductions in carbon intensity documented among digi -
tally transforming tourism firms [ 3] and efficiency gains reported across IoT- and big-
data-enabled operations [ 12, 28, 31, 57]. The integration of AI with inventory control
and digital traceability across hospitality supply chains further curbs spoilage and plate
waste, reinforcing circular practices [ 2, 4, 58]. At a broader scale, AI and advanced ana -
lytics support dynamic allocation and load balancing across energy, mobility, and logis -
tics, synchronising demand with periods of higher renewable availability and reducing
carbon intensity [ 3, 32]. Digital platforms also enable circular exchanges where one
operation’s by-products become another’s inputs [32, 58, 59]. These efficiency gains con-
tribute directly to SDGs 6, 7, 12, and 13, with moderate contributions to SDGs 9 and
14. However, adoption barriers persist, including high upfront costs, skills gaps among
SMEs, interoperability concerns, and the risk of rebound effects, in which efficiency
gains stimulate additional demand [32, 43, 49]. These obstacles can be mitigated through
targeted financial support, commissioning processes, measurement and verification rou-
tines, and structured training for operations staff [11, 49].
4.2 Environmental intelligence and impact monitoring
This pathway examines how AI enhances the observation, measurement, and prediction
of tourism’s environmental impacts, enabling earlier and more evidence-based market -
ing and management decisions. The primary causal mediator is information: real-time

data and predictive models convert raw signals into actionable intelligence that informs
both operational and marketing decisions.
Proposition 2 Real-time environmental monitoring through IoT and AI , combined with
predictive analytics and digital twin simulations, enhances proactive environmental man-
agement in tourism destinations and informs more sustainable marketing and planning
decisions.
By integrating IoT monitoring with AI analytics, destinations can maintain continuous
oversight of sustainability pressures, including water usage, air and noise conditions,
crowding, and early indicators of habitat stress [12, 28]. In urban heritage areas and pop-
ular precincts, interconnected monitoring systems and managerial dashboards facilitate
the identification of anomalous density or disturbance patterns, prompting timely inter -
ventions such as re-timed entries or flow redirection [ 38–41]. Beyond real-time moni -
toring, big data analytics and AI allow destinations to detect trends, forecast pressures,
and test policies before implementation. Digital twins enable managers to simulate the
effects of timed entry, pricing, or shuttle systems on crowding and emissions [ 41, 42].
These predictive capabilities primarily support SDGs 13, 14, and 15, with secondary
contributions to SDGs 6, 7, 9, 11, and 12. Effectiveness depends on data quality, valida -
tion of results, clear roles and collaboration across actors, and safeguards for privacy and
consent to maintain legitimacy [45, 46].
Proposition 3  Blockchain and distributed ledger technologies create transparent , tam-
per-proof systems for tracking sustainability metrics in tourism supply chains , enhancing
accountability and trust in green marketing. However, concerns remain about complexity
and energy consumption.
Blockchain can serve as an audit layer for sustainability claims in tourism by record -
ing carbon accounting, material provenance, and certification status across transport,
accommodation, food, and activity chains. This provides travellers, buyers, and auditors
with access to tamper-resistant records, reducing the incidence of greenwashing [ 2, 29,
30]. Smart contract logic can automate sustainability actions such as triggering verified
offset purchases, deposit refunds for reusable items, and pay-for-performance clauses
[2]. These contributions primarily align with SDGs 12, 13, and 17, with secondary links
to SDGs 7 and 9. However, deployment requires attention to energy consumption trade-
offs when selecting consensus architectures, to data quality risks at the point of entry,
and to adoption challenges for SMEs regarding investment, skills, and usability [43, 58].
4.3 AI-powered demand shaping and immersive marketing
This pathway addresses the demand side of sustainable tourism marketing, examining
how AI and immersive technologies shape consumer choices, redistribute visitor flows,
and ultimately influence the environmental footprint of tourism at the destination level.
The primary causal mediator is behaviour change: immersive experiences, nudges, and
recommendation systems influence travellers’ decisions before and during trips. These
propositions are most directly aligned with the marketing focus of this paper. The micro-
foundations of this pathway draw on the Theory of Planned Behaviour [ 52], in which
attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioural control shape behavioural inten -
tion. AI-mediated and immersive marketing can act on all three: virtual and augmented

features make pro-environmental norms salient (P5), and recommendation and book -

propositions in these terms clarifies why design quality and user engagement, rather
than mere exposure, condition their effectiveness.
Proposition 4 Virtual reality and AI tourism platforms reduce the carbon emissions and
environmental impacts of tourism mobility and broaden market access by serving as par -
tial substitutes for physical travel. Boundary condition: this substitution effect holds only
when the virtual experience satisfies the traveller’s core motivation (for example , educa-
tion, preview, or access); where the dominant motivation is physical presence, virtual real-
ity may instead stimulate subsequent travel (a preview-to-place effect).
Virtual reality facilitates immersive explorations of parks, heritage sites, and cultural
venues from remote locations, offering partial substitutes and pre-trip alternatives that
ease pressure on fragile destinations and transport systems while broadening access for
consumers facing cost, health, or mobility barriers [ 22–24, 54]. Research demonstrates
that VR can influence understanding and pro-environmental attitudes, supporting more
mindful choices when individuals do travel [34, 35]. For destination marketers, VR offers
a tool to showcase experiences without requiring physical visitation, potentially reduc -
ing discretionary trips while maintaining brand engagement. The primary alignments
are with SDGs 11, 12, and 13, with secondary links to SDGs 9 and 10 [ 57, 60, 61]. How-
ever, substitution is conditional. VR may fulfil certain motivations such as education,
preview, and access, but it can also stimulate future travel through a preview to place
effect. The energy and device footprint of VR ecosystems also remain considerations [22,
24]. The judicious marketing approach integrates VR as a complementary tool alongside
visitor education and carefully crafted hybrid offerings to minimise unnecessary jour -
neys, enhance inclusivity, and bolster on-site stewardship.
Proposition 5 Augmented reality and AI applications enhance tourists’ environmental
awareness and encourage pro-environmental behaviour on-site, reducing negative impacts
such as littering and wildlife disturbance. However , effectiveness depends on user engage -
ment and design quality.
Augmented reality extends the real world with context-aware narratives and guidance
accessible via smartphones or wearable devices, transforming visits into learning expe -
riences that guide individuals toward behaviours that minimise environmental impact.
Research demonstrates that AR and AI applications influence intentions and on-site
actions by rendering information both salient and personal [ 34, 36]. Gamified features
and smart tourism apps further enhance engagement and compliance with recom -
mended practices [ 18, 33, 37]. From a marketing perspective, AR enables destinations
to embed sustainability messaging directly into the visitor experience, making green
values part of the brand proposition rather than an afterthought [ 22, 24]. This aligns
strongly with SDGs 11, 12, and 15, with secondary contributions to SDGs 13 and 14.
Practical effectiveness depends on design quality, usability, and inclusivity. Poorly
designed content can distract or mislead users, while access gaps can limit reach. Legiti -
macy is anchored in transparent governance and collaboration between site manag -
ers and service providers [ 31, 45, 46, 49, 61, 62]. When employed as a component of a

visitor education strategy rather than as a standalone technology, AR and AI can convert
awareness into consistent pro-environmental behaviour and gentler patterns of use.
Proposition 6 AI-driven recommendation systems , dynamic pricing , and booking plat -
forms manage the spatial and temporal distribution of tourist demand, redirecting visitors
across sites and time periods to mitigate overtourism and environmental stress. Whereas
Proposition 5 concerns travellers’ conduct once on-site , this proposition concerns where

Destinations are increasingly using real-time analytics, predictive modelling, and AI-
powered recommendation systems to distribute visits across sites and time periods,
redirecting demand to alternative locations and off-peak hours through online reserva -

influence consumer choices toward lower-impact modes of travel [ 56, 63]. Smart pric -
ing strategies, including dynamic and congestion-based pricing, can simultaneously
generate revenue and discourage overcrowding at sensitive sites. These capabilities align
strongly with SDGs 11, 12, and 13, with moderate links to SDGs 7, 9, and 16. However,
the design of these systems is critical. Poorly tuned recommenders can aggregate atten -
tion rather than disperse it. Dynamic access or pricing rules raise questions of fairness
and inclusion. Fragmented platforms undermine clarity and trust [ 14, 45, 55]. Effective
practice combines transparent governance, accessibility and literacy support especially
for SMEs and digitally reluctant visitors, and stakeholder collaboration so that AI sys -
tems guide demand in ways communities accept and destinations can sustain.
4.4 Governance, ethics, and systemic transformation
This pathway delineates the institutional and ethical conditions necessary for AI-driven
sustainable marketing to achieve durable, equitable outcomes. Rather than operat -
ing through a single mediator, governance serves as a cross-cutting moderator that
determines whether the efficiency, information, and behaviour-change mechanisms
in Pathways 1 through 3 produce net positive outcomes. Without robust governance,
the marketing innovations described in the preceding pathways risk producing unin -
tended consequences that undermine both sustainability and public trust. This pathway
is grounded in institutional theory [ 53], which explains how coercive pressures (regu -
lation, carbon and visitor caps, data-protection law), normative pressures (professional
standards, sustainability certification, industry codes), and mimetic pressures (bench -
marking against peer destinations) steer AI-marketing adoption toward legitimate,
sustainability-aligned configurations. This lens reframes inclusion (P7) and ethical gov -
ernance (P8) not as optional add-ons but as the institutional mechanisms through which
technical efficiency is converted into durable, socially sanctioned outcomes.
Proposition 7  The digital transformation of tourism marketing must be inclusive and
equitable, supported by multi-stakeholder platforms and open data initiatives; otherwise ,
unequal access to AI technologies will exacerbate sustainability gaps between destinations
and stakeholders.

The adoption of AI, IoT, and big data capabilities varies significantly. Large, well-con -
nected urban destinations and chains are well-positioned to adopt these technologies. At
the same time, SMEs, rural areas, and operators in lower-resource settings face substan -
tial barriers, including constraints in financing, infrastructure, and skills [ 30, 43, 49, 50].
This creates a dichotomy in which digital leaders reduce their environmental footprint
and capture value while others remain locked into resource-intensive practices [4, 32, 64,
65]. Multi-stakeholder digital platforms and open data dashboards can address this gap
by aggregating sustainability signals for all actors, enabling communities, researchers,
and NGOs to scrutinise claims and propose solutions [45, 46]. Digital participation tools
such as virtual town halls and collaborative mapping broaden voice and buy-in around
investment priorities [ 48, 50, 61]. Practical levers include targeted finance for SMEs,
shared data commons, low-barrier tools, training and mentoring, and governance that
covers privacy, auditability, and open participation [ 14, 45, 46]. This proposition aligns
primarily with SDGs 10, 17, and 13, with moderate links to SDGs 7, 9, 11, 12, and 16.
Inclusion is not a secondary condition but a prerequisite for both the effectiveness and
legitimacy of AI-driven sustainable marketing.
Proposition 8 Without proper governance and ethical frameworks , efficiency gains from
AI in tourism marketing can trigger rebound effects , algorithmic bias , and privacy vio -
lations that undermine sustainability objectives; therefore , policies must channel savings
into further sustainability investments, ensure algorithmic transparency, and protect con-
sumer rights.
AI-driven efficiency improvements in tourism, from optimised routes and automated
processes to smart building controls, can reduce costs and enhance convenience. How -
ever, these same improvements may increase total demand, potentially offsetting envi -
ronmental benefits through the well-documented rebound effect [13]. Hotels conserving
energy through AI may expand capacity. Platform efficiencies may accelerate visitor
flows. To ensure absolute, rather than merely relative, improvements, governance must
lock in savings by earmarking verified efficiencies for renewable energy or biodiversity
funds, establishing carbon and visitor caps aligned with carrying capacity, and imple -
menting auditable tracking and disclosure [ 2, 32, 48]. Simultaneously, as AI marketing
systems increasingly rely on dynamic pricing, location tracking, and automated rec -
ommendations, ethical concerns demand attention. These systems can raise issues of
privacy, fairness, and accountability, from intrusive monitoring and data breaches to
models that inadvertently disadvantage lower-income travellers or specific communities
[55, 66, 67]. Governance responses must include privacy-by-design and data minimi -
sation, plain-language transparency for automated decisions, routine fairness and bias
audits, and human-in-the-loop escalation for sensitive cases [5, 45, 46, 61]. This proposi-
tion aligns strongly with SDGs 12, 13, 16, and 17, with moderate links to SDGs 7, 9, 11,
and 14. Effective ethical governance requires clear rules on data ownership and consent,
transparent model documentation, inclusive participation, and a deliberate shift toward
quality over quantity in tourism, characterised by longer stays, deeper engagement, and
fewer, better-timed trips (Table 1).

5 Discussion
The framework proposed in this paper shifts the discourse on AI and sustainable tour -
ism from isolated case studies toward an integrated theoretical account that connects
AI-driven marketing mechanisms with SDG-aligned outcomes [ 12, 16, 17]. The eight
propositions, mapped across four pathways, reveal a consistent pattern of alignment.
The strongest contributions cluster around SDG 13 (climate action), SDG 12 (respon -
sible consumption), followed by SDG 9 (industry, innovation and infrastructure), SDG 7
(affordable and clean energy) and SDG 11 (sustainable cities), with further contributions
to other SDGs as in Table 1.
Two mechanisms dominate across the propositions. First, AI-driven efficiency on
the supply side enables leaner operations and superior decision-making. IoT and AI
reduce avoidable energy and water consumption while highlighting emerging environ -
mental issues in time for corrective action [ 3, 31]. These efficiency gains underpin cred -
ible green marketing claims, but they also carry the risk of rebound effects that must be
addressed through caps, earmarking, and demand management [ 32]. Second, and more
central to the marketing focus of this paper, demand-side levers prove equally impor -
tant as supply-side efficiency. Immersive substitution, on-site behavioural nudging, and
algorithmic management of visitor flows [ 22, 33, 34, 36, 37, 40, 41, 47] together address
overcrowding and detrimental practices at their origin in traveller decisions, rather than
merely remedying consequences after they materialise. This represents a fundamental
reorientation of the marketing function from demand stimulation toward demand opti -
misation for sustainability.

These observations point to three conceptual implications that constitute the paper’s
principal theoretical contribution. The first concerns the marketing function. In the
sustainability context, AI shifts marketing from demand generation, the stimulation of
undifferentiated visitor volume, toward demand optimisation, the active shaping of who
travels and when, where, and how. This inverts the growth logic that has historically gov-
erned destination marketing and recasts marketing as an instrument of environmental
management rather than solely of revenue, with consequences for how marketing suc -
cess is defined and measured. The second concerns the locus of explanation. Because the
same capability can yield net benefit or net harm depending on its governance, the deci -
sive variable is not the sophistication of the AI but the institutional configuration: the
caps, earmarking, audits, and inclusion mechanisms that determine whether its effects
are locked in or dissipated. The framework thus shifts the explanation of sustainability
outcomes from technology to institutions, implying that governance and policy research
are as central as technical research, which is consistent with Pathway 4. The third con -
cerns the twin digital–ecological transition. Rather than asserting in the abstract that
digitalisation and sustainability reinforce one another, the framework specifies the
mediators through which they connect, identifying where digital capability advances
or undermines ecological goals and moving the twin-transition idea from analogy to a
testable account. None of these implications claims that AI marketing is sufficient for
sustainability; each identifies a condition under which a digital capability becomes an
environmental asset rather than a liability.
The framework also emphasises that institutions determine whether AI marketing
tools deliver lasting benefits. Multi-stakeholder platforms and open data facilitate joint
situational awareness and coordinated action across the tourism value chain [ 4, 45, 46].
An inclusive digital transition is essential for ensuring that SMEs and lower-resource
destinations can adopt and benefit from AI-driven marketing tools [ 11, 46]. Ethics, pri -
vacy, and bias safeguards are crucial for protecting consumer trust and social legitimacy
[66, 67]. This governance layer aligns strongly with SDG 17 and SDG 16 while facilitating
progress on climate and circularity goals.
The propositions give rise to practical tensions that marketing practitioners must navi-
gate. The tension between personalisation and privacy is especially salient, as AI-driven
recommendation and pricing systems require extensive consumer data. Destinations
must ensure clear consent, transparency, and redress mechanisms (P8). The tension
between competitiveness and sustainability requires attention, since AI marketing
tools that lower costs and improve convenience can stimulate additional demand, erod -
ing environmental gains. Governance instruments such as carbon caps, access rules,
and earmarking of efficiency savings must accompany marketing strategies to prevent
rebound (P8). A deliberate shift from volume-oriented metrics toward quality-oriented
indicators, such as emissions per trip, local value creation, and visitor satisfaction, would
help align marketing incentives with sustainability outcomes.
From a global perspective, the framework is generalizable but context sensitive. Some
destinations have already piloted sensor networks, digital twins, and AR guides, while
others remain in early stages. This suggests a phased learning progression, with early
adopters providing proof of concept and subsequent adopters focusing on knowledge
diffusion and capacity building (P7). International bodies and large operators can assist
by sharing standards and playbooks. Still, the focus should extend beyond technology

deployment to the enabling conditions for training in data use, community engagement
to support acceptance of immersive technology, and policy work on data governance
and interoperability.
Several destinations already illustrate the pathways. On the environmental-intelligence
pathway (P2), Venice’s Smart Control Room fuses closed-circuit television (CCTV),
sensors, and anonymised mobile-phone signals to monitor density and anticipate con -
gestion, complementing its day-tripper fee; Amsterdam and Barcelona use crowd-
monitoring dashboards and open city data to manage visitor pressure [ 38–41]. On the
demand-shaping pathway (P6), recommender systems that weigh carbon, seasonality,
and popularity redistribute trips across sites and times [ 47], and mobility-as-a-service
platforms nudge travellers toward lower-impact modes of transport [ 63]. In smart oper-
ations (P1), international hotel groups run IoT building management systems to reduce
energy and water intensity [ 3, 12, 28]. On immersive marketing (P4/P5), museums and
heritage bodies use VR and AR to broaden access and embed conservation messaging
[22, 34, 35]. These cases are unevenly distributed and only partially evaluated. Few des -
tinations integrate all four pathways or pair efficiency with the caps and earmarking that
prevent rebound, the promising but fragmented adoption of the framework is designed
to make it coherent and testable.
Regarding risks, the paper identifies several categories requiring proactive manage -
ment. Rebound effects represent a high-likelihood, high-severity threat that demands
auditable tracking, absolute caps aligned with carrying capacity, and reinvestment of
verified savings into renewable energy or biodiversity funds [ 13, 48]. Privacy and sur -
veillance risks require data protection impact assessments, data minimisation, and
retention limits. Algorithmic bias in pricing and recommendation systems necessitates
pre-deployment and post-deployment fairness audits, representative training data, and
human-in-the-loop overrides. Cybersecurity vulnerabilities across interconnected IoT
and AI platforms, though not formally addressed in a dedicated proposition, constitute
an additional operational concern that demands strict access controls, regular penetra -
tion testing, and incident-response preparedness. The digital divide, in which SMEs and
lower-resource destinations lack access to finance, skills, and tools, requires targeted
grants, shared services, and inclusive interface design. Platform rules should facilitate
independent verification to combat greenwashing, alongside grievance channels and co-
governance with local communities.
6 Conclusion, implications, and future research
6.1 Conclusion
This paper has proposed a conceptual framework theorising how AI-driven marketing
can enable and accelerate the green transition in tourism. The framework integrates four
interconnected pathways: smart operations and resource optimisation; environmental
intelligence and impact monitoring; AI-powered demand shaping and immersive mar -
keting; and governance, ethics, and systemic transformation. It advances eight testable
propositions that articulate specific mechanisms linking AI marketing capabilities to
SDG-aligned sustainability outcomes. The central argument is that AI can transform
the marketing function from a driver of undifferentiated demand growth into a strate -
gic instrument for demand optimisation, one capable of personalising sustainable travel
recommendations, redistributing visitor flows, nudging pro-environmental behaviour,

Page 17 of 21
Zeqiri Discover Global Society            (2026) 4:184
and verifying green claims. However, realising this potential requires robust governance,
inclusive capacity building, and vigilant management of rebound effects, privacy risks,
and algorithmic bias. The framework thus positions digital transformation not as a pan -
acea but as a conditional enabler whose contribution to sustainability depends on the
institutional and ethical architecture within which it operates.
6.2 Policy implications
Several policy implications follow from this analysis. Policymakers and destination
authorities should integrate an AI marketing strategy with a destination-level sustain -
ability strategy. When formulating climate action plans for the tourism sector, gov -
ernments should consider incorporating AI tools as integral components, such as
embedding smart grids in tourist facilities as part of climate mitigation strategies or
using big data in environmental impact assessments. The propositions advocate for
policies that incentivise the adoption of sustainable technologies, including grants or
tax reductions for hotels implementing certified smart energy systems (P1), support
for startups developing augmented-reality educational content about heritage sites
(P5), and funding for pilot projects involving destination digital twins (P2). Regulatory
frameworks concerning data privacy, cybersecurity, and AI ethics in tourism contexts
may require updating in light of the governance propositions (P8). Standards for sus -
tainability data reporting through digital means, initially voluntary and subsequently
mandatory, would encourage the transparency envisioned in the framework. The call for
inclusivity suggests that development agencies and governments must ensure that digital
infrastructure, including internet connectivity and open data portals, and training reach
less developed tourism areas (P7). International bodies may coordinate resources for this
purpose, recognising that the digital divide threatens to widen, rather than narrow, sus -
tainability gaps across regions.
6.3 Managerial implications
For marketing practitioners and destination managers, the framework provides a stra -
tegic blueprint for leveraging AI in sustainability initiatives. Managers can identify
untapped opportunities across the four pathways. A hotel chain, for example, might
recognise from the smart operations proposition (P1) that investing in an integrated
property management system with IoT sensors could dramatically reduce costs and
emissions, a strong business case aligned with sustainability. A destination marketing
organisation may draw on demand-shaping propositions to develop an official applica -
tion that guides tourists toward eco-friendly practices while enhancing their experience.
The propositions also caution practitioners against pitfalls. Technology providers should
heed the rebound effect analysis and incorporate features encouraging users to set and
adhere to sustainability targets. The blockchain proposition (P3) may motivate tourism
companies to explore novel instruments for sustainability reporting and supply chain
transparency, thereby enhancing credibility with environmentally conscious consumers.
The governance propositions imply that industry cannot proceed independently; collab -
oration with governments, community stakeholders, and even competitors will be essen-
tial. Tourism councils and industry associations may use this framework to advocate for
supportive infrastructure and policies. Furthermore, the near-future tourism workforce

implement these AI marketing tools effectively to support sustainability.
6.4 Limitations and future research directions
This study has several limitations that open avenues for future research. As a concep -
tual paper, the framework does not provide direct empirical validation. The hypothe -
sised relationships may simplify complex realities or overlook contextual nuances across
diverse tourism settings, which can limit immediate generalisability. Not all propositions
will apply equally to all forms of tourism; for instance, the role of augmented reality and
sensors may differ substantially between urban and wilderness contexts. Three further
limitations should be made explicit. First, conceptual framing bias: because the frame -
work is built deductively from selected theories, it foregrounds the mechanisms those
theories make visible and may underweight phenomena they do not make visible, so the
propositions should be read as one defensible structuring of the field rather than the
only one. Second, literature selection bias: despite explicit inclusion and exclusion crite -
ria, reliance on three English-language databases and on backward citation tracing can
over-represent highly cited and recent work and under-represent grey literature, non-
English scholarship, and null or contrary findings; the evidence-intensity coding in Fig.
2 inherits this bias and is a qualitative heuristic rather than a validated grading. Third,
the framework is deliberately directional and therefore carries a positive-outcome ori -
entation; it does not formalise competing or null hypotheses, and several propositions
could fail or reverse under plausible conditions (for example, recommender systems that
concentrate rather than disperse demand, or virtual reality that stimulates rather than
substitutes for travel). Future tests should be designed to both falsify and confirm these
relationships and to weigh them against rival explanations, such as price effects or desti -
nation life-cycle dynamics. Relatedly, the framework would benefit from deeper engage -
ment with trade-offs that pit one sustainability dimension against another, including
how dynamic pricing can improve environmental outcomes while reducing affordability
for lower-income travellers, and how automation can increase efficiency while displac -
ing tourism labour. Future research should employ rigorous qualitative and quantitative
designs to test, validate, and refine the propositions, tracking net outcomes over time
rather than intensity metrics alone [ 12, 17]. Field trials, comparative case studies, and
realistic simulations would be particularly valuable for establishing causal links between
AI marketing interventions and sustainability outcomes. Future work could also explore
economic and social sustainability dimensions alongside environmental ones, as AI-
driven automation may influence labour conditions and cultural sustainability in ways
not fully addressed here. Longitudinal studies examining how AI marketing tools per -
form over extended periods, including whether substitution or stimulation effects domi -
nate in virtual reality tourism, would be especially informative. Finally, as AI technology
continues to evolve rapidly, sustained scholarly attention is needed to ensure the model’s
continued relevance. The interaction between AI-driven marketing and the green transi-
tion in tourism is complex but holds considerable potential. The proposed framework
suggests a paradigm in which the marketing function serves not merely to attract visi -
tors but to guide them toward choices that protect the destinations that inspire their
travel. Realising this vision requires innovation not only in technology but also in mind -
set, policy, and models of collaboration. This paper offers a conceptual roadmap that
