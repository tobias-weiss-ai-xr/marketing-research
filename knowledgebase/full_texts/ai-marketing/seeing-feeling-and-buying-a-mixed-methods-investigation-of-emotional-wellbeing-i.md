Abstract
As e-commerce platforms increasingly adopt augmented reality (AR), understand -
ing how immersive technologies influence consumer wellbeing has become es -
sential. Drawing on the stimulus-organism-response (S-O-R) model and cognitive
appraisal theory, this article develops and empirically tests a framework that ex -
amines how AR features shape affective satisfaction, buyer empowerment, emo -
tional wellbeing, and purchase intention. A mixed-methods design was employed,
involving qualitative interviews and a cross-national survey of AR users. Findings
indicate that personalized interactivity and usability realism strengthen immersive
experiences and perceived value, which subsequently drive emotional outcomes and
behavioral intentions. Moderating effects of perceived artificial intelligence (AI)
intrusiveness and AR-related anxiety suggest that psychological discomfort can di -
minish satisfaction’s impact on purchase intention. Overall, the study integrates
cognitive and emotional psychological pathways to extend digital marketing theory
and offers actionable insights for designing e-commerce experiences that support
consumer wellbeing and ethical engagement, understood as the protection of con -
sumer autonomy, privacy, and psychological comfort during immersive shopping,
in e-commerce.

1 Introduction
Augmented reality (AR) has emerged as a powerful driver of transformation in online
retail, as continuous digital advancements reshape how shoppers interact with prod -
ucts. More broadly, e-commerce has evolved from a transaction-focused channel into
a comprehensive digital ecosystem that emphasizes convenience, personalization,

and immersive customer experiences [ 1–4]. This technology allows consumers to
visualize items in their real-world surroundings, explore product features from dif -
ferent perspectives, and personalize products in highly engaging ways. E-commerce
sales surpassed $5.8 trillion in 2023 and are expected to exceed $8 trillion by 2027
[5]. AR holds significant promise for enhancing online purchasing experiences by
facilitating interactive product displays and simulated trials in an environment that
transcends conventional digital offerings [ 6]. Earlier work has emphasized how AR
can boost engagement, enjoyment, and purchase intent [7].
Many studies have focused on practicality gains such as convenience and satisfac-
tion, yet potential emotional drawbacks remain underexplored [ 6, 8, 9]. Immersive
interactions can generate anxiety, discomfort, and impressions of reduced control,
particularly when AI-enabled personalization accompanies these technologies.
Although some attention has been devoted to these concerns, deeper understanding
of AR’s cognitive and emotional psychological impacts is still lacking. Failing to
account for these issues may undermine consumer wellbeing and ethical engagement.
This article employs a mixed-methods exploratory design to address these gaps.
The initial phase consisted of in-depth interviews with 20 online shoppers, capturing
their cognitive and emotional psychological reflections on AR. Insights from these
interviews informed the development of a conceptual model grounded in the stimu -
lus-organism-response (S-O-R) model [10] and cognitive appraisal theory [11]. This
model incorporates personalization, usability, immersiveness, intrusiveness, and anx-
iety as key AR elements that influence perceived value, affective satisfaction, buyer
empowerment, emotional wellbeing, and purchase intention. Additional perspectives
on immersive technologies, psychological responses, and ethics in digital contexts
[8, 12–14] provided further conceptual grounding. The second phase involved sur -
veying 477 international AR users to statistically evaluate the proposed model.
This article advances understanding by integrating theoretical insights with first -
hand accounts of AR usage. Findings shed light on how specific AR attributes can
foster or hinder emotional wellbeing, pointing to the significance of responsible
design and ethical engagement in digital commerce. Ethical engagement here refers
to designing AR that respects consumer autonomy, privacy, and psychological com-
fort rather than exploiting attention or personal data, a concern the study operational-
izes through perceived artificial intelligence (AI) intrusiveness, AR-induced anxiety,
and buyer empowerment. Embracing these considerations can help organizations
craft immersive experiences that appeal to consumers’ aspirations for convenience
and enjoyment while acknowledging wider psychological and social implications.
This article thereby enriches discussions on digital ethics, transformative service, and
potential over-personalization in online retail contexts, a connection that is concrete
rather than rhetorical, whereby the moderating role of perceived intrusiveness speaks
to digital ethics and over-personalization, whereas the modeling of emotional well -
being as a central outcome speaks to transformative service research, which treats
consumer wellbeing as a legitimate end of service design [15].

2 Literature review
2.1 Stimulus-organism-response (S-O-R) model
The S-O-R model has served as an influential approach for examining how technol -
ogy and environmental elements influence consumer behavior through emotional and
cognitive pathways. Grounded in environmental psychology [ 10], this framework
posits that behavioral responses (R) stem from internal states (O), which are shaped
by external stimuli (S). Researchers have adapted S-O-R to explore how design
factors, interface features, and technological capabilities can sway users’ feelings
and decisions in digital and service contexts. Hoyer et al. [ 16] contend that immer -
sive technologies, such as AR interfaces, can trigger a spectrum of psychological
responses, from feeling overwhelmed to experiencing deep enjoyment. In e-com -
merce contexts, these stimuli frequently involve interactivity, personalization, and
realism, each significantly impacting perceived value and enjoyment [9]. The organ-
ism phase, typically encompassing emotional and cognitive appraisals, often includes
affective satisfaction and experiential value [7]. These internal states guide responses
such as purchase intention, loyalty, empowerment, and wellbeing, highlighting S-O-
R’s efficacy for understanding how immersive design can influence behavior.
2.2 Cognitive appraisal theory
Although S-O-R delineates a general route from environment to behavior, it does not
specify the cognitive mechanisms that mediate emotional and behavioral outcomes.
Cognitive appraisal theory [ 11] addresses this gap by proposing that emotional
responses do not emerge from stimuli alone; they stem from an individual’s evalua -
tion of those stimuli. This process involves two appraisals: a primary appraisal, which
judges whether a stimulus is relevant or threatening to an individual’s goals, and a
secondary appraisal, which assesses whether the individual can cope with or benefit
from that stimulus. In digital settings, users’ risk perceptions and desired outcomes
shape these appraisals. Yin et al. [ 17] observe that AI-based personalization can be
seen as invasive if users appraise the experience as intrusive, sparking discomfort or
constrained choice. In contrast, AR features that are appraised favorably may induce
competence and satisfaction [18].
A combined perspective of S-O-R and cognitive appraisal theory yields deeper
insights into the ways AR influences consumer wellbeing, either by enhancing or hin-
dering engagement and emotional welfare. The S-O-R framework highlights how AR
functions as an external stimulus driving users’ reactions while cognitive appraisal
theory clarifies the internal evaluations that determine whether the stimulus is benefi-
cial or stressful, enabling a matching and parsimonious account of the studied phe -
nomenon [19]. Moderators such as technological invasiveness further shape users’
appraisals, reflecting calls for more emotion-aware technology design [ 8]. The next
section outlines the qualitative study that investigates AR’s psychological effects on
consumer wellbeing in the context of online shopping.

3 Study 1 - qualitative exploratory study
3.1 Approach
This exploratory phase clarifies how AR influences consumers’ cognitive and emo -
tional psychological responses in online shopping contexts. A qualitative approach
was adopted to investigate immersive commerce experiences, involving 20 online
shoppers from the United States, Germany, South Korea, and India. All partici -
pants had used AR features within the previous six months to shop for items such
as apparel, cosmetics, electronics, or furniture. Interviews were conducted in Eng -
lish or a local language, then transcribed and translated to maintain consistency.
The selection of New York, Berlin, Seoul, and Hyderabad was based on their strong
e-commerce infrastructures and substantial AR adoption, enabling cross-cultural per-
spectives (Table 1). More specifically, New York, Berlin, and Seoul sit in advanced,
high-income digital economies with well-established e-commerce [20] and relatively
mature AR adoption [ 21], whereas Hyderabad was included deliberately as a con -
trasting case from a high-growth emerging market. India ranks among the fastest-
expanding e-commerce markets in the world [ 22] and the country’s AR market is
projected to expand at one of the steepest rates globally on the strength of retail and
e-commerce applications [ 23]. Hyderabad, in turn, ranks as India’s second-largest
software-exporting city and one of its leading technology hubs [24]. Therefore, pair-
ing Hyderabad with the three mature markets allows the qualitative phase to capture
experiences across both established and rapidly developing AR contexts, which, in
turn, widens the cross-cultural reach of the study and prevents the emergent themes
from reflecting only affluent Global North usage [25].
3.2 Methods
The interview protocol followed [26] and is reproduced in full in the Appendix, fea-
turing both structured and open-ended queries, allowing participants to share cogni -
tive and emotional psychological reflections on AR-enhanced e-commerce. Minor
cultural adjustments ensured comprehension without altering core content. Inter -
views were conducted in person or virtually, depending on participants’ preferences
and researchers’ availability. An inductive thematic analysis was applied, following
open, axial, and selective coding phases. Intercoder reliability exceeded 0.80, con -
firming analytic consistency. A cross-cultural lens illuminated shared themes and cul-
ture-specific nuances in AR usage toward theoretical saturation [ 27]. Triangulation
with reviews from major AR platforms reinforced the emergent findings.

3.3 Results
Ten principal themes arose from the analysis, forming the basis of the proposed
framework: AR personalized interactivity, usability realism, immersive experience,
perceived value, affective satisfaction, perceived intrusiveness, induced anxiety,
empowerment, emotional wellbeing, purchase intention (Figs. 1 and 2). AR’s per-
sonalized interactive features (personalization options, user control and flexibility)
and usability realism (visual realism, system usability) contributed to its immer -
sive experience (cognitive absorption, emotional engagement) and perceived value
(functional utility, perceived worthiness), which, in turn, determine users’ affective
satisfaction (hedonic enjoyment, positive emotional response). This satisfaction
contributes to users—as buyers—empowerment (decision-making confidence, per -
ceived autonomy), emotional wellbeing (emotional comfort, psychological positiv -
ity), and purchase intention (purchase readiness, behavioral attachment), though its
impact on the latter may be influenced by perceived intrusiveness (personalization
intrusiveness, privacy concerns) and induced anxiety (sensory overload, interaction
anxiety) in AR e-commerce environments driven by AI. Indeed, concerns about AI
intrusiveness and over-personalization in AR appeared frequently among participants
in Hyderabad and New York, with descriptions such as “creepy” or “overwhelm -

while personalization increases relevance and elicits strong emotional responses

Personalization embedded in AR also boosts perceived value, especially when users
notice a close alignment between the technology’s functionality and their individual
needs and preferences [ 31]. Tailored interactions can amplify both enjoyment and
perceived utility, reinforcing the cognitive and emotional evaluations that drive posi-

4.1.2 AR usability realism, immersive experience, and perceived value
Usability, defined by intuitive interfaces and manageable cognitive demands, enables
users to remain engaged and present during AR sessions [32]. Realism, operational-
ized through lifelike visuals, credible spatial representations, and stable sensory feed-
back, promotes an environment where users can suspend disbelief and experience
an impression of presence [ 33]. In combination, strong usability and realism have
been linked to deeper immersion and greater satisfaction. Weak interface designs
or unrealistic simulations can negate these benefits, reducing users’ immersion and
dampening their overall evaluations. Clear alignment of usability and realism fos -
ters trust, functional benefits, and emotional fulfillment, which heightens perceived
value in technology-mediated commerce [18, 34, 35]. Shoppers who navigate a fluid
and believable AR environment can interpret product information more effectively,
strengthening the user’s perception of both usefulness and enjoyment. Based on these
considerations, the following hypotheses are proposed:
H2a  AR usability realism positively influences immersive experience.
H2b  AR usability realism positively influences perceived value.
4.1.3 Immersive experience and perceived value
Immersive experiences stand as key precursors to users’ perceived value in e-com -
merce. Heightened involvement and emotional connection increase perceptions of
functional and hedonic value [36]. An immersive AR environment facilitates explo-
ration, trial, and interactive product assessment, improving the user’s ability to make
informed and rewarding decisions. Realism, interactivity, and multisensory engage -
ment often strengthen perceptions of utility, pleasure, and relevancy, reinforcing per-
ceived value [ 37]. These qualities become especially vital in e-commerce, where
users rely on immersive cues to substitute for physical product interactions. Reduced
uncertainty and heightened engagement arising from immersion are likely to aug -
ment perceived value. Consequently:

4.1.4 Perceived value and affective satisfaction
Perceived value exerts substantial influence on users’ emotional reactions to AR
applications, given its functional and emotional components. Individuals who per -
ceive AR technology as worthwhile, relevant, and rewarding typically report stronger
affective satisfaction [38]. Positive perceptions of utility and pleasure reduce anxiety
about time and effort spent, bolstering comfort and contentment [ 18, 39]. Online

emotionally gratifying experience. Hence:
H4  Perceived value positively influences affective satisfaction.
4.1.5 Affective satisfaction, buyer empowerment, emotional wellbeing, and
purchase intention
Affective satisfaction represents a critical psychological process in augmented
environments that offer engagement and enjoyment [ 40]. Users who experience
excitement or pleasure through AR may feel deeper emotional fulfillment, which
encourages positive psychological outcomes, including emotional wellbeing [ 41].
Positive emotions often strengthen feelings of control, optimism, and confidence,
contributing to a sense of empowerment. Greater empowerment heightens the user’s
agency, which may translate into stronger readiness to buy. Studies have linked enjoy-
able interactions in AR to emotional bonding, enhanced trust, and favorable purchase
intentions [18]. Immersive product displays, tailored content, or interactive features
can stimulate a psychological readiness to engage in commercial transactions [ 35].
Affective satisfaction in turn reinforces a sense of competence in decision-making,
promoting buyer empowerment and a sense of personal wellbeing. In line with these
observations:

Buyer empowerment contributes to emotional wellbeing by enhancing perceived
control, capability, and ownership within technology-based interactions. Users who
feel empowered report less uncertainty and stress, particularly in immersive AR set-
tings [35]. Greater control over product information and decision processes is associ-
ated with higher psychological satisfaction and reduced anxiety [42]. The emotional
wellbeing that emerges from empowerment often leads to stronger purchase inten -

4.2 Methods
This study examines the ways in which AR features in e-commerce can shape con -
sumer behavior. Data were gathered from diverse cultural contexts, reflecting the
global reach of e-commerce and varying AR adoption patterns. A structured online
survey (Table 3) was conducted using purposive sampling to target users who had
experienced AR in their online shopping within the last six months. Two screening
questions ensured only qualified participants proceeded: (a) whether they had used
AR features while shopping online, and (b) whether those features offered interac -
tivity or personalization. Respondents who responded “Yes” to both questions were
included. The survey link was shared via academic and professional networks, digital
marketing panels, online shopping forums, and social media ads to reach shoppers
familiar with AR. Measures such as single-entry authentication and required fields
reduced incomplete responses, yielding 477 valid responses.
Armstrong and Overton’s [49] procedures were employed to assess possible non-
response bias by comparing early and late respondents on key demographic and atti-
tudinal variables. No significant differences arose, suggesting minimal non-response
concerns. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and structural equation modeling
(SEM) were then performed using AMOS v.24 to evaluate measurement reliability,
validity, and the hypothesized structural model.
Participants consisted of 225 females (47.17%), 248 males (51.99%) while 4
respondents (0.84%) who identified as either non-binary or chose not to disclose
(Table 2). The largest age cohort (38.16%) fell between 25 and 34 years, followed
by 35 to 44 years (25.79%). Roughly half of the sample resided in North America
or Europe, while the remaining half lived in South or East Asia, reflecting balanced
representation across regions. Monthly income levels ranged from under USD 1,000
(29.56%) to over USD 2,500 (28.93%), illustrating economic diversity. Fashion and
apparel (31.66%) represented the most common AR-shopping category, followed
by electronics (25.57%), furniture (22.85%), and cosmetics (19.92%). Respon -
dents reported experiences with a variety of AR features, from basic visual over -
lays (21.38%) to interactive try-ons (45.91%) and fully personalized AR interfaces
(32.71%). This breadth of demographic and shopping backgrounds facilitated a well-
rounded exploration of how AR shapes emotional and behavioral outcomes in global
e-commerce.

5 Discussion
This research offers empirical support for a consumer-centered model linking AR in
online shopping to affective satisfaction, buyer empowerment, emotional wellbeing,
and behavioral outcomes. The model integrates insights from prior work, qualitative
analysis, and a subsequent quantitative analysis. Adopting a two-phase study design
has clarified how users cognitively and emotionally process immersive shopping
experiences, particularly regarding interactive personalization and usability-driven
realism.
Findings related to H1a and H1b confirm that personalized interactivity through AR
fosters both immersive experience and perceived value. These observations build on
earlier work highlighting how user control and customized features increase emo -
tional engagement and relevance [51], aligning with S-O-R model [10] and appraisal
theory [11]. Similarly, H2a and H2b were supported, indicating that AR usability real-
ism, realized through intuitive interface design and lifelike imagery, generates higher
levels of immersion and perceived value. These findings extend previous evidence
that seamless navigation and credible visual representations enhance users’ sense of
presence, utility, and enjoyment [18, 34]. Whereas, H3 shows that immersion exerts a
decisive impact on perceived value, paralleling other studies suggesting that experi -
ential quality influences both hedonic and functional judgments [37], while H4 under-
scores that perceived value can elevate affective satisfaction, confirming the role of
cognitive appraisals in shaping emotional outcomes [18, 39].
Affective satisfaction was also found to heighten buyer empowerment, emotional
wellbeing, and purchase intention (H5, H6, and H7), affirming the view that rewarding
interactions with AR can bolster user confidence, positive emotions, and purchas -
ing readiness [ 35]. H8 further indicates that buyer empowerment raises emotional
wellbeing, linking consumer autonomy and perceived control in technology-enabled
contexts [18] while H9 uncovers a link-albeit modest- between emotional wellbeing
and purchase intention, echoing earlier findings regarding the role of psychological
safety and positivity in shaping buying decisions [8].
The moderating analyses (H10a and H10b) reveal that perceived AI intrusiveness and
AR-induced anxiety diminish the positive effect of affective satisfaction on purchase
intention. These results align with concerns about extreme personalization, data intru-
sion, and excessive sensory demands in immersive technology [8]. Feelings of unease
or reduced control can weaken the power of positive emotions to drive behavior, even
when satisfaction is high. Cognitive appraisal theory [ 11] explains why, whereby a
positive emotional state raises purchase intention only when the surrounding experi-
ence is also judged safe and controllable, which means a secondary appraisal of threat
can override the behavioral pull of satisfaction. Perceived AI intrusiveness supplies
exactly such a threat appraisal, signaling surveillance or manipulation, whereas AR-
induced anxiety signals an inability to cope with sensory or interactional demands.
Each appraisal shifts the shopper from an approach orientation toward avoidance
even while affective satisfaction remains high. This, in turn, clarifies why emotional
gains do not convert into behavior automatically and why psychological safety oper-
ates as a gatekeeping condition rather than a peripheral comfort. These insights sug-
gest that designers and managers should consider user comfort and psychological

safeguards when introducing immersive AR features, given the complex interplay
among emotional engagement, wellbeing, and perceived intrusiveness. Therefore,
this research advances extant understanding of immersive technology adoption by
bridging cognitive and emotional factors and emphasizing consumer wellbeing and
ethical engagement in the design of AR-enhanced e-commerce.
5.1 Theoretical contributions
Consistent with extant guidelines on theory development [52], this article strengthens
theoretical perspectives on digital consumer behavior by bridging the S-O-R model
[10] with cognitive appraisal theory [ 11]. Previous studies often adopted the S-O-R
model to account for how environmental cues influence consumer decisions, yet they
rarely explained in detail the cognitive filters that shape how individuals process
these cues. Hence, by embedding appraisal mechanisms into the organism phase, this
article clarifies the pathways through which AR design elements prompt cognitive
and emotional responses, including perceived value and affective satisfaction, which
then influence buyer empowerment and emotional wellbeing.
The mixed-method approach involving exploratory qualitative and confirma -
tory quantitative studies not only refines the conceptual understanding of immersive
retail, but also highlights the interplay between utilitarian and hedonic appraisals
in online shopping. More specifically, this research demonstrates that immersive
features—such as personalized interactivity and usability realism—invoke both
rational evaluations of functional benefits and emotional assessments related to plea-
sure, control, and psychological safety [ 7, 18]. These findings move beyond sim -
pler S-O-R applications by identifying how specific cognitive and emotional triggers
can enhance or undermine user wellbeing. The addition of perceived AI intrusive -
ness and AR-induced anxiety as moderating conditions extends current knowledge
on the boundary constraints of personalization [ 8]. While personalization has often
been portrayed as beneficial [ 53], the findings call attention to adverse psychologi -
cal effects arising from data intrusiveness or sensory overload. These results con -
nect the study to three wider conversations in concrete terms. The dampening role
of perceived AI intrusiveness gives empirical weight to debates on digital ethics
and over-personalization, showing that intrusive tailoring is not only normatively
questionable but commercially self-defeating once it crosses into discomfort [ 53].
The positioning of emotional wellbeing as a modeled outcome, rather than an after -
thought, situates immersive commerce within transformative service research, which
evaluates services by their capacity to support rather than erode consumer wellbeing
[15]. Reading the two in combination implies that responsible AR design and com -
mercial performance are complementary rather than opposed, which is the sense in
which wellbeing sits at the center of value creation in immersive retail.
This article also speaks to research on consumer wellbeing and ethical usage of
immersive technologies by showing that affective reactions and perceptions of con -
trol are not mere afterthoughts in AR design [43]. Instead, they are central factors that
can strengthen or diminish technology acceptance. Revealing how positive emotions
can be offset by intrusiveness or anxiety emphasizes the ethical mandate for respon -
sible innovation. A rich theoretical perspective emerges in which consumer wellbe -

intentions. This emphasis on the connections between emotional outcomes, decision
confidence, and user acceptance enriches existing discourse on technology-enabled
commerce. Hence, by conducting qualitative and quantitative inquiries, this research

alization, immersion, and wellbeing intertwine in AR e-commerce. Consequently,
this research deepens understanding of how immersive elements, coupled with ethi -

environments.
5.2 Managerial implications
The validated measures (Table 3) and relationships (Table 6) herein offer a set of
actionable recommendations for practice. AR developers and managers in e-com -
merce can leverage these findings by systematically embedding interactivity and
realism into their systems. Specifically, offering users clear ways to customize and
control the AR environment—such as trying on items or rearranging virtual fur -
niture—prompts stronger engagement and perceived value. The item statements
that underscore consumers’ desire to tailor AR to their individual preferences (for
instance, “I can customize the AR experience according to my preferences”) indicate
that platforms need easily accessible menus or toggles that adjust the AR settings
or level of personalization [ 31]. Hence, by doing so, brands can encourage a sense
of user agency and functional benefit that drives satisfaction and eventual purchase
intention.
Realism also emerges as a critical pillar. The study’s usability realism indicators
show that fluid visuals, intuitive system responses, and lifelike representations boost
both immersion and perceived value [32]. This means retail platforms should invest
in quality image rendering, minimal lag times, and responsive controls so that users
feel the virtual environment is natural and trustworthy. Even small enhancements—
such as accurate lighting and scaling—can strengthen consumers’ impression that the
AR environment closely aligns with their real-world settings, thus promoting deeper
engagement and enjoyment.
Affective satisfaction and emotional wellbeing were strongly linked to consumer
empowerment and buying decisions. Users who experience positive emotions dur -
ing AR sessions tend to feel more confident, more in control, and more inclined to
purchase [ 34, 37]. Retailers might foster these beneficial emotions by integrating
pleasing visuals, game-like interactions, and immediate feedback mechanisms that
affirm user choices and accomplishments. This could include progress indicators,
augmented “try-on” confirmations, or interactive messages that highlight how the AR
environment is adapting to user preferences.
The findings also reveal potential pitfalls when technology becomes too invasive
[46, 47] or induces stress [ 48]. Excess personalization—where users sense that the
system is “learning” too much about them—or confusion arising from overly com -
plex AR interfaces can reduce the likelihood that emotional satisfaction translates into
actual purchasing. Providing transparent data-usage guidelines and placing users in
charge of the extent and type of personalization can mitigate these risks. For instance,

shoppers may decide whether they want the platform to recommend items based on
their past browsing habits or location. Clear communication around data collection
and the option to opt out can help maintain a comfortable sense of privacy and trust.
Cultural nuances further complicate AR adoption across international markets
[25]. Some shoppers might value detailed customization while others may prefer
minimal interference in their browsing. In regions with higher privacy sensitivity,
developers should highlight security features, data anonymity, and user autonomy
more prominently. For sectors requiring greater realism—like cosmetics, fashion,
or furniture—platforms can tailor visuals to reflect local environments, styles, and
preferences, allowing users to see items in contexts that feel familiar and personally
relevant.
Translating these principles into practice calls for concrete implementation rather
than general intent. Platforms can A/B test the intensity of personalization and the
default privacy setting against the very outcomes modeled here, comparing immer -
sion, perceived intrusiveness, anxiety, and conversion across an opt-in arm in which
personalization is off by default and an opt-out arm in which it is on, which yields
direct evidence on where helpful tailoring tips into intrusion. Staged rollouts of new
AR features to a small user segment before full release allow the same emotional
indicators to be monitored as guardrails, which catches a feature that lifts conversion
while raising anxiety before it reaches the whole user base. Visible controls also work
best when concrete, such as a one-tap toggle to disable behavioral tracking, a slider
that scales recommendation aggressiveness, and a short panel that states what data
the session uses, each of which converts the principle of transparent personalization
into an interface element a shopper can see and operate.
These considerations, when taken collectively, draw attention to the need for emo-
tional safety and ethical design in retail technology. AR developers who emphasize a
balance between personalization and control—rather than one at the expense of the
other—are likely to see stronger consumer loyalty and higher purchase intentions.
When users perceive that an AR feature supports their decision-making process while
respecting their comfort zones, they are more apt to find both short-term and long-
term value in digital shopping experiences.
6 Conclusion
This study draws attention to the impact of AR features in shaping affective satisfac-
tion, buyer empowerment, emotional wellbeing, and purchase intention. Personal -
ized, realistic, and intuitive AR features have been found to enhance users’ sense
of control, foster greater immersion, and strengthen both hedonic and functional
appraisals. The S-O-R model [ 10] integrated with cognitive appraisal theory [ 11]
highlights how individuals’ cognitive and emotional responses arise from technologi-
cally driven stimuli, which then shape key behavioral outcomes.
Nonetheless, some caution is warranted, given that the cross-sectional nature of
this research precludes definitive causal inferences. While international participation
was part of the study design, cross-cultural differences were not explicitly tested, so
any cultural insights remain speculative. Another limitation involves reliance on self-

selected AR users, which restricts generalizability to more hesitant or inexperienced
populations. Future studies might adopt experimental or longitudinal approaches to
clarify causal pathways, as well as investigate other emotional consequences, includ-
ing digital fatigue or shifts in brand trust. Further research on how cultural back -
ground moderates AR usage could shed new light on global adoption patterns and
user satisfaction. The practical and scholarly need to design immersive technologies
that preserve moral alignment and foster psychological comfort remains an urgent
priority, especially as digital engagement becomes more prevalent among diverse
consumer groups.
The scope of the conclusions also carries product category boundaries that future
work should map directly. The four categories examined here (apparel, cosmetics,
electronics, furniture) differ in how much they gain from visualization, which implies
that the strength of the modeled paths is likely to vary between utilitarian goods that
AR helps evaluate through fit and placement and experiential goods whose value
rests on sensations that AR cannot fully reproduce [54]. Stating this boundary condi-
tion bears on transfer to other settings, since the same immersive feature that reduces
uncertainty for a sofa or a pair of glasses may add little for categories defined by taste,
scent, or social context. A further boundary concerns measurement, given that the
constructs here were captured through validated multi-item self-report scales rather
than behavioral or platform-side indicators, an approach standard in this literature yet
open to common-method and self-presentation concerns. Purchase intention is itself
a stated psychological outcome rather than observed purchase, which keeps the pres-
ent findings bounded to intention and leaves any behavioral claim contingent on later
corroboration [55]. Future studies could supply that corroboration by pairing survey
measures with objective behavioral, physiological, or log-based signals of immer -
sion, anxiety, and purchase.
Appendix 1: Interview protocol
The semi-structured protocol below guided the 20 interviews in Study 1. The ques -
tions were developed following the guidelines in Lim [ 26] and were used across all
four sites, with minor wording adjustments for local comprehension. Each interview
opened with a broad recollection of a recent augmented reality shopping experience
and then turned to progressively more specific aspects of that experience, with the
order kept flexible so that participants could lead and elaborate in their own terms.
Follow-up probes such as “Could you say more about that?” or “What made you feel
that way?” were used throughout to surface the cognitive and emotional reflections
that the thematic analysis later coded inductively.
1. To begin, could you tell me about a recent time you used an augmented reality
feature while shopping online? What were you shopping for, and what did you
hope the feature would help you do?
2. Walk me through what the experience was like as you used it. How much could
you tailor or adjust it to your own preferences, and how much control did you
feel over how it unfolded?

1 3
S. Pahari, W. M. Lim
3. How easy or difficult was the experience to navigate? How lifelike did the visu-
als appear, and did it run smoothly enough to feel natural and trustworthy?
4. While using the feature, how absorbed did you feel? Did you ever lose track of
time or feel as though you were inside the scene rather than looking at a screen?
5. How did using it make you feel emotionally? Were there moments of enjoyment,
pleasure, or satisfaction, and what brought them on?
6. Were there moments when it made you feel uneasy, nervous, or stressed? If so,
what triggered that, and how did you respond?
7. Did any part of it feel intrusive or too personal, for instance, in how much the
system seemed to know about you or how it recommended items? Were there
moments that felt manipulative or uncomfortable in terms of privacy?
8. Thinking about the time and effort involved, was the experience worth it for you?
In what ways, if any, did it add value beyond a standard product page?
9. After using the feature, how did you feel about your purchase decision? Did it
make you feel more confident, more in control, or more capable as a shopper?
10. How did the experience affect your willingness to buy? Were you more or less
likely to purchase the product afterward, and why?
11. Stepping back from the details, how did the experience leave you feeling over -
all? Did it leave you feeling more positive and refreshed, or more drained and
unsettled? What do you think shaped that?
12. Is there anything about your augmented reality shopping experience, positive
or negative, that we have not discussed and that feels important to you? What
would make the experience better?
