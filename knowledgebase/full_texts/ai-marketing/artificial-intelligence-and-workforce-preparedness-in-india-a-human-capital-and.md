Abstract
Artificialintelligence(AI)isincreasinglychangingtheorganisationofwork,thecompositionofoccupational
tasks and the skills required to remain employable. For India, these changes are particularly important
because the country combines a large working-age population with substantial differences in education,
occupationalexposure,digitalcapability andaccesstotraining. Consequently, theemployment consequences
of AI are likely to depend not only on the extent of technological adoption but also on the preparedness of
workersandinstitutionstoadapt.Thisstudyexaminesthepreparednessof theIndianlabourforcetoadaptto

environment perspective and a sociotechnical view of AI-enabled work, the study conceptualises workforce
preparedness as a multidimensional capability involving AI-related skills, organisational training, continuous
learning,skill-gapmanagementandinstitutionalsupport.
Primary data were collected from 600 respondents working across manufacturing, IT services, HR and
finance, healthcare, and sales and marketing. Reliability analysis, principal component analysis, Pearson
correlation, regression analysis, independent-samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA were employed. The
workforce preparedness scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach's α = .775). The

The findings indicate that Indian workers perceive themselves as moderately well prepared for AI-driven
changes, but preparedness remains uneven across demographic and career groups. The study contributes to
theemergingAI-workforceliteraturebyarguingthatreadinessshouldbeunderstoodnotasastaticindividual
attribute but as a jointly produced capability arising from worker skills, organisational support, institutional
learning systems and the wider technological environment. The results suggest that India's AI transition will
require continuous learning, workplace reskilling, practical AI literacy, industry–academia collaboration and
inclusivepolicysupport.

1. Introduction
Artificial intelligence has moved beyond
experimental applications and is becoming
embedded in production, services, recruitment,
finance, healthcare, marketing, software
development and knowledge-intensive work. The
diffusion of generative AI has accelerated this
transition further by making advanced AI capabilities
accessible to employees who may not have specialist
programming backgrounds. Recent research suggests
that the implications of AI extend beyond job
replacement: AI can alter tasks, redistribute
responsibilities, change skill requirements and
reshape how employees learn and collaborate (Jia et
al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026; Quan et al., 2025).
This distinction is especially important when
considering workforce preparedness. An occupation
may survive technological transformation while
many of its constituent tasks change. Workers
therefore need to acquire complementary
capabilities rather than merely protect existing job
descriptions. Evidence from experimental and field
studies illustrates this possibility. Noy and Zhang
(2023), for example, found substantial productivity
improvements when professionals used generative
AI for writing tasks. Similarly, Brynjolfsson et al.

The question for India is therefore not simply

Recent international evidence reinforces the

organisational transformation. The OECD similarly
argues that AI adoption is increasing demand for

These developments make continuous learning

The Indian context presents a particularly interesting
case. India's labour market is heterogeneous, with
significant differences in educational attainment,

employment, sectoral technology intensity and
access to professional training. The India
Employment Report 2024 emphasises the continuing
importance of education and skills in shaping
employment outcomes, particularly for young
workers. Thus, AI preparedness cannot reasonably
be assumed to be evenly distributed across the
workforce.
The present study addresses this gap by focusing
specifically on the fourth objective of the broader
research programme:
To evaluate the preparedness of the Indian labour
force to adapt to AI-driven changes.
Rather than examining job displacement or job
creation independently, this paper focuses on the
human capability required to manage technological
transformation. It asks whether workers perceive
themselves and their organisations as prepared,
whether preparedness differs across demographic
and occupational groups, and how workforce
readiness relates to the perceived economic impact
of AI.
The paper makes three contributions. First, it
develops a theoretically grounded conceptualisation
of workforce preparedness by combining human
capital theory with the technology–organisation–
environment perspective and sociotechnical thinking.
Second, it provides empirical evidence from 600
respondents across five major sectors of the Indian
economy. Third, it demonstrates that workforce
preparedness is multidimensional and uneven,
suggesting that AI-readiness policies must move
beyond generic digital-skilling programmes toward
continuous and context-specific capability
development.
2. Theoretical Framework
2.1 Human Capital Theory
Human capital theory provides the first foundation
for understanding workforce preparedness. In this
perspective, knowledge, education and skills
represent productive assets that influence workers'
capacity to respond to changes in technology and
labour demand. In an AI-driven economy, human
capital is not limited to formal educational
qualifications. It increasingly includes digital literacy,
analytical reasoning, AI literacy, problem-solving,
communication and the ability to work effectively
with intelligent technologies.
The rapid evolution of AI reduces the expected
lifetime of some technical skills. Consequently, the
value of human capital increasingly depends on the
ability to update knowledge. Recent research on AI-
related reskilling emphasises that workers require
both technical competencies and broader cognitive
and social capabilities to remain effective as AI
changes task structures.
This perspective explains why the present study
includes technical and analytical skills, training and
continuous learning within workforce preparedness.
2.2 Technology–Organisation–Environment
Perspective
The Technology–Organisation–Environment (TOE)
perspective provides a second theoretical layer. AI
adoption is not determined by technology alone.
Organisational readiness, resources, skills and
external institutional conditions influence whether
technological investments translate into productive
outcomes.
Recent meta-analytic evidence confirms the
importance of technological, organisational and
environmental factors in explaining AI adoption
across industries (Sousa Pinto et al., 2025). This is
particularly relevant to workforce preparedness
because employees cannot become AI-ready
independently of the environment in which they
work.
An employee may be willing to learn AI tools, for
example, but lack access to training, appropriate
software, managerial encouragement or time for
learning. Conversely, organisations with strong
learning cultures may enable workers with initially
limited AI knowledge to develop relevant capabilities.
The TOE perspective therefore suggests that
workforce preparedness should include both
individual and institutional dimensions.

Based on these perspectives, this study
conceptualises workforce preparedness as an
outcome emerging from the interaction between:
 AI-related technological change
 Employee human capital and AI-related

 Organisational training and support
 Continuous learning and reskilling
 Government and institutional support
 Industry–academia collaboration
 The conceptual argument is:
 AI-driven transformation → changing task
and skill requirements → need for
continuous learning and reskilling →
workforce preparedness
 This relationship is strengthened by
organisational training, institutional
support and industry collaboration.

4. Literature Review and Hypothesis
Development
4.1 AI transformation and the changing nature of
work
The literature increasingly rejects the assumption
that AI affects employment only through direct
substitution. Generative AI can automate selected
tasks while simultaneously increasing demand for
complementary cognitive and interpersonal
capabilities. Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) demonstrate
that AI assistance can increase productivity,
particularly for less experienced workers. Noy and
Zhang (2023) similarly show that generative AI can
improve both speed and quality in professional
writing tasks.
At the occupational level, AI therefore creates a
continuous process of task recomposition. Employees
must understand which activities can be delegated to
AI, which require human judgement and how to
combine both efficiently.
Recent workplace research supports this
interpretation. Jia et al. (2025), in their systematic
review, show that workplace AI can produce both
opportunities and challenges for employees
depending on organisational and psychological
conditions. Liu et al. (2026) further demonstrate that
AI adoption can influence employees' job-crafting
behaviour through both positive and negative
psychological pathways.
Consequently, workforce preparedness should be

4.2 AI literacy and technical capability
AI literacy represents a particularly important
component of preparedness. Unlike traditional digital
literacy, AI literacy involves understanding what AI
can and cannot do, selecting appropriate tools,
interpreting outputs and recognising ethical and
reliability concerns.
Recent work on organisational AI literacy argues that
effective AI literacy includes conceptual, practical and
ethical competencies and should be developed across
organisational levels rather than being restricted to
specialist employees (Kelley et al., 2025). Passlack et
al. (2025/2026) similarly emphasise the importance
of responsible interaction with AI among knowledge
workers.
The implication is that AI literacy should not be
equated with programming. A finance employee,
healthcare professional, HR manager or marketing
specialist may require different forms of AI
competence even though all may need to understand
basic AI principles.
4.3 Continuous learning and reskilling
AI makes continuous learning increasingly important.
The rapid emergence of generative AI means that
employees may need to update their skills repeatedly
rather than acquire a single set of competencies at
the beginning of their careers.
Recent research on workforce co-skilling highlights
the importance of combining employee skill
development with organisational support. Zhang et al.
(2025) show that co-skilling and perceived
organisational support can help employees manage
AI-related job insecurity. Similarly, OECD evidence
indicates that employer-provided training is
becoming an important mechanism through which
workers adjust to AI-related changes.
The present study therefore expects continuous
learning to form a central component of workforce
preparedness.
4.4 Organisational training and support
Training is unlikely to be effective if it is
disconnected from employees' actual work. Recent
research indicates that organisational support,
leadership and opportunities for practical
experimentation influence how employees engage
with AI.
Hu et al. (2025) found that organisational AI
adoption can influence employee knowledge sharing,
while Zhao et al. (2025) demonstrated that
organisational AI adoption and employee AI skills
need to be aligned for better organisational outcomes.
This suggests that organisations should not simply
purchase AI systems and expect workers to adapt
automatically. They need to create a supportive
environment in which workers can experiment, learn
and gradually incorporate AI into their tasks.
4.5 Institutional support and education
Workforce preparedness also depends on education
and institutional structures. Higher education
institutions have to update curricula, while
vocational and professional training systems need to
respond to rapidly changing occupational
requirements.
Hughes et al. (2025) highlight the challenges that
generative AI creates for educational institutions and
the need for educational systems to reconsider how
learning is designed. The same principle applies to
workforce development: education should provide
transferable capabilities rather than narrowly
specialised knowledge that can quickly become
obsolete.
5. Hypothesis Development
The preceding theoretical discussion suggests that
AI-related transformation creates pressure for
workers to acquire new capabilities. Where AI
adoption and its economic influence become stronger,
workers and organisations have greater incentives to
invest in skills, training and adaptation.
The empirical model therefore tests the relationship
between AI-related economic impact and
workforce/employment outcomes.
H1
H1: AI-related economic impact is significantly and
positively associated with workforce preparedness
for AI-driven employment changes.
The corresponding null hypothesis is:
H0: AI-related economic impact is not significantly
associated with workforce preparedness for AI-
driven employment changes.
6. Methodology
6.1 Research design and sample
A quantitative cross-sectional survey design was
adopted. Data were collected from 600 respondents
working across manufacturing, IT services, HR and
finance, healthcare, and sales and marketing.
The sample was dominated by respondents aged 25–
34 years (54.5%). Respondents below 25 years
represented 20.8%, while 18.2% were aged 35–44
years. Male respondents accounted for 79.3% and
female respondents for 20.7%.
In terms of educational attainment, 57.2% held
bachelor's degrees, 17.7% master's degrees and 6.0%
doctoral qualifications. Entry-level employees
represented 39.5% of the sample and mid-level
employees 47.5%. IT services contributed 37.0% of
respondents, HR and finance 27.5%, healthcare
16.0%, sales and marketing 10.7%, and
manufacturing 8.8%.

6.2 Measurement
The questionnaire employed five-point Likert-type
items covering AI adoption, employment trends, job
creation, workforce preparedness and AI economic
impact.
 The workforce-preparedness construct
contained six items addressing:
 AI-related technical and analytical skills;
 workforce readiness for AI-driven change;
 organisational training;
 skill gaps;
 continuous learning; and
 AI-related employment growth.
 The six-item construct demonstrated
acceptable internal consistency with
Cronbach's α = .775.
6.3 Analytical strategy
SPSS was used for statistical analysis. Reliability
analysis assessed internal consistency. Principal
component analysis examined the dimensional
structure. Pearson correlations assessed associations
between constructs. Regression analysis was used for
hypothesis testing. Independent-samples t-tests
examined gender differences, while one-way ANOVA
examined age, education, job level, industry and
experience differences.

13. Discussion
The results provide a generally positive but qualified
picture of workforce preparedness in India. The
overall workforce mean of 3.807 suggests that
respondents believe Indian workers possess a
reasonable capacity to adapt to AI-driven change.
However, the relatively high score should not be
interpreted as evidence that the workforce is fully
prepared.
The most important finding is the coexistence of
preparedness and vulnerability. Respondents
recognise the importance of technical and analytical
skills, organisational training and continuous
learning while simultaneously identifying skill gaps
as a major challenge. This finding is consistent with
contemporary research showing that AI adoption
changes the composition of skills required at work
rather than simply eliminating occupations.
The factor analysis reinforces this interpretation. The
KMO value of .957 and four-component structure
indicate that the employment and workforce items
are closely related but multidimensional. Workforce
preparedness therefore emerges as a broader
organisational and institutional phenomenon rather
than a single measure of individual confidence.
The results are consistent with recent research on AI
literacy. Kelley et al. (2025) conceptualise

The strong correlation between AI adoption and

The demographic results provide an additional
contribution. Preparedness is not evenly perceived
across the labour force. Gender, age, job level and
experience show statistically significant differences.
Education, in contrast, is less consistently associated
with workforce preparedness.
The education finding is particularly important for
policy. If formal educational attainment alone were
sufficient, higher qualifications should consistently
produce higher preparedness scores. The results do
not support such a simple relationship. Instead, they
point toward the importance of lifelong learning and
practical exposure.
The job-level finding also deserves attention. Entry-
level workers reported considerably higher
preparedness than senior-level workers. One
possible explanation is that newer workers are more
accustomed to digital environments, while
experienced managers may be more cautious
because they understand the organisational
consequences of AI more broadly. Another possibility
is that entry-level workers perceive AI as a career
opportunity whereas senior employees may
associate it more strongly with organisational
restructuring. Future research should test these
explanations directly.
The experience results reinforce this interpretation.
Workers with less than one year of experience
reported the highest preparedness, whereas the 5–
10-year group reported the lowest. This suggests that
AI preparedness may follow a non-linear career
pattern. Employees who entered the labour market
during the digital era may be more comfortable with
AI, while mid-career employees may face a greater
adjustment challenge because they have accumulated
experience in work systems that AI is now changing.
The findings are therefore consistent with a human-
capital perspective but also reveal its limitations.
Human capital accumulated in the past does not
necessarily guarantee preparedness for future
technological change. Human capital has to be
renewed.
14. Theoretical Contributions
 This study contributes to the literature in
four ways.
 First, it reconceptualises workforce
preparedness as dynamic human capital.
 Traditional measures of workforce
readiness often emphasise education or
technical skills. The present findings
suggest that AI preparedness is broader
and includes the capacity to continuously
renew human capital.
 Second, it integrates individual and
organisational dimensions.
 The results indicate that workers'
preparedness is connected with
organisational training and institutional
support. This supports a move away from
treating AI adaptation as an individual
responsibility.
 Third, it adds an Indian empirical
perspective.
 Much of the recent empirical literature on
AI and work is based on North American,
European or East Asian settings. The
present evidence from 600 Indian
respondents adds a large emerging-
economy perspective to the literature.
 Fourth, it highlights heterogeneity.
The statistically significant differences across gender,
age, job level and experience demonstrate that
workforce preparedness cannot be treated as a
uniform national characteristic. Policies based on a
single national AI-skilling strategy may therefore
miss important differences between worker groups.
15. Managerial and Policy Implications
15.1 From AI training to AI capability
Organisations should move beyond one-off AI
workshops. Training should be continuous, practical
and connected to employees' actual work.
15.2 Develop role-specific AI literacy
AI training should differ by occupation. A healthcare
worker, HR professional, financial analyst and
software developer do not need identical AI
capabilities.
15.3 Create internal mobility pathways
Where AI automates particular tasks, employers
should identify adjacent roles into which affected
workers can transition. Reskilling should therefore
be linked to redeployment rather than treated as an

16. Limitations and Future Research
Several limitations should be acknowledged.
First, the study relies on self-reported perceptions.
Perceived preparedness may differ from objectively
measured AI competence.
Second, the cross-sectional design prevents strong
causal inference. The significant correlations
demonstrate association, not temporal causality.
Third, the sample is uneven across demographic
categories. Male respondents and IT-service
employees constitute relatively large proportions of
the sample.
17. Conclusion
The study examined the preparedness of the Indian
labour force to adapt to AI-driven employment
changes. The evidence presents neither a purely
optimistic nor a pessimistic picture. Instead, it
indicates that Indian workers possess a reasonably
strong perceived foundation for adaptation, but that
preparedness remains dependent on continuous skill
development and institutional support.
The workforce-preparedness construct
demonstrated acceptable reliability (α = .775), while
the overall mean of 3.807 indicated moderately high
perceived readiness. The factor analysis confirmed
that preparedness is multidimensional,
encompassing skills, training, continuous learning
and broader employment adaptation. Strong positive
correlations were observed between AI adoption,
workforce outcomes and AI economic impact.
The reported regression model also revealed a strong
statistical relationship, with R² = .615 and a
significant coefficient for AI economic impact.
However, the variable specification should be
rechecked before publication to ensure that
workforce preparedness is directly tested as the
dependent construct.
The demographic findings add an important
dimension. Preparedness differs across gender, age,
job level and experience, suggesting that AI transition
policies cannot treat the Indian workforce as
homogeneous. Formal education alone does not
appear sufficient to explain preparedness. Instead,
practical exposure, organisational training and
continuous learning are likely to become increasingly
important.
The central conclusion is therefore that AI readiness
should be understood as a process rather than a
destination. Workers cannot prepare once and
remain prepared indefinitely because AI itself
continues to evolve. India's ability to capture the
productivity and employment benefits of AI will
consequently depend on whether its institutions can
create an environment in which workers continually
acquire, apply and renew relevant capabilities.
AI may ultimately create greater opportunities for
workers who can complement rather than compete
directly with intelligent systems. Achieving that
outcome, however, requires coordinated investment
in human capital, organisational learning and
institutional capacity. Workforce preparedness
should therefore become a central component of
India's broader AI and employment strategy.
