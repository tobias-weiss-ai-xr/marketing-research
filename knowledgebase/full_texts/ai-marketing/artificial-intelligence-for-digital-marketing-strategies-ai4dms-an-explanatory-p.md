This study proposes and empirically tests the AI4DMS (Artificial
Intelligence for Digital Marketing Strategies) framework to examine how AI
Impact Perceptions (AIB), AI Knowledge (AKM), Analytical-Digital Skills
(ADS), AI Influence on Decisions (AID), and AI Marketing Applications
(AIM) interact across seven hypotheses. Grounded in absorptive capacity
and dynamic capability perspectives, the model was tested using PLS-SEM
(SmartPLS 4.1.1.2) with data from 258 digitally active higher education
students (pre-organizational population with incipient exposure to AI-
related activities in business contexts). The measurement model achieved
satisfactory reliability and validity ( α, CR > 0.70; AVE > 0.50; HTMT
< 0.85), and all hypotheses were supported. The strongest paths were AIB
→ AKM ( β = 0.771) and ADS → AIM ( β = 0.610). AI4DMS follows a
recursive logic in which perceptions enhance knowledge, knowledge
supports analytical-digital skills, and these shape perceived readiness for
AI-supported marketing decision processes. While conceptually linked to
digital marketing, the findings reflect pre-organizational perceptions of AI-
related capabilities, rather than firm-level adoption or actual consumer
behavior.

1. Introduction
Artificial Intelligence (AI) is reshaping digital marketing by enhancing targeting precision, automat-
ing tasks, and enabling real-time consumer analysis. In today’s competitive, data-driven environment,
AI has shifted from being optional to a strategic necessity. Functions such as customer segmentation,
automated content creation, campaign optimization, and hyper-personalization are now AI-driven, fos-
tering business success in the digital era (Balakrishnan et al., 2020). This trend aligns with broader
academic discussions on the transformative impact of AI on business and engineering paradigms (Vial,
2019; Duan et al., 2019). According to the World Economic Forum (2020, October 20), projections
suggest that AI and automation could displace 85 million jobs and create 97 million new roles by 2025,
many related to innovation and marketing. These figures represent forward-looking estimates and are
not empirically assessed in the present study. As a result, professionals with AI competencies—such
as digital literacy, analytical thinking, and innovation—are increasingly in demand. Firms adopting AI
in marketing report 5–10 % revenue growth and operational gains through forecasting, pricing, and
dynamic content delivery (Balakrishnan et al., 2020).
This study aims to explain how AI-related perceptions, knowledge, and analytical-digital skills
interact to shape perceived readiness for AI-supported marketing processes and decision environments.
In response, the AI4DMS model is proposed as an explanatory framework that integrates these dimen-
sions within a recursive capability-building logic.
This model aligns with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 4, 8, and 9 and supports eth -
ical, inclusive, and sustainable innovation. It also resonates with global frameworks such as the Oslo
Manual (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, & Eurostat, 2018) and the 2030
Agenda (United Nations, 2015), positioning AI not only as a tool for competitiveness but also for
responsible development.
However, existing studies largely adopt linear or fragmented perspectives, focusing on isolated
relationships between AI-related capabilities and marketing outcomes within pre-organizational con -
texts. This limits the understanding of how AI-related perceptions evolve into knowledge, how knowl-
edge is transformed into analytical-digital capabilities, and how these capabilities are strategically
deployed and refined through consumer decision-making feedback. The AI4DMS model addresses
this gap by offering an integrated explanation of these relationships at a pre-organizational level.
Accordingly, this study focuses on pre-organizational perceptions of AI-related capabilities rather
than on observed firm-level implementation. The research question is how do AI-related perceptions,
knowledge, and analytical-digital skills interact to explain perceived readiness for AI-supported mar -
keting processes and decision environments?
2. Related Work and Background
Recent studies highlight AI’s expanding role in marketing. Recent academic evidence indicates that
artificial intelligence enables personalized customer journeys, automated content generation, and scal-
able engagement strategies in digital marketing environments (Ziakis & Vlachopoulou, 2023). Accord-
ing to Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (2024), prior evidence indicates that
AI adoption has been associated with higher firm productivity, particularly among top-performing
organizations, although these outcomes depend on factors such as digital infrastructure, leadership,
and skill development.

3. Theoretical Framework and Hypotheses Development
The AI4DMS ex-ante model offers a structured conceptual framework mapping dynamic inter -
relationships among five core constructs essential for AI integration in digital marketing: AI Impact
Perceptions on Business (AIB), AI Knowledge in Digital Marketing (AKM), AI Analytical and Digital
Skills (ADS), AI Influence on Consumer Decisions (AID), and AI Applications in Marketing Strate -
gies (AIM). Each construct is measured through nine reflective indicators. The model outlines seven
directional hypotheses (H1–H7), forming a logical sequence from perception to application within a
capability-building process.
To enhance conceptual clarity, the constructs included in the AI4DMS framework are briefly char-
acterized as follows. AIB captures how individuals perceive the impact and strategic value of AI in
business contexts; AKM reflects individuals’ level of familiarity and understanding of AI in digital
marketing; ADS represents the analytical and digital skills associated with the use of AI tools; AID
refers to the perceived influence of AI on consumer decision processes; and AIM reflects the perceived
role of AI in marketing strategy applications.
Although related, these constructs represent distinct but interrelated dimensions of AI-related capa-
bility formation. AIB reflects evaluative perception, AKM reflects conceptual understanding, ADS
reflects applied capability, AID reflects perceived influence on decisions, and AIM reflects perceived
strategic application. This distinction preserves the conceptual integrity of each construct while allow-
ing their integration into a sequential explanatory model.
In conceptual terms, individuals in pre-organizational contexts who perceive the impact of AI on
business (AIB) are more likely to develop AI knowledge in digital marketing (AKM) (H1) and analyt-
ical and digital skills (ADS) (H2). AI knowledge in digital marketing (AKM) enhances both the per -
ceived influence of AI on consumer decisions (AID) (H3) and the development of analytical and digital
skills (ADS) (H4), while analytical and digital skills (ADS) influence AID (H5) and AI applications in
marketing strategies (AIM) (H6). AID, in turn, contributes to the refinement of AIM (H7), completing
a recursive capability-building cycle.
Grounded in recent literature (2020–2025) and informed by absorptive capacity and dynamic
capability perspectives, the model was empirically validated using Partial Least Squares Struc -
tural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM), confirming the robustness of its constructs and structural
relationships.

3.1. AI Impact Perceptions on Business and AI Knowledge in Digital Marketing
Ziakis and Vlachopoulou (2023) report that artificial intelligence is increasingly adopted in digital
marketing contexts, providing a relevant background for examining how individuals in pre-organiza -
tional settings perceive and develop AI-related capabilities. Prior literature indicates that when AI is
perceived as strategically valuable, it is associated with increased investment in AI-related knowledge.
Aljehani et al. (2024) and Chatterjee et al. (2021) confirm that positive managerial perceptions foster
organizational learning and motivate investment in AI knowledge and capability development. Accord-
ingly, the following hypothesis is proposed: H1: «AI Impact Perceptions on Business (AIB) positively
influence AI Knowledge in Digital Marketing (AKM)».
3.2. AI Impact Perceptions on Business and AI Analytical and Digital Skills
When individuals in pre-organizational contexts perceive AI as transformative (AIB), they are more
likely to develop AI Analytical and Digital Skills (ADS), reflecting early-stage capability formation
rather than organizational investment. These perceptions shape readiness for innovation and promote
employee upskilling. Empirical studies support this: Oraif (2024) found that positive AI perceptions in
education predicted digital skill acquisition. Similarly, Gurjar et al. (2024) noted that firms recognizing
AI's strategic value prioritize building analytical capabilities (ADS). Hence: H2: «AI Impact Percep -
tions on Business (AIB) positively influence AI Analytical and Digital Skills (ADS)».
3.3. AI Knowledge in Digital Marketing and AI Influence
on Consumer Decisions
AI knowledge in digital marketing (AKM) supports individuals in interpreting how tools such
as predictive analytics and personalization influence consumer decisions (AID) at a perceived level.
Rabby et al. (2021) assert that AI-driven marketing improves the buying experience and responsive -
ness. Similarly, Gurjar et al. (2024) emphasize that deeper AI understanding enhances strategic digital
engagement, providing a theoretical basis for considering AKM as relevant for the perceived influence
of AI on consumer decisions (AID). Therefore: H3: «AI Knowledge in Digital Marketing (AKM) pos-
itively influences AI Influence on Consumer Decisions (AID)».
3.4. AI Knowledge in Digital Marketing and AI Analytical and Digital Skills
Proficiency in AI for digital marketing (AKM) fosters the development of analytical and digital
skills (ADS) required for effective strategy. Reed et al. (2025) stress that integrating AI demands strong
analytical abilities to avoid bias in marketing. Vangalla (2024) adds that AI’s data-processing power
improves targeting and personalization (ADS), enhancing the relevance of marketing content. Hence:
H4: «AI Knowledge in Digital Marketing (AKM) positively influences AI Analytical and Digital Skills
(ADS)».
3.5. AI Analytical and Digital Skills and AI Influence on Consumer Decisions
Proficiency in AI analytical and digital skills (ADS) supports individuals in understanding how
personalized, data-driven strategies may influence consumer decisions (AID) at a perceived level. Hal-
eem et al. (2022) show that skilled professionals using AI enhance the customer experience. Similarly,

applications. Ziakis and Vlachopoulou (2023) stress that AI–consumer interactions drive adaptive
strategies, while Brewis et al. (2023) show that AI insights improve targeting and planning, providing
a theoretical basis for considering AID as a relevant input for the perceived development of AI appli-

H7: «AI Influence on Consumer Decisions (AID) positively influences AI Applications in Market-
ing Strategies (AIM)».
Beyond supporting the seven hypotheses, the AI4DMS model aligns with international frameworks
for responsible and inclusive innovation. Its emphasis on digital competencies, adaptability, and AI-driven
strategy reflects principles from the Oslo Manual (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Devel-
opment, & Eurostat, 2018) and the UN’s 2030 Agenda (United Nations, 2015). Specifically, it advances
SDG 4 (Quality Education), SDG 8 (Decent Work), and SDG 9 (Innovation), positioning the model as a
tool for both digital marketing and sustainable development in educational and organizational contexts.
See Figure 1. The theoretical novelty of the AI4DMS model lies in its recursive capability-building logic,
integrating learning, capabilities, and strategic application within a dynamic framework.

4.2. Data Analysis Technique
Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) is a multivariate statistical technique
used to analyze complex relationships between observed and latent variables. It is especially suitable
for exploratory research, theory development, and predictive modeling. Unlike covariance-based SEM,
PLS-SEM is variance-based and does not require data normality or large sample sizes, making it ideal
for models with multiple constructs and indicators (Hair et al., 2023). It estimates both the measurement
model (relationships between constructs and indicators) and the structural model (relationships between
constructs) simultaneously. After data collection, the responses were analyzed using this technique,
which allowed for the validation and refinement of the model. The resulting ex-post model confirmed
the reliability, validity, and significance of the hypothesized relationships among the constructs.

(AIB)—which serve as foundational antecedents in the development of AI-related capabilities at a

These antecedents influence two intermediate constructs: AI Influence on Consumer Decisions

Sample size adequacy was assessed using statistical power analysis following Cohen’s (1988) cri-
teria. In the proposed structural model, the construct with the highest number of direct predictors is
ADS, which is explained by three antecedent variables. Assuming a medium effect size ( f² = 0.15),
a significance level of α = .05, and a desired statistical power of 0.80, Cohen’s guidelines indicate a
minimum required sample size of approximately 77–85 observations for detecting meaningful effects
in multiple regression models.
The final sample of 258 valid responses collected through an online questionnaire substantially
exceeds this minimum requirement, ensuring adequate statistical power, stable parameter estimation,
and robust hypothesis testing within the PLS-SEM framework. Although this sample size is not suf -
ficient to reliably detect small effects ( f² = 0.02), this does not represent a methodological limitation,
as the primary objective of the study is to examine substantively meaningful and practically relevant
relationships rather than marginal effects.
Overall, the sample size employed is methodologically appropriate for PLS-SEM, a variance-based
technique that prioritizes prediction accuracy and explanatory power over global model fit and is well
suited for models with moderate sample sizes and complex mediation structures (Hair et al., 2023).
4.5. Data Collection
This study employed a virtual snowball sampling strategy, a non-probabilistic method commonly
used to reach interconnected participants within digital environments. This technique was appropriate
for identifying and recruiting higher education students enrolled at undergraduate, master’s, and doc -
toral levels with digital experience and awareness of AI in educational and marketing contexts—key to
the focus of the AI4DMS (Artificial Intelligence for Digital Marketing Strategies) framework.
The initial invitation was distributed through academic networks, institutional mailing lists, and
online learning communities affiliated with the AI4DMS initiative. Participation was strictly volun -
tary, anonymous, and without compensation. Prior to answering the questionnaire, participants were
provided with a concise explanation of the key concepts related to AI and digital marketing, ensuring
a consistent baseline of understanding.
From an initial pool of approximately 1,000 invited participants, 500 individuals accessed the
survey, and 258 complete and valid responses were retained for analysis. The instrument was adminis-
tered via Google Forms between January 6 and May 6, 2025.
Thus, the resulting sample reflects the perceptions of digitally active higher education students,
representing a pre-organizational population with incipient exposure to AI-related activities in busi -
ness contexts, offering meaningful insights into pre-organizational perceptions of AI-related capabili-
ties within digital marketing contexts, as conceptualized in the AI4DMS model.

5. Results
This section presents the findings derived from the structural equation modeling using Partial Least
Squares (PLS-SEM) with SmartPLS 4.1.1.2 software. The results are organized into three main com -
ponents: the evaluation of the measurement model, the structural model assessment, and the confirma-
tion of the ex-post model based on empirical data.
5.1. Measurement Model Evaluation
A comprehensive evaluation was conducted using established PLS-SEM guidelines to assess the
quality of the reflective measurement model for the AI4DMS framework (Hair et al., 2023).
Internal consistency reliability was confirmed by examining Cronbach’s alpha ( α) and composite
reliability (CR) values for each construct. All constructs (AIB, AKM, AID, ADS, AIM) exceeded the
recommended threshold of 0.70 (Hair et al., 2023), indicating high internal consistency.
Convergent validity was supported by Average Variance Extracted (A VE) values, all above the
0.50 threshold. This demonstrates that each construct explains more than 50 % of the variance of its
observed indicators, fulfilling the minimum criterion for convergent validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981).
Indicator reliability was evaluated through outer loadings. All retained indicators loaded above
0.70 and were statistically significant at p < .001 based on 5,000 bootstrap samples, confirming the
reliability of each indicator (Hair et al., 2023).
Together, these results confirm that the measurement model demonstrates acceptable levels of reli-
ability and validity, thus meeting the requirements for proceeding with structural model assessment.
Item purification followed established PLS-SEM guidelines (Hair et al., 2023). Indicators with
outer loadings below 0.70 were considered for removal when their exclusion improved composite
reliability (CR), average variance extracted (A VE), and discriminant validity (HTMT). Cross-loadings
were also examined to ensure that each indicator loaded higher on its intended construct. In total, 10
out of 45 indicators were removed due to insufficient outer loadings or cross-loading concerns. Nota -
bly, several behaviorally anchored items (e. g., AIM4, ADS2) were excluded because respondents, as
a pre-organizational population, reported limited direct experience with AI implementation. Conse -
quently, these items showed lower reliability and consistency, reflecting the perception-based nature
of the sample rather than measurement error. Their removal improved the overall robustness of the
measurement model while preserving the conceptual integrity of the constructs. See Table 2.
Discriminant validity was assessed using the Fornell-Larcker criterion and the Heterotrait–Monotrait
(HTMT) ratio. As shown in Table 3, the square roots of the A VEs (diagonal values) are greater than the
inter-construct correlations, satisfying the Fornell-Larcker criterion. Additionally, all HTMT values are
below the conservative threshold of 0.85 (Henseler et al., 2015), confirming discriminant validity.

insights derived from AI-influenced consumer behavior inform ongoing adjustments in AI-enabled
marketing strategies. Overall, the model explains AI-related capability formation and perceived read-
iness as a structured yet adaptive process in which learning and capability development are continu -

exposure to AI-related activities in business contexts.

7. Conclusion
This study set out to explain how AI-related perceptions, knowledge, and analytical–digital skills
interact to shape perceived readiness for the application of artificial intelligence in digital marketing
contexts. By validating and interpreting the AI4DMS framework, the research contributes to both the-
oretical understanding (Scientia) and practical application (Praxis) of AI-driven digital transformation,
in line with the journal’s emphasis on multidisciplinarity and innovation for sustainable development.
Rather than reiterating hypothesis testing, the conclusions synthesize the model’s explanatory insights.
The findings demonstrate that AI-related capability formation at a pre-organizational level is best
understood as a capability-building process, in which perceptions of AI impact initiate learning, learn-
ing enables skill development, and these capabilities support strategic AI applications. Importantly,
AI-influenced consumer decision-making contributes to the perceived refinement of such strategies,
reinforcing a recursive logic of learning and adaptation rather than a linear adoption pathway.
7.1. Theoretical and Practical Contributions
From a theoretical perspective (Scientia), the AI4DMS model advances existing research on digi-
tal transformation by integrating cognitive, behavioral, and technological dimensions within a single
explanatory framework. The study provides empirical insights into how AI-related capability devel -
opment and perceived readiness depend not only on technological availability but also on percep -
tion-driven learning processes within a pre-organizational context, extending prior models that treat
digital transformation as predominantly technical or infrastructural. By conceptualizing consumer
interaction as a feedback mechanism, the framework also contributes to theoretical discussions on
dynamic capabilities and organizational learning in AI-related contexts at a pre-organizational level.
From a practical perspective ( Praxis), the model offers conceptual and educational insights for
organizations and educational institutions seeking to align AI-related capabilities at a pre-organiza -
tional level with human capital development and strategic objectives. The progression from perception
and knowledge to skills, strategic application, and market feedback provides a structured reference for
designing training programs, capability-building initiatives, and AI-supported marketing strategies (at
a perceived and preparatory level). In this regard, the AI4DMS framework supports broader policy and
development priorities related to quality education, innovation, and inclusive economic growth.

tion students across undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral levels, predominantly aged 18–29, resulting
in a relatively homogeneous group in terms of demographic and professional experience. Consequently,
the findings should be interpreted as reflecting pre-organizational stages of AI capability formation,
