Abstract
Amid the global wave of digital transformation, generative artificial intelligence (AIGC) has emerged as a critical
force reshaping the tourism industry. Drawing on platform ecosystem theory as its analytical framework, this study
conducts a qualitative case study of Xi’an Tang Paradise. Based on interviews and multiple sources of textual data,
it develops a four-dimensional analytical framework for AIGC-enabled destination smart marketing, encompassing
intelligent content generation, immersive experience in teraction, data-integrated operations, and collaborative
value ecosystem as four subsystems. The findings reveal that the model operates with AIGC technology as its
infrastructure, connecting multiple participants through a standardized and scalable content supply architecture,
and employs data integration mechanisms as governance tools to coordinate stakeholder behavior. The alignment
among subsystems determines the operational efficacy of  the overall marketing system, while the sustained
functioning of multi-actor participation depends on the effective coordination of divergent interests by governance
mechanisms. This study enriches our understanding of AIGC-driven transformation in destination marketing and
provides a systematic analytical framework for future research.
Keywords: generative artificial intelligence, platform ecosystem, tourism destination marketing
1. Introduction
Amid the global wave of digital transformation, ge nerative artificial intelligence (AIGC) has emerged as a
significant technological force reshaping industries across the board. AIGC refers to the technology and its output
that utilizes deep learning algorithms to automatically generate multimodal digital content—including text, images,
audio, and video. It has evolved rapidly from early text generation to more complex forms such as virtual digital
humans, intelligent narrative systems, and immersive content creation. Its core potential lies in improving service
quality at scale and enabling innovative forms of marketing content that were previously unattainable. As a
quintessential experience economy, the cultural tourism industry faces dual challenges of efficiency and depth in
meeting consumers’ growing demands for personalizatio n, interactivity, and emotional engagement. Traditional
marketing models, which rely heavily on static promotional content and standardized visitor experiences,
increasingly fall short in responding to these evolving expectations. Against this backdrop, integrating AIGC
technology into tourism destination marketing has become not merely an option but a strategic imperative.
The academic research on the application of AIGC and intelligent technologies in tourism has shown a clear
trajectory of evolution, with the research focus shifting from early-stage back-end support functions to front-end
experience creation centered on generative AI. Existing scholars have examined the application of AIGC in tourism
marketing from multiple perspectives, including content generation effectiveness, consumer perception and trust,
immersive experience design, and human-computer interaction modes. In the domain of tourism decision-making,
Seo, Liu, Li, and Lee (2025) conducted a qualitative anal ysis of AI-generated tour ism destination videos and
identified 17 thematic dimensions influencing tourist decision-making. Carvalho, Loureiro, Ivanov, Björk, and
Seyitoğlu (2025) employed an experiment al approach to compare the marke ting effectiveness of AI-generated,
human-generated, and hybrid-generated tourism promotional texts. Despite these advances, significant gaps
remain in the existing literature. First, most studies focus on isolated AIGC applications or the effect of a single
scenario, lacking a holistic understanding of destinatio n marketing systems. Second, existing frameworks are

2. Literature Review
2.1 Research on Artificial Intelligence Generated Content in Tourism
Artificial intelligence (AI) can be defined as a system’s ab ility to correctly interpret external data, to learn from
such data, and to use those learnings to achieve specifi c goals and tasks through flexible adaptation (Kaplan &
Haenlein, 2018). AI-generated content (AIGC) refers to  digital information—including text, images, video, and
audio—that is produced by AI systems (Seo et al., 2025). Its technical capabilities have evolved from single-modal
content generation to more complex forms such as cross-modal understanding and multimodal collaborative
creation. The core promise of AIGC lies in enhancing service quality through the automated production of diverse,
contextually appropriate content and in restructuring the production chain of marketing content with a cost
efficiency that is difficult to achieve through traditional methods.
In the tourism sector, the application of AIGC has expa nded rapidly from early use cases such as intelligent
customer service and automated copywriting to more advanced domains including content generation, immersive
experience design, and personalized marketing promotion. Wang, S., Wang, Q., Cui and Lan (2025), in their
systematic literature review, demonstrated that artificial intelligence technologies have been deeply applied across
multiple core areas of the tourism industry, encompa ssing intelligent customer service, personalized itinerary
planning, precision recommendation systems, dynamic pricing mechanisms, and diversified content generation. In
the domain of destination visual content production, Song, Wang, Guo and Law (2025), drawing on schema theory,
conducted four experimental studies and found that AI painting-enhanced city landmark images significantly
increased consumers’ travel intentions compared to original images. In the domain of travel information content,
Wong, Lai, and Lin (2025) employed a mixed-method approach and found that tourists perceived human-generated
travel plans as more reliable than GenAI-generated travel plans. Florido-Benítez (2024) demonstrates that GenAI
enables tourism organizations to achieve unprecedented levels of hyper-segmentation and hyper-personalization
by creating tailored content in real-time with individu al scalability. A recent systematic review (Zhang, Yi, &
Gursoy, 2025) reveals that existing research on GenAI in hospitality and tourism has predominantly examined its
impact on operational efficiency from industry prac titioners perspectives’, while critical consumer-level
concerns—including ethics, psychology, social dynamics, and security—remain significantly underexplored.
However, the above studies predominantly focus on the eff ect of a single content type  or a specific application
scenario of AIGC, and thus lack sufficient explanation of how AIGC can be integrated as a systematic tool into the
overall destination marketing architecture, and how differen t application dimensions can interact synergistically.
Moreover, most existing studies adopt a single tourist perspective, but pay insufficient attention to how destination
management practitioners systematically embed and employ  AIGC in practice, as well as the mechanisms and
challenges involved in this process.

Smart marketing for tourism destinations emphasizes the use of technologies including the Internet of Things, big
data, and artificial intelligence to enable data-driven, automated, and precision-oriented decision-making across

the entire marketing chain. Its essence lies in the para digm shift toward “data-intelligence-driven” marketing
activities, a transformation closely tied to the broader empowerment of the cultural tourism sector by the digital
economy. Existing scholars have examined the application of smart technologies in destination marketing from
multiple perspectives, including technology adoption path ways, tourist experience reconstruction, destination
governance models, and ecosystem construction. Research on how artificial intelligence and digital technologies
reshape destination brand communication, tourist deci sion-making, and value creation processes has also
accumulated valuable insights in areas such as persona lized services, real-time in teraction, and multi-actor
coordination.
The evolution of smart marketing has undergone a transition from digital marketing to intelligent marketing—a
shift that involves not only the upgrading of technological tools but also a deeper transformation in marketing
philosophy from “traffic acquisition” to “value deepening.” At the technological enablement level, Florido-Benítez
and Del Alcázar (2024), in their comprehensive literature review of smart tourism destinations, demonstrated that
artificial intelligence has become a core tool for destin ations to develop new business models and marketing
services, enhance tourist experiences, and improve sales performance. At the destination management
transformation level, Bingöl and Ya ng (2025), based on qualitative data from ten U.S. cities, proposed a smart
destination model to guide destinations in adopting smart technologies and artificial intelligence initiatives. At the
ecosystem construction level, Fatma and Bhatt (2026) pr oposed a multi-level concep tual framework for smart
tourism ecosystems, elucidating how human and non-human actors—including tourists, businesses, policymakers,
and technology—interact and co-create value across the pre-visit, on-site, and post-visit stages. At the generative
AI marketing application level, Bilgihan, Dogru, Ha nks, Line, and Mody (2024) proposed the GAI marketing
model, systematically exploring the adoption factors, marketing stage delineation, and downstream outcomes of
generative AI in hospitality and tourism marketing.
The above studies have revealed, from various angles, th e application value of smart technologies and artificial
intelligence in destination marketing. Their contributions span operational efficiency improvement, ecosystem
construction, management paradigm transformation, and consumer behavior shaping, thereby providing
multifaceted theoretical resources fo r understanding the digital and in telligent transformation of tourism
destination marketing. However, most studies either focus on the independent effect of a single type of
technological tool or a single marketing link, or treat artificial intelligence as a supplementary back-end operational
tool. There remains insufficient explanation of how artificial intelligence (especially AIGC) can be integrated as a
systemic force into the overall architecture of destination marketing, and how different technological applications
can form synergistic effects.
3. Method
3.1 Theoretical Foundation and Case Selection
This study adopts platform ecosystem theory as its core analytical framework. A platform ecosystem refers to an
organizational structure in which a set of interdependent actors coordinate through shared digital infrastructure,
interact around a common value proposition, and are governed by specific governance mechanisms. Tiwana (2014),
in his seminal work, argued that the evolution of platform ecosystems cannot rely on top-down management
directives, but must be guided by the synergistic ali gnment of architecture and governance mechanisms. Parker,
Van Alstyne, and Choudary (2016) further defined a platform as “a digital organizational form that facilitates
interaction and value exchange between producers and consumers.” They noted that the core functions of a
platform include matching supply and demand, reducing transaction costs, leveraging data-driven decision-making,
and stimulating user participation in content creation to  unlock new value. Kretschmer, Leiponen, Schilling, and
Vasudeva (2020), from an organizational theory perspective, contended that the successful operation of a platform
requires the coordination of multiple participants. They further emphasized that the interests of these participants
are not naturally aligned, which makes governance and coordination mechanisms a central issue in platform
strategy.
Building upon this foundation, this study further extends platform theory by drawing on the service ecosystem
perspective. A service ecosystem is defined as “a relatively self-contained and self-adjusting network of resource-
integrating actors connected by shared institutional arra ngements and mutual value creation” (Vargo & Lusch,
2015). While platform ecosystem theory focuses analytical attention on the platform owner’s architectural design
and governance strategies, the service ecosystem perspective places greater emphasis on resource integration and
institutional coordination among multiple actors around a common value proposition. The combination of these
two perspectives helps to conceptualize the AIGC-enabled marketing system as a socio-technical system in which
multiple actors—including destination management organizations, technology providers, content creators, local

merchants, and tourists—participate and co-evolve. Within this system, AIGC technology is no longer merely a
tool for content production, but a structural element embedded in the institutional arrangements and value creation
logic of the entire system.
Based on this theoretical consideration, this study select s Xi’an Tang Paradise as its case site. The selection is
based on three main considerations. First, the case is highly representative. As the only cultural tourism landmark
in China built around the theme of Tang Dynasty culture, its digital transformation has been designated as a
national “5G+Smart Tourism” application pilot project, ma king it a benchmark in the integration of culture and
tourism with technology. Seco nd, the case offers rich informational de nsity. In recent years, Tang Paradise has
systematically deployed AI virtual humans, AR interactive experiences, and data-driven loyalty platforms, forming
a relatively comprehensive AIGC marketing application matrix. This provides ample empirical material for
examining the synergistic relationships among various dimensions. Third, the case is theoretically significant from
a platform ecosystem perspective. Its AIGC marketing practice involves interactions and collaborations among
multiple actors—including destination management orga nizations, technology partners, content creators, local
merchants, and tourists—making it an ideal analytical context for examining, from a platform ecosystem
perspective, the operational mechanisms of AIGC-enabled destination marketing. For these reasons, Tang Paradise
provides an ideal analytical setting for investigating the operational model and internal logic of AIGC-enabled
destination marketing.
3.2 Data Sources and Collection
This study adopts a multi-source data collection strategy, with the data collection period spanning from April 2026
to June 2026. The research team conducted two rounds of field visits to Tang Paradise—the first in April 2026 and
the second in May 2026. A total of 20 interviews were  completed, consisting of 12 on-site interviews and 8
supplementary online interviews, with each interview la sting between 20 and 60 minutes. The interviewees
encompassed three groups: tourists, local merchants, a nd destination management practitioners. The interviews
focused on the respondents’ role perceptions, specific practices, and subjective ev aluations regarding AIGC-
enabled digital experience projects. All interviews were  recorded and transcribed with the consent of the
participants.
In addition to the interview data, the study collected three types of supplementary materials. The first type
comprises official textual materials, including project descriptions, press releases, and strategic planning
documents published through Tang Paradise’s official WeChat public account and mini-program. These materials
reflect the destination management organization’s goal se tting and self-evaluation regarding AIGC applications.
The second type comprises authoritative media reports, including in-depth interviews and feature stories on Tang
Paradise’s managers, technology partners, and local merchants from mainstream media outlets such as Xinhua
News Agency and People’s Daily. These reports provid e direct accounts from vari ous participating actors
regarding AIGC marketing practices. The third type comprises government documents and industry reports,
including policy documents and work reports issued by the Ministry of Culture and Tourism and the Shaanxi
Provincial Department of Culture and Tourism. These materials reflect the institutional support from the policy-
making level for the direction of smart tourism developm ent and AIGC applications. The multi-source nature of
the data not only helps capture the operational realities of AIGC-enabled destination marketing from different
angles, but also lays the foundation for triangulation in subs equent analysis. The inte rview data provide each
actor’s perceptions and evaluations of practice, while the supplementary materials offer institutional context,
objective facts, and third-party observations. Together, they constitute the empirical basis of this study.
To enhance the credibility of the research findings, this study conducted cross-verification between the interview
data and the three types of supplementary materials. Data from different sources were mutually supplemented and
cross-examined in the analysis. This multi-source cross-validation strategy is intended to ensure that the research
findings are not based on isolated judgments from a single source, but are grounded in multiple perspectives that
mutually support one another.

4.1 Intelligent Content Generation: Building the Platform’s Content Supply Infrastructure
In a platform ecosystem, content supply constitutes the in frastructure layer that connects the supply side and the
demand side. Prior to the introduction of AIGC, content production at Tang Paradise relied primarily on periodic
creation by professional teams, supplemented by sporadic user-generated content. This model struggled to meet
the growing demands of digital marketing in terms of both production capacity and timeliness. The introduction
of AIGC fundamentally transformed this landscape. By leveraging large language models to equip its AI virtual
human with content generation capabilities, the destin ation shifted the production of marketing materials—
including images, videos, and interactive scripts—from labor-intensive to automated and scalable processes,
substantially reducing the marginal cost of content production. On this basis, the destination further extended
content production to the tourist side and incorporated  external creative forces through creator  recruitment
programs, transitioning content supply from single-institution output to multi-actor collaboration.
From a platform architecture perspective, the core significance of this transformation lies in the establishment of
a standardized and scalable content supply infrastructure. AIGC tools, as core components, provide unified content
production capabilities for the entire marketing system. M eanwhile, the role of professional creators has shifted
from direct production to that of “cultural gatekeepers” and “creative architects,” responsible for defining cultural
tone, setting creative boundaries, and auditing content quality. At the same time, tourists participate in content
production through low-threshold AI creation tools, transitioning from consumers to contributors within the
content ecosystem. Through this process, a tripartite  content supply model has emerged—one in which the
destination sets the cultural direction, AIGC enables large-scale production, and users facilitate dissemination.
4.2 Immersive Experience Interaction: Restructuring the Platform’s User Interface
Immersive experience interaction constitutes the core interface through which the platform connects with users on
the demand side. In Tang Paradise’s practice, this interface has been restructured into a continuum spanning three
stages: pre-visit, on-site, and post-visit. During the pre-visit stage, AI virtual humans establish emotional
connections with potential tourists through social media, lowering the threshold for platform user acquisition.
During the on-site stage, virtual human guided tours and AR scene overlays transform static landscapes into
dynamic narratives, shifting the tourist experience from passive viewing to active exploration. AR technology
extends the platform’s service boundaries by converting physical space into an interactive digital interface. During
the post-visit stage, the generation and sharing of digital souvenirs extend the lifecycle of the experience, creating
a cycle of “experience—sharing—customer acquisition.”
From a platform perspective, immersive experience interaction serves as the vehicle through which the platform’s
value proposition is materialized—translating AIGC’s technological capabilities into user-perceivable experiential
value. From a multi-actor perspective, this interface simultaneously serves both the demand side and the supply

immersive experience interaction the core interface th rough which the platform coordinates both supply and

4.3 Data-Integrated Operations: The Governance Tool of the Platform
Data-integrated operations serve as a governance tool within Tang Paradise’s AIGC marketing system. Through
omnichannel touchpoints—including mini-programs, smart sensors, payment terminals, and Wi-Fi probes—the
system collects real-time behavioral data on tourists, such as spatial trajectories, content preferences, dwell time,
and consumption records. This data constitutes the foundational resource for pl atform governance, enabling
platform owners to “see” participants’ behavioral patterns and providing a basis for governance decisions. Based
on this collection, the data platform further performs three governance functions. First, dynamic resource allocation
leverages real-time heat maps and predictive models to automatically allocate network bandwidth, security
personnel, and merchant service resources, thereby impr oving operational efficiency. Second, precision supply-
demand matching identifies tourists’ locations and preferences to deliver personalized offers from nearby
merchants through virtual humans or messaging, reducing information asymmetry between supply and demand.
Third, performance attribution and strategy iteration tr ack the performance of different AIGC content versions,
enabling marketing strategy adjustments to shift from experience-based judgment to data-driven decision-making.
From a platform governance perspective, data-integrated operations essentially transform behavioral information
into actionable governance resources. They serve both as the basis for monitoring and evaluating participant
behavior, and as the mechanism for coordinating stakeholder interests and optimizing resource allocation. One
destination manager noted in an interview that the core va lue of the data platform lies in “giving us a basis for
decisions.” This statement precisely cap tures the essence of data as a governa nce tool: it shifts platform owners
from managing by experience to governing by data.
4.4 Collaborative Value Ecosystem: Multi-Actor Participation and Platform Governance
The collaborative value ecosystem concerns the interaction and coordination mechanisms among multiple actors
within the platform ecosystem. Tang Paradise’s AIGC ma rketing system involves five categories of actors:
destination management organizations, technology providers, content creators, local merchants, and tourists. These
actors form interdependent complementary relationships through the platform. The destination management
organization assumes the functions of architectural design and rule-making. Technology providers constitute the
platform’s technological in frastructure. Content creators supply cultu ral and creative content. Local merchants
serve as the final monetizers of the platform’s commercia l value. Tourists function as both the core of the
experience and the producers of data. The “Tang Paradi se Benefit Card” serves as an institutional vehicle
connecting all parties, integrating ticketing, consumption benefits, and behavioral data into a unified interface,
thereby providing an actionable framework for benefit distribution.
However, the key to platform governance lies not in the design of structure, but in how diverging interests are
coordinated. Interview data reveal that merchants’ perceptions of digital operating benefits are differentiated—
some small and medium-sized merchants feel that the retu rns from participation are “ not as high as expected.”
Managers also acknowledged that balancing technological investment with commercial returns, and ensuring that
small and medium-sized merchants genuinely benefit, re main ongoing challenges. These divergences confirm a
central proposition of platform ecosyst ems: the interests of participants are not naturally aligned, and the
effectiveness of governance mechanisms  determines whether the ecosystem can  sustain itself. In the context of
AIGC enablement, this governance challenge has not disa ppeared; rather, it has become more refined as data
capabilities have strengthened. Platform owners possess more powerful coordination tools, yet they also bear more
complex responsibilities for balancing interests.
5. Discussion
5.1 Conclusion
5.1.1 Four Subsystems Constitute the Platform Ecosystem
AIGC-enabled smart marketing for tourism destinations can be conceptualized as a platform ecosystem composed
of four subsystems: content production, experience interaction, data operations, and ecosystem coordination. The
system operates with AIGC technology as its infrast ructure, connecting multiple participants through a
standardized and scalable content supp ly architecture, using immersive expe rience interfaces as the vehicle for
user access and value delivery, and employing data integr ation mechanisms as govern ance tools to coordinate

stakeholder behavior and optimize resource allocation. Ultimately, it forms a service ecosystem in which multiple
actors interact around a shared value proposition. The four subsystems are characterized by synergistic alignment:
the quality of content generation affects the effectiveness of experience interaction; the data feedback from
experience interaction drives the iteration of operational strategies; and improvements in operational efficiency, in
turn, support the expansion of the content ecosystem and the optimization of governance structures.
5.1.2 Multi-Actor Participation Depends on Governance Coordination
AIGC-enabled smart marketing presupposes multi-actor participation, yet its sustained operation depends on the
effective coordination of divergent interests through platform governance mechanisms. The AIGC marketing
system at Tang Paradise involves five categories of actors: destination management organizations, technology
providers, content creators, local merchants, and tour ists. These actors form interdependent complementary
relationships around AIGC technology. However, the interests and participation capacities of these actors are not
naturally aligned. Merchants focus on operational revenue and foot traffic conversion, managers emphasize return
on investment and brand enhancement, and tourists prioritize experience quality and emotional satisfaction. These
differentiated demands constitute the practical tension facing platform governance. Interview data reveal that some
small and medium-sized merchants perceive the benefits of digital operations as “not as high as expected,” and
managers acknowledge challenges such as extended payback periods for technological investment and uneven
benefits among small and medium-sized merchants. This finding suggests that the synergistic effects of multi-actor
participation are not automatically realized, but requir e sustained maintenance through institutionalized benefit
coordination mechanisms.
5.1.3 System Sustainability Depends on Dynamic Balance
The transformation of destination marketing enabled by AIGC is a continuously evolving dynamic process, the
long-term viability of which depends on a dynamic balance between efficiency enhancement and relationship
maintenance. The embedding of AIGC not only alters the cost structure of content production and the modalities
of experience delivery, but also reshapes the interacti on rules among participants and the patterns of value flow.
However, if efficiency gains driven by technology are not accompanied by in stitutional safeguards for the rights
and interests of all participants—particularly small and medium-sized merchants—the social sustainability of the
ecosystem may be undermined. The findings indicate that the interest differentiation and governance tensions
revealed in the collaborative value ecosystem dimension represent the practical challenges to this dynamic balance.
Therefore, the long-term sustainability of AIGC-enabled smart marketing depends not only on the sophistication
of its technological architecture, but  also on whether platform governan ce can establish effective coordination
mechanisms between efficiency pursuit and equity assurance.
5.2 Theoretical Contributions
Using platform ecosystem theory as its analytical lens, this study conducts a qualitative case study of Xi’an Tang
Paradise and systematically examines the operational model and underlying mechanisms of AIGC-enabled smart
marketing for tourism destinations. The study makes two primary theoretical contributions.
First, this study extends platform ecosystem theory to the context of AIGC-enabled tourism destination marketing.
Existing platform research has predominantly focused on in dustries such as e-commerce  and social media, with
limited attention given to tourism destinations as a distinctive type of platform. Through the Tang Paradise case,
this study reveals how tourism destinations can build platform ecosystems using AIGC technology as infrastructure.
A longitudinal evolutionary analysis (Ivasciuc, 2026) reveals that tourism platforms have progressively
transformed from digital infrastructure ecosystems to adaptive coordination systems and, ultimately, to AI-driven
intelligent ecosystem orchestrators, reflecting a fundamenta l shift in their role from facilitating transactions to
orchestrating interconnected stakeholder relationships . It identifies four core subsystems—content supply
architecture, user interface experien ce, data governance tools, and mu lti-actor coordination structure—and
elucidates their interrelationships. This provides a new theoretical perspective for understanding how AIGC can
be embedded in and restructure destination marketing systems.
Second, this study develops a four-dimen sional analytical framework that offe rs a systematic analytical tool for
understanding AIGC-enabled transformation in destination marketing. Existing research has largely proceeded
along a single theoretical pathway, focusing either on technology acceptance or content effectiveness, and lacks a
holistic account of how AIGC is embedded in the overall marketing architecture of destinations. By extending the
unit of analysis from isolated technological applications  to system-level organizational capability restructuring,
the framework proposed in this study reveals the synergistic alignment among the four dimensions, thereby
providing an operational analytical tool for future research.Hu and Lin (2025) examined GAI’s functional
integration in tourism product development from dual organizational and tourist perspectives, demonstrating that

5.3 Managerial Implications
The findings of this study offer three practical implications for the tourism industry.
First, destination managers should shift from isolated technology adoption to systematic framework construction.
The Tang Paradise case demonstrates that the effectiveness of AIGC marketing does not depend on the introduction
of any single technology, but rather on the degree of alignment among the four subsystems: content production,
experience interaction, data operations, and ecosystem co ordination. Managers should plan the priorities and
synergy paths of AIGC applications from a holistic architectural perspective, avoiding the piecemeal deployment
of isolated technologies.
Second, for destination managers and technology devel opers alike, the governance function of data-integrated
operations should be positioned alongside content innovation as a strategic priority. The findings indicate that the
core value of the data platform lies not only in enabling precision marketing, but also in providing platform owners
with governance instruments for coordinating stakeholder interests and optimizing resource allocation. In practice,
attention should be devoted to building data collection and analytical capabilities, thereby upgrading the data
platform from a “decision support tool” to a “governance infrastructure.”
Third, policymakers and platform operators should pay attention to the quality of participation and benefit
protection for small and medium-sized merchants within the platform ecosystem. The findings reveal that, in the
context of AIGC enablement, enhanced technological  capacity does not automati cally eliminate interest
differentiation. In fact, the asymmetric distribution of  data capabilities may exacerbate  structural inequities.
Policymakers should establish mechanisms for digital skills training and benefit distribution safeguards targeting
small and medium-sized merchants. Platform operators, in designing data rules and benefit allocation
arrangements, should balance efficiency and fairness to ensure the long-term sustainability of the ecosystem.
5.4 Research Limitations and Prospects
This study has several limitations that also point to avenues for future research. First, while the single-case design
enables an in-depth examination of the operational mechanisms, the external validity of the findings awaits further
testing through multi-case comparisons. Second, the cross-s ectional data employed in this study cannot capture
the dynamic evolution of AIGC applications over time. Future research could adopt a longitudinal design to trace
the evolutionary patterns of AIGC-enabled marketing systems across different stages of development. Third, the
study primarily focuses on supply-side pattern extraction, with relatively limited analys is of tourist perceptions
and behavioral responses. Subsequent research could complement the current framework by incorporating
quantitative measurements from the tourist perspective.
