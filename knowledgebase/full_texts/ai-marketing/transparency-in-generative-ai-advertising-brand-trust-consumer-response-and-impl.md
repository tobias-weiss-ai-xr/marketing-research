Abstract: The rapid integration of generative artificial intelligence into advertising has intensified
questions of transparency, consumer trust, and ethical marketing leadership, particularly where firms must
balance technological efficiency with responsible commun ication and informed consumer choice. Existing
research has not sufficiently disentangled the effects of actual content authorship, artificial intelligence
disclosure, and brand positioning within one experimental framework, leaving a gap in the business ethics
literature on responsible artificial intelligence governance. This study examines how disclosure of generative
artificial intelligence authorship affects brand trust and purchase inten tion in automotive advertising,
whether these effects vary by content source and brand positioning, and whether human - and machine -
generated copy differs in perceived linguistic quality when authorship is concealed. The analysis is based on
a two-by-three-by-two between-subjects factorial experiment conducted in 2026 with 184 participants aged
18–45 recruited online in Poland, who viewed BMW or Tesla advertisements authored by a human, OpenAI
GPT-4o, or Google Gemini 1.5 Pro under unlabelled or artificial-intelligence-labelled conditions. The empirical
strategy combined reliability analysis, Type III factorial ordinary least -squares analysis of variance,
heteroskedasticity-consistent robust inference, estimated marginal means, Holm -adjusted simple effects,
difference-in-differences contrasts, and supplementary equivalence testing in Python 3.13.5. Under concealed-
authorship conditions, perceived linguistic quality did not differ significantly across human, OpenAI GPT -4o,
and Google Gemini 1.5 Pro copy, with F(2, 89) = 0.445 and p = 0.642, while equivalence tests did not establish
formal equivalence. Artificial intelligence disclosure significantly reduced brand trust, with F(1, 172) = 9.11,
p = 0.003, and ηp² = 0.050, and purchase intention, with F(1, 172) = 14.02, p < 0.001, and ηp² = 0.075; the
author-by-disclosure interaction was also significant, with F(2, 172) = 3.20 and p = 0.043. The strongest
disclosure penalties occurred for BMW advertisements generated by OpenAI GPT -4o (−1.275, Holm-adjusted
p = 0.002) and Google Gemini 1.5 Pro (−1.111, Holm-adjusted p = 0.011), while the pooled genuine-artificial-
intelligence difference-in-differences contrast showed a larger penalty for BMW than Tesla, with an estimate
of −0.791, a 95% confidence interval from −1.491 to −0.091, and p = 0.027. These findings extend business
ethics and ethical marketing leadership research by showing that transparent artificial intelligence
disclosure can impose measurable trust and behavioral costs even when linguis tic quality is not detectably
inferior, supporting future work on brand –technology congruity, disclosure design, human oversight, and
responsible artificial intelligence governance.

INTRODUCTION
The rapid diffusion of generative artificial
intelligence into marketing communication has
transformed AI -generated advertising from an
experimental practice into a mainstream managerial
issue, while simultaneously intensifying concerns
about transparency,  authenticity, and consumer
trust. The Interactive Advertising Bureau (IAB)
reported in 2026 that 83% of advertising executives
had already deployed AI in the creative process, up
from 60% in its 2024 study, illustrating how quickly
generative AI is becomi ng embedded in advertising
production. At the same time, consumer acceptance
has not necessarily progressed at the same rate as
industry adoption: IAB research continues to identify
a perception gap between advertisers and younger
consumers, particularly r egarding the authenticity
and desirability of AI -generated advertising
(Williamson et al., 2026).
Similar concerns emerge from Deloitte's 2024
Connected Consumer survey of 3,857 US consumers:
among respondents familiar with or using generative
AI, 70% stated that AI -generated content made it
more difficult to trust information encountered
online, while  84% supported mandatory labeling of
AI-generated content. These developments indicate
that the managerial challenge is no longer limited to
whether generative AI can increase the efficiency and
scalability of advertising production; it increasingly
concerns whether firms can exploit these
technological advantages while preserving
authenticity, consumer confidence, and informed
choice (Arbanas et al., 2024).
Transparency has therefore become a central
dimension of responsible AI governance and ethical
marketing leadership. Evidence from the World
Federation of Advertisers (2026)  has shown that
78% of major global brands already use AI-generated
or AI -enhanced content in consumer -facing
marketing, while 82% regard transparency about AI
use as essential for protecting brand reputation and
79% consider it important for maintaining consumer
trust. This concern extends beyond the advertising
industry: Edelman's (2025)  Trust Barometer
reported that only 44% of respondents globally felt
comfortable with businesses using AI, emphasizing
the importance of transparency, fairness, and clearly
communicated uses in building acceptance.
The regulatory environment is moving in the
same direction, with the European Union's AI Act
introducing transparency requirements for certain
forms of AI -generated and manipulated content and
reinforcing the broader principle that users should be
able to recognize when AI materially shapes the
content they encounter ( European Commission,
2024). Against this background, investigating how
the explicit disclosure of generative AI authorship
affects brand trust and purchase intention, and
whether these effects vary with actual authorship
and brand positioning, is particularly relevant to
business ethics and leadership.
It addresses a practical governance dilemma faced
by contemporary marketing leaders: how to
reconcile the efficiency and creative potential of
generative AI with transparency, consumer
autonomy, and the long -term preservation of brand
trust. The growing reliance on generative AI in
marketing also raises a broader question about the
ethical boundaries of managerial decision -making in
technology-mediated business environments.
Decisions concerning the generation, modification,
and disclosure of synthetic content are ultimately
organizational decisions: firms determine where AI is
introduced into communication processes, what
degree of human oversight is retained, how
responsibility for the resulting messages is allocated,
and what information about technol ogical
involvement is communicated to consumers.
Generative AI adoption in advertising should
therefore be considered not only as an operational
innovation but also as an issue of managerial
responsibility and ethical leadership.
As AI applications increasingly influence
marketing performance and customer relationships,
organizations face the challenge of ensuring that
technological efficiency remains compatible with
trustworthy business conduct and responsible
oversight (Do Hai & Le, 2026; Nguyen et al., 2026a).
This challenge is particularly important because
transparency can create a tension between
immediate commercial objectives and broader
ethical responsibilities towards consumers. From a
purely performance-oriented perspective, firms may

communicate the use of AI in a manner that enables

about the messa ges they receive. Recent research
linking AI -generated communication with
authenticity, perceived ethics, and consumer trust
has reinforced the importance of examining this
tension as a substantive business issue ( Avidar &
Mashiah, 2026; Khalfallah & Keller, 2025 ; Yıldırım &

The leadership dimension becomes especially
salient when the potential benefits and reputational
risks of generative AI coexist. Marketing leaders
increasingly have to decide not simply whether AI
should be adopted, but under what governance
arrangements i t should be incorporated into
organizational communication.
Such decisions encompass the degree of human
supervision, accountability for generated content,
verification procedures, disclosure practices, and the
alignment of AI use with the values communicated by
the organization. Trustworthy AI adoption therefore
depends partly on the organi zational conditions
surrounding technology use rather than exclusively
on the capabilities of the technology itself (Dwivedi et
al., 2026 ). This shifts the managerial debate from
technological substitution towards responsible
integration: the relevant question is not merely
whether an AI system can perform a communication
task successfully, but whether its deployment is
compatible with the organi zation's responsibilities
towards consumers and with the credibility of the
brand relationship.
Brand context further complicates this ethical and
managerial problem. Consumers do not encounter
AI-generated communication independently of their
existing expectations concerning an organization.
The same technological practice may be interpreted
differently when it is associated with a brand built
around technological innovation than when it is
associated with a brand whose symbolic value is
more strongly connected with heritage,
craftsmanship, or established human expertise.
Consequently, responsible AI governance cannot
necessarily be reduced to a uniform disclosure rule
detached from brand identity and stakeholder
expectations.
The rapid expansion of generative AI therefore
creates a need for leadership approaches that
combine technological innovation with transparency,
accountability, and sensitivity to the relational
foundations of brand trust. Understanding consumer
responses to disclosed AI use is particularly
important in this context because it can clarify the
ethical and managerial tensions that organizations
face when transparency obligations, technological
efficiency, and market acceptance do not necessarily
point in the same direction.
LITERATURE REVIEW
The rapid incorporation of generative artificial
intelligence into marketing communication has
shifted the research agenda from questions of
technical capability towards questions of
transparency, authenticity, and ethical responsibility.
A central concern is that AI -enabled personalization
can move along a continuum from legitimate tailoring
to misrepresentation when consumers cannot clearly
identify how content was produced or why it was
presented to them, making disclosure an important
element of responsible communication rather than
merely an informational detail ( Anderson & Nguyen,
2026). This concern is reinforced by broader
communication research that treats trust and
transparency as core ethical challenges in both the
creation and consumption of AI -generated content
(Avidar & Mashiah, 2026).
Advertising research has therefore increasingly
examined whether explicit disclosure of AI
authorship changes consumers’ evaluation of
persuasive messages, including prosocial advertising
and other contexts in which perceptions of

Similar issues are evident in virtual -influencer

ethical conduct are closely linked to consumer trust

influencers, for whom disclosure can influence

Within this transparency debate, trust emerges as

behavior. Research on AI -generated social media
content places consumer trust at the center of brand

may influence not only message evaluation but also
perceptions of the organization behind the message
(Mohammad Rahall, 2025).
More recent work has directly linked AI disclosure
to purchase intention via trust, suggesting that
transparency is not merely a formal disclosure
obligation but rather part of the relational process by
which consumers interpret a brand’s communication
practices (Nguyen et al., 2026b ). Verification signals
have similarly been examined as mechanisms for
strengthening trust and purchase intentions in AI -
generated advertising, indicating that consumers
may respond not only to whether AI is used but also
to the credibility cues surrounding that use (Sarran &
Datt, 2026 ). Authenticity is closely related to this
process because consumers may judge AI -generated
content by whether it appears genuine, appropriate,
and consistent with expected marketing
communication, rather than by its technical quality
alone (Shetty et al., 2026).
Research distinguishing user -generated, brand -
generated, and AI -generated content has likewise
suggested that digital brand engagement depends on
the perceived source and nature of communication,
reinforcing the importance  of authorship as a
psychologically meaningful cue ( Gabelaia, 2026 ).
Related research on AI-enabled ethical consumerism
has likewise link ed next-generation technologies
with trust formation among younger consumers
(NaveenKumar et al., 2026).
Consumer response to AI -generated marketing is
also shaped by the fit between technological cues,
message characteristics, and the symbolic context in
which the communication appears. Schema -based
research comparing AI and human models in fashion
advertising indicates that consumer responses can
depend on whether the perceived source of the
message is congruent with expectations about the
brand and the advertising context ( Kim, 2026 ). A
related argument appears in research on AI -
generated emotional advertising, where matching AI
use to the advertisement's thematic characteristics
has been examined as a way to overcome negative or
uncanny reactions (Choi & Magee, 2026).
Authorship disclosure also affects perceptions of
AI-generated graphic design, further demonstrating
that the same creative output may be evaluated
differently once consumers become aware of its
origin ( Ekart & Ahtik, 2026 ). AI -related purchase
decisions have likewise been linked to consumer
attitudes, reinforcing the need to distinguish
technical exposure to AI from the attitudinal
processes through which purchase intentions are
formed (Hoa, 2025).
Personalization research further demonstrates
that AI -powered marketing can influence consumer
decision-making processes (Lad et al., 2026). Broader
evidence has also show n that digital marketing
practices can materially shape purchasing behavior
across contemporary consumer settings (Bacik et al.,
2025). Taken together, these studies suggest that AI
disclosure may operate as a context-dependent signal
whose consequences can vary according to source
cues, consumer characteristics, and brand meaning
rather than representing a universally positive or
negative treatment.
From a managerial perspective, the growing use
of AI in marketing creates both performance
opportunities and governance responsibilities.

Broader assessments of AI applications in

require governance rather than purely technical

evidence from the creative economy has similarly
documented simultaneous opportunities and
implementation challenges associated with AI

Evidence from small and medium -sized
enterprises has shown that AI applications are
already being assessed in relation to marketing
performance, illustrating the organizational pressure
to translate AI capabilities into measurable business
outcomes ( Do Hai & Le, 2026 ). At the same time,
research on the adoption of trustworthy AI
demonstrates that managerial acceptance of AI
depends on confidence in the technology and the
institutional conditions surrounding its use, making
transparency and responsible oversight relevant
beyond consumer-facing advertising alone ( Dwivedi
et al., 2026). The literature on AI, big data, and digital
consumer analysis has also show n an expanding
methodological toolkit for understanding behavioral
responses.
However, this expansion increases the need for
careful interpretation of algorithmically influenced
consumer data ( Theodorakopoulos &
Theodoropoulou, 2026 ). In more immersive
environments, generative AI is expected to intensify
personalization, interactivity, and storytelling,
further increasing the importance of managerial
rules governing how synthetic content is created and
disclosed (Tomminen et al., 2026).
Similar governance concerns also appear in
research on AI -enhanced customer relationship
management, where technological adoption is
increasingly linked to trust, strategic capability, and
organizational responsibility ( Nguyen et al., 2026a ).
Practitioner-oriented discussion likewise identifies
trust-building as a central organizational challenge in
an AI-mediated future (Papp, 2026).
The emerging literature therefore points towards
a shift from evaluating whether generative AI can
produce effective marketing content to evaluating
how organizations should govern its use responsibly.
Research on consumer attitudes towards generative
AI has shown that marketing strategies  increasingly
need to reflect heterogeneous consumer perceptions
rather than assume uniform acceptance of AI -
mediated communication ( Kheiri et al., 2026 ).
Systematic evidence on generative AI and customer
engagement has indicated that affective and
behavioral outcomes, disclosure effects, and human -
in-the-loop moderation are becoming interconnected
research streams, thereby supporting a more
integrated view of AI-enabled marketing governance
(Pokhrel & Somasiri, 2026 ). Collectively, the
literature establishes that transparency, trust,
authenticity, consumer response, and organizational
oversight are closely related dimensions of
generative AI marketing. However, they are often
examined separately or in contexts such a s social
media, virtual influencers, graphic design, e -
commerce, or digital services.
The present study addresses this gap by
integrating actual content authorship, explicit AI
disclosure, brand trust, purchase intention, blind
linguistic-quality evaluation, and brand positioning
into a single factorial experiment, while interpreting
disclosure as a transparency mechanism with
implications for ethical marketing leadership. In this
way, the study moves beyond the question of whether
AI can generate persuasive advertising. Instead, it
examines how transparent use of AI interacts with
consumer trust and brand meaning, thereby
contributing to the literature on responsible AI
governance in marketing.

effects vary according to the actual source of the
advertising copy and the symbolic positioning of the
advertised brand, and whether human - and AI -
generated advertisements differ in perceived

METHODOLOGY
Research Design and Analytical Framework
The empirical component of the study was
organized as a quantitative 2 × 3 × 2 between -
subjects factorial experiment. The design examined
whether consumer responses to automotive
advertising depended jointly on the advertised
brand, the actual source of the advertising copy, and
the presence or absence of an explicit artificial -
intelligence (AI) authorship disclosure.
Disclosure was treated as a transparency
mechanism through which the use of generative AI
becomes visible to consumers. This framing is
relevant to business ethics and ethical marketing
leadership because the disclosure decision affects the
information ava ilable to consumers when they
evaluate the trustworthiness and legitimacy of
organizational communication.
The three experimental factors were brand,
content author, and disclosure. Brand had two levels:
BMW and Tesla.
The two brands were used as theoretically
contrasting exemplars rather than as a
representative sample of the automotive sector:
BMW was treated as a legacy -oriented premium
brand associated with engineering heritage and
craftsmanship, whereas Tesla was tr eated as an
innovation-oriented premium brand associated with
software, automation, and technological disruption.
The content author had three levels: human -
authored copy, GPT -4o-generated copy, and Gemini
1.5 Pro -generated copy. Disclosure had two levels:
unlabelled and AI -labelled. Crossing these factors
produced 12 independent experimental conditions.
A between-subjects design was used so that each
participant evaluated only one advertising stimulus,
thereby limiting learning, comparison , and demand
effects. The design also included placebo -label
conditions in which human -authored copy was
presented with an AI-authorship disclosure.
These conditions provided a counterfactual for a
general label-stigma effect and allowed the disclosure
effect to be distinguished from differences associated
with the actual origin of the advertising text.
Random allocation was applied at the treatment -
assignment stage. The resulting cell sizes were close
to balanced, which supports factorial comparison
across the 12 conditions.
No preregistered a priori  power calculation is
documented in the retained study materials.

Experimental Controls

premium-automotive communication contexts. BMW
and Tesla were selected as contrasting brand
exemplars because their established market
identities provide a theoretically meaningful
comparison between a heritage -oriented and a
technology-oriented communication context. The
analysis therefore concerns these two brand contexts
specifically; it does not treat the brands as
statistically representative of all legacy or
innovation-oriented firms.

psychological emphasis, and prohibited wording.

automotive advertisement aimed at high -net-worth
consumers, with the BMW condition emphasizing
heritage-oriented sovereignty and the Tesla
condition emphasizing innovati on-oriented future -

Measures and Operationalization
The three principal constructs were measured as
multi-item composites on seven -point Likert -type
scales ranging from 1 ( strongly disagree ) to 7
(strongly agree). A seven-point response format was
retained because it provides comparatively fine
discrimination while remaining cognitively
manageable for respondents ( Preston & Colman,
2000). The use of multi -item composites was
intended to reduce dependence on any single item
wording.

Purchase intention  (PI) was the principal
behavioral-intention outcome. Three items assessed
the likelihood of considering the vehicle, seeking a
test drive, and intending to purchase the vehicle
conditional on sufficient financial capacity.
The operationalization was informed by
established advertising and purchase -intention
research, including Putrevu and Lord (1994) . PI was
calculated as the arithmetic mean of the three items,
producing a composite score from 1 to 7. The
construct therefore represents stated behavioral
intention rather than realized sales or return on
advertising expenditure.
Brand trust (BT) captured respondents' relational
evaluation of the manufacturer after exposure to the
advertisement. Three items assessed trust in the
brand's engineering and product claims, confidence
that the manufacturer would deliver what was
communicated, and t he perceived honesty and
transparency of the communication style.
The scale was conceptually informed by the
brand-trust literature, particularly Chaudhuri and
Holbrook (2001). The three items were averaged to
form a 1 –7 composite, with higher values indicating
stronger post-exposure brand trust.
Linguistic quality (LQ) assessed the technical and
rhetorical quality of the advertising copy. Four items
covered professional style, evocative and premium
vocabulary, logical narrative flow, and
structural/grammatical quality. These dimensions
were operationalized for the present advertising
context and averaged into a 1 –7 composite. LQ is
directly relevant to RQ1 because the unlabelled
conditions permit comparison of perceived writing
quality without an explicit authorship cue.
Because the LQ measure was tailored to the
present context, its internal consistency is reported,
and its interpretation is restricted to the dimensions
represented by the four items rather than treated as
a universal measure of linguistic quality.  Internal
consistency was assessed using Cronbach's alpha
(Cronbach, 1951). Coefficients of 0.70 or above were
treated as the conventional minimum for acceptable
internal consistency, while values above 0.80 were
interpreted as indicating good internal consistency.
The exact wording of the substantive measurement
items is provided in Appendix A.
For each respondent, the composite scores were
calculated as the arithmetic mean of the
corresponding items: PI = mean(E1, E2, E3), BT =
mean(D1, D2, D3), and LQ = mean(C1, C2, C3, C4).
Higher composite scores indicate stronger purchase
intention, stronger brand trust, and higher perceived
linguistic quality, respectively.

Statistical Analysis
The analytical strategy combined descriptive,
reliability, correlational, and factorial procedures.
Frequencies, percentages, means, and standard
deviations were calculated for the analytical sample
and experimental conditions. Internal consistency of
PI, BT, and LQ was assessed using Cronbach's alpha.
Pearson correlations were used descriptively to
characterize associations among the three
composites and were not interpreted as evidence of
mediation or causal ordering.  PI and BT were
analyzed using three -way factorial ordinary least -
squares models containing the main effects of brand,
content author, and disclosure; all two -way
interactions; and the Brand × Content Author ×
Disclosure interaction.
Sum-to-zero contrasts and Type III sums of
squares were used to accommodate the small
imbalance in cell sizes and to estimate each effect
conditional on the complete factorial specification
(Fox & Weisberg, 2019 ). The factorial specification
follows established model-comparison principles for
experimental designs ( Maxwell et al., 2018 ).
Statistical significance was assessed at the 5% level,
and partial eta squared ( ηp²) was reported as the
principal effect-size measure.
Model assumptions and robustness were
examined using the median-centered Levene test for
homogeneity of variance ( Levene, 1960 ), residual
diagnostics based on the Shapiro -Wilk statistic,
residual skewness, excess kurtosis and Cook's
distance, and heteroskedasticity -consistent HC3
covariance estimates. These diagnostics are
consistent with standard guidance for regression and
factorial-model assessment (Field, 2018).
HC3 inference was included because it offers
improved finite-sample performance when leverage
and variance are not perfectly homogeneous
(MacKinnon & White, 1985; Long & Ervin, 2000).
Omnibus effects were supplemented with
estimated marginal means and 95% confidence
intervals (Searle et al., 1980). Disclosure effects were
estimated within each Brand × Content author
combination, and Holm's sequential procedure was
used to adjust for multiple comparisons ( Holm,
1979). The main disclosure effect in the BT model
was used to evaluate H1, and the corresponding
disclosure effect in the PI model was used to evaluate
H2. The Content author × Disclosure interaction and
associated simple effects were used to assess H3. H4
was evaluated using planned difference -in-
differences contrasts comparing the disclosure -
induced change in PI between BMW and Tesla within
the GPT-4o and Gemini 1.5 Pro conditions, followed
by a pooled contrast across the two AI authors.
RQ1 was assessed in the unlabelled  subsample
using a one -way analysis of variance comparing LQ
across Human, GPT -4o, and Gemini 1.5 Pro
conditions. For descriptive completeness, a
supplementary three-way LQ model using the same
factorial structure was used only to obtain the cell -
level est imated marginal means reported alongside
PI and BT; inferential evaluation of RQ1 remained
based on the blind one -way ANOVA and the
supplementary equivalence analysis.
Because a non -significant conventional test does
not establish statistical equivalence, supplementary
two one -sided tests (TOST) were also performed
(Lakens et al., 2018 ). An exploratory equivalence
margin of ±0.35 Likert points was used as the
smallest effect size of interest. Because this threshold
was not preregistered, the equivalence analysis is
interpreted as sensitivity evidence rather than as a
confirmatory test.
All descriptive statistics, reliability estimates,
correlation estimates, factorial models, HC3 -robust
inference, estimated marginal means, multiplicity -
adjusted contrasts, and supplementary equivalence
analyses were implemented in Python 3.13.5 using
pandas 2.2.3, NumPy 2.3.5 (Harris et al., 2020), SciPy
1.17.0 (Virtanen et al., 2020), and statsmodels 0.14.6
(Seabold & Perktold, 2010 ). Figures were generated
using Matplotlib 3.10.8 (Hunter, 2007).

DISCUSSION
The findings reveal a clear distinction between the
linguistic performance of generative AI and
consumer acceptance of its disclosed use. When
authorship was concealed, perceived linguistic
quality did not differ significantly across human, GPT-
4o, and Gemini copy, F(2, 89) = 0.445, p = 0.642. The
appropriate interpretation is therefore that no
statistically detectable quality disadvantage was
observed for AI -generated automotive advertising
under blind conditions, rather than that human and
AI copy were formally equivalent. This disti nction is
important because the supplementary equivalence
analysis did not establish statistical equivalence. The
result is broadly consistent with Shetty et al. (2026) ,
who show ed that consumer responses to AI -
generated marketing content cannot be reduced to
technical content quality alone, as authenticity
perceptions remain important even where the
content itself is competently produced. It also
complements Ekart and Ahtik (2026), whose findings
indicated that evaluations of AI -generated creative
work change when authorship information becomes
available. In the present study, the relatively stable
linguistic quality assessments contrast sharply with
the significant decline in purchase intention after
disclosure, F(1, 172) = 14.02, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.075,
and the corresponding reduction in brand trust, F(1,
172) = 9.11, p = 0.003, η p² = 0.050. This pattern is
consistent with Avidar and Mashiah (2026) , who
identified trust and transparency as central ethical
issues in AI-generated communication, but it further
indicated that disclosure may alter relational and
behavioral evaluations even when the observable
linguistic quality of the communication is not
detectably inferior.
The negative disclosure effect also corresponds
with prior research, although the present experiment
refines several earlier conclusions. Baek et al. (2026)
demonstrated that disclosure of AI -generated
content can influence advertising evaluations, while
Nguyen et al. (2026b)  explicitly linked AI disclosure
to purchase intention through trust. The present
findings are consistent with both studies in showing
that transparency about AI involvement can have
behavioral consequences and that these
consequences are closely connected to brand trust. At
the same time, the results extend this evidence
because disclosure was experimentally crossed with
actual authorship rather than treated as a simple
binary information cue. This distinction is
particularly important when the findings are
compared with Sarran and Datt (2026) , who
emphasized the role of verification signals in shaping
trust and purchase intentions in AI -generated
advertising. The current experiment suggests that
source information does not operate independently
of the underlying content origin: the Author ×
Disclosure interact ion for purchase intention was
significant, F(2, 172) = 3.20, p = 0.043, η p² = 0.036,
while the descriptive placebo -labelled human -copy
changes were small. Accordingly, the results are not
consistent with a fully uniform label-aversion pattern
across author conditions. The largest disclosure
penalties appeared when the label accurately
identified genuinely AI -generated content,
particularly for BMW/GPT -4o (−1.275, Holm -
adjusted p = 0.002) and BMW/Gemini (−1.111, Holm-
adjusted p = 0.011), whereas the corresponding
placebo-labeled human conditions showed only small
descriptive changes. Together with the significant
Author × Disclosure interaction, this indicates
heterogeneity in the disclosure effect across actual
authorship condi tions rather than a uniform
mechanical penalty for the phrase "AI-generated."
The interpretation of this result is also consistent
with research on authenticity and perceived ethical
appropriateness. Anderson and Nguyen (2026)
argued that AI -enabled marketing communication
raises concerns about misrepresentation when
consumers are unable to understand how persuasive
content is produced, whereas Khalfallah and Keller
(2025) showed that authenticity and transparency
are closely connected with consumer trust in
technologically mediated marketing environments.

The present findings support the importance of

logic to automotive advertising and shows that the
behavioral cost of disclosure is not uniform across all
disclosure conditions.
From a business -ethics perspective, this
distinction is important. The observed penalty cannot
reasonably be interpreted as evidence that firms
should conceal AI involvement. Rather, it
demonstrates that ethically desirable transparency
may involve short-term relational or behavioral costs
that organizations must manage without
compromising informed consumer choice,
authenticity, and accountability.
The brand-specific findings provide an additional
layer of interpretation. The strongest disclosure

the brand -specific pattern emerges only in the

interpreted as a significant global three -way

with broader evidence that consumer reactions to AI-
enabled marketing are not uniform. Hoa (2025)
demonstrated that attitudes play an important role in
AI-related online purchase decisions, while Kheiri et

technology-related evaluations should not be

designed to estimate generational moderation

disclosure effects as context -specific rather than
universally positive or negative. Future extensions
could therefore test whether the brand -specific
disclosure effects observed here are further
conditioned by age, AI familiarity, or other consumer
characteristics.

simultaneously introduces challenges of responsible
implementation and consumer acceptance. The

tension. Generative AI may produce advertising copy

CONCLUSIONS
This study examined how transparency regarding
generative AI authorship shapes consumer responses
to automotive advertising, with particular attention
to brand trust, purchase intention, actual content
authorship, and the symbolic positioning of the
advertised brand. It also assessed whether human -
and AI -generated advertising differs in perceived
linguistic quality when authorship is concealed. By
combining these elements within one experimental
framework, the stu dy connects the effectiveness of
generative AI with broader questions of ethical
marketing leadership, organizational accountability,
and responsible AI governance.
The empirical analysis was based on a 2 × 3 × 2
between-subjects factorial experiment involving 184
respondents assigned to 12 conditions defined by
brand (BMW versus Tesla), author (Human, GPT-4o,
and Gemini 1.5 Pro), and disclosure ( unlabelled
versus AI -labelled). The analysis combined
descriptive statistics, reliability and correlation
analyses, Type III factorial OLS/ANOVA models, HC3-
robust inference, estimated marginal means, Holm -
adjusted simple-effects analyses, planned difference-
in-differences contrasts, and supplementary
equivalence testing.
Five principal findings emerge from the analysis.
First, no statistically significant difference in
perceived linguistic quality  was detected between
human, GPT-4o, and Gemini copy under blind
conditions, F(2, 89) = 0.445, p = 0.642, although the
supplementary equivalence tests did not establish
formal equivalence. Second, AI-authorship disclosure
significantly reduced brand trust, F(1, 172) = 9.107, p
= 0.003, η p² = 0.050. Third, disclosure also
significantly reduced purchase intention, F(1, 172) =
14.020, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.075, with the overall mean
declining from approximately 5.25 in the unlabelled
condition to 4.70 in the labelled condition. Fourth, the

ethical significance of AI transparency cannot be
evaluated solely in terms of immediate consumer
acceptance. Transparent disclosure may carry
measurable short -term costs for brand trust and
purchase intention, yet this does not provide an
ethical justification for concealing AI involvement.
The central leadership challenge is therefore not
whether transparency should be avoided when it
reduces favorable consumer response, but how
transparency can be implemented responsibly while
preserving consumer autonomy, informed choice,
and long-term trust. In this sense, the study indicates
that transparency is behaviorally consequential and
should be treated as a core responsibility of ethical AI
governance where AI materially shapes persuasive
communication.
The findings also clarify the role of ethical
marketing leadership in the governance of AI -
generated communication. Decisions about whether
AI is used, how extensively it is used, whether human
review is retained, how authorship is disclosed, and
who remai ns accountable for the resulting content
are organizational leadership decisions rather than
purely technical choices. The findings therefore
suggest that ethical marketing leadership may benefit
from internal rules that define responsibility for AI -
generated communication, allocate clear approval
authority, specify the level of required human
oversight, and help keep synthetic content aligned
with the values communicated by the organization.
Such leadership is particularly important where
commercial incentives may encourage firms to
minimize information that could weaken persuasive
effectiveness. From an ethical -governance
perspective, responsible decision -making should
balance commercial objectives against duties of
transparency, honesty, and respect for consumer
autonomy.
This distinction also highlights the difference
between formal compliance and broader ethical
responsibility. Meeting a legal or procedural
disclosure requirement should be treated as a
minimum standard rather than as the full extent of
responsible AI gove rnance. Ethical governance may
also need to consider whether disclosure is
understandable, proportionate, and sufficiently
visible; whether AI -generated claims are subject to
meaningful human verification; whether
responsibility for misleading or inappropriate
synthetic content is clearly assigned; and whether
consumers are given enough information to make
informed judgments about the communicati on
directed towards them.
For firms using generative AI at scale, responsible
governance may therefore extend beyond disclosure
mechanisms to documented review procedures,
accountability structures, and escalation processes
for problematic content.
From a managerial perspective, the results
suggest that AI governance policies should not rely on
uniform assumptions about consumer response. The
stronger disclosure penalty observed in the BMW
condition indicates that the consequences of
transparent AI use m ay depend on the symbolic
meaning of the brand and on the extent to which AI -
generated communication is perceived as congruent
with that meaning.
This does not imply that disclosure standards
should be weakened for brands associated with
heritage, craftsmanship, or human expertise. Rather,
such brands may need stronger forms of human
involvement, more careful integration of AI into
communication workflows, and more deliberate
explanation of how AI is used. Monitoring should
therefore extend beyond short -term performance
indicators such as purchase intention and include
brand trust; future governance frameworks may also
incorporate perceived authenticity, pe rceived
transparency, and other indicators of relational
credibility that were not measured directly in the
present study.

The study also contributes conceptually to

disclosure, actual authorship, and brand context. The
findings suggest that responsible AI governance in
marketing cannot be reduced to a binary choice
between using or not using generative AI. Instead,
governance should address how AI is integrated,
supervised, disclosed, and aligned with stakeholder
expectations. This perspective is particularly relevant
to business ethics because it places organizational
responsibility, consumer autonomy, and long -term
trust at the center of technology adoption rather than
treating them as secondary constraints on efficiency.
Several limitations define the scope of these
conclusions. First, BMW and Tesla were used as
single representatives of legacy - and innovation -
oriented brand positioning. The brand -specific
results should therefore not be generalized to all
automotive manufacturers or to other sectors.
Replication across several brands within each
archetype would be necessary to establish whether
the observed pattern reflects a broader brand -
positioning effect.
Second, the sample consisted of adults aged 18–45
recruited online in Poland through non -probability
convenience and snowball sampling. Although
randomization was applied to experimental -
condition allocation, the sample was not nationally
representative. T he findings should therefore  be
interpreted as evidence of experimental treatment
effects within the observed sample rather than as
population-level estimates for Polish consumers or
premium-vehicle buyers.
Third, purchase intention represents a stated
behavioral intention rather than an observed market
action. Although it provides an established indicator
of downstream consumer response, it does not
capture realized purchasing behavior, actual
expenditure, o r commercial conversion. Future
studies could therefore incorporate behavioral
outcomes such as click -through rates, requests for
additional information, test -drive bookings,
willingness to pay, or actual purchasing decisions.
Fourth, the study focused on text -based automotive
advertising, whereas contemporary generative A I
increasingly produces integrated text, image, audio,
and video content. The ethical and behavioral
consequences of disclosure may differ when AI
involvement is more visually salient or when several
synthetic modalities are combined. Fifth, the retained
materials do not document a separate manipulation
check establishing that participants noticed and
correctly interpreted the “AI -Generated Copy”
disclosure; the disclosure effect should therefore be
interpreted as the effect of assignment to the labelled
condition rather than as a verified perceptual
response to the label. Sixth, no preregistered a priori
power calculation is documented, so higher -order
interactions and cell -specific estimates should be
interpreted with appropriate caution, particularly
given the modest cell sizes. Seventh, PI, BT, and LQ
were analyzed as context -specific multi -item
composites with evidence of internal consistency, but
the retained materials do not establish a full latent
measurement model or discriminant validity among
the constructs. Finally, some supplementary brand
trust descriptives and correlation -based statistics
depend on the reconstructed analytical structure
described in the Methodology and should not be
treated as equivalent to a complete original
respondent-level raw-data archive.
Further research should also investigate the
psychological mechanisms that underlie the
observed disclosure penalty. Perceived authenticity,
manipulation, fairness, competence, transparency,
and consumer autonomy are particularly relevant
candidates. It would also be useful to examine
whether these mechanisms vary according to age,
culture, AI familiarity, technological literacy, and
prior attitudes towards the advertised brand.
Longitudinal research could determine whether
disclosure penalties persist as consumers become
more accustomed to generative AI, while cross -
cultural studies could clarify whether social norms
and regulatory expectations alter the relationship
between AI transparency and trust.
Future work should additionally extend the
leadership and governance dimension of the present
study. In particular, research could examine whether
different forms of ethical leadership, organizational
AI governance, and human oversight influence
consumer reactions to disclosed AI use. Comparative
