Abstract
Context: Current debates on Vocational Education and Training (VET) increasingly emphasise
the need to equip learners and workers with artificial intelligence (AI) skills, particularly in
cultural and creative sectors. European policy initiatives often frame this c hallenge through
competence development, qualification reform, and employability agendas. While important at
system level, such perspectives provide limited insight into how vocational learning is enacted
in everyday work settings where knowledge is socially, materially, and historically situated.
Research on work-based learning has long demonstrated that vocational competence develops
through participation in occupational practice, mentoring relations, and engagement with work-
place affordances (Billett, 2001; Lave & Wenger, 1991). This perspective is particularly rele-
vant in artisanal and creative occupations, where learning relies on embodied judgement, tacit
knowledge, and situated problem-solving (Sennett, 2008; Marchand, 2016). Against this back-
ground, the study examines how generative AI is entering work -based learning practices in
Tuscany’s craft and creative sector.
Approach: This first-stage study adopts a qualitative exploratory multi -source design with fo-
cused ethnographic elements. Over a twelve-month period (April 2025 – April 2026), data were
drawn from one-day focused visits to four settings — Santa Chiara Lab – FabLab Siena, FabLab
Conventino – Officina Creativa Florence, Manifattura Tabacchi Florence, and the Mostra In-
ternazionale dell'Artigianato (MIDA) — combining workplace observation, informal conver-
sational encounters with craft practitioners, and documentary and digital materials. A prior em-
pirical mapping of hybrid craft and digital workplaces in Tuscany (Sgorla, 2025) provided the
basis for purposive site selection. Data were analysed through iterative thematic coding in-
formed by sensitising concepts from work-based learning, situated learning, and sociomaterial
theory.
Findings: Early findings indicate that generative AI is being incorporated unevenly across craft
settings, used more frequently for ideation, marketing and design support than for core manual
production. Learning about AI occurs informally through self -directed experimentation and
peer exchange rather than formal training. Tensions emerge between innovation narratives and
concerns regarding authenticity, authorship, and occupational identity. The notion of «the
hand» (la mano) functions as a boundary marker between tasks where AI may support the cre-
ative process and those where embodied execution remains non-negotiable. Craft practitioners
evaluate AI-generated outputs through sensory, aesthetic, and practical judgement developed
over years of material engagement, positioning AI as raw material to be filtered rather than as
a replacement for craft knowledge.
Conclusions: The study contributes to VET research by moving beyond competence -based
framings of AI and examining how technological change is mediated through situated voca-
tional practice. Conceptually, it advances the notion of hybrid learning ecologies (Sgorla et al.,
2025), understood as learning environments shaped through the interaction of embodied

sectoral networks and events, and AI-mediated knowledge resources. The Tuscan case suggests
that a critical capacity for craft workers may reside not merely in the ability to use AI tools, but
in the evaluative competence to contextualise and integrate AI outputs within situated practice.

1 Introduction
Digital technologies are increasingly reshaping how work is undertaken and how workers
interact with materials, clients, and one another. In the craft and creative sector, this transfor-
mation raises distinctive questions about the relationship between technological innovation and
the embodied, situated forms of knowledge that have historically sustained artisanal practice.
Current debates on Vocational Education and Training (VET) emphasise the need to equip
learners and workers with artificial intelligence  (AI) skills, and European policy initiatives —
from the Digital Education Action Plan (2021 –2027) to Cedefop and OECD frameworks on
apprenticeships and digital transition — frame this challenge through competence development,
qualification reform, and employability agendas. While important at system level, such per-
spectives provide limited insight into how vocational learning is enacted in everyday work set-
tings.
Recent research on the digital transformation of vocational education and training under-
lines that the modernisation of apprenticeships to meet digital skill needs requires not only
technical upskilling but also attention to how learning processes are reconfigured in technology-
mediated environments (Cedefop & OECD, 2024). Billett (2021) has shown that digitalisation
mediates worklife learning in ways that do not replace but reorganise workplace affordances,
demanding new forms of discretion and reflexivity from workers. In the craft and creative sector
specifically, studies on digital fabrication technologies (Song, 2022), platform -mediated arti-
sanal production (Gandini & Gerosa, 2025), and AI-driven design processes (Filimowicz, 2023)
have begun to document how emerging technologies reshape occupational practices without
necessarily displacing embodied knowledge. However, most of this literature addresses either
the technical dimension of AI adoption or its labour -market effects, with limited attention to
the situated learning processes through which craft practitioners appropriate, evaluate, or resist
generative AI in everyday work.
Research on work-based learning has long demonstrated that vocational competence de-
velops through participation in occupational practice, access to tasks, mentoring relations, and
engagement with workplace affordances rather than through formal instruction  alone (Billett,
2001, 2021; Lave & Wenger, 1991). This perspective is particularly relevant in artisanal and
creative occupations, where learning relies on embodied judgement, tacit knowledge, sensory
evaluation, and situated problem-solving that are difficult to codify or formalise (Sennett, 2008;
Ingold, 2013; Marchand, 2016). As Billett (2025) demonstrates in the healthcare sector, work-
ers engaging with digital technologies exercise high levels of discretion, engage in both routine
and non-routine problem-solving, and learn through their work at high frequency. These dy-
namics have direct resonance with how artisans encounter, appropriate, or resist generative AI
within their everyday practice.
In the craft sector, the convergence between artisanal practice and digital technologies has
attracted renewed scholarly attention. Recent work has examined how digitisation reshapes
craft techniques, identity, and cultural transmission (Song, 2022; Gandini & Gerosa, 2025), and
how sustainable learning ecologies may balance tradition and innovation (Sgorla et al., 2025).
A prior study of the Tuscan artisanal landscape (Sgorla, 2025) identified a distinctive ecosystem
of hybrid learning spaces — including fab labs, artisan workshops linked to universities, and

innovation residencies — where traditional making and digital fabrication coexist, generating
what was characterised as «expanded manual skill» (manualità espansa ), in which algorithms,
data and digital visualisations become extensions of the intelligence of the hand. That study
also revealed how digital platforms create new forms of distributed learning and professional
socialisation, while introducing risks of aesthetic standardisation, «two-speed digital ecologies»
(Flanagan & Xue, 2024), and new dependencies on algorithmic visibility.
Conceptually, the study builds on the notion of hybrid learning ecologies, defined here as
learning environments constituted through the dynamic interaction of five analytically distin-
guishable components: (a) embodied workplace practice, where knowledge is produced through
gesture, perception, and material engagement; (b) mentoring and peer relations, through which
tacit knowledge is transmitted, evaluated, and contested; (c) institutional training arrangements,
including VET pathways; (d) public sectoral networks and events, where innovation narratives
circulate and are negotiated; and (e) AI -mediated knowledge resources, which introduce new
sources of design, information, and creative stimulation ( Sgorla et al., 2025). This concept is
related to but distinct from two other notions developed in prior work on the Tuscan craft land-
scape: expanded manual skill (manualità espansa), which refers to the individual competence
whereby the artisan integrates digital capabilities into manual dexterity without losing embod-
ied grounding (Sgorla, 2025); and expanded embodiment, which denotes the process through
which this integration occurs — the artisan's sensory field extends through digital interfaces
while maintaining its connection to bodily making (Sgorla et al., 2025). Hybrid learning ecol-
ogies is thus the ecological and contextual concept describing the learning environment; ex-
panded manual skill is the individual competence that develops within it; and expanded em-
bodiment is the mediating process through which such competence is enacted.
The study addresses the following research question: How is generative artificial intelli-
gence being integrated into work-based learning practices in the craft and creative sector, and
how may this reshape learning processes, expertise, and knowledge transmission? This question
responds to three gaps in current research: limited empirical understanding of how vocational
learning is organised in everyday craft work; insufficient evidence on how craft practitioners
appropriate, negotiate, or resist generative AI in practice; and underdeveloped conceptual mod-
els linking AI adoption with work-based learning processes.
In this short paper, the method and procedures used for the data gathering are presented,
followed by discussion of early findings and considerations about the implications for work -
based learning and the development of hybrid learning ecologies in craft and creative VET.
2 Method
The approach adopted for this first-stage study can best be described as qualitative explor-
atory, drawing on multiple complementary sources of data to generate early empirical insights
into the integration of generative AI within work -based learning in craft and creative settings.
Unlike large-scale survey designs, the intention is not to establish generalisable patterns, but to
identify emergent processes, tensions, and learning dynamics as they unfold in situated practice.
The study aligns with traditions of workplace learning research that prioritise close observation
of how knowledge is enacted, negotiated, and transmitted in occupational contexts (Billett,
2001; Lave & Wenger, 1991), while remaining attentive to the material and technological con-
ditions that increasingly mediate those processes.
2.1 Sample
The study focuses on artisanal and creative settings connected to VET pathways in Tus-
cany. This regional focus is analytically motivated: Tuscany concentrates a density of heritage
industries, design-oriented small enterprises, and established vocational training arrangements
that make it a productive site for examining how generative AI enters occupational practices

where embodied judgement, tacit knowledge, and sensory evaluation remain central. A prior
empirical mapping (Sgorla, 2025) identified key hybrid learning spaces across the region,
providing an informed basis for purposive site selection.
Four settings were included in this first-stage study, selected according to three criteria:
documented connection to VET pathways or vocational learning, evidence of hybrid craft-dig-
ital practices, and sectoral diversity within the artisanal ecosystem. Th e selected settings en-
compassed different forms of craft, creative, and vocational activity in Siena and Florence. In
Siena, Santa Chiara Lab – FabLab was included as a setting combining cultural heritage and
digital fabrication. Empirical engagement consisted of a one-day focused observation of work-
space organisation, tools in use, and visible learning dynamics. In Florence, FabLab Conventino
– Officina Creativa was selected for its activities related to digital artisan training and co-mak-
ing. Here, a one-day focused observation similarly concentrated on the organisation of the
workspace, the tools being used, and observable learning dynamics.
Two additional settings in Florence broadened the empirical scope beyond dedicated fab-
rication and training spaces. Manifattura Tabacchi Firenze, characterised by design residencies
and interdisciplinary innovation, was examined through a one -day focused observation com-
plemented by informal conversational encounters. MIDA – Mostra Internazionale dell'Artig-
ianato, a sectoral exhibition and networking environment, was likewise included through a one-
day field observation and informal conversational encounters. At these two sites, conversations
with craft practitioners, exhibitors, and visitors provided contextual insight into their awareness
of, engagement with, or resistance to generative AI tools.
The selection of these four settings is not intended to be representative of the Tuscan craft
sector as a whole. Rather, it was designed to provide analytical diversity across different types
of artisanal, creative, and vocational environments, consistent with the exploratory character of
this first-stage study and its aim of identifying emergent processes and tensions surrounding the
incorporation of generative AI into craft-related practices and learning environments.
2.2 Procedure
Fieldwork was conducted between April 2025 and April 2026. Each of the four settings
was visited once for a full day. At Santa Chiara Lab and FabLab Conventino, focused observa-
tions concentrated on specific episodes involving workspace layout, tool use, the coexistence
of traditional and digital fabrication, and the presence or absence of AI -mediated resources.
Fieldnotes were recorded during and immediately after each visit using a structured observation
template capturing the activity setting, practitioners present, tools and materials in use, the na-
ture of any learning episode observed, and the visibility of AI-related practices.
At Manifattura Tabacchi and MIDA, focused observation was complemented by informal
conversational encounters with craft practitioners and, at MIDA, with exhibitors and visitors.
These were not structured interviews but situated exchanges initiated in the flow of observation,
consistent with the focused ethnographic strategy outlined by Knoblauch (2005). At Manifat-
tura Tabacchi, conversations arose during or after observed activities in the residency and work-
shop spaces. At MIDA, the concentrated exhibition setting enabled encounters with a broader
range of artisans in a single day, and the distinction between formal ex hibition discourse and
the more cautious, selective accounts offered in informal exchanges proved analytically pro-
ductive.
Documentary and digital materials were selected according to three criteria: (a) public
availability; (b) relevance to AI adoption or digital innovation in the Tuscan artisanal sector;
and (c) production by or in connection with the settings observed or by relevant sectoral net-
works. These included VET training resources, institutional communications, sector reports,
and publicly accessible examples of AI-related practices.

Data were analysed through iterative thematic coding. Coding was conducted manually by
the author using analytical tables organised by site and data source. The process followed an
iterative logic: an initial round of open coding identified recurrent patte rns in the field notes
and conversational data; these were then interrogated against sensitising concepts from the lit-
erature. From the work-based learning literature, the concepts of workplace affordances and
individual engagement (Billett, 2001, 2015) oriented attention to how craft workers encounter,
appropriate, or decline AI-mediated resources. From situated learning theory (Lave & Wenger,
1991), the concept of legitimate peripheral participation informed the analysis of mentoring
dynamics and whether AI reconfigures pathways through which novices move toward fuller
participation. From sociomaterial perspectives (Fenwick et al., 2011), attention was directed to
the entanglement of human actors, material artefacts, and digital tools. Additionally, four con-
ceptual dimensions derived from a prior study of Tuscan craft workplaces (Sgorla , 2025) —
expanded manual skill, human–machine co-authorship, situated learning, and educational sus-
tainability — served as an interpretive lens. Observational data, conversational insights, and
documentary materials were combined through an iterative proc ess of cross-referencing:
emerging themes identified in field observations were interrogated against the accounts offered
in informal exchanges and the representations present in documentary sources. As a single -
researcher first-stage study, no multiple-coder procedures were employed. Reflexivity was pur-
sued through analytical memos recorded during and after observation sessions, and through
interpretive discussions with colleagues familiar with workplace learning research. Subsequent
phases will incorporate inter-coder reliability procedures.
The limited scale of the empirical engagement — one-day visits and informal conversa-
tional encounters rather than sustained ethnographic immersion — is a deliberate feature of this
first-stage design, conducted over a twelve-month period (April 2025 – April 2026). The aim
is to identify emergent processes and generate analytical propositions, not to establish saturated
findings. Subsequent phases will involve extended observation, purposive interviewing, and
inter-coder reliability procedures.
3 Findings
The early data reveal four analytically distinguishable dimensions of how generative AI is
entering work-based learning in the craft sector.
AI Adoption Patterns Across Craft Settings
Generative AI is being incorporated unevenly across the artisanal and creative settings ob-
served. Craft practitioners were observed using AI tools — particularly image generation, text-
based assistants, and design software with AI-integrated features — more frequently for idea-
tion, marketing, communication, and design exploration than for core manual production pro-
cesses. In several workshops, AI-generated images were used as starting points for client con-
versations or as visual references for new product lines, but the transition from digital sugges-
tion to material execution remained firmly governed by embodied skill and material judgement.
In one workshop, a practitioner was observed using an AI image generator to produce visual
references for a client meeting. The generated images were displayed on a tablet and discussed
as starting points for a new product line. However, when the practitioner moved to the work-
bench, the transition from digital suggestion to material execution was governed entirely by
manual judgement: the form, texture, and proportions of the final piece departed significantly
from the AI-generated reference. The digital output functioned as a conversational resource
rather than a production template (fieldnote, FabLab Conventino, October 2025). The use of AI
for design-adjacent tasks coexists with a clear reluctance to extend it into the domain of manual
making, where sensory perception and material responsiveness are considered essential. Table

Informal Learning and Peer Exchange
Learning about AI occurred predominantly through informal channels: self-directed exper-
imentation, peer exchange within craft networks, social media communities, and occasional
exposure at sector events such as MIDA. None of the craft practitioners observed reported hav-
ing participated in formal training specifically focused on generative AI. The learning process
was iterative and exploratory, characterised by trial-and-error engagement with free or low-cost
AI tools, followed by evaluative conversations with peers or family members involved in the
enterprise. At MIDA, several exhibitors described their engagement with AI tools as entirely
self-taught. One craftsman recounted having experimented with multiple free applications over
a period of weeks, eventually retaining only one for preliminary sketching. The learning process
was characterised as iterative and evaluative: practitioners tried tools, assessed their usefulness
against the demands of their craft, and shared their conclusions with peers at sector  events or
through digital networks (conversational notes, MIDA, April–May 2025). Digital platforms
such as Instagram and Instructables function as expanded communities of practice (Wenger,
1998), in which the learning is collaborative, distributed, and multimodal. Table 2 summarises

Tensions Between Innovation and Authenticity
A recurring theme across both workplace and field observations was the tension between
public enthusiasm for AI and more cautious orientations in everyday workshop practice. In
informal exchanges during observation, craft practitioners expressed concern that AI-generated
design elements might compromise the perceived authenticity and authorship of their work.
Across settings where conversational data were gathered, practitioners consistently distin-
guished between tasks where digital tools were considered ac ceptable — design exploration,
marketing, client communication — and tasks where manual execution was viewed as non -
negotiable. The expression «la mano» (the hand) recurred in these exchanges as a boundary
marker, signifying the domain of embodied skill and material judgement that practitioners were
unwilling to cede to technological mediation (conversational notes, Manifattura Tabacchi, Feb-
ruary 2026; MIDA, April–May 2025). This finding resonates with what Gandini and Gerosa
(2025) characterise as «neo-craft work,» where the symbolic value of manual labour coexists
with the logics of visibility and commercialisation mediated by digital platforms. The artisan's
identity is simultaneously that of a tradition custodian and an agent of innovation — a condition
that creates productive but unresolved tensions around authorship and the cultural meaning of
making.
Embodied Judgement in Evaluating AI Outputs
Across settings, the continued importance of embodied and tacit judgement was evident.
When AI-generated outputs — images, patterns, text — entered the workflow, craft practition-
ers evaluated them not through formal criteria but through sensory, aesthetic, and practical as-
sessment developed over years of material engagement. During an observation at Santa Chiara
Lab, a practitioner was seen evaluating an AI-generated design by handling a printed prototype
of the proposed pattern. The evaluation was conducted through tactile and visual assessment —
feeling the weight and drape of the material, examining how the pattern would behave in three-
dimensional form — rather than through formal criteria. The practitioner's commentary indi-
cated that the algorithmic out put was judged against sensory knowledge developed through
years of material engagement: the capacity to anticipate how a design would perform physi-
cally, a form of evaluative competence that no digital rendering could substitute (fieldnote,
Santa Chiara Lab, July 2025). This evaluative capacity, which Marchand (2016) characterises
as craftwork-as-problem-solving, functioned as a filter through which AI suggestions were ac-
cepted, modified, or rejected. The craft worker’s body and trained perception thus ope rated as
a mediating instance between the algorithmic output and the material reality of production. In
this sense, the «human touch» does not disappear with AI; rather, it manifests in the choice of
parameters, in decisions regarding texture, rhythm, mate rial, and scale — a form of expanded
embodiment (Sgorla et al., 2025) in which the artisan’s sensory field extends through digital
interfaces without severing the connection to bodily making.
4 Discussion
The early findings presented above resonate with broader patterns identified in research on
digital work and learning. Billett's (2025) analysis of healthcare workers demonstrates that pro-
fessionals engaging with digital technologies exercise high levels of discretion, engage in both
routine and non-routine problem-solving, and learn through their work at high frequency. The
craft workers observed in this study display comparable dynamics: they exercise discretion in
deciding when, how, and whether to engage with AI tools; they encounter non-routine problems

when translating AI-generated outputs into material form; and they learn informally and con-
tinuously through experimentation with these technologies.
However, the craft context introduces distinctive dimensions. Whereas healthcare workers’
engagement with digital artefacts is largely institutionally structured, craft workers’ engage-
ment with generative AI is predominantly self -initiated, informally mediated, and shaped by
cultural-aesthetic concerns about authenticity and authorship that have no direct equivalent in
clinical settings. Moreover, the craft sector is uniquely exposed to the dynamics of digital plat-
forms, where algorithmic visibility — through metrics of engagement on platforms such as
Instagram, Etsy, and TikTok — increasingly mediates the symbolic and economic circulation
of artisanal production (Gandini & Gerosa, 2025). The digital artisan is not only a maker but
also a data manager, an image curator, and a communicator of meaning, operating within an
economy of attention that reshapes occupational identity and learning demands.
The concept of hybrid learning ecologies provides a framework for interpreting these find-
ings. In the Tuscan case, the learning environment of craft workers is constituted through the
interaction of five analytically distinguishable components: (a) embodie d workplace practice,
where knowledge is produced through gesture, perception, and material engagement; (b) men-
toring and peer relations, through which tacit knowledge is transmitted, evaluated, and con-
tested; (c) institutional training arrangements, including VET pathways; (d) public sectoral net-
works and events, such as MIDA, where innovation narratives circulate and are negotiated; and
(e) AI-mediated knowledge resources, which introduce new sources of design, information, and
creative stimulation into the learning ecology.
The notion of expanded embodiment is particularly productive for understanding how craft
workers relate to generative AI. The craft practitioners observed do not treat AI as a replacement
for embodied skill, nor do they reject it as external to their pract ice. Rather, they position AI
outputs as raw material to be filtered, refined, and transformed through the same evaluative
capacities they apply to physical materials. Generative AI enters the learning ecology not as a
substitute for craft knowledge but as an additional affordance — one whose value is determined
by the worker’s embodied competence to evaluate and transform its outputs.
These early findings have preliminary implications for VET policy and curriculum design.
Rather than framing AI adoption through the lens of competence gaps, a work -based learning
perspective suggests that a critical capacity for craft workers may reside not merely in the ability
to use AI tools, but in the evaluative competence to contextualise and integrate AI outputs
within situated practice. This evaluative capacity is itself a form of vocational knowledge —
developed through experience, mentoring, and sustained engagement with materials — that
cannot be reduced to formal instruction in digital literacy. These preliminary observations point
towards a challenge for VET systems: the development of hybrid curricula that articulate prac-
tical knowledge, reflexivity, and technological mastery as inseparable dimensions of profes-
sional development — what the prior Tuscan study characterised as a «pedagogy of care, ex-
perimentation, and interdependence» ( Sgorla, 2025). This proposition, derived from a first-
stage exploratory study, should be understood as a conceptual direction to be tested and refined
in subsequent phases of research.
At the same time, the transition is not without risks. The formation of «two -speed digital
ecologies» (Flanagan & Xue, 2024), in which technological innovation remains inaccessible to
many artisans in peripheral contexts, constitutes a real contradiction. AI systems trained on
geographically and aesthetically biased datasets may reproduce dominant perspectives at the
expense of peripheral artisanal expressions, converting the promise of technological democra-
tisation into a new form of global homogenisation. Additionally, the precariousness of artisanal
work within the platform economy — where algorithmic visibility progressively replaces com-
munity reputation — exposes workers to new regimes of dependency and instability.

A key caveat is that the study's first-stage design is based on one-day focused visits to four
settings: two involving observation only (Santa Chiara Lab, FabLab Conventino) and two com-
bining observation with informal conversational encounters (Manifattura  Tabacchi, MIDA).
This design provides focused but temporally limited access to workplace dynamics and does
not permit claims about the consistency of practices over time or the representativeness of the
patterns observed across the Tuscan craft sector as a whole. The findings are intended as early
analytical insights and conceptual propositions — preliminary in character — to be tested and
refined through extended observation and systematic interviewing in subsequent phases. Addi-
tionally, the Tuscan context, with its distinctive concentration of heritage industries and inno-
vation infrastructure, may not be representative of craft work in other European regions.
