ABSTRACT
Scaling the training of Large Language Model (LLM) agents typically requires
a large collection of executable environments, whose construction, maintenance,
and operation constrain the expansion of training experience. Existing offline tra-
jectories offer an opportunity to reuse interaction experience, yet effectively re-
fining the current policy without revisiting these environments remains challeng-
ing: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) can lead to catastrophic forgetting, while online
reinforcement learning requires real-time interaction with the environment. To
address this challenge, we proposeStep-AdvantagePolicyDistillation (SAPD), a
method for learning from offline trajectories using relative step advantages. SAPD
decomposes multi-turn trajectories into individual decision contexts for on-policy
distillation, allowing the student to generate responses conditioned on recorded
histories, and introduces relative step advantage signals to stabilize training. The
entire process requires neither new environment observations nor additional multi-
turn interactions. Experiments on ALFWorld, WebShop, and Search-based QA
show that SAPD improves performance across different teacher configurations,
mitigates catastrophic forgetting compared with SFT, and runs at least4.7×faster
per training step than online OPD. We will release our code soon.
1 INTRODUCTION
Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have enabled LLM agents to tackle increasingly
complex, long-horizon tasks. Scaling agent training across these long-horizon tasks typically re-
quires diverse executable environments in which models can take actions and receive feedback (Liao
et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026). Recent models such as DeepSeek-V3.2 and Qwen3-Coder-Next
further highlight this trend through large-scale construction of executable environments for agent
training (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025; Cao et al., 2026). However, deploying, maintaining, and oper-
ating these environments introduces substantial infrastructure demands and engineering complexity,
making agent training difficult to scale across heterogeneous tasks (Liao et al., 2026).
Existing offline trajectories offer an opportunity to reuse interaction experience, yet effectively refin-
ing the current policy without revisiting the original environments remains challenging. Supervised
fine-tuning (SFT) learns from these trajectories by imitating expert actions. However, when tra-
jectories span heterogeneous domains, their supervision signals may conflict, causing interference
between domain-specific behaviors. Sequential SFT can further lead to catastrophic forgetting, with
gains on new tasks coming at the expense of previously acquired capabilities (Lu & Thinking Ma-
chines Lab, 2025). These challenges limit the use of SFT for continually expanding an agent’s task
coverage. In contrast, online reinforcement learning (RL) and on-policy distillation (OPD) optimize
trajectories generated by the current policy and can substantially mitigate forgetting compared with
SFT (Shenfeld et al., 2026; Lu & Thinking Machines Lab, 2025). However, their application through
online multi-turn rollouts requires continued environment interaction, retaining the associated de-

ployment and execution costs (Liao et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026). These limitations raise a central
question:How can we continually improve an agent’s policy using offline interaction experience
without revisiting the environment, while retaining previously acquired capabilities?
To address this challenge, we proposeStep-AdvantagePolicyDistillation (SAPD), which refines the
student policy using offline prefixes and teacher supervision. SAPD consists of two components: of-
fline trajectory decomposition and context-relative step-advantage optimization.Offline trajectory
decompositionconverts recorded multi-turn trajectories into decision contexts, each retaining the
preceding interaction history and current observation as a fixed prefix. For each prefix, the student
generates a group of single-turn responses, and a fixed teacher provides token-level supervision on
the generated responses. This enables SAPD to reuse offline interaction histories while optimizing
the student’s own decisions, without executing new actions or obtaining additional environment ob-
servations. However, offline prefix replay faces a two-sided mismatch: recorded histories may differ
from those the student would encounter through its own interactions and may also be unfamiliar to
the teacher. This mismatch can introduce context-dependent errors into teacher supervision, mak-
ing updates based on absolute teacher–student discrepancies less reliable. To address this challenge,
SAPD introducescontext-relative step-advantage optimization, which aggregates token-level sig-
nals into action-level scores and compares candidate responses under the same decision context.
Comparing responses under the same prefix yields relative advantages that provide decision-level
guidance alongside token-level supervision. We incorporate these advantages into a joint objective
to stabilize policy updates, alongside token-level OPD for efficiency and rule rewards for further
stability. Experiments on three publicly available agentic benchmarks show that SAPD significantly
improves performance across teacher configurations, substantially mitigates catastrophic forgetting
compared with SFT, and runs at least4.7×faster per training step than online OPD in the measured
configurations. In summary, our contributions are as follows:
• We introduce a framework that combines offline trajectory decomposition with policy op-
timization, enabling environment-free learning while mitigating catastrophic forgetting.
• We proposeStep-AdvantagePolicyDistillation (SAPD), which constructs relative step ad-
vantages to provide more stable decision-level supervision under behavior-prefix shift.
• Experiments on three publicly available agentic benchmarks demonstrate improved per-
formance across teacher configurations, reduced forgetting compared with SFT, and lower
per-step training time than online OPD.
2 RELATED WORK
LLM agentic learning.LLM-based agents are increasingly trained on long-horizon tasks that re-
quire sustained interaction with external environments (Yao et al., 2023). Supervised fine-tuning
and behavior cloning enable learning from expert trajectories, but can suffer from gradient interfer-
ence during joint training across heterogeneous tasks (Yu et al., 2020) and catastrophic forgetting
during sequential training (Luo et al., 2025). Reinforcement learning methods such as GRPO opti-
mize policy-generated behavior using task feedback (Shao et al., 2024), while on-policy distillation
(OPD) supplies dense teacher supervision on student-generated responses (Agarwal et al., 2024;
Gu et al., 2024; Lu & Thinking Machines Lab, 2025). However, these online methods rely on
environment interaction throughout training. The deployment, maintenance, and operation of het-
erogeneous environments incur substantial engineering and resource costs, limiting the scalability
of these methods across multiple environments (Liao et al., 2026).
Prefix-based OPD.OPD derives supervision from teacher probabilities rather than environment
rewards, potentially enabling environment-free training. Recent work has explored reducing rollout
requirements of OPD through prefix-based learning (Zhao et al., 2026; Gui et al., 2026). Reasoning-
Prefix OPD shows that teacher-generated prefixes can guide subsequent student reasoning (Zhang
et al., 2026). BPO reuses prefixes to explore alternative actions, but still requires environment in-
teraction to evaluate their rollouts (He et al., 2026). Prefix-GRPO replays teacher trajectories and
optimizes student using task rewards from environment feedback, whereas ReOPD uses teacher su-
pervision on student rollouts conditioned on the teacher’s prefixes without further environment inter-
action (Wang et al., 2026a; Liao et al., 2026). In practice, these approaches rely on teacher-generated
2

trajectories, whose collection requires deploying and maintaining the corresponding environments,
which limits their ability to learn directly from arbitrary offline interaction trajectories.
Reliable OPD under prefix mismatch.However, replaying prefixes from arbitrary offline tra-
jectories faces a two-sided mismatch: recorded histories may differ from those the student would
encounter through its own interactions and may also be unfamiliar to the teacher, which can substan-
tially destabilize OPD training. Recent work has investigated OPD instability under prefix mismatch
and explored ways to improve the reliability of teacher supervision (Zhu et al., 2026; Yang et al.,
2026). TA-OPD shows that some token-level teacher–student discrepancies are unreliable (Wang
et al., 2026b). ReNIO aggregates token signals into sample-level distillation weights, which can be
too coarse for credit assignment in long-horizon tasks (Lin et al., 2026). Furthermore, SOD adjusts
step-level distillation weights by comparing discrepancies across steps within the same trajectory,
but these comparisons span different decision contexts and may therefore be unreliable (Zhong et al.,
2026). SAPD instead computes relative advantages from aggregated action-token signals under the
same offline prefix, supporting more stable credit assignment.

4 METHOD
SAPD decouples policy refinement from the environment execution required by the online meth-
ods in Section 3. As illustrated in Figure 1, we first decompose recorded multi-turn trajectories
into a pool of decision prefixes that retain the preceding actions and environment observations. For
each prefix, the student generates a group of single-turn responses, which are scored by a fixed
teacher without executing their actions. We combine token-level distillation with a step-level rela-
tive advantage computed by comparing aggregated teacher–student signals within the group. This
provides decision-level supervision alongside fine-grained token feedback. Locally computable re-
wards, such as repetition penalties and format checks, further support stable training. Together, these

components optimize the student’s responses using existing interaction histories, requiring neither
new environment observations nor additional multi-turn rollouts during training.

5 EXPERIMENTS
5.1 EXPERIMENTALSETUP
Benchmarks.We evaluate SAPD on three widely used long-horizon agent tasks: ALFWorld, Web-
Shop, and Search-based QA.ALFWorld(Shridhar et al., 2021) is a text-based environment in which
agents follow instructions to complete household goals. It comprises six task categories: Pick, Look,
Clean, Heat, Cool, and Pick2.WebShop(Yao et al., 2022) is a simulated online shopping environ-
ment in which agents search a product catalog, navigate product pages, and select products and
options that satisfy user-specified requirements.Search-based QAfollows the setup of Search-
R1 (Jin et al., 2025), requiring agents to interleave reasoning with search, gather relevant evidence,
and produce a final answer. It includes three single-hop datasets, Natural Questions (Kwiatkowski
et al., 2019), TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017), and PopQA (Mallen et al., 2023), and four multi-hop
datasets, HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018), 2WikiMultihopQA (Ho et al., 2020), MuSiQue (Trivedi
et al., 2022), and Bamboogle (Press et al., 2023). Following the setup of SDAR (Lu et al., 2026),
we evaluate 128 episodes per evaluation round on each of ALFWorld and WebShop, and all 51,713
questions across the seven Search-based QA evaluation datasets.
Baselines.To evaluate the effectiveness of SAPD, we compare against the following three categories
of baselines.(1) Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)(Zeng et al., 2024) learns to imitate expert actions
in fixed demonstration trajectories. As a fully off-policy baseline, SFT allows us to assess SAPD’s
task performance and resistance to catastrophic forgetting under sequential task training.(2) Group
Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)(Shao et al., 2024) collects trajectories by having the stu-
dent interact with the environment and updates the policy using advantages computed from relative
rewards within each rollout group. Its reliance on extensive environment interaction provides a
comparison for SAPD in both task performance and training efficiency.(3) On-Policy Distillation
(OPD)(Agarwal et al., 2024) applies token-level teacher supervision to student-generated responses
conditioned on offline SFT prefixes. OPD+Rule further incorporates rule-based rewards to stabilize
training. Its objective differs from SAPD only by omitting our step-advantage policy distillation,
isolating the contribution of action-level guidance.
Implementation Details.We use Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct as the student backbone and SFT- or
GRPO-trained Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct models as frozen teachers (Yang et al., 2024). The student
samples eight responses per decision prefix extracted from offline SFT trajectories without addi-
tional environment interaction. The training framework and experimental hyperparameters follow
SDAR (Lu et al., 2026). Full details of implementation are provided in Appendix B.

Teacher strength alone does not determine distillation quality.On WebShop, the GRPO teacher
achieves a success rate of 64.8%, substantially exceeding the SFT teacher’s 59.7%. However, the
student trained with the SFT teacher reaches 59.4%, compared with 53.4% for the GRPO teacher.
This difference highlights the distinction between a teacher’s standalone performance and its ef-
fectiveness as a supervisor. In our experimental setting, all distillation methods condition student
generation on prefixes extracted from offline SFT trajectories, and teachers provide feedback on the
resulting student-generated responses. These prefixes reflect SFT behavior and may differ from the
histories encountered by the GRPO teacher under its own policy. Consequently, stronger perfor-
mance during autonomous execution does not guarantee more useful supervision in the distillation
setting. The WebShop results therefore show that teacher selection should account for compatibility
with the distillation contexts, rather than standalone task performance alone.

5.3 ABLATIONSTUDY
Table 2 presents the ablation results for SAPD, from which we draw two key observations.
Step-level advantages provide the primary learning signal, while token-level OPD plays an
auxiliary role.To assess the contribution of step-advantage policy optimization, we remove the
token-level OPD branch while retaining step-level advantages and rule-based rewards, yielding a
variant denoted as w/o OPD. The w/o OPD variant exhibits only modest performance declines rel-
ative to SAPD. Compared with OPD+Rule in Table 1, it outperforms the baseline across all bench-
marks and teacher configurations. Specifically, it improves ALFWorld performance by 4.1 points
with a GRPO teacher and 1.8 points with an SFT teacher; the corresponding WebShop gains are 2.6
and 2.9 points. These results demonstrate that step-advantage policy optimization accounts for most
of gains, while token-level OPD provides dense supervision for further performance improvements.
Rule-based rewards help stabilize the training.To discourage undesirable output behaviors,
we supplement teacher-derived supervision with rule-based rewards. We isolate their contribution
through w/o Rule, which removes these rewards while retaining step-level advantages and token-
level OPD. Removing rule-based rewards consistently degrades performance across all evaluated
configurations. For example, ALFWorld success falls from 69.3% to 62.0% with a GRPO teacher,
while WebShop success declines from 59.4% to 55.2% with an SFT teacher. These results suggest
that rule-based rewards complement teacher-derived learning signals. Meanwhile, SAPD retains
substantial task performance even without rule-based rewards, indicating its effectiveness.

6 CONCLUSION
We presented SAPD, a method for refining LLM agents from recorded interaction histories without
further environment interaction. SAPD decomposes multi-turn trajectories into decision prefixes and
compares student-generated responses under each shared prefix to construct relative step advantages,
providing decision-level guidance alongside token-level distillation. Experiments on ALFWorld,
WebShop, and Search-based QA show improved performance over OPD-based baselines across
SFT and GRPO teacher configurations, reduced catastrophic forgetting relative to sequential SFT,
and at least a4.7×reduction in per-step training time compared with online OPD under the evaluated
configurations. These results demonstrate the potential of reusing recorded interaction experience
to expand agent capabilities while reducing reliance on executable training environments.

A.5 SCOPE ANDEMPIRICALIMPLICATIONS
Three limitations are material. First, the common-offset assumption is at the aggregate action-
score level. As Equation 8 shows, a shared token errorb tok(h)leaves the residualb tok(h)(Ni − ¯N)when action lengths vary. Thus, action-token summation does not automatically turn a shared
token error into a shared sequence error. This provides an additional motivation for the action-
only scoring mask: it excludes reasoning-length variation from the aggregate error. It does not
guarantee lower error for every response group, since token biases and residuals can also differ
between reasoning and action segments. The bound must still account for action-length variation;
fixed-length or length-controlled comparisons can isolate it empirically.
Second, centering does not repair incorrect relative teacher preferences. For example, a teacher that
reverses the ranking of two responses also reverses their centered advantages. Nor does centering
alterq B: histories absent from the offline pool remain unavailable, and familiarity to either policy
is not guaranteed. Offline replay can introduce an additional mismatch relative to online OPD, but
need not be more out of distribution for every dataset or teacher.
Third, the guarantees apply to the step branch, not automatically to the combined objective. Token-
level OPD remains sensitive to its own residual errors, and the weights in Equation 13 determine
how the branches interact. Baseline terms can also cancel in expectation in conventional score-
function estimators, so the projection analysis alone does not establish a universally less biased or
lower-variance update than original token-level OPD.
The analysis motivates testing three separate predictions: exact invariance of the step branch to in-
jected groupwise score offsets; sensitivity to response-dependent perturbations and teacher ranking
errors; and the effect of standard-deviation normalization under weak within-group score differ-
ences. These controls should be complemented by comparisons across trajectory sources at fixed
teacher and student checkpoints, with matched local rewards and action-length statistics. Such ex-
periments are needed to establish whether the structured-error regime explains practical tolerance to
unfamiliar prefixes.

B.3 RULE-BASEDREWARDS
To discourage malformed responses and repetitive actions, we use two locally computable rule
checks. For format checking, we verify the required response tags and complete decision blocks:
<action>blocks for ALFWorld and WebShop, and<search>or<answer>blocks for Search-
based QA. Missing or incomplete required tags receive a negative format score. For repetition check-
ing, we extract the generated action and assign a negative score if it exactly matches the immediately
preceding action in the recorded prefix. Rule scores are aggregated and standardized within each
prefix group following Equation 12. The resulting advantages supervise all generated response to-
kens through Equation 13, withλ rule = 0.1following SDAR (Lu et al., 2026). All checks use
generated text and recorded histories without additional environment interaction.
C ADDITIONALEXPERIMENTS
C.1 LEARNINGDYNAMICS
To understand how SAPD’s benefits develop over training, we compare its learning dynamics with
OPD+Rule on ALFWorld and WebShop. Both methods train a Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct student us-
ing offline SFT prefixes and a frozen, SFT-trained Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct teacher. Figure 3 shows
checkpoint evaluation results together with smoothed trends. On ALFWorld, SAPD improves more
rapidly during training, while SAPD maintains a higher success-rate trend during later training on
WebShop. These observations suggest that SAPD supports faster learning progress on ALFWorld
and stronger sustained performance on WebShop, complementing the results in Table 1.
C.2 DISTILLATION WITHTEACHER-GENERATEDTRAJECTORIES
Our main experiments focus on reusing existing offline trajectories, avoiding the need to collect
teacher-specific rollouts through environment interaction. As a complementary analysis, we follow
ReOPD (Liao et al., 2026) in reusing teacher-generated trajectories to construct decision prefixes,
which are expected to better align with the teacher’s interaction distribution and reduce teacher–
prefix mismatch. Although obtaining these trajectories requires environment interaction, subsequent
student training remains environment-free. We compare SAPD and ReOPD on ALFWorld and Web-
Shop using a 1.5B student and a GRPO-trained 3B teacher. As shown in Table 6, SAPD achieves
76.6% on ALFWorld and 70.8% on WebShop, compared with 75.0% and 66.7% for ReOPD, yield-
ing gains of 1.6 and 4.1 percentage points, respectively. These gains are smaller than those on SFT
prefixes under the GRPO teacher in Table 1. These results suggest that SAPD remains effective on
teacher-generated prefixes and may offer larger gains under stronger teacher–prefix mismatch.

D ABLATION ONSTEP-ADVANTAGESCORING
A generated response contains both reasoning text and an executable action, raising the question of
whether step advantages should be computed from action tokens alone or from all response tokens.
18

We compare these two scoring scopes using offline SFT trajectories under both SFT and GRPO
teachers. Both variants apply policy updates to the complete response; only the tokens used to
compute step scores differ. As shown in Table 7, action-only scoring performs better on ALFWorld
and WebShop under both teacher configurations. It improves ALFWorld success from 60.8% to
69.3% with the GRPO teacher and WebShop success from 50.0% to 59.4% with the SFT teacher.
On Search-based QA, the differences are small. These results support action-focused scoring and
suggest that excluding thinking tokens may provide more reliable step-level guidance when prefix
mismatch makes teacher supervision less reliable.
