Abstract
Volatility control converts risk estimates into portfolio exposure,
yet many existing approaches rely on a fixed estimator or a pre-
specified control rule across changing market conditions. We pro-
poseVolRouter, a modular framework that formulates volatil-
ity control as state-conditioned routing over estimator–controller
pairs.VolRoutersummarizes the current market environment
into a control-relevant state profile and separates routing into state
inference, switch review, and pair selection. The Router can be in-
stantiated by rule-based, learnable, or LLM-based decision modules,
while portfolio actions remain generated by predefined control poli-
cies. We evaluateVolRouteron S&P 500, Multi-Asset, Bitcoin, and
USDT volatility-control settings.VolRouterachieves the highest
Sharpe ratio in three of the four settings. On S&P 500, it increases
Sharpe from 0.952 for RV + Naive Scaling to 1.222 while reduc-
ing maximum drawdown from 15.10% to 12.58% and daily CVaR
from 1.76% to 1.32%. On Multi-Asset, it improves Sharpe from 1.498
to 1.540 and reduces CVaR from 1.56% to 1.18%. Bitcoin shows a
similar improvement in risk-adjusted performance, whereas USDT
provides a boundary case in which simpler state-aware selectors
remain competitive. Ablations and sensitivity analyses further indi-
cate that the gains depend on relative policy evaluation and selec-
tive, persistent switching rather than simply enlarging the policy
library. Overall, the results support viewing volatility control as a
policy-selection problem in settings where control requirements
vary across market states.

1 Introduction
Volatility control turns a risk estimate into a portfolio exposure [1,
8, 11, 25]. Forecasting risk, however, is only part of the decision. The
same increase in estimated volatility can require different actions
in different market states. At the start of a sharp drawdown, fast
de-risking may be useful. During a noisy reversal, the same reaction
can create unnecessary turnover. In a quiet market, keeping more
exposure may be preferable. Thus, a volatility-control system must
answer two questions:what risk is present, andwhich control rule
should act on that risk now. Risk-management practice has long
recognized this need for judgment beyond a single mechanical
statistic [5, 30].
∗Corresponding author.
Most existing work emphasizes one side of this pipeline. Volatil-
ity models aim to improve the risk estimate using realized volatility
[4, 28], EWMA [ 5], ARCH/GARCH-family models [ 2, 3, 27, 31],
downside-risk measures [23, 24], or implied volatility such as VIX
[6, 7]. A second line of work studies how a chosen signal is con-
verted into exposure, for example through target-volatility scaling
[1], index-level risk-control rules [8, 9], drawdown-aware control
[10], or regime-aware allocation [ 26, 29]. These approaches are
useful, but they usually keep the estimator–controller design fixed
once the strategy is specified. They do not make the choice among
multiple valid control policies an explicit decision at each state.
Figure 1 highlights the missing layer. We represent a control pol-
icy as anestimator–controller pair: the estimator measures risk, and
the controller converts that risk object into a portfolio action. For
example, an EWMA estimator can be paired with inverse-volatility
scaling, while a different pair can combine a richer volatility esti-
mate with a drawdown-aware controller. The question is no longer
only which estimator is most accurate. The system must decide
whether the currently active pair remains appropriate and, if not,
which alternative should take control.
We formulate this decision aspersistent, state-conditioned
routingand proposeVolRouter. The framework first summarizes
the current market into a control-relevant state, then performs three
simple decisions:state inference,switch review, andpair selection.
The switch review is important because changing policies every
time rankings move slightly can create unstable behavior and extra
turnover. A replacement is therefore chosen only after the system
decides that the active pair should not be retained.
The Router is a high-level decision module rather than a trad-
ing policy. It can be rule-based, learnable, or LLM-based. In the
LLM instantiation studied here, the language model acts like a con-
strained supervisory agent: it reads structured, historical state and
candidate diagnostics, returns a state label, decideshold orswitch,
and, when needed, selects one valid pair from a closed candidate
list. It never predicts the next return or directly generates portfolio
weights. Tool-routing and agent systems motivate this use of an
LLM as an orchestrator rather than as the downstream executor
[38–40].
Empirically,VolRouterachieves the highest Sharpe ratio in
three of four reported settings. On S&P 500 and Multi-Asset, the
main effect is better risk allocation: Sharpe improves while down-
side risk falls, even when raw return does not increase. Bitcoin
shows a similar improvement in risk-adjusted performance. USDT
is a useful boundary case, where simpler state-aware selectors re-
main competitive. Ablations further show that the benefit depends
on relative candidate evaluation, temporal context, and selective
switching rather than on merely enlarging the policy library.

Our contributions are threefold.First, we make policy selection
an explicit layer between risk estimation and portfolio execution.
Second, we introduce a modular routing framework that separates
market interpretation, the decision to switch, and replacement-pair
selection.Third, we characterize when this routing layer helps
through four markets, component ablations, sensitivity studies, and
multiple Router backbones.
Section 2 positions the formulation relative to volatility control
and routing. Section 3 defines the three-stage Router and its LLM
instantiation. Section 4 describes the comparisons and evaluation
protocol, and Section 5 reports the main empirical results.
2 Related Work
Traditional volatility control.One line of work improves the risk
estimate using realized volatility, EWMA, ARCH/GARCH-family
models, downside-risk measures, and implied-volatility signals [2–
6, 23, 27, 28, 31]. Another line studies how a chosen estimate is
translated into exposure through volatility-managed portfolios,
target-volatility scaling, VIX-based control, or drawdown-aware
rules [1, 7–11].VolRouterdiffers by asking a separate question:
among several admissible estimator–controller pairs, which pair
should control the portfolio now?
Portfolio regimes and execution frictions.Dynamic alloca-
tion is sensitive to estimation error, regime changes, and trading
costs [25, 26, 29, 30, 32]. We keep these concerns explicit through
state features, persistence constraints, and turnover costs, but do
not solve a new cross-sectional allocation problem. Instead, the
Router chooses among reusable control policies whose execution
rules are already specified.
Routing architectures.Mixture-of-experts, sparse conditional
computation, contextual bandits, and model-routing systems all
separate context representation from expert selection [34–37, 41].
We transfer this idea to volatility control: the “experts” are not
neural subnetworks or language models, but estimator–controller
pairs with distinct risk and execution behavior.
LLMs as routers.LLM-agent systems show that a language
model can inspect structured context, select tools or experts, and
delegate execution [38–40]. Our main instantiation uses an LLM
in this supervisory role. The architecture is not LLM-specific: the
same state, hold/switch, and pair-selection interfaces can also be
implemented by deterministic or learned selectors.
Section 3 turns these connections into the concrete state–switch–
selection interface used byVolRouter.
3 Methodology
The goal is simple: at each decision date, keep the current control
policy if it still fits the market, or route to a better-suited policy if it
does not. The Router never replaces the downstream portfolio logic.
It only decides which predefined estimator–controller pair is active.
This separation is useful for both interpretability and safety: a
routing decision can change the policy, but portfolio actions remain
generated by known control rules.

3.3 LLM-Based Routing
The same interface can be implemented by rules, learned selectors,
contextual bandits, or an LLM. In our LLM instantiation, the model
acts as a constrained routing agent. It does not see an unconstrained
natural-language trading task. Instead, it receives structured records
built from decision-time information and performs the same three
steps defined above.
First, it returns a valid state label. Second, it returns exactlyhold
orswitch. Third, only afterswitch, it returns one pair identifier
from the supplied candidate set. It does not predict returns, change
controller parameters, or directly output portfolio weights. All
responses are parsed as JSON and checked against closed admissible
sets; invalid or failed responses use deterministic fallback logic. The
prompt schemas, parsers, and fallbacks are in Appendix E, and the
full routing specification is in Appendix A.
Section 4 evaluates whether this explicit routing layer improves
risk-adjusted control, which parts of the three-stage decision matter,
and how sensitive the result is to deployment choices and Router
backbones.
4 Experimental Setup
The experiments answer four questions in order: does routing im-
prove risk-adjusted control over fixed and adaptive alternatives;
which parts of the routing decision matter; how sensitive is per-
formance to switching, execution cost, risk target, and library size;
and does the behavior persist across Router backbones? This or-
der mirrors the framework in Section 3: we first test the complete
system, then remove components, vary deployment choices, and
finally change the routing model.

Routing methods draw from the same eligible estimator–controller
library; fixed baselines use their corresponding fixed policies. The
library combines realized/EWMA, GARCH/HAR, machine-learning,
and market-specific risk estimators with scaling, regime-aware,
trend, drawdown, tail-risk, and multi-asset controllers. Appendix B
lists the implementation-level library and eligibility rules.
The baseline progression is intentional.RV + Naive Scalingand
EWMA Targetingask what happens when one standard pair is
used throughout.Regime-Aware Fixedasks whether state de-

Mixture-of-Expertssoftly combines specialized policies, whereas
Contextual Banditexplicitly selects a strategy from context.Vol-

5 Results
5.1 Overall Performance
Table 1 shows a consistent pattern in three settings: routing im-
proves risk-adjusted performance mainly by changing risk deploy-
ment, not by mechanically raising raw return. On S&P 500, Sharpe
rises from 0.952 for RV + Naive Scaling to 1.222 while MDD falls
from 15.10% to 12.58% and CVaR from 1.76% to 1.32%. Annualized
return is slightly lower (10.84% versus 11.11%), so the improvement
is not explained by higher exposure.
Multi-Asset shows the same trade-off. RV + Naive Scaling has
the highest raw return (16.35%), butVolRouterreaches a higher
Sharpe (1.540 versus 1.498) and a lower CVaR (1.18% versus 1.56%).
In other words, the Router gives up some raw return while using
the risk budget more efficiently across market states.
Bitcoin is the strongest case:VolRouterreaches 30.81% annual-
ized return and a 1.123 Sharpe while reducing MDD from 51.69% to
39.39%. Regime-Aware Fixed and Mixture-of-Experts have smaller
drawdowns, but their realized volatilities are only 6.73% and 7.51%
against a 35% target, indicating substantial under-exposure.Vol-
Routerremains much closer to the intended risk budget.
USDT is the boundary case. Contextual Bandit and Regime-
Aware Fixed achieve higher Sharpe thanVolRouter, and all three
operate at realized volatility far below the 2% target. We therefore
read this setting as evidence that an explicit pair-routing layer is
not always necessary: when the environment is unusually stable,
a simpler state-aware selector can already capture much of the
benefit.
Figure 3 is deliberately a separate 2 bp path-level diagnostic,
not a second estimate of the 5 bp headline table. Within that run,
VolRouterstays close to strong alternatives for long periods and
separates in specific episodes, showing how repeated local routing
decisions accumulate into different portfolio paths.

5.3 Sensitivity and Robustness
Figure 5 varies one deployment choice at a time; the four panels
are separate diagnostics rather than one shared numerical grid.
Switching sensitivity changes how readily the Router reacts, trans-
action cost changes execution friction, target volatility changes the
risk budget, and candidate-pool size changes the available policy
diversity.
Target volatility produces the largest movement: Sharpe is 1.55
at the 5% target and falls toward roughly 0.9–1.0 at the more aggres-
sive 15–25% targets. Transaction-cost changes are narrower, while
switching sensitivity is non-monotonic. Candidate-pool expansion
helps overall but not at every intermediate size. These results have
a common interpretation: more risk, more switching, or more can-
didate policies do not automatically improve control; the Router
must use that additional freedom selectively.
5.4 Router Backbones and Cross-Market
Stability
Figure 6 asks whether routing behavior depends on one language-
model backbone. In the S&P 500 sweep, the regime series is held
fixed, so that panel isolates switch review and pair selection rather
than end-to-end state inference. Backbone rankings change across
markets and risk dimensions; no model is uniformly strongest.
Rolling-Sharpe distributions also vary by market, with Bitcoin
showing the widest temporal variation and USDT the narrowest.
Useful routing behavior nevertheless appears across multiple back-
bones, indicating that the reported effect is not tied to one specific
LLM.

7 Conclusion
We presentedVolRouter, a persistent Router over validated
estimator–controller pairs. Its value comes from selectingwhen
different risk-control rules should act, not from predicting re-
turns directly. Routing helps most when candidate policies make
meaningful state-dependent trade-offs; it helps less when markets
are stable or the library adds little behavioral contrast. Volatility
management therefore concerns both estimating risk and choosing
which validated response should act now.

E Prompt and Routing Decision Format
This section provides a normalized, implementation-faithful speci-
fication of the prompt interfaces used byVolRouter. The boxes
below preserve the audited decision roles, constraints, admissi-
ble labels, and output schemas; they arenot claimed to be byte-
for-byte transcriptions of the source constants. The purpose is to
specify the decision interface and admissible outputs rather than
expose provider-specific prompt boilerplate. Rather than asking a
language model to directly generate portfolio weights,VolRouter
decomposes routing into structured decisions over a fixed library
of estimator–controller pairs. The language model is used only to
infer the current market state, determine whether the active pair
should be retained, and, when necessary, select a replacement from
an explicitly supplied candidate set.
The prompted decision process follows three stages:
State Inference→Switch Review→Selection.
The single-asset and multi-asset implementations use the same
general structure, but differ in the state representation and regime
labels. All reported routing runs use structured JSON outputs so that
the language model cannot directly modify estimator parameters,
controller parameters, portfolio constraints, or the policy library.

I Evaluation Scope and Statistical Reporting
The main paper reports the common headline metrics available for
all four settings: annualized return, annualized volatility, Sharpe
ratio, maximum drawdown, and CVaR. Additional implementation
outputs include Sortino, turnover, switch counts, selected-pair iden-
tities, and volatility-tracking diagnostics for subsets of experiments.
Headline portfolio statistics in the current study are reported as
point estimates. The manuscript does not attach block-bootstrap
confidence intervals or formal multiple-comparison-adjusted sig-
nificance tests to the main table. Accordingly, statements about
outperformance are descriptive of the reported held-out backtests
rather than claims of population-level statistical dominance. For
hosted LLM inference, temperature is fixed at zero, but provider-
side nondeterminism can still produce run-to-run variation.
J Component Ablations
The ablation study intervenes on three parts of the routing process:
state context, candidate/reference evaluation, and adaptive routing.
The non-ablated configuration is reported asFull VolRouter. The
main figure reports Sharpe under the ablation driver; its error bars
are properties of that driver and are not confidence intervals for
the headline table.
A material implementation distinction is that “baseline reference”
has setting-specific meaning: in S&P 500 it refers to benchmark-
comparison context, whereas in Multi-Asset it can refer to the deter-
ministic train-window champion. These interventions are therefore
not interpreted as identical cross-setting ablations.
K Router Backbone Study
Figure 6 compares the four router backbones labeled in the figure

N Reproducibility Notes
Structured router outputs are validated before execution, and
failed or invalid outputs follow the deterministic fallback logic
described in Appendix E. Several machine-learning estimators use
random_state=42, while the pool-reduction diagnostic uses seed
20260703. Hosted LLM generation is not fully seed-controlled even
at temperature zero; the backbone analysis is therefore interpreted
as an empirical comparison of the reported runs rather than a
deterministic model ranking.
