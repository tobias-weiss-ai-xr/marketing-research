# Digital Assistants in Online Retail Shopping

Digital Assistants in Online
Retail Shopping
This book tackles a critical paradox in contemporary e-commerce: despite
rapid proliferation of AI-powered digital assistants—from chatbots to
voice-enabled shopping tools like Alexa and Siri—consumer adoption remains
surprisingly low. Why do shoppers hesitate to trust these technologies, and
what motivates those who embrace them? Through rigorous empirical
research and innovative theoretical frameworks, this book addresses these
pressing questions, offering insights into the psychological, social, and
technological factors shaping consumer acceptance of AI-driven shopping
assistance.
The book introduces the Acceptance of Digital Assistants in Online Shopping
(ADAiOS) model, extending the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) by
integrating Social Presence Theory (SPT) and the Computers Are Social
Actors (CASA) paradigm. Through systematic literature review, bibliometric
analysis, and advanced multi-criteria decision-making techniques—including
Gray-DEMATEL and Gray Influence Analysis (GINA)—the authors identify
12 key factors influencing consumer acceptance: usefulness, ease of use,
performance, enjoyment, anthropomorphism, social presence, sociability,
privacy concerns, technology anxiety, and trust. Drawing on empirical
data from India’s rapidly developing e-commerce market, it reveals that
human-like attributes, social validation, and perceived presence are critical to
acceptance, providing actionable strategies for designing trustworthy digital
assistants.
This book will interest students, researchers, and academics in digital
marketing, consumer behavior, e-commerce, artificial intelligence,
human-computer interaction, and decision sciences. It will also serve industry
practitioners, including marketing managers, digital strategists, e-commerce
developers, UX designers, and policymakers seeking to optimize AI-powered
retail technologies.

Background
Electronic commerce (e-commerce) was introduced in the 1960s and has con-
tinued to grow since then ( Tian & Stewart, 2011 ). Technological advances
such as artificial intelligence (AI), internet penetration, prevalent smart -
phone use, and social media penetration have steered e-commerce growth
(Chawla & Kumar, 2021 ). According to a SkyQuest (2023) report, “the
global e-commerce market is estimated to reach $62,415 billion by 2030,
growing at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 11% from 2023 to
2030.” A Statista survey (2026) estimates that the total number of global
e-commerce customers will reach four billion by 2030. Online shopping is
a form of e-commerce that allows customers to purchase products and ser -
vices from retailers over the internet via websites and online stores, mobile
applications, or social media platforms (Zhang et al., 2017). E-commerce has
radically altered the global marketplace by offering consumer convenience,
24/7 accessibility, greater product and service variety, and more discounts
and offers (Chawla & Kumar, 2021).
However, the impersonal nature of online shopping, that is, a lack of
in-person assistance provided by sales agents, physical touch, and social
interaction with other customers, hinders its growth (Holzwarth et al., 2006).
Moreover, traditional customer service agents are inefficient in online shop -
ping due to limited availability ( Ping et al., 2019). To overcome these chal -
lenges, e-commerce businesses are actively deploying digital assistants (DAs).
DAs are revolutionizing businesses’ communication methods, and brands are
actively adopting them to engage with consumers (Brill et al., 2019). They
automate customer support and facilitate business-consumer conversations
(Luo et al., 2019).
The use of AI-based conversational bots as online shopping assistants,
either as alternatives to or complements of human frontline employees, is
on the rise (Ruan, 2022). A Statista report (2025) concludes that there were
more than 140 million DA users in the United States in 2022 who used smart
devices and mobile phones to perform tasks and assist customers. There

Conversational commerce (c-commerce), coined by Messina (2016), is

As shown in Figure 1.1, the concept of DA acceptance has emerged from
multiple scientific disciplines and processes, including social psychology,
marketing, behavioral sciences, and information systems. Social psychology
studies people’s thinking, emotions, and actions as they are influenced by
the coexistence of others, both real and virtual ( Snyder & Stukas, 1999).
The American Marketing Association defines marketing as the process of
exploring consumer needs and wants and creating, promoting, and delivering
value to achieve customer satisfaction. Behavioral science is the study of peo-
ple’s behaviors, integrating psychology (i.e., the study of human thought and
behavior (Skinner, 1995)), sociology (i.e., relating an individual with society
(Hegtvedt & Turner, 1989)), and anthropology (i.e., the study of what it is to
be a human (Levi-Strauss, 1966)). An information system is a seamless inte -
gration of individuals, activities, information, and technology to gather, ana-
lyze, and share insights. Figure 1.2 shows the DA acceptance-driven online
shopping revolution.
Businesses are deploying DAs on online shopping platforms to offer cus -
tomer assistance. This is due to the continuous availability, functional utility,
and responsiveness of DAs. Also, AI-powered DAs enable human-like inter -
action with consumers. These advantages of DAs enhance customer online
shopping experience and help build long-term relationships with them. This
way, DAs have revolutionized the online shopping landscape. However, spe-
cific problems with DAs’ acceptance in online shopping should be addressed
to increase their adoption. The following section explains these problems,
with a focus on the need for the study.
Issues and challenges of digital assistant acceptance
in online shopping
An InMoment (2018) study states that 75% of consumers value in-person
experiences and feel like “someone is watching them” while talking to DA,

which leads to an adverse reaction toward their use. A PricewaterhouseCoop-
ers (PwC) survey indicates that only 10% of consumers use DAs for advanced
tasks such as shopping ( McCaffrey et al., 2018 ). The main reasons for less
acceptance of DAs for shopping are limited knowledge of features, a lack of
trust, and hesitation due to complexity ( McCaffrey et  al., 2018). Massive
collection and analysis of user information to serve customers increases the
potential for risks and security vulnerabilities ( Rabassa et al., 2022 ). These
practices may violate consumer privacy and make consumers vulnerable to
marketing manipulation, cyberattacks, and fraud (Thaichon et al., 2022).
DAs often fail to humanize the online shopping experience because
of “uncanny valley” effects ( Mori, 2000), which suggest that imperfectly
human-like AI can trigger a sense of eeriness or discomfort in users (Ratajczyk
et al., 2026). Present DAs display an “AI empathy gap,” in which interactions
between humans and DAs feel standardized in comparison to human repre -
sentatives (Howcroft & Blake, 2025 ). DA designers’ concerns have shifted
from implementing functional DAs to fostering genuine, relevant conversations
by replicating human-like intelligence, social skills, and empathy (Khan et al.,
2025). Therefore, it is essential to understand the drivers and barriers to DAs’
acceptance in online shopping to guide DA design strategies and enhance their
use. This book seeks to answer the broad questions listed in the next section.

Findings from the literature survey and knowledge gap analysis
The findings from the literature survey and research gaps are as follows:
• There is no systematic review of the literature or a bibliometric study on
DA acceptance in online shopping. There is less consumer acceptance of

DAs in online shopping, and the possible causes have not been compre -
hensively researched (Ewers et al., 2020). A structured analysis of litera -
ture on consumer acceptance of DAs in online shopping is lacking ( Han,
2021). A systematic literature review (SLR) and a bibliometric analysis of
DA acceptance in online shopping are needed to identify prominent sub-
areas and expand the existing literature (Alsharhan et al., 2023).
• There are a few works on the identification of DA acceptance factors in
online shopping. Past studies have examined a few factors influencing cus-
tomer acceptance of DAs in online shopping ( Gursoy et al., 2019). There
is little research on human-computer interaction factors related to DA
acceptance (Pizzi et al., 2021). Most past studies have focused on func-
tional factors of DAs, such as “usefulness” and “ease of use” ( Klein &
Martinez, 2022). Few models have included privacy as a factor negatively
affecting DA usage (Rabassa et al., 2022). Studies indicate that empathy,
as depicted by DA, increases consumer acceptance (Coker & Thakur,
2024). Knob et al. (2024) propose designing DAs that express emotions
to enhance consumer interactions and make conversations more real and
enjoyable. Hence, it is valuable to identify all potential factors of DA
acceptance in online shopping.
• There is limited knowledge of the relationship between DA acceptance
factors and consumer trust, attitudes, and acceptance in online shopping.
Trust is an essential factor in consumer acceptance of an emerging technol-
ogy (Choung, 2022). A few studies model Indian customers’ acceptance of
DAs in online shopping (N. Kumar & Singh, 2022). There are restricted
surveys on DAs for customer service ( Silva et al., 2023). Hence, a com-
prehensive model of consumer trust, attitudes, and acceptance of DAs in
online shopping needs to be tested, incorporating variables from social
science disciplines.
• There is scant research examining the moderating effects of demographic
characteristics on DA acceptance in online shopping. Customer accept -
ance of service technology varies across demographic groups, including
gender, age, and prior technology experience ( Lu et al., 2019). However,
the moderating effect of customer demographic characteristics such as
“gender, age, income, and educational qualifications” is not examined in
models of consumer intention to use DAs (Ashfaq et al., 2020). To formu-
late strategies tailored to each demographic segment with high acceptance
rates, it is vital to understand how demographics affect DA acceptance in
online shopping.
• There is a scarcity of work analyzing the causal interrelationships between
factors of DA acceptance in online shopping. Work on critical success fac-
tors is needed to successfully deploy DAs in online shopping (V. Kumar &
Ayodeji, 2021). The decision-making trial and evaluation laboratory
(DEMATEL) is a multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) method for
analyzing cause-and-effect relations in complex systems (Si et al., 2018).
However, past studies on DA acceptance have either used multivariate
techniques such as structural equation modeling (SEM) ( Kasilingam,

2020) or employed experimental designs (Zhu et al., 2022). None have
employed DEMATEL to evaluate the causes and effects. Gray set theory,
introduced by Julong (1989), is used to address the imprecision of human
judgments in solving complex decision-making problems. The combined
gray-DEMATEL approach is used across different industries to assess
cause-and-effect factors (Ozcan & Tuysuz, 2016; Ritika et al., 2022). This
book employs gray-DEMATEL to analyze the cause-and-effect factors of
DA acceptance in online shopping.
• Insufficient knowledge exists about the influential factors of DA acceptance
in online shopping from a strategic perspective. Knowledge of the influ -
ential factors affecting consumer adoption of an innovation is important
for developing strategies to ensure its long-term usage ( C.-Y. Chen et al.,
2018). Gray influence analysis (GINA) is a novel, expert-survey-based
method for examining the influence relations among factors in a system
(Rajesh, 2023). Using gray system theory to identify the most influential
factors of DA acceptance in online shopping is an entirely novel line of
research. The book identifies the most influential factors of DA accept -
ance using the GINA method and proposes strategies for managers and
policymakers.
Research objectives
Based on the knowledge gaps highlighted in the previous section, the book
fulfills the following research objectives (ROs):
• RO1. To systematically review the knowledge on DA acceptance in online
shopping and identify the key topics.
• RO2. To identify the factors of DA acceptance in online shopping.
• RO3. To develop a structural model of DA acceptance in online shopping
and find the moderating effects of demographic characteristics.
• RO4. To analyze the cause and effect of DA acceptance factors in online
shopping.
• RO5. To explore the influence of DA acceptance factors in online shopping.
• RO6. To develop strategies for DA acceptance in online shopping.
Phases of the book
We have broadly divided this book into four phases to fulfill the six ROs.
Figure 1.3 presents the book’s flowchart. In the first phase, we conducted
an exploratory study to understand DA acceptance in online shopping thor -
oughly. We systematically reviewed the literature on DA acceptance in online
shopping and identified research topics through an SLR and a bibliometric
analysis. Next, we conducted an expert focus group discussion (EFGD) to
finalize the DA acceptance factors. Then we proposed a hypothetical model
of the selected factors (independent variables), consumer trust and attitude

Introduction to digital assistant acceptance 7
(mediating variables), acceptance (dependent variable), and demographic
characteristics (moderating variables). In the second phase, we descriptively
analyzed the respondents’ profiles and validated the proposed hypothetical
structural framework. To this end, we developed a structured questionnaire
and collected data from frequent online shoppers and DA users. We employed
a partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) technique
and moderation analysis to estimate the proposed structural relationships.
In the third phase, we analyzed the causes, effects, and influence of the fac-
tors. To this end, we designed another survey questionnaire and collected
data from academic and industry experts. We employed a gray-DEMATEL
and GINA approach for the analyses. In the fourth phase, we discussed the
research outcomes and developed strategies for managers and policymakers.

model fit evaluation, hypothesis testing, and moderating analyses of con-
