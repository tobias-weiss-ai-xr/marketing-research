The rapid development of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) has
transformed social media communication by enabling AI systems to produce
increasingly human -like language and content. Although research on AI -
generated communication and user trust ha s expanded, findings remain
fragmented across platforms, communication contexts, and linguistic
characteristics. This study systematically synthesizes empirical evidence on
the relationship between AI-generated language and user trust in social media
communication. A Systematic Literature Review (SLR) was conducted
following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Literature was retrieved from the
Dimensions database in July 2026 using predefined search terms related to
Generative AI, language, trust, and social media.  Of 205 records initially
identified, 54 full -text studies were assessed for eligibility, and 23 empirical
studies were included in the final synthesis. The findings show that research is
concentrated in recent years, particularly in marketing and digital
communication, health communication, and misinformation and synthetic
media. Across the studies, trust is associated with recurring linguistic and
communicative features, including human -likeness and conversational style,
emotional expression and empathy, personalization, formality and readability,
narrative and experiential language, semantic and informational
characteristics, transparency and disclosure, and explanatory language. The
findings indicate that user trust is not determined by AI authorship alo ne but
emerges through the interaction of relational and epistemic cues within specific
communication contexts. This review contributes to human–AI communication
research by identifying linguistic and communicative mechanisms through
which AI -generated soc ial media content shapes perceptions of trust,
credibility, and authenticity.
1. Introduction
The rapid advancement of Generative Artificial Intelligence (Generative AI) has fundamentally transformed the way
people produce, distribute, and consume information in digital environments. The emergence of Large Language Models
(LLMs) such as ChatGPT, Ge mini, Claude, and Copilot has enabled artificial intelligence systems to generate coherent,
context-aware, and human-like text. As a result, AI has evolved beyond serving merely as a writing assistant to becoming
a communication actor capable of producing messages, responding to conversations, fostering interactions, and
influencing public opinion across various social media platforms. This development marks a transition from communication
that is entirely human -mediated to human, AI communication, in which AI actively participates in the communication
process (Hohenstein et al., 2023; Asif & Zhou, 2024 ; Yaumi et al., 2023 ). Furthermore, research has shown that users
often struggle to accurately distinguish between human-written and AI-generated text, thereby increasing the potential for
manipulation and misperception in digital communication (Jakesch et al., 2023 ; Junaidi et al., 2020 ; Pratiwi et al., 202 6;
Anggawirya et al., 2021).
This transformation has profound implications for  the dynamics of digital communication. Social media, once
dominated by human-to-human interaction, is now increasingly populated by content that is either generated or assisted
by AI. In many situations, users can no longer readily determine whether a post, comment, product review, or persuasive

message originates from a human author or an AI system. This ambiguity presents new challenges for communication
research, particularly in understanding how users evaluate message quality and develop trust in the information they
encounter. Experimental evidence suggests that AI-generated language can even be perceived as more "human-like" than
human-written text by leveraging specific linguistic characteristics, while users continue to rely on relatively inaccurate
heuristics when judging the origin of a text (Jakesch et al., 2023; Yaumi et al., 2024; Andini et al., 2026; Hafid et al., 2026).
Trust is a fundamental concept in communication studies because it shapes how individuals evaluate source
credibility, message authenticity, information reliability, and ultimately their willingness to accept, share, or act upon
information. Within social media environments, trust has become increasingly complex, as message evaluation is
influenced not only by informational content but also by linguistic characteristics, source identity, platform norms, and the
broader communication context. Consequently, understanding how AI-generated language influences trust formation has
become an increasingly important issue from both theoretical and practical perspectives. Previous studies have
demonstrated that when users know, or even suspect, that a message has been generated by AI, their evaluations of the
message sender tend to become more negative, even when the message content remains unchanged. This finding
suggests that perceptions of AI authorship play a significant role in shaping interpersonal trust (Hohenstein et al., 2023).
As the adoption of Generative Artificial Intelligence (Generative AI) continues to accelerate, empirical research
examining the relationship between AI -generated language and user trust has expanded rapidly. Existing studies have
explored how various linguistic characteristics of AI-generated content shape users' perceptions through features such as
emotional language, conversational style, human -likeness, transparency, AI disclosure, authenticity, credibility, framing,
personalization, and linguistic clari ty. Collectively, these studies suggest that user trust is determined not merely by the
fact that content is generated by AI, but also by how its linguistic characteristics are perceived and interpreted by users.
For example, more natural language, positiv e emotional expressions, and appropriate levels of anthropomorphism have
been shown to enhance perceptions of social closeness and trust, whereas the effects of AI disclosure vary depending on
the communication context and users' expectations (Hohenstein et al., 2023; Greilich et al., 2025).
Despite this growing body of research, several important limitations remain. First, most empirical studies focus on
only one or two specific linguistic characteristics, providing little comprehensive understanding of the broader range of
language features that contribute to trust formation. Second, existing studies span highly diverse communication contexts
including health communication, digital marketing, political communication, education, and misinformation, resulting in
fragmented findings that are dif ficult to integrate into a coherent body of knowledge. Third, methodological approaches
vary considerably, encompassing experiments, surveys, discourse analysis, content analysis, corpus linguistics, and
computational social science. Although this methodological diversity enriches the literature, it also makes it challenging to
identify overarching research patterns and develop a comprehensive understanding of how AI-generated language shapes
user trust across different social media contexts.
Several previous review studies have examined Generative AI from perspectives such as technology adoption, AI
ethics, education, human-AI interaction, and digital misinformation. However, relatively little attention has been devoted to
systematically synthesizing empirical evidence on how the linguistic characteristics of AI -generated social media content
influence user trust. This gap is particularly significant because language constitutes the primary interface through which
humans interact with AI systems. In other words, users do not interact directly with AI algorithms; rather, they engage with
the language those algorithms produce. Understanding which linguistic features foster, or undermine, user trust is therefore
essential for advancing communication theory in the era of generative AI.
To address this gap, the present study conducts a Systematic Literature Review (SLR) of empirical research
investigating the relationship between AI-generated language and user trust in social media communication. Following the
Preferred Reporting Items fo r Systematic Reviews and Meta -Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, this review synthesizes
empirical evidence retrieved from the Dimensions database to identify major research trends, dominant communication
contexts, and the linguistic features that have been examined in relation to user trust.
Specifically, this review addresses the following research questions:
RQ1. What are the major research trends and contexts in studies examining AI-generated language and user trust
in social media communication?
RQ2. What linguistic features of AI-generated social media content have been examined in relation to user trust?
This study contributes to the fields of communication and linguistics by providing a comprehensive synthesis of the
linguistic mechanisms underlying trust formation in AI -generated communication. Unlike previous reviews, which have

2. Methodology
2.1 Research Design
This study employed a Systematic Literature Review (SLR) to systematically identify, evaluate, and synthesize
empirical evidence on the relationship between AI-generated language and user trust in social media communication. The
SLR approach was selected b ecause it provides a transparent and reproducible method for integrating findings across
empirical studies and identifying research trends, communication contexts, and linguistic features associated with trust.
The review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 framework,
covering identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and inclusion of studies (Page et al., 2021).
2.2 Search Strategy
The literature search was conducted in July 2026 using the Dimensions database. The search was limited to
publications from 2023 to 2026 and filtered by publication type: Article. Dimensions was selected because it provides broad
multidisciplinary coverage of scholarly publications in communication, linguistics, social sciences, and artificial intelligence,
making it an appropriate database for identifying relevant empirical studies. The search strategy combined four core
concepts: Generative Artificial Intelligence, trust, language, and social media. To ensure that only empirical studies were

2.3 Eligibility Criteria
Studies were eligible when they examined generative AI or AI -generated communication, were situated in a social
media context, addressed trust or related constructs, and presented empirical evidence. An empirical study was defined
as an original investigat ion that collected, analyzed, or systematically examined observable data using quantitative,
qualitative, computational, or mixed -methods approaches. AI -generated communication was defined as communicative
content produced, substantially generated, or assi sted by generative AI or large language models, including text, posts,
comments, responses, explanations, recommendations, and similar content. Social media was defined as digital
communication platforms that enable users to create, distribute, interact wi th, or respond to publicly or semi -publicly
accessible content.

2.5 Data Extraction and Synthesis
Data extraction was performed using a structured extraction form developed for this review. The extracted
information included publication characteristics, research objectives, communication contexts, social media platforms,
research methods, sample or dat a characteristics, trust-related constructs, linguistic features of AI -generated language,
principal findings, and research limitations.
The extracted data were analyzed using thematic synthesis, which enabled the integration of findings from empirical
studies employing diverse research designs. Similar findings were grouped into broader conceptual themes to identify
recurring patterns acro ss the literature. The synthesis focused on identifying major research trends and communication
contexts and examining the linguistic and communicative features of AI -generated social media content investigated in
relation to user trust. Methodological quality findings were also considered when interpreting the strengths and limitations
of the available evidence.

3.1. Major Research Trends and Contexts in Studies on AI -Generated Language and User Trust in Social Media
Communication
The 23 studies included in the final synthesis indicate that research on AI -generated language and user trust in
social media communication is a relatively recent and developing field. The included studies were published between 2024
and 2026, with 7 studies published in 2024 (30.4%), 7 in 2025 (30.4%), and 9 in 2026 (39.1%). Thus, 16 of the 23 studies
(69.6%) were published in 2025 –2026, indicating that empirical attention to AI -generated communication and trust has
become increasingly prominent in the most recent years covered by this review. The studies examine diverse forms of AI-
mediated and AI -generated communication, including social media posts, conversational responses, product
recommendations, explanations, advertising and marketing messages, misinformation rebuttals, and synthetic media.
This recent concentration is consistent with the broader development of research on AI -mediated communication,
in which generative AI has increasingly become embedded in online communication environments. Previous research has

The analysis of the 23 studies included in the final synthesis shows that research on AI -generated language and
user trust has examined a broad range of linguistic and communicative features rather than a single linguistic characteristic.
The features iden tified across the corpus include human -likeness and conversational style, emotional language and
empathy, personalization, formality and readability, narrative and experiential language, semantic and informational
characteristics, transparency and disclosure, and explanatory language. These features have been examined in relation
to trust-related constructs such as trustworthiness, credibility, authenticity, reliability, perceived information quality, source
credibility, and perceived objectivity.
3.2.1. Human-likeness and Conversational Style
Human-likeness and conversational style represent recurring features across the reviewed studies. Saha et al.
(2026), for example, directly compared the linguistic characteristics of AI - and human-written responses in online mental
health communities. AI -generated responses were more verbose, formal, and analytically structured, whereas human
responses demonstrated greater linguistic diversity and personal narrative. The study also examined empathy, politeness,
personalization, and stance neutrality, linkin g these characteristics to trustworthiness, authenticity, factual accuracy, and
perceived supportiveness.
Similarly, Lee et al. (2026) identified substantial semantic differences between AI -generated product
recommendations and human influencer communication. Human influencers relied more heavily on experiential and
emotional narratives, whereas AI -generated r esponses were characterized by systematic conversational structures,
functional language, technical terminology, and objective evaluation criteria. These differences were examined in relation
to trustworthiness, source credibility, authenticity, and perceived objectivity.
Møller et al. (2026) also examined the perceived quality and authenticity of AI -assisted social media content. The
study identified comment length, informativeness, perceived quality, and language perceived as “robotic” or “generic” as
relevant characteris tics. Participants perceived AI -assisted content as more generic and less personal, which was
associated with lower perceptions of authenticity and quality.
Taken together, these findings indicate that human -likeness is broader than simply using informal language. It
encompasses conversational naturalness, interpersonal responsiveness, linguistic variation, personalization, and other
characteristics that users associate with human communication.
3.2.2. Emotional Language and Empathy
Emotional language and affective expression constitute another recurring linguistic dimension. Rungruangjit et al.
(2024) examined emotional and rational appeals in human -like virtual influencer -generated content on Instagram.
Emotional appeals included relational and entertainment-oriented content as well as positive and negative emotions, while
authenticity and sponsor disclosure were also considered. Relational and entertainment-oriented emotional appeals were
more strongly associated with consumers’ willingness to follow and purchase intentions than rational appeals, while
negative emotions could also generate engagement.
Saha et al. (2026) found that AI-generated responses demonstrated relatively high cognitive empathy but were less
capable of producing deep emotional empathy and personal narratives. This distinction is relevant to trust because users
may distinguish betwe en language that merely contains emotional expressions and communication that demonstrates
deeper interpersonal understanding.
Emotional and interpersonal language also appears in Gallegos (2026), who examined everyday Facebook
discussions about ChatGPT. The study identified humor, irony, satire, confession, politeness, emotional expression, moral
evaluation, and vernacular conver sational styles as important features through which users negotiated authenticity,
legitimacy, credibility, sincerity, and dependence on AI.
Thus, the reviewed studies suggest that emotional language may contribute to trust-related judgments by providing
interpersonal and authenticity cues, although its effects are context dependent and interact with other characteristics of
AI-generated communication.
3.2.3. Personalization and Interpersonal Address
Personalization is another feature identified across the corpus. Saha et al. (2026) examined personalization through
linguistic indicators such as first -person pronoun use and the extent to which responses were adapted to users’ queries.
However, AI-generated responses were found to contain less diverse personal narratives than human responses.

Rajkumar et al. (2025) examined personalization in AI-supported digital marketing for student admissions. The study
considered personalized advertising alongside emotional sentiment, transparency, and multilingual support, and examined
these characteristic s in relation to student trust and institutional trust. The findings indicated that personalization was

Kubovics (2024) similarly examined personalization and sentiment -related characteristics in AI -supported social

levels of trust and acceptance toward AI-generated content.
These findings indicate that personalization functions as an interpersonal communication cue. AI-generated content
that appears more relevant and tailored to individual users may enhance acceptance and trust, although personalization
can also raise concerns about privacy, bias, and transparency.
3.2.4. Formality, Readability, Complexity, and Linguistic Structure
Several studies examined formality, readability, complexity, verbosity, and structural organization as characteristics
of AI-generated communication. Saha et al. (2026) explicitly compared verbosity, readability, repeatability, complexity, and
formality between AI- and human-written responses. AI-generated responses were more verbose, formal, and analytically
structured, while human responses demonstrated greater linguistic diversity and narrative characteristics.
Sokolova and Yanitskiy (2026) similarly compared human - and AI -generated university promotional posts. AI -
generated content was characterized by a more neutral, informational, and formal style, whereas human -generated
content contained more emotional -evaluative expressions, metaphors, direct personal addresses, and more varied
linguistic structures. The study also found differences in engagement and perceived authenticity between the two types of
content.
These findings suggest that formal and highly structured language may signal informational competence, but it does
not necessarily produce stronger trust. Excessively rigid, generic, or formulaic language may simultaneously reduce
perceptions of naturalness and authenticity.
3.2.5. Narrative and Experiential Language
Narrative and experiential language also emerged as an important feature. Lee et al. (2026) found a clear distinction
between AI-generated and human influencer communication. Human influencers relied more on experiential language,
sensory descriptions, and emotional narratives, whereas AI-generated recommendations relied more heavily on functional
language, technical terminology, objective evaluation criteria, and systematic discourse.
Saha et al. (2026) similarly reported that human responses contained greater personal narrative and linguistic
diversity than AI-generated responses.
In the context of CSR communication, Illia et al. (2025) examined AI-generated employee testimonials and identified
personal anecdotes, balanced positive tone, human -likeness, and non -verbatim repetition as relevant characteristics of
AI-generated communication. These characteristics were examined in relation to perceptions of CSR authenticity.
Collectively, these studies suggest that personal narratives and experiential language can function as signals of
human presence and authenticity, particularly in communication contexts in which users expect personal experience or
testimony.
3.2.6. Semantic, Informational, and Evidence-Based Language
Beyond interpersonal characteristics, several studies focused on semantic and informational characteristics of AI -
generated language. Lee et al. (2026) found that AI -generated recommendations were characterized by functional
language, technical terminology, objective evaluation criteria, and systematic discourse structures. These characteristics
distinguished AI communication from the more experiential and emotionally oriented language used by human influencers.
In health communication, Lee et al. (2024) evaluated ChatGPT -generated responses in terms of information
accuracy, authenticity, believability, informativeness, usefulness, and empathy. This demonstrates that users’ evaluation
of AI -generated language invo lves not only how a message is expressed but also the informational characteristics
conveyed through the language.

Malek et al. (2026) examined AI-generated rebuttals to health misinformation and considered formal versus informal

context, language functions as a mechanism for communicating corrective information and supporting the credibility of the

The corpus therefore reveals a distinction between interpersonally oriented language and informationally oriented
language. The former emphasizes experience, emotion, and relational cues, whereas the latter emphasizes evidence,
accuracy, coherence, and informational usefulness.
3.2.7. Transparency, Disclosure, and Authorship Cues
Transparency and disclosure represent another important communication feature. Li and Yang (2024) examined
the effects of AI-generated content labels on perceived accuracy, message credibility, and sharing intentions in the context
of misinformation. The study directly addressed the role of AI-content labeling as a cue through which users evaluate the
credibility and accuracy of information.
Lin and Zhang (2026) examined AI -generated content disclosure in a simulated social media environment and
considered conversational style, factual accuracy, warmth, competence, and explanation quality in relation to perceived
credibility.
Amina (2026) explicitly investigated the role of AI -generated content transparency in shaping public trust and
information acceptance across social media platforms. Transparency was operationalized through disclosure labels,
explanatory statements, and met adata indicators, while public trust was assessed through perceived reliability, honesty,
credibility, and confidence in AI-generated information.
These findings demonstrate that trust is influenced not only by the linguistic content of an AI -generated message
but also by cues that communicate its origin and authorship. Transparency therefore represents a boundary feature
between linguistic characteristics and broader communication cues.
3.2.8. Explanatory Language and Interpretability
Explanatory language constitutes another important feature identified in the reviewed literature. Bao et al. (2024)
examined natural -language explanations generated by AI models for depression -symptom detection in social media
content. The study considered extractive explanations, symptom markers, emotional language, and transparency through
explanations of model decisions. Trust, explainability, interpretability, and trustworthiness were assessed through
computational metrics and expert evaluation.
Ahmed et al. (2025) similarly examined AI-generated explanations for altered and synthetic images in social media
contexts. The explanations included contrastive and teleological forms, with particular attention to narrative structure,
clarity, linguistic organization, complexity, and readability. These features were evaluated in relation to trust, credibility,
contextual understanding, recall, and opinion stability.
The findings indicate that clear, structured, interpretable, and contextually informative language can function as a
trust-related cue, particularly when users need to understand the basis of an AI-generated claim or decision.
3.3. Cross-Study Synthesis, Contradictions, and Research Gaps
The synthesis of the 23 included studies shows that the relationship between AI-generated language and user trust
is not determined simply by whether content is produced by AI or humans. Instead, trust-related judgments are shaped by
the interaction of lin guistic cues, information quality, source cues, and communication context. This finding is consistent
with previous research showing that AI -generated language can closely resemble human communication, making the
distinction between human- and AI-generated messages increasingly difficult for users (Jakesch et al., 2023).
Across the reviewed studies, human -likeness, conversational style, emotional expression, personalization,
authenticity, transparency, and informational quality repeatedly emerged as relevant trust -related cues. However, the
findings were not entirely consistent. Some studies associated human-like and emotional communication with authenticity
and engagement ( Rungruangjit et al., 2024; Gallegos, 2026), whereas others found that AI -generated communication
tended to be more formal, structured, and analytical than human communication (Saha et al., 2026; Sokolova & Yanitskiy,
2026). These differences suggest that no single linguistic feature consistently increases trust across contexts. Rather, the
relevance of a feature depends on the communication purpose and users’ expectations. For example, emotional and

A similar inconsistency appears in relation to transparency and AI disclosure. Several studies identify disclosure

The review also identifies several research gaps. First, most studies examine linguistic features separately, while
limited evidence exists on how combinations of features —such as human -likeness, personalization, emotionality,
transparency, and factual accuracy—interact to shape trust. Second, trust is operationalized inconsistently across studies
through constructs such as trustworthiness, credibility, authenticity, reliability,  and information quality , making direct
comparison difficult. Third, most studies focus on single messages, posts, or short-term exposure, leaving limited evidence
on how trust develops or changes through repeated and multi -turn interactions. Finally, cross -cultural and multiling ual
evidence remains limited, particularly regarding whether the same linguistic cues produce similar trust judgments across
different cultural and language contexts.
Overall, the findings indicate that trust in AI-generated social media communication is conditional rather than linear.
The evidence suggests two broad groups of trust -related cues: relational cues, including human-likeness, conversational
style, emotion, empathy, personalization, and narrative; and epistemic cues, including accuracy, informativeness,
coherence, objectivity, explanatory quality, and transparency. The main research gap therefore lies in the limited
integration of these cues into a unified ex planation of how different forms of AI -generated language shape different
dimensions of user trust across social media contexts.
4. Conclusion
This systematic literature review synthesized 23 empirical studies examining AI-generated language, social media
communication, and user trust. The findings show that research has expanded rapidly in recent years, particularly in
marketing and digital comm unication, health information, and misinformation contexts. X/Twitter, Reddit, Instagram, and
Facebook were the most frequently examined platforms.
For RQ2, the review identified eight recurring linguistic and communicative features associated with trust: human -
likeness and conversational style, emotional expression and empathy, personalization, formality and readability, narrative
and experiential la nguage, semantic and informational characteristics, transparency and disclosure, and explanatory
language. These features were associated with trust -related constructs such as trustworthiness, credibility, authenticity,
reliability, and information quality.
The synthesis further indicates that these features can be grouped into two broad categories: relational cues,
including human -likeness, emotion, empathy, personalization, and conversational style; and epistemic cues, including
accuracy, informativeness, c oherence, objectivity, explanatory quality, and transparency. Their relationship with trust is
not uniform. Human-like and emotional language may support authenticity and engagement in some contexts, while formal,
structured, and evidence-oriented language may be more relevant to credibility and information quality.
The studies also reveal several inconsistencies. For example, human-like communication is sometimes associated
with authenticity, but AI -generated language may also be perceived as generic, formal, or less emotionally authentic.
Similarly, AI disclosure ma y improve transparency while simultaneously increasing users’ awareness and scrutiny of AI -
generated content. These findings suggest that trust is context -dependent rather than a simple positive or negative
response to AI-generated communication.
The main research gaps concern the limited examination of interactions among multiple linguistic features,
inconsistent operationalization of trust, limited longitudinal and multi -turn studies, and insufficient cross -cultural and
multilingual evidence. Future studies should therefore examine how combinations of linguistic cues shape different forms
of trust across platforms, cultures, and communication contexts.
