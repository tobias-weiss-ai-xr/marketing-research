Abstract
This study investigates consumer attitudes and buying behaviour toward Artificial Intelligence (AI)-based
shopping platforms in the metropo litan city of Chennai, India. Grounded in the Technology Acceptance
Model (TAM) and Trust Transfer Theory, the research examines how perceived usefulness, perceived ease
of use, conversational AI responsiveness, algorithmic personalization, and perceived r isk influence
consumer purchase intentions. A structured quantitative methodology was deployed, surveying a justified
sample of 200 digital consumers across various zones in Chennai. Statistical techniques, including
exploratory factor analysis, Pearson correlation, multiple linear regression, chi-square tests, and one-way
ANOV A, were executed to test empirical hypotheses. The results indicate that perceived usefulness and
algorithmic personalization exert a statistically significant positive effect on purc hase intention, whereas
perceived privacy risk acts as a notable inhibitor. Furthermore, demographic factors such as age and digital
literacy significantly differentiate consumer trust levels. The paper provides actionable empirical insights
for e -commerce retailers, marketing strategists, and system developers seeking to optimize AI -driven
consumer engagement in emerging urban retail landscapes.

Keywords: Artificial Intelligence, Consumer Attitude, Purchase Behaviour, E -Commerce
Personalization, Chennai Retail Market.

1. Introduction
The integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) into retail and e -commerce ecosystems has fundamentally
altered the paradigm of consumer decision -making. Over the past few years, intelligent technologies —
ranging from machine -learning-driven predictive recommendation engines, conversational generative
chatbots, voice -assisted commerce, and computer vision visual search tools —have transitioned from
novelty features into core infrastructure for modern retail enterprises (Bhojwani et al., 2026; LatentView,
2026). In emerging digital economies such as India, retail markets have undergone unprecedented
digitization driven by high smartphone penetration, affordable high -speed internet, and sophisticated
digital payment rails (YuVerse, 2026). Among Indian metropolitan areas, Chennai represents a
strategically vital hub for consumer technology adoption. Characterized by a blend of strong technical
literacy, an expanding IT workforce, and traditional shopping cultural norms, Chennai provid es a unique
setting to examine how consumers adapt to, evaluate, and act upon AI-driven commercial interventions.
The modern consumer purchase journey is no longer a linear sequence of problem recognition, information
search, evaluation of alternatives, p urchase decision, and post -purchase evaluation. Instead, AI -based

Despite these computational advancements, consumer attitude toward AI shopping systems remains
complex, multidimensional, and fraught with behavioral friction (Bhojwani et al., 2026). While
algorithmic personalization significantly reduces cognitive search costs and decision fatigue, it
simultaneously triggers consumer skepticism regarding data privacy, surveillance capitalism, and
autonomous system reliability (Teepapal, 2024). Consumers frequently oscillate between algorithmic
appreciation—valuing speed, convenience, and superior product matching —and algorithmic aversion,
characterized by unease over automated persuasion and lack of human empathy in service recovery (Jiang
et al., 2024; Longoni et al., 2019).
In urban centers like Chennai,  this tension is particularly pronounced. Chennai ’s retail landscape is
traditionally anchored in personal relational trust, tactile evaluation of goods, and merchant bargaining.
As consumers transition to AI -assisted digital platforms (e.g., automated gro cery delivery, AI -curated
fashion apparel, intelligent electronics marketplaces), their cognitive and affective evaluations are
continually negotiated across dimensions of perceived utility, ease of interaction, and institutional trust
(Venugopal & Prakash , 2025; Benny et al., 2025). While younger demographics (Generation Z and
Millennial cohorts) in tech corridors such as OMR (Old Mahabalipuram Road) exhibit swift acceptance
of automated recommendation widgets, broader consumer segments exhibit hesitance w hen autonomous
systems request extensive behavioral data or provide inaccurate recommendations (Zungu et al., 2025;
Dwivedi et al., 2023).
Theoretical foundations such as Davis's (1989) Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and Venkatesh et
al.'s (2012) Exten ded Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT2) posit that
functional utility (Perceived Usefulness) and cognitive effort (Perceived Ease of Use) serve as primary
antecedents to technology adoption. However, in the context of autonomous AI shopping assistants,
functional attributes alone do not fully explain actual buying behavior (Bhojwani et al., 2026).
Psychological constructs such as perceived risk, algorithm trust, and user autonomy significantly moderate
the transition from initial platform interest to actual monetary transactions (Glikson & Woolley, 2020).
Understanding the specific determinants of consumer attitudes toward AI -based shopping in Chennai is
essential for both academic theory and commercial enterprise. Academic literatur e has largely examined
AI consumer adoption in Western markets or within pan-Indian aggregates, frequently obscuring regional
socioeconomic and cultural nuances that dictate shopping behavior in specific tier -1 cities (YuVerse,
2026). Moreover, much of the  existing research remains conceptual or exploratory, lacking empirical
validation of how multidimensional AI attributes directly correlate with conversion rates and repeat
purchase intentions (Pillai et al., 2020). Therefore, this study presents a structu red empirical inquiry into
the cognitive attitudes, trust mechanisms, and purchase patterns of retail consumers interacting with AI -
based shopping tools in Chennai.

2. Literature Review
2.1 AI-Driven Personalization and Decision-Making Support
Recent scholarship underscores the transformative capacity of predictive personalization algorithms in
shaping consumer purchase journeys. Bhojwani et al. (2026) conducted an empirical examination of AI -
enabled features on retail buying behavior, demonstrating that r ecommendation engines significantly
reduce choice overload and cognitive fatigue. Their findings revealed that when consumers perceive
recommendation algorithms to be accurate and non -intrusive, their evaluation confidence and purchase
intention rise proportionally. Complementing this, Amaretta and Manggabarani (2026) demonstrated that
personalized marketing triggers and dynamic product filtering serve as direct catalysts for consumer
conversion, provided that system transparency is maintained throughout the customer journey.
2.2 Conversational AI, Chatbots, and Consumer Trust
The role of conversational agents and natural language processing interfaces in retail has expanded rapidly
from rule-based scripts to context -aware assistants. Falah and Alghaswyneh (2025) explored the ethical
and relational aspects of conversational AI within consumer shopping environments. They reported that
perceived conversational responsiveness, human -like empathy, and real -time query resolution directly
foster user trust, which subsequently mediates consumer willingness to finalize transactions. Conversely,
their study established that unnatural responses, repetitive loops, and inability to resolve complex shopping
exceptions rapidly deteriorate consumer trust, triggering cart abandonment.
2.3 Perceived Privacy Risk and Algorithmic Skepticism
Despite operational efficiencies, the extensive data harvesting required for AI personalization frequently
creates psychological resistance. Teepapal (2024) investigated the privacy –personalization paradox in
digital shopping channels, demonstrating that perceived privacy risk negatively moderates the relationship
between perceived usefulness and buying intentions. When consumers perceive that an AI shopping
platform tracks their sensitive bi ometric, location, or financial data without explicit consent or
transparency, feelings of psychological reactance and vulnerability emerge. This finding aligns with Jiang
et al. (2024), who showed that transparent AI governance and explainability mechanis ms are essential
prerequisites for mitigating algorithmic skepticism among modern online consumers.
2.4 System Usability, TAM Constructs, and Behavioral Intention
Empirical validations extending traditional technology adoption frameworks highlight the fou ndational
role of usability in AI shopping platforms. Benny et al. (2025) integrated the Technology Acceptance
Model (TAM) with consumer trust variables in an Indian shopping context. Their partial least squares
structural equation modeling (PLS -SEM) revea led that perceived ease of use (PEOU) and perceived
usefulness (PU) maintain strong direct effects on consumers’ purchase intentions, while institutional trust
serves as a critical bridge converting positive attitudes into actual financial transactions. Si milarly,
Venugopal and Prakash (2025) confirmed that seamless interface navigation and instant voice search
capabilities significantly enhance the perceived value of AI retail platforms.
2.5 Cross-Demographic and Cultural Receptivity to AI Shopping
The receptivity of consumers toward autonomous retail interfaces varies substantially across demographic
cohorts and regional market structures. Zungu et al. (2025) analyzed automated self-service technologies
and reported that age, educational attainment, and t echnical self -efficacy significantly moderate user
acceptance. Earlier, Dwivedi et al. (2023) and Glikson and Woolley (2020) highlighted that cultural
settings prioritizing high personal interaction and relational commerce —such as traditional retail

3. Research Gap
While the global literature on artificial intelligence in digital comme rce has grown substantially, critical
research gaps remain unaddressed. First, existing empirical studies (Amaretta & Manggabarani, 2026;
Falah & Alghaswyneh, 2025; Jiang et al., 2024) have predominantly focused on macro -level national
surveys or North Ame rican and European consumer bases, leaving regional urban markets in emerging
Asian economies underexplored. Second, while contemporary literature widely establishes the positive
direct effects of perceived usefulness on general platform usage (Benny et al., 2025; Teepapal, 2024), there
is limited empirical modeling examining the simultaneous, competing interplay between algorithmic
personalization benefits and perceived privacy risk within specific Indian tier-1 consumer ecosystems such
as Chennai. Third, prior studies frequently treat AI -based shopping as a monolithic construct, failing to
differentiate consumer attitudes across distinct operational facets (e.g., conversational chatbots versus
predictive recommendation engines) across diverse demographic cohorts (Zungu et al., 2025; Dwivedi et
al., 2023). This study addresses these specific theoretical and empirical gaps by evaluating consumer
attitudes and buying behaviour in Chennai using a comprehensive multi-technique statistical framework.

4. Objectives and Hypotheses
Research Objectives:
1. To evaluate the impact of AI -based shopping attributes (Perceived Usefulness, Algorithmic
Personalization, and Conversational Responsiveness) on consumer purchase intention in Chennai.
2. To examine the relationship between perceived privacy risk and consumer trust toward AI -driven
shopping platforms.
3. To determine whether significant differences exist in consumer attitudes and buying behaviour toward
AI shopping platforms across different demographic groups (e.g., age cohorts and monthly expenditure
levels).
Research Hypotheses:
 𝐻1: There is a significant positive relationship between AI shopping attributes (Perceived Usefulness,
Personalization) and consumer purchase intention.
 𝐻2: AI shopping attributes significantly predict consumer purchase intention among online shoppers
in Chennai.
 𝐻3: Perceived privacy risk has a significant negative association with consumer trust in AI shopping
platforms.
 𝐻4: There is a significant difference in consumer attitude toward AI -based shopping across different
age groups.
 𝐻5: There is a significant association between consumers ’ monthly online shopping expenditure and
their primary mode of AI shopping interaction.

The study utilized a cross-sectional survey design targeting active digital consumers residing in Chennai
who have interacted with AI-enabled shopping features (e.g., smart recommendation carousels, automated

5.2 Data Collection and Sampling Technique
Primary data was collected using a structured questionnaire administered through purposive and stratified
random sampling across key residential and commercial zones of Chenn ai (including Anna Nagar, T.
Nagar, Velachery, Adyar, and OMR). Screening questions ensured that all participants had engaged with
AI-driven shopping platforms (e.g., Amazon, Flipkart, Myntra, Zepto, Tata Neu) within the preceding
three months. The survey instrument captured demographic variables alongside 5-point Likert scale items
(1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree) measuring five key constructs: Perceived Usefulness (PU),
Algorithmic Personalization (AP), Conversational Responsiveness (CR), Per ceived Privacy Risk (PPR),
and Purchase Intention (PI).
5.3 Data Analysis Techniques
The collected data were screened for normality, outliers, and internal consistency using Cronbach’s alpha
(𝛼 > 0.70). The statistical analytical battery included:
 Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA): To validate construct validity and factor loadings.
 Pearson Correlation Analysis: To examine the linear association among the study constructs.
 Multiple Linear Regression:  To evaluate the predictive strength of AI attributes on pu rchase
intention.
 One-Way ANOV A: To test for variance across age cohorts.
 Chi-Square Test of Independence: To evaluate associations between categorical demographic and
behavioral variables.

7. Discussion
The empirical findings of this study shed light on the cognitive and behavioral dynamics governing
consumer adoption of AI-based shopping in Chennai. In congruence with the latest findings of Bhojwani
et al. (2026), perceived usefulness emerged as the foremost determinant of purchase intention ( 𝛽 =
0.366). Consumers in Chennai view AI shopping technologies primarily as utilitarian instruments that
optimize search efficiency, reduce choice friction, and streamline checkout operations. Furthermore,

However, the findings reveal a clear manifestation of the privacy –personalization paradox (Teepapal,

The demographic variance identified through ANOV A (𝐹 = 11.416, 𝑝 < 0.001) affirms the observations
of Zungu et al. (2025) and Benny et al. (2025), indicating that digital self -efficacy and generational

lower risk tolerance. Finally, the chi -square analysis indicates that retail AI utilization in Chennai is
segmented by economic orientation: price -sensitive consumers leverage AI for cost optimization, while
premium buyers seek curated experiential efficiency, aligning with findings from Cloud 9 Digital (2026)
and BlazeDream (2025).

8. Source and Implications of Research
Theoretical Implications:
This study cont ributes to retail technology literature by extending the Technology Acceptance Model
(TAM) into the domain of autonomous AI shopping assistants within an emerging market context. By
integrating algorithmic personalization and perceived privacy risk into a unified empirical framework, the
study captures both the enablers and inhibitors of AI adoption, providing empirical validation for dual -
calculus decision models (Bhojwani et al., 2026; Davis, 1989).
Managerial and Practical Implications:
For e -commerce e xecutives, retail marketers, and AI platform designers targeting urban Indian
demographics:
1. Explainable AI and Consent Management: Retail platforms must deploy clear, transparent privacy
toggles that explain why specific recommendations are generated. Transparent data practices directly
reduce perceived risk and boost conversion.
2. Segment-Specific AI Tooling: Retail apps should tailor their AI features based on user spending
profile. For value-conscious consumers, dynamic price trackers and bargain prediction widgets should
be highlighted, while high-value shoppers should be presented with visual search and stylistic curation.
3. Conversational AI Enhancement:  Brands must upgrade customer -support chatbots from rigid
decision-trees to context-aware conversational models capable of resolving shopping issues in regional
vernacular languages such as Tamil and Hindi (YuVerse, 2026; BlazeDream, 2025).
