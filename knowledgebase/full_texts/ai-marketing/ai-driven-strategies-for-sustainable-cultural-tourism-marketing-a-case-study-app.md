Introduction: While artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) has revolutionized real-time
personalizationindestinationmarketing,itsstrategicdeploymenttomitigatethe
negative externalities of cultural overtourism remains undertheorized.
Methods: Grounded in the Tourism 4.0 paradigm, this paper addresses this gap
by introducing the AI–Sustainability–Engagement Nexus (ASEN) framework; a
novel conceptual model integrating the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R)
paradigmwiththeTechnologyAcceptanceModel(TAM).Toevaluatethemodel’s
structural propositions, we conduct an institutional multi-case desk study of
secondary data from eight globally signiﬁcant cultural heritage sites (e.g., The
Louvre, The Palace Museum, Dunhuang Mogao Grottoes, and Machu Picchu).
Results: The ﬁndings suggest that AI-driven smart demarketing and spatial-
temporal ﬂow optimization is associated with alleviating localized overcrowding
and carbon footprints in the cases where such effects are directly evidenced.
Discussion: Crucially, the analysis indicates that sustainability returns are
not incidental byproducts of marketing efficiencies; rather, they require
explicit environmental design parameters and robust institutional governance
architectures. The paper concludes with an actionable, agenda-setting
framework for destination management organizations (DMOs) to balance
economic demands with heritage preservation.

1 Introduction
Cultural tourism is an economically signiﬁcant, fast-growing global sector
encompassing heritage sites, museums, historic cities, and living cultural landscapes
(Antczak, 2025). It generates signiﬁcant foreign-exchange revenue and helps preserve
intangible and tangible heritage; approximately 40% of international tourist arrivals
are attributed to cultural tourism ( WorldTourismOrganization(UNWTO), 2023 ;
RaniandAngraini, 2015 ). Yet this growth creates structural tensions: visitor volume at
majordestinationsdamagestheheritagefabric,andthetransportandenergyconsumption
involvedproducecarbonemissionsandsocio-economicpressureonresidentcommunities
(Buhalisetal.,2023 ;Citaristi,2022).

2 Literature review and conceptual
framework
In tourism applications, AI encompasses a diverse array of
computational tools that share the capacity to learn from data,
identify patterns, and generate outputs typically requiring human
cognition (Samaraetal., 2020 ). These tools have been categorized
into fourfunctionalclusters:(1)intelligentpersonalizationsystems,
which include AI-driven travel recommenders, customized
itinerary planners, and localized chatbot assistants; (2) predictive
and demand management systems, including visitor-ﬂow
forecasting, capacity optimization, and dynamic pricing; (3)
operational eﬃciency systems, including energy management
AI, waste-reduction analytics, and smart building management;
and (4) immersive experience systems, encompassing virtual and
augmented reality guides, digital-twin visitor interfaces, and AI-
generated cultural interpretation platforms (BuhalisandSinarta,
2019; Gretzeletal., 2015 ). Digital-twin architectures for smart
destinations more broadly are documented by Florido-Benítez
(2024b), providing a comparative framework for evaluating
twin-based visitor management systems that directly informs this
study’sclassiﬁcationoftheDunhuangcase.
At the Dunhuang Mogao Caves, for example, digital twins
tailor tours to individual visitor interests (personalization),
manage physical visit quotas within the cave chambers
(demand management), and monitor visit-induced humidity
and microclimate changes (site preservation), with the latter
managed by artiﬁcial intelligence (DunhuangAcademy, 2023a ;
Yuetal., 2022 ). This convergence signals AI’s maturation from
isolated point solutions to systemic integration within destination
managementarchitectures.
Cultural tourism is deﬁned by the motivational orientation
Richards (2018): tourists’ interest in visiting and interacting with
tangible and intangible culture of the destination communities.
This motive peculiarity brings forth certain challenges in
marketing. Cultural tourism (CT) is based on real, often
irreplaceable,heritageresourceswithﬁnitecarryingcapacity,which
are aﬀected by physical and institutional conditions, unlike resort
or adventure tourism which is based on artiﬁcial and eﬀectively
unlimited capacity resources. The structural contradiction of
commercialization of heritage: the higher the success of its
marketing to visitors, the more it is degraded (Franquesa, 2013;
Tileagˇa,2025).
Overtourism—demand exceeding a destination’s ecological,
social, and experiential carrying capacity—has become the
hallmark management crisis of 21st-century cultural tourism
(Citaristi, 2022). Several iconic sites have reported extreme
congestion events threatening both visitor safety and the integrity
of the physical heritage. The post-pandemic travel boom of
2023-2024, particularly in China, strained key heritage sites the
Palace Museum complex, West Lake, and the access corridors
to Dunhuang forcing administrators to treat AI-driven demand
management as a necessity rather than an optional add-on
(Ministry of Culture Tourism of the People’s Republic of China,
and Ministry of Industry and Information Technology 2023 ;
SunandXi,2024 ).
The concept of sustainability in tourism is theorized
traditionally as the Triple Bottom Line (TBL) framework
(SinghandSrivastava, 2022 ; ZahariaandZaharia, 2021 )
combining environmental, social, and economic performance.
In tourism, environmental sustainability includes carbon
reduction, biodiversity conservation, water and energy eﬃciency,
and waste minimization; social sustainability includes community
wellbeing,culturalpreservation,andequitableincomedistribution;
and economic sustainability includes long-term destination
competitivenessandlocallivelihoodsecurity.

A systematic literature review shows that the domains of
AI architectures for systemic cultural heritage preservation have
three common gaps. First, the Intentionality Gap: scholarship
recognizes AI’s theoretical sustainability potential but does not
distinguish incidental environmental beneﬁts from marketing
eﬃciencies vs. outcomes deployed intentionally for preservation.
Second, the Methodological Gap: existing evidence is highly
fragmented, dominated by single-site studies, creating an urgent
need for multi-case research across institutional and geographical
contexts to ensure generalizability. Third, the Measurement Gap:
no commonly used, multi-dimensional metrics exist for assessing
downstream sustainability impacts, and many studies measure
short-term visitor satisfaction rather than objective, macro-level
ecological and material preservation. These same three gaps also
structure how AI adoption is discussed in adjacent risk and ethics

2.2 The ASEN model architecture
Core Nexus Constructs . The smart tourism ecosystem is
activated through a linear Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-
R) process (see Figure1). First, AI Inputs (Stimulus) connect
the physical and virtual worlds through predictive ﬂow-mapping
algorithms, conversational NLP personalization and sensor-
driven digital twin conﬁgurations. Second, Visitor Engagement
(Organism) is the internal psychological process through which
AI stimuli are manifested in its actions as outputs. TAM is
the foundation of Perceived Usefulness (PU) and Perceived Ease
of Use (PEOU), reducing technological friction and navigation
concerns, thus lowering the cognitive cost of interacting with AI-
mediated interfaces. These perceptions lead to three consecutive
states: (1) attitudinal change, where sustainability framed itinerary
options seem to be socially normative options; (2) learning about
sustainable itineraries, where individuals encounter information
about sustainable itineraries; (3) willingness to adopt sustainable
itineraries,whereindividualsaremoreinclinedtoadoptsustainable
itineraries (Stern, 2000). (2) cognitive learning, resulting in an
increased understanding of the heritage as a place and an
increasedplaceattachmentbasedonculturallycontextualizedNLP
interpretation; and (3) formation of behavioral intention, from
passive compliance to active sustainable choice, by increasing the
salience of environmental identity. The sequence is the Organism
layerthatdiﬀerentiatesASENfromregularTAMapplications.
Third, Sustainability Outcomes (Response) emerge through
a sequentially ordered Triple Bottom Line structure. Primary
environmental gains are treated as necessary preconditions that
enable, but do not automatically generate, downstream socio-
economic beneﬁts—without prior ecological stabilization, neither
heritage integrity nor local economic continuity is sustainable
long-term.Secondaryoutcomes arediﬀerentiatedacross economic
sustainability (balanced SME revenue distribution) and socio-
culturalsustainability(heritagefabricpreservationandcommunity
continuity).Theseindicatorsalignwith( Ljubisavljevi´cetal.,2025 ).
Thedirectionalityofthissequenceisatheoreticalpropositionofthe
ASENmodel,notacausalmechanismconﬁrmedbythisdeskstudy.
Moderating Pathways. Three anchored variables moderate
these transitions. The path from AI inputs to visitor engagement
(P1) is dual moderated by macro-organizational and micro-
individual dimensions. At the systemic level, Marketing Strategy
(Moderator 1) enforces a smart demarketing layer to intentionally
shift demand away from congested zones. Concurrently, Visitor
Attributes (Moderator 2) condition this relationship, where a
tourist’s digital literacy and baseline eco-motivation dictate their
acceptance of AI interventions. Finally, converting altered tourist
behavior into macro-level sustainability metrics (P3) is regulated
by Governance Capacity and Law (Moderator 3). Institutional
readiness, infrastructure gaps, and regional regulatory parameters
(e.g., GDPR) serve as friction points dictating the destination’s net
sustainabilityeﬃciency.

The institutional multi-case desk study approach has two

eliminated, through multi-source triangulation. Second, the 2020–

Futureprimaryresearchincludingvisitorsurveysandmanagement

5 Discussion
To assess the conceptual applicability of the ASEN framework,
Table7 cross-references case observations with the proposed
structural pathways in Section 2.8. The “Result” column reports
the degree of conceptual alignment between the observed pattern
and the proposition it maps to; given the desk-study design, these
are illustrative pattern-matches rather than conﬁrmatory tests and
shouldbereadasevidencethepropositionsareplausibleandworth
testingwithprimarydata,notthattheyhavebeenestablished.
The cross-case analysis shows AI use increases visitor
engagement at all eight sites, but gains in the sustainability
dimension are far less uniform. Where marketing competitiveness
is the primary motivation for AI adoption, gains in visitor
experience consistently follow, but gains in environmental
and social sustainability do not—supporting P2: sustainability
outcomes are realized through design, not as a by-product
of engagement. The British Museum illustrates the point: its
marketing-oriented AI implementation generated comparatively
low sustainability returns, underscoring the importance of
deliberatedesign.
Among the strongest-performing sites, Machu Picchu,
Dunhuang, and West Lake share a common governance feature:
sustainability objectives were imposed by external regulatory
pressure (Machu Picchu: UNESCO monitoring), national policy
directive (West Lake and Dunhuang: China’s Smart Tourism
Policy), or institutional mission (the Dunhuang Academy’s
conservation mandate). By contrast, the British Museum and
Acropolis Museum, where AI was used mainly for marketing and
visitor experience, show strong engagement but less proactive
sustainability integration. The four Chinese cases collectively
suggest a governance model worth further investigation: each
deploys AI within a multi-scalar institutional ecosystem spanning
national policy mandate (Ministry of Culture and Tourism),
municipal smart-city infrastructure (Hangzhou and Shanghai
Smart City programs), site-level governance (the Dunhuang
Academy’s conservation mission; the Palace Museum’s visitor-
management authority), and technology partnerships (Alibaba
Cloud; Chinese Academy of Sciences)—producing accountability
structures, data-sharing architectures, and funding mechanisms
structurally absent from the Western cases, where AI adoption is
managedper-siteviacommercialtechnologypartnerships.
The cross-case evidence strongly aligns with the logic of
Proposition 3 (P3), demonstrating that macro-level sustainability
outcomes are positively moderated by institutional governance
capacity. The Chinese cases indicate that eﬀective AI-for-
sustainability governance requires top-down, uniﬁed policy
architectures at the national level, rather than fragmented,
decentralized decision-making at the individual site level. It
is a contribution to tourism governance theory within an era of
acceleratedAIintegration( Hall,2019;Scott,2021)thatgoesbeyond
thespeciﬁcareaoftourismtheseemergingdigitaltechnologieswill
shape,inrelationtowiderdebatesonsustainabilityandgovernance
incomplexheritagesystems.
China’s model also generates governance tension between
visitor data privacy, algorithmic bias in access management, and
state-driven power asymmetries from biometric surveillance of
public heritage sites (Ying, 2024). BineshandGhaharian (2025)
scoping review of AI ethics oﬀers a useful cross-sector parallel,
cataloging the same tension between engagement-optimized AI
deployment and unaddressed ethical/harm design in a diﬀerent
(gambling/hospitality) setting. Such tensions are not purely
ChineseLouvercrowd-analyticssystemssimilarlyaggregatevisitor
location data at scale but are more pronounced where integration
with state infrastructure extends data ﬂows beyond the site
itself. Further research on governance frameworks for AI-driven
sustainability, balanced against visitor rights and community trust,

personalization has a positive eﬀect on visitor engagement)

(2023); Yingetal. (2024) is drawn from the cross-case evidence.

6C o n c l u s i o n
This study investigated the strategic use of AI in tourism
marketing across eight cultural heritage destinations worldwide.
Visitor engagement gains were consistently observed across all AI
personalization systems studied (conversational NLP interfaces,
VR experiences), but gains in macro-level sustainability outcomes
were far less uniform, consistent with the intentionality distinction
(P2). Comparing the Western and Chinese smart tourism models
shows that it is the systemic, multi-scalar nature of governance
architecture that enables individual-level behavioral shifts to
translateintomacro-levelecologicalrelief.
Beyond the theoretical contributions of including the ASEN
framework, the Intentionality Gap, and the extension of Tourism
40′s empirical generalizability, this research is the one of the
ﬁrst systematic, institutional multi-case study to place governance
capacity as the ultimate boundary condition of AI-enabled
heritagepreservation.
Three main constraints qualify these ﬁndings. Multi-source
triangulation reduces, but does not eliminate, the risk of
institutional bias inherent in secondary data. The analysis window
(2020–2026) precludes a longitudinal view of multi-decadal
preservation outcomes. And the well-known, well-resourced sites
sampled here may not represent smaller, less institutionalized
destinationsintheGlobalSouth,limitingtransferability.
A mid-tier or Global South heritage site is unlikely to have
access to the national-level policy mandates, cloud-technology
partnerships, or dedicated conservation-science institutions (e.g.,
the Dunhuang Academy) underpinning this study’s strongest-
performingcases.Forsuchsites,“governancecapacity”wouldmore
realistically mean: (1) a single accountable role for sustainability
KPIs rather than a distributed multi-agency structure; (2) low-
cost, oﬀ-the-shelf digital visitor management as a ﬁrst step, with a
clear upgrade path once baseline data exists; (3) donor, NGO, or
regional-body partnerships (e.g., UNESCO ﬁeld oﬃces) standing
infornationalpolicyarchitecture;and(4)simple,low-costmetrics
(visitor counts, basic wear/erosion photo-monitoring) rather than
sensor-fusion environmental monitoring. Future work should
test whether this minimum-viable model produces measurable
sustainability gains at lower-resourced sites, where the practical
