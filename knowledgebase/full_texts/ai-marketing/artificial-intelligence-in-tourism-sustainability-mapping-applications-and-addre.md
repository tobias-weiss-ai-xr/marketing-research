Abstract
Artificial intelligence (AI) is inc reasingly being integrated into the tourism sect or, offering promising
opportunities to advance sustainability across environmental, economic, and socio -cultural dimensions.
This study reviews how AI applications contribute to resource efficiency, overtourism management,
demand forecasting, personalised experiences, and inclusive accessibility, noting their potential
alignment with sustainable development goals  (SDGs), although empirical evidence linking specific AI
applications to SDG target achievement remains limited . Despite demonstrated benefits suc h as energy
optimisation in hospitality, improved destination management, and tailored marketing for responsible
consumption, AI adoption also presents signifi cant risks,  including data privacy concerns, algorithmic
bias, workforce displacement, and environmental impacts of AI infrastructure. The effectiveness of AI in
promoting sustainable tourism depends heavily on governance quality, ethical implementation, and
contextual factors,  such as institutional capacity and infrastructure. By mapping AI applicati ons,
critically interrogating their sustainability outcomes, and identifying critical risks and governance gaps,
this review positions AI as a conditional enabler rather than a guaranteed solution, emphasising the need
for integrated governance frameworks and further empirical research to realise AI’s potential for
socially-just and ecologically viable tourism futures.

Keywords: Artificial Intelligence, Sustainable Tourism, Ethical Governance, Tourism Sustainability, Ai
Risks, Smart Destinations

1. Introduction
Tourism is one of the world's largest and most dynamic economic sectors, contributing substantially to
employment, foreign exchange earnings, and regional development across both adva nced and emerging
economies (Martin-Merino, 2021; Zhao et al., 2023) . However, its rapid growth h as long been
associated with significant environmental, social, and cultural costs, including pressure on local
ecosystems, rising greenhouse gas emissions, cultural commodification , and social tensions in host
communities (Alamineh et al., 2023; Henriques et al., 2024 ). As destinations of  all types, from heritage
cities and coastal resorts to protected nat ural areas,  confront overtourism, resource strain, and
intensifying climate -related risks, the imperative to transition towards more sustainable models of
tourism development has become both urgent and politically salient (Bisht et al., 2025) . Against this
backdrop, artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a technology frequently proposed to reconcile
continued tourism growth with sustainability objectives by enhancing effi ciency, enabling more

value chain, including demand forecasting, dynamic pricing, smart mobility and congestion
management, personalisation of visitor experiences, resource and energy optimisation in hospitalit y
operations and data -driven management. Simultaneously, governments and international organisations
have begun to position AI as a strategic lever for steering tourism systems towards more resilient and

operation and Development (OECD), for example, has recognised AI’s potential to support evidence -

technology (Gössling & Mei, 2025; Montañés Del Río & Rodríguez Cornejo, 2026).
These developments raise important questions about whether and how AI can deliver on its promise to
advance sustainability outcomes in tourism, and under what conditions such technologies may reproduce
or exacerbate existing inequalities, environmental burdens, and governance deficits. Therefore, this
study aims to examine how AI technologies (including machine learning, predictive analytics, and
natural language pro cessing) are being applied across the tourism and hospitality sectors to advance
environmental, social, and economic sustainability, with particular attention to applications explicitly
linked to the SDG targets. This review critically assesses the empirical evidence for sustainability
outcomes, as well as the risks, trade -offs, and contextual limitations that accompany AI adoption.
Focusing on studies published in tourism, hospitality, and sustainability, this paper p rovides an account
of a fast -moving research area characterised by rapid technological innovation but a relatively limited
systematic evaluation of its broader implications. By synthesising emerging empirical evidence and
conceptual contributions, this stu dy makes three contributions that extend beyond prior systematic
reviews (López -Naranjo et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025), which focused primarily on technical
capabilities and operational applications. First, this review explicitly maps AI applications acc ording to
their alignment with sustainability dimensions and SDG targets rather than by technology type alone.
Second, it interrogates the extent to which these applications demonstrably support environmental
protection, social inclusion, cultural preserva tion, and long -term destination resilience, rather than
simply improving operational efficiency or short -term competitiveness. Third, it identifies critical risks
and blind spots, such as data biases, digital divides, governance challenges, ethical concern s, and
rebound effects, that may undermine sustainability goals if left unaddressed. This study seeks to inform
both academic debates and policy discussions on the role of AI i n shaping a more sustainable future for
tourism. Underpinning these contribution s is an analytical framework that positions AI as a contingent
enabler of sustainability, a perspective that contrasts with both technological optimism and pessimism by
emphasising the mediating role of governance, context, and implementation quality.

2. Conceptual Background
Sustainable tourism is generally understood as a model that balances economic viability, environmental
protection, and sociocultural preservation, rather than optimising any single dimension in isolation (
Ghaderi, 2026; Krasnokutska et al., 2024; Rodrigues et al., 2025)  (). It is grounded in the “triple bottom
line” logic that long -term competitiveness in tourism depends on maintaining the ecological and social
systems upon which tourism ultimately relies, not only on maximising short-term financial returns

(Saviano et al., 2025; Stoddard et al ., 2012) . Within this framework, AI refers to a broad class of
technologies, including machine learning, natural language processing, predictive analytics, and, more
recently, generative and agentic systems that are capable of performing tasks that trad itionally required
human judgment (Ali & Ali, 2026; Filimonau et al., 2026; Kaushik, 2021) . These technologies enable
the analysis of large, heterog eneous datasets, automation of complex, context -sensitive decisions, and
delivery of adaptive, personalised services at scale.
Scholars increasingly link the diffusion of AI in tourism to the United Nations Sustainable Development
Goals (SDGs), arguing tha t AI's capacity for data -driven decision -making, personalisation, and
operational efficiency can support several SDG targets simultaneously (Gössling & Mei, 2025; Meitei et
al., 2023) , from responsible consumption and production (SDG 12) and decent work and econo mic
growth (SDG 8) to sustainable cities and communities (SDG 11) and climate action (SDG 13) (Sultana,
2025; Wang et al., 2025 ). In principle, AI can help tourism stakeholders transition from reactive and
fragmented sustainability practices to more proactive, integrated, and evidence -based approaches that
internalise environmental and social externalities in daily decision-making.
Simultaneously, recent systematic reviews caution that the tourism literature on AI remains fragmented,
with many studies focused narrowly on technical applications, specific tools, or isolated case studies
rather than on how these applications translate into measurable sustainability outcomes at the destination
or sectoral levels (López-Naranjo et al., 2025; Singh & Hassan, 2024; Wang et al., 2025) . Much of the
existing work documents what AI can do in operati onal or marketing terms but pays less attention to
questions of distributional impact, institutional change, or trade -offs between different sustainability
dimensions (Singh & Hassan, 2024) . This gap motivates a closer look at the specific mechanisms
through which AI is expected to contribute to or undermine sustainable tourism and highlights the need
to treat AI not as a sustainability solut ion per se,  but as a contingent enabler whose impa cts depend on
how it is designed, governed, and embedded in broader tourism systems.

3. AI Applications Supporting Tourism Sustainability
3.1 Environmental Efficiency and Resource Management
One of the most consistently documented contributions of AI to sustainable tourism is operational
resource efficiency. In the hospitality sector, AI -based systems are used to optimise heating, ventilation,
and air conditioning use, manage water consumption , and reduce food waste through predictive demand
modelling and dynamic control systems (Bakshi & Singh, 2024; Filimonau et al., 202 5, 2026; Sultana,
2025). These systems are designed to leverage real -time data from sensors, smart meters, and guest
behaviour patterns to continuously adjust operations. While qualitative evidence from industry
professionals suggests operational benefits (Bakshi & Singh, 2024; Fil imonau et al., 2025) , quantitative
assessments of actual resource savings are limited. Such systems can, for example, learn occupancy
patterns to pre-condition rooms only when needed, detect leaks or abnormal usage early, and adjust the
lighting and tem perature in shared spaces according to actual use. Over time, this can translate into
lower emissions, reduced operating costs, and improved compliance with regulatory standards and
voluntary certification schemes.
Industry analyses propose that AI -supported procurement and analytics may enable hotels and tourism
businesses to reduce emissions at a lower cost (Manoharan et al., 2024), although empirical validation of
cost-benefit claims across diverse operational contexts remains necessary. Scenario modelling tools
make it possible to compare alternative suppliers, transport modes, and packaging options based on their

3.2 Destination Management and Overtourism Mitigation
AI is also being positioned as a governance tool for managing visitor flows and mitigating overtourism
(Bingöl & Yang, 2025). Ghaderi (2026) frames AI as a governance enabler capable of strengthening the
resilience and responsiveness of increasingly platform -mediated urban tourism systems, while
cautioning that its effectiveness depends heavily on data quality, institutional capacity, and ethical
oversight, rather than on the technology being neutral or sufficient. In practice, AI -driven systems can
integrate data from mobile devices, social media, transportation networks, and booking platforms to
generate real-time and forecast insights into visitor concentration (Bingöl & Yang, 2025; Fontanari &
Traskevich, 2022).
AI-driven forecasting systems are inc reasingly bei ng deployed to predict visitor numbers at popular
destinations. While such tools are theoretically positioned to support crowd management and potentially
reduce environmental strain (Ghaderi, 2026; Jiao & Chen, 2018), direct evidence linking the deployment
of forecasting systems  to measurable reductions in environmental impacts remains scarce. Authorities
can use these forecasts to implement dynamic pricing or reservation systems, reroute visitors to less
sensitive sites, adjust public transport s chedules, and trigger temporary caps on access during peak
times. AI applications in smart cities and destinations extend to monitoring air and water quality,
managing waste collection routes, and detecting illegal dumping or environmental rule violations,
thereby supporting adaptive and evidence-based destination management (Najafi & Costa, 2026).
Predictive and smart city analytics have also been used to an ticipate demand and support more
sustainable destination planning and management. For instance, long -term demand models can inform
infrastructure investments, land-use planning, and resilience strategies by identifying emerging hotspots
and vulnerable area s. In rural or nature -based destinations, AI -driven tools can help calibrate trail
capacities, schedule maintenance work, and design interpretive materials that disperse visitors more
evenly across space and time (Topsakal, 2024; Vijai, 2025) . When combined with participatory
governance and transparent communication, these AI -enabled approaches can help align visitor
management with community priorities and environmental constraints.
3.3 Demand Forecasting, Marketing, and Economic Sustainability
Beyond environmental applications, AI contributes to the ec onomic pillar of sustainability by improving
demand forecasting, pricing, and marketing processes. A systematic review of AI in the tourism business
found that AI technologies are gradually being integrated to optimi se operations, personali se customer
experiences, and improve resource management, thereby transforming how companies operate and
connect with travellers (Doğan & Niyet, 2024; Eswaran et al., 2024; López -Naranjo et al., 2025; Padma
& Nabi, 2024) . Advanced  forecasting algorithms can process a wide range of internal and external
variables, such as macroeconomic indicators, online search trends, weather patterns, and competitive
actions to generate more accurate and granular projections of demand.
These capabilities support revenue management and pricing strategies that help firms maintain financial
viability in volatile and uncertain environments, including those shaped by climate change, geopolitical
instability, and public health crises (Buntić et al., 2023) . Simultaneously, better alignment between

3.4 Personalization, Accessibility, and Socio-Cultural Sustainability
AI's capacity for personalisation al so extends to the socio -cultural pillar of sustainability (Andrianto et
al., 2026; Suanpang & Pothipassa, 2024) . AI -driven systems can tailor experiences to individual
preferences and accessibility needs, supporting more inclusive tourism for travellers with disabilities,
older adults, and those with specific linguistic or dietary requirements. Examples include AI -based
translation and interpretation tools, route planners that consider mobility constraints, and
recommendation systems that highlight culturally appropriate and inclusive services (Vujko et al., 2025).
By lowering the barriers to participation, these tools can broaden  the social base of tourism and
contribute to equitable access to its benefits.
AI can also assist destination management organisations in balancing visitor demand with heritage
preservation through de -marketing and spatial -temporal flow optimisation stra tegies aimed at reducing
localised overcrowding and associated carbon footprints. For instance, recommendation algorithms can
be adjusted to promote lesser -known sites, encourage visits during off -peak times, or emphasise
experiences that support local cul tural practices instead of commodifying them (Eswaran et al., 2024;
Milenović, 2025) . Thus, AI can help protect fragile cultural a nd natural heritage while enabling
meaningful engagement with visitors.
Previous studies have found that AI technologies can improve numerous facets of tourism operations
and services in ways that benefit both customers and providers, while noting that con cerns requiring
further investigation remain before widespread adoption (Henriques et al., 2024; Panjrolia et al., 2023) .
These concerns include the  potential homogenisation of experiences, the risk that personalisation may
reinforce existing biases or stereotypes, and the question of who controls the data and narratives through
which destinations are represented in the media. Addressing these issues is essential if AI -driven
personalisation is to strengthen, rather than erode, socio-cultural sustainability.

4. Risks and Constraints
4.1 Data Privacy, Bias, and Ethical Governance
The same capabilities that make AI attractive for sustainability also pose ethical risks. Reviews of
generative AI in hospitality and tourism highlight persistent concerns about data privacy, algorithmic
bias, content hallucination, and a lack of explainability, alongside the continued absence of systematic
frameworks for ethica l AI deployment in this sector (Kumar et al., 2024; Zeqiri et al., 2025) . Tourism
businesses often collect and process sensitive personal data, including location histories, spending

systems may reproduce or amplify existing social in equalities, for example, by privileging certain
customer segments, neighbourhoods, or destinations over others or by embedding cultural stereotypes

These findings suggest that ethical governance, rather than merely technical capability, will determine
whether AI adoption genuinely advances sustainability or simply displaces costs elsewhere. Robust
governance entails clear regulatory gui delines, sector -specific codes of conduct, transparent audit
mechanisms, and meaningful stakeholder participation, including workers, local communities, and
marginalised groups. This implies the need for capacity building among tourism organisations, many of
which currently lack the expertise or resources to implement and oversee complex AI systems
responsibly and effectively.
4.2 Workforce Displacement and Economic Inequality
While AI-enabled automation can improve efficiency, it also carries the risk of job displacement and the
concentration of financial gains among large technology and platform providers, raising broader
concerns about economic inequality in tourism -dependent economies (Zvaigzne et al., 2025) . Frontline
service roles, back -office functions, and even some managerial tasks may be partially or full y
automated, potentially reducing employment opportunities in sectors where tourism has traditionally
played a major role in job creation. Scholars have noted that the tangible risks associated with A I
adoption, including job losses, increased surveillance  of workers and customers, and the potential
intensification of overtourism through algorithmically optimised  marketing, raise legitimate questions
about the compatibility of unmanaged AI diffusion with the Sustainable Development Goals
(Phatthanachaisuksiri et al., 2025; Verma & Bhalla, 2024). If AI-driven productivity gains are captured
primarily by multinational platforms or a small number of large firms, the result may be a further
concentration of market power and value extraction from local communities rather than broad -based
development benefits. Addressing these risks will require proactive labour policies, social dialogue, and
business models that prioritise the fair distribution of AI -enabled gains, including skill upgrading, new
forms of human–AI collaboration, and support for small and medium-sized enterprises.
4.3 Environmental Costs of AI Infrastructure
A further, often underexamined tension concerns the environmental footprint of AI systems. Critically,
none of the tourism sustainability studies reviewed provide full life cycle assessments comparing AI's
operational benefits of AI with its infrastructure costs, representing a significant gap in the evidence base
that prevents definitive claims about net environmental impacts. The data centres and computational
infrastructure that underpin AI tools carry their own electricity, water, and carbon costs, meaning that
AI's application of AI within tourism does not automatically yield a net sustainability benefit once these
upstream costs are considered (Herrera et al., 2025; Robbins & Van Wynsberghe, 2022) . Training and
running large models can be especially energy -intensive, and depending on the energy mix of the grid,
they may contribute signifi cantly to the greenhouse gas emissions. This tension underscores the
importance of evaluating AI's sustainability contribution of AI holistically across its full lifecycle, rather
than only at the point of application. For tourism stakeholders, this implies asking not o nly whether an

4.4 Contextual and Institutional Limitations
Research on emerging tourism destinations further shows that the ethical and practical challenges of AI
adoption are not uniform acros s contexts or time . Institutional capacity, regulatory maturity, and data
infrastructure vary considera bly between destinations, meaning that AI's sustainability contribution in
well-resourced destinations may not easily transfer to emerging or resource -constrained destinations
(Ghaderi, 2026; Jiang, 2025; Kashif & Muhammad, 2026) . In many cases, basic digital infrastructure,
reliable electricity, and skilled personnel are lacking, making the implementation or sustainabi lity of
advanced AI deployment difficult . These disparities raise concerns about a widening “AI divide” in
tourism, where some destinations harness AI to enhance resilience and sustainability, while others fall
further behind or become dependent on externa l technology providers who control data and decision -
making tools. This reinforces the argument that AI functions as a conditional enabler of sust ainability,
rather than guaranteeing it. To avoid deepening existing inequalities, efforts to promote AI in to urism
must be coupled with investments in foundational infrastructure, regulatory capacity, and inclusive
governance, as well as context-sensitive approaches that prioritise local needs and capabilities.

5. Discussion
Taken together, the literature reviewed here suggests that AI's relationship between AI and tourism
sustainability is best understood as an amplifier rather than a determinant of outcomes. Where
destinations and businesses combine AI adoption with strong governance, transparent data practices, and
a genuine commitment to the triple -bottom-line of sustainability, AI appears capable of delivering
measurable environmental and economic gains, as demonstrated in hospitality energy management and
destination fore casting applications (Bakshi & Singh, 2024; Filimonau et al., 2025, 2026; Sultana,
2025). In such contexts, AI can help make sustainability goals operational by embedding them into daily
decisions, monitoring systems and feedback loops.
However, where governance is weak, the same technologies risk reinforcing existing inequalities,
deepening privacy and bias concerns, eroding labour  protections, and adding a further layer of
environmental cost through AI infrastructure (Herrera et al., 2025; Robbins & Van Wynsberghe, 2022) .
The capacity of AI to target and influence behaviour  can, for example, be deployed to drive ever -greater
volumes of travel and consumption, exacerbating overtourism and environmental  pressures, rather than
shifting demand toward more sustainable products and patterns. Similarly, opaque algorithms may
obscure value extraction and power imbalances in increasingly data -driven tourism ecosystems that rely
on AI. This conditional picture aligns with recent calls in the literature for more empirically grounded,
destination-specific research, as much of the current  evidence base remains exploratory, review -based,
or reliant on qualitative interviews with a limited number of stakeholders  (Filimonau et al., 2025; Wang
et al., 2025) . Consequently, this review's ability to establish causal relationships or generalise across
contexts is constrained by the predominantly conceptual and case -based nature of the available studies.
Future research would benefit from longitudinal and comparati ve studies that track sustainability
outcomes over time as AI adoption matures, alongside stronger integration of ethical and equity

6. Conclusion
AI is reshaping tourism across enviro nmental, economic, and sociocultural dimensions, offering tools
for energy optimisation, overtourism management, demand forecasting, and personalised, more inclusive
visitor experiences (Doğan & Niyet, 2024; Eswaran et al., 2024; López -Naranjo et al., 2025; Padma &
Nabi, 2024) . When designed and governed with sustainability goals in mind, these tools can support
more efficient resource use, improved resilience to shocks, and enhanced quality of life for both visitors
and host communities. They can also enable new forms of participation, transparency, and collaboration
by making complex systems more visible and tractable to a wider range of stakeholders.
However, the same body of research that documents these benefits also identifies substantial risks,
including data privacy violations, algorithmic bias, workforce displacement, concentration of market
power, and environmental costs of AI infrastructure (Filimonau et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025) . The
overall conclusion emerging from recent peer -reviewed literature is that AI's contribution to tourism
sustainability is nei ther automatic nor guaranteed; it depends on the quality of governance, the ethical
rigor of implementation, and the extent to which AI adoption is paired with genuine institutional
commitment to sustainable outcomes rather than being treated as a sustaina bility solution. For
policymakers, practitioners, and scholars, this underscores the importance of moving beyond
technological optimism or pessimism toward a more nuanced, context -sensitive engagement with AI as
one element in broader efforts to steer tourism toward socially just and ecologically viable futures.
