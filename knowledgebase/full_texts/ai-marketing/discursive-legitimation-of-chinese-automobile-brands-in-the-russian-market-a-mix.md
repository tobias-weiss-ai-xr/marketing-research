Abstract: Given China’s rise as the world’s leading automotive producer and the radical
transformation of the Russian market, societal acceptance of new market entrants remains a dif -
ficult communicative task. Against the backdrop of growing sales and persistent consumer am -
bivalence, this study examines how Russian consumers discursively construct, challenge, and
renegotiate the legitimacy of Chinese automobile brands in online forums. Drawing on Such -
man’s multidimensional legitimacy framework and Van Leeuwen’s legitimation mechanisms, we
conduct a mixed-methods content analysis of 1,144 comments sampled from two leading Russian
automotive platforms (2014–2023). The approach combines manual polarity coding, which traces
the temporal shift from early skepticism to societal acceptance, with a thematic analysis that maps
consumer narratives across the pragmatic, moral, and cognitive dimensions of legitimacy. Within
each dimension, we specify the discursive strategies and linguistic means consumers use to secure
(de)legitimation. The findings reveal an asynchronous legitimation process: pragmatic legitimacy,
anchored in cost–benefit and reliability discourse, consolidates first; cognitive legitimacy is ac -
complished through normalization and historical analogies; moral legitimacy, by contrast, proves
the most resistant to change, sustained by value-laden metaphors and social identity debates. We
further show that legitimacy formation is not linear but a dialogic exchange between professional
automotive journalism and lay consumer discourse on digital platforms. By reworking market
categories through everyday communicative practices, ordinary consumers act as active legit -
imacy agents. Taken together, the results provide a processual, empirically grounded account
of brand legitimation in transitional markets with practical implications for market entrants and
policy-makers, and extend research on digital professional communication by documenting the
growing weight of electronic word-of-mouth in institutional market debates.
Keywords: discursive legitimation, Chinese automobile brands, Russian market, digital dis-
course, electronic word-of-mouth (e-WOM), lay discourse, thematic analysis, consumer com -
ments.

1. INTRODUCTION
The automotive sector in developed and developing countries has historically been dominated
by manufacturers from industrialized Western countries. Meanwhile, China has occupied the lead-
ing position in automobile sector production since 2009 1. While within the country, the legitima-
tion of Chinese automobile brands is facilitated by the “Made in China 2025” governmental policy
(Baan et al., 2017), societal acceptance in other countries is a challenging task.
Chinese automobiles first appeared on the Russian market in 2005. However, bearing a “made
in China” halo (Han, 1989), sales of Chinese automobiles did not show a substantial rise during
the period from 2005 to 2022. Thus, in 2005 and 2006, 6,900 automobiles were sold under brands
such as Great Wall, Chery, FAW, BYD, Tianye, and Xinkai2. In 2020, the most popular Chinese
automobile brands still failed to enter the top 10 most sold automobile brands in the country 3. In
2021, the market leader, AvtoV AZ, was followed by KIA, Hyundai, Renault, Toyota, Škoda, VW,
Nissan, BMW, and Mercedes-Benz4. However, in 2022, the market situation underwent significant
changes. General Motors was the first automobile manufacturer to declare its intention to leave.
Later, it was followed by Jaguar, Land Rover, Mazda, Mercedes-Benz, Nissan, Renault, Toyota,
Lexus, V olvo, and commercial Ford5. By the end of 2022, only 14 automobile brands (compared
to 60 at the beginning of the year) continued operating in the market. Three were Russian; the
rest were Chinese: Changan, Chery, Dongfeng, Exeed, FAW, Foton, GAC, Geely, Haval, Jac,
and Omoda. When Western, Japanese, and Korean automobile brands exited the market, sales of
Chinese brands rose significantly, from 9% in 2020 to 60% in 2024. Chery, Haval, and Geely oc -
cupied positions in the top 10 most-sold brands 6, which may be perceived as a sign of normative
and cognitive legitimation among consumers (Aldrich, 1996).
However, a recent survey conducted by VCIOM in April 2024 reveals certain ambivalence in
consumers’ perceptions. Some consumers highlight availability, attractive appearance, and tech -
nological advancement as advantages of Chinese cars. That confirms public legitimation. On the
contrary, others (37%) consider low quality to be the major drawback7. The abovementioned facts
highlight the tension between the apparent societal legitimation of Chinese automotive production
among Russian consumers on the one hand and the lingering public doubts on the other. The study
addresses the gap between de facto market legitimation and consumers’ perceptual doubts and
examines how consumers legitimize Chinese automobiles on social media platforms.

Despite the growing significance of Chinese automobiles in the market, the amount of research
on consumer perceptions of new market entrants is rather limited. Thus, Wang and Yang (2008) stud-
ied the country-of-origin and brand personality effects on automobile perception in China. Fetscherin
and Toncar (2009) compared consumers’ perceptions of automobiles from developed (USA) and de-
veloping (China) countries. Chandrasen and Paliwoda (2009) investigated the Country of Assembly
(COA) effect on perceived automobile quality from a Thai consumer perspective, with consumers
preferring automobiles assembled in a highly industrialized country. However, the existing body of
research possesses a range of limitations. First, most of the COO research utilizes a quantitative re-
search approach that fails to provide a comprehensive understanding of the dynamic discursive pro-
cess through which legitimacy is constructed and renegotiated. Second, the consumers’ role as active
legitimacy agents in online debate remains underexplored. Third, the context of emerging markets
undergoing a substantial institutional transformation presents a unique research context.
We aim to address this gap by analyzing the evolution of the legitimation process towards
Chinese automobiles among Russian consumers. Drawing on Suchman’s (1995) legitimacy frame-
work, we adopt a mixed research approach to trace consumers’ narratives on the specialized au -
tomotive forums to answer, “How do Russian consumers discursively construct, challenge, and
renegotiate pragmatic, moral, and cognitive legitimacy for Chinese automotive brands on special-
ized forums? How does legitimation of Chinese automobile brands evolve over time (2014–2023)
in response to market changes?” We gathered a total of 1,144 comments from 2014 to 2023 to map
shifts in consumer perceptions. Thus, in the exploratory quantitative consumer sentiment analysis,
we trace the evolution of legitimacy and identify transformation points. In contrast, the qualitative
thematic analysis allows us to delve into the discourse of consumer narratives.
The remaining part of the article unfolds as follows. The theoretical framework section out -
lines the theoretical foundation of the research by addressing institutional and legitimacy theories.
We highlight the significance of the online forum discourses as a debate platform where consumers
are active legitimacy constructors. The methodology section justifies the combination of manual
qualitative polarity coding and qualitative thematic content analysis. In the Results section, we
elaborate on the evolution of legitimacy in the consumer online discourse. The Discussion and
Conclusion section focuses on the theorization of the research findings.
2. THEORETICAL BACKGROUND
Social expectations, government regulations, and other institutional factors facilitate organiza-
tional structure similarity, leading to organizational isomorphism (Meyer & Rowan, 1977; Zucker,
1977). Thus, in the research framework, the automotive market is seen as an isomorphic organiza-
tion. Organizational isomorphism poses a significant challenge for new market players undergo -
ing isomorphic adaptation. Therefore, entering the automotive sector is challenging. Institutional
theory asserts that a company’s successful operation does not depend only on technical efficiency.
It is essential to conform to the institutional environment’s structural and procedural dimensions
(Deephouse, 1996; Scott, 1995; Suchman, 1995) and to gain legitimacy (DiMaggio & Powell,
1983; Verbeke & Tung, 2013). This idea is reflected in legitimacy theory, which holds that to
ensure a company’s success, it’s essential to follow socially accepted norms (Dowling & Pfeffer,
1975; Hutchins et al., 2019). When a company communicates its commitment to societal values,
it seeks to shape perceptions of legitimacy through media channels, thereby aligning its economic
pursuits with social expectations. Thus, scholars generally define legitimacy as social acceptance
(Crane, 2013; Palazzo & Scherer, 2006; Schultz et al., 2013).

The term “legitimacy” has attracted extensive scholarly attention (Aldrich & Fiol, 1994).
However, the definition provided by Suchman (1995) is considered a conventional one, and in the
present research framework, legitimacy is referred to as “the generalized perception or assumption
that an entity’s actions can be considered desirable, appropriate, or reasonable within a socially
constructed system of norms, values, beliefs, and definitions” (Suchman, 1995, p. 574). Scholars
argue that legitimacy serves as an indicator of societal approval (Deephouse & Suchman, 2008;
Şahiniz et al., 2024; Washington & Zajac, 2005).  Thus, consumers perceive legitimate companies
as more respectable, prestigious, and trustworthy. On the contrary, companies that lack societal le-
gitimacy provoke negative associations and are perceived as less valuable (Fernando & Lawrence,
2014). It is worth mentioning that legitimacy is a collective rather than individual phenomenon and
provides a specific link between society and organizations (Dowling & Pfeffer, 1975).
Suchman (1995) differentiates three legitimacy dimensions: pragmatic, moral, and cogni -
tive. The pragmatic dimension is reflected in positive reputation building; the moral dimension is
achieved by following laws and regulations; and the cognitive dimension is reflected in the belief
that the organization is trustworthy and predictable, leading to unconditional acceptance and reli -
ability among stakeholders (Suchman, 1995).
The strategies used for legitimation have drawn extensive scholarly attention. Van Leeuwen’s
(2007) framework is a conventional approach to discursive legitimation strategies (Yu & Zheng,
2023). It incorporates four distinct legitimation mechanisms: authorization, moral evaluation, ra -
tionalization, and mythopoiesis (Van Leeuwen, 2007). Thus, authorization refers to legitimation
through customs, law, and traditions; moral evaluation ensures legitimation through the value sys-
tem; rationalization is the legitimation by goals and uses of institutionalized social action; mytho-
poiesis shapes legitimation through narratives “whose outcomes reward legitimate actions and
punish non-legitimate actions” (Van Leeuwen, 2007, p. 92).
To operationalize the study, we draw upon critical discourse studies of branding and consumer
identity (e.g., Koller, 2008; Machin & Mayr, 2023) to develop an integrative analytical blueprint
that maps Van Leeuwen’s (2007) discursive mechanisms onto Suchman’s (1995) three dimensions
of legitimacy. Although we acknowledge that no single discursive strategy is exclusively bound
to one dimension, our analytical model anticipates distinct dominant pairings based on the socio -
linguistic functions of the text. Specifically, pragmatic legitimacy is hypothesized to rely heavily
on rationalization (e.g., utility-driven price comparisons) and authorization (e.g., invoking expert
or experiential authority). Moral legitimacy is predominantly constructed or challenged through
moral evaluation, using value-laden lexemes and culturally resonant metaphors to assess the eth -
ical standing of the brand. Cognitive legitimacy (i.e., the perception of a brand as a “natural”
market actor) is primarily enacted through mythopoiesis and normalization narratives, relying on
temporal markers and generic pronouns to signal inevitability and market assimilation. By fusing
these frameworks, we move the analytical lens away from macro-institutional management theory
toward the micro-linguistic construction of value in digital communication.
Following Palazzo and Scherer’s (2006) approach, we advocate that legitimacy is constructed
through public debate. Nowadays, the online environment serves as a universal platform for public
debate and offers people a unique opportunity to share their views with others. Scholars highlight
the potential granted to social media platforms to enable consumers to make public judgements
and, as a consequence, contribute to the public discussion (Etter et al., 2018). These judgments con-
tribute to the organization’s legitimacy formation (Joutsenvirta & Vaara, 2015; Palazzo & Scherer,
2006; Schultz et al., 2013). Thus, “ordinary citizens” or consumers are seen as empowered agents
(Dauvergne & LeBaron, 2014; Etter et al., 2018) who are entitled to play a vital role and to express

3. METHODOLOGY
The present study focuses on the Russian automotive sector due to several reasons and per -
forms a content analysis integrating qualitative and quantitative methods. Previously, the Russian
automotive industry offered a diverse range of choices, while Russian consumers demonstrated a
strong tendency toward conspicuous consumption. Therefore, the automotive sector should have
been able to meet the needs of comfortable transportation and satisfy status expectations. Since
2022, the market landscape has experienced significant transformations; however, consumer needs
remained unchanged. At this point, we focus our research on the legitimacy transformation of Chi-
nese automotive brands, which currently dominate the sector. We aimed to identify the legitimacy
strategies consumers use when addressing Chinese automotive brands. Two large and influential
automobile platforms were chosen for data collection. Adhering to strict ethical norms, the plat -
forms were anonymized. Both are among the top 10 most visited automobile platforms 8 which
ensures access to rich user-generated content. Both platforms are focused on automobile news,
test-drive overviews with photos and videos, automobile catalogs with detailed descriptions and
prices, and automobile owners’ feedback. Below test-drive articles, there is a comment section that
lets us assess the temporal and topical content of each comment. The target audience of both plat-
forms is in Russia. In both cases, the gender distribution is 30% female to 70% male consumers.
Most commonly, these platforms are visited by the 25-35 age group (about 22-23%), followed by
the 45-54 (about 20-21%) and 35-44 (about 18%) age groups 9, matching the demographic profile
of Russian car owners.
To analyze the evolution of consumer perceptions, we initially extracted all available top-level
comments and replies from webpages devoted to test-drive overviews of various Chinese auto -
mobile brands (including Changan, Chery, Dongfeng, Exeed, GAC, Geely, and Haval) published
between 2014 and 2023. This yielded an initial raw corpus of 4580 comments. To ensure equal
selection probability across different brands and years, and to mitigate the bias of overrepresented,
highly commented models, we used a manual random number generator (Excel RAND) to extract
a randomized working sample of 1,144 unique comments. During the subsequent qualitative cod-
ing phase, we iteratively monitored the emergence of new themes. By the conclusion of coding
the 1,144-comment sample, no new thematic codes emerged, confirming that thematic saturation
(Guest et al., 2006) had been achieved within our randomized subset.
The study employs content analysis as the primary research technique (Krippendorff, 2018).
Based on the definition provided by Bardin (1986), content analysis is a technique designed to “ob-
tain, by means of systematic and objective procedures of the content description of the messages,
indicators (quantitative or not) which enable the inference of knowledge regarding the production
and reception conditions” (Piñeiro-Naval, 2020, p. 29). In the research framework, we combine

qualitative and quantitative content analysis. At the quantitative analysis stage, we perform con -
sumer sentiment analysis to track trends in consumer perception. At the second, qualitative stage,
we perform a thematic analysis to identify discursive legitimacy strategies. Thus, the present study
doesn’t aim for statistical generalization (Jackson, 2009) but instead attempts to answer the “why”
and “how” questions that are concerned with “discovery” and “description” (Mahoney & Goertz,

We refer to a comment as a unit of analysis. Online consumer reviews and comments serve as a
source of unsolicited data, a form of one-to-many communication between the reviewer/comment-
er and readers (Litvin et al., 2008). In the Internet age, consumers frequently rely on peers’ online
reviews to make informed decisions. Thus, electronic word-of-mouth (e-WOM), “any positive or
negative statement made by potential, actual, or former consumers about a product or company,
which is made available to a multitude of people and institutions via the Internet” (Hennig-Thurau
et al., 2004, p. 39), becomes essential for decision-making.
Data gathered from online forums and social media platforms offer multiple advantages, pro-
viding researchers with access to diverse consumer voices (Castelló et al., 2016). Thus, within the
research framework, we acknowledge consumers’ roles in constructing the legitimacy of Chinese
automotive producers through e-WOM and online debates.
All data extracted from the automotive platforms included the username, the comment date,
the comment text, and, when applicable, other users’ replies. Then, we sorted the extracted com -
ments by date to analyze the temporal evolution of the legitimation process. The original language
of the comments was retained to preserve linguistic nuances. Thus, we translated the key phrases
used in the article for reporting the research findings.
Drawing on Suchman’s (1995) legitimacy approach, the coding framework was developed
to incorporate three legitimacy dimensions: pragmatic, moral, and cognitive. The coding sample
is presented in Appendix A. The coding process followed the standard qualitative data coding
approach (Bingham, 2023). At first, all the comments were read through to gain a thorough un -
derstanding of the context (Braun & Clarke, 2021). Then, at the open coding stage, we initially
tagged the comments with an emergent theme. Each comment was evaluated for the presence of at
least one of the three legitimacy dimensions. To perform a further sentiment analysis, we manually
assigned each comment within the identified dimensions a sentiment score: positive, neutral, or
negative (Bingham, 2023). Positive scores were assigned to the comments expressing approval,
trust, or a general favorable evaluation (e.g., “Great value for money”). The comment was consid-
ered negative when it contained criticism, dissatisfaction, or a negative evaluation (e.g., “This car
is unsafe”). Neutral comments state a fact or express an ambivalent view without clear positive or
negative connotation.
At the quantitative stage, we eschewed automated computational tools in favor of manual
qualitative polarity coding. Two independent coders manually assigned a polarity score (positive,
neutral, or negative) to each comment to capture the highly contextual irony, slang, and metaphors
typical of Russian automotive discourse, which automated sentiment analysis often misinterprets.
Prior to the main coding phase, the two coders were trained on a randomly selected 10% subset
of the data (approx. 115 comments) to align their interpretations of the codebook. Inter-coder
reliability was calculated using Cohen’s kappa, yielding κ=0.79 for the assignment of legitimacy
dimensions and κ=0.81 for polarity assignment, indicating substantial agreement (Landis & Koch,
1977). All minor discrepancies were resolved collaboratively through peer discussion.
Thus, a temporal analysis, conducted as a year-by-year breakdown of consumer sentiment,
allowed us to trace the evolution of consumer legitimacy perception.

4. DATA ANALYSIS AND RESULTS
4.1. Consumer sentiment dynamics
At the initial stage of the data analysis, we conducted consumer sentiment analysis. The as -
signed sentiments were summarized and presented in Figure 1 to trace the dynamics of consumer
sentiment throughout the analyzed period. We calculated the total number of consumer comments
for each year and calculated the shares of positive, negative, and neutral comments. At this stage of
analysis, we do not aim for statistical significance and generalizability but instead use the summa-
rized dynamics for the trend, consistent with the exploratory sentiment studies in online consumer
discourse (Pang & Lee, 2008).
As shown in Figure 1, negative consumer sentiment has prevailed since 2014. Thus, the 2014-
2016 period is characterized by an overwhelming number of comments (about 60%) reflecting
skepticism and a negative attitude towards early Chinese automobile models. At that time, Chinese
automobiles were seen as low-end and lacked consumer trust.  As one of the comments states:
“Они (китайцы) только недавно рис собирали, а уже мнят себя автопроизводителями”
(They (Chinese) were only recently collecting rice and already fancy themselves automakers) 10
(2014). The comments of this period clearly reflect the poor reputation of Chinese automobiles,
which is most likely predetermined by the “halo” effect of the country (Han, 1989). The domi -
nant consumer narrative stresses low price, lack of security and reliability, which are essential for
high-involvement products (Verlegh & Steenkamp, 1999).
It must be acknowledged that the volume of comments varies significantly by year, with ear -
lier years (e.g., 2014–2016) yielding much smaller sub-samples (e.g., N=25 in 2016) due to the
limited market presence of these brands at the time. Year-on-year percentage shifts in this early
phase should be interpreted with statistical caution. To mitigate this fragility, our analysis aggre -
gates the qualitative discussion into broader temporal epochs (2014-2016; 2017-2019; 2020-2023)
to capture macro-level discursive shifts rather than isolated annual fluctuations.
In the period 2017-2019, we observe the gradual change in consumer narratives. Negative
sentiments are still dominant; meanwhile, the number of both neutral and positive sentiments
is growing. It signifies consumer recognition of the country’s technological advancements, re -
sulting in higher-quality automotive production. At this time, new models with better design
and features appear on the market, as reflected in one of the comments: “Китайские машины
подтягиваются. Скоро Крета будет отставать от Чанган”  (Chinese cars are improving.
The day is near when the Creta will lag behind the Changan) (2018). However, along with im -
proved design, automobile prices increase, intensifying the price–value debate among consumers
(Govorova, 2023).

(especially around pricing), the proportion of positive and neutral responses has grown steadily,

4.2. Thematic analysis
The thematic analysis results were divided into three major thematic groups aligning with
Suchman’s (1995) legitimacy paradigm. Pragmatic legitimacy is assessed based on consumers’
perceived benefits: the price/value dichotomy; perceived reliability and quality reflected in tech -
nical characteristics; after-sales service; and vehicle comfort. When addressing the moral legiti -
macy dimension, we focused on consumers’ concerns about the ethical considerations of design
and the originality of spare parts; manufacturers’ honesty and transparency; contributions to the
local economy and adherence to business ethics principles; and social responsibility. The cognitive
legitimacy dimension is reflected in consumer narratives about the ability to recognize Chinese au-
tomobile brands, perceived market position, and whether automobiles are seen as a natural and un-
derstandable part of the market. Within each dimension, we outline specific mechanisms consum-
ers use to ensure (de)legitimation based on Van Leeuwen’s legitimation mechanisms framework.

4.2.1. Pragmatic legitimacy dimension
When assessing dimensions at the sentiment level, we observe that pragmatic narrative pre -
vails over moral and cognitive dimensions (Table 1). The pragmatic dimension becomes the most
discussed, as it reflects practical benefits and losses for consumers. The debate over pragmatic le-
gitimacy is intense. On the one hand, consumers emphasize the positive value-for-money attitude,
constructing pragmatic legitimacy through rationalization: Comments such as “За те же деньги
у корейцев голый салон, а китаец уже в полном фарше” (For the same money, you get a bare-
bones interior with the Koreans, but the Chinese car comes fully loaded) (2019). Here we observe
a direct feature-to-price comparison “за те же деньги”  (for the same money). The metaphor
“голый салон” (bare-bones interior) implies lack, poverty, and constructs a negative image of
the Korean cars. On the contrary, the idiom “в полном фарше” (fully loaded) effectively demon-
strates an equipment advantage. Consumers state that Chinese automobile brands offer superior
features and equipment at equivalent or lower prices than established competitors. For example:
“За полтора ляма вполне себе хорошая машина…почему бы и нет?” (For 1.5 million — that’s
a really good car... why not?) (2020). Extended warranties (typically 5-7 years) and improving
reliability perceptions further bolstered pragmatic legitimacy, though concerns about long-term
durability and resale value persisted throughout the period. On the other hand, some consum -

reflects specific macro-discourses in the Russian automotive subculture. The lexeme “голый” (na-
ked) triggers a semantic field of vulnerability, asceticism, shortage, and lack, thus subtly criticizing
established brands for perceived corporate greed. The phrase “в полном фарше” is a culturally
embedded colloquialism rooted in the 1990s Russian slang, denoting excess, completeness, and

Another powerful legitimacy mechanism widely applied by the forum participants is author-

represented the most significant challenge. Consumers seek reliable proof of durability and safety
statistics typical for novel market brands (Khoirina et al., 2025). However, Chinese brands do not
publish European crash test results, challenging their honesty and transparency. Thus, comments
anchoring personal ownership incorporating durability statistics serve as a powerful legitimation
tool.

powerful metaphor comparing Chinese car engines to the lottery. The long-term automobile usage

creates an image of an asymmetry.

elevate their own status to that of lay-experts.

4.2.2. Moral legitimacy dimension
As indicated in Table 1, the moral dimension is uniquely characterized by a persistent nega -
tivity, with 66.7% of comments assigned a negative polarity. Discursively, this intense polarization
manifests itself because moral framing in the corpus is predominantly focused on the social iden -
tity of the consumer rather than the mechanical attributes of the product. The qualitative polarity
split reflects a harsh discursive dichotomy of consumer typologies: the “rational holdout” versus
the “foolish early adopter.”
In contrast to the pragmatic dimension, the moral legitimacy dimension remained highly po -
larized over the analyzed period, and the prevalence of negative sentiments remained unchanged,
demonstrating a slight decrease in the final analyzed period. Moral legitimacy is mainly con -
structed through moral evaluation mechanisms. The negative attitude remains persistent in a
great number of comments. Они (китайцы) только недавно рис собирали, а уже мнят себя
автопроизводителями (They (Chinese) were only recently collecting rice and already fancy
themselves automakers) (2014). The use of the pronoun “они” (they) creates a distance between
him and “collective” Chinese. The temporal marker “только недавно…а уже” (only recently…
and already)” stresses the commentator’s doubts about Chinese rapid technological advancement,
implying a delegitimation connotation. It is intensified by the synecdoche “рис собирали” (col-
lecting rice), where the agricultural past stresses doubts in technological capability.
From the discursive viewpoint, this sentence introduces a culturally loaded synecdoche to
deny moral legitimacy. The phrase “рис собирали” (collecting rice) taps into deeply entrenched
post-Soviet macro-discourses that historically stereotyped China as a purely agrarian, technolog -
ically backward neighbor. The temporal contrast created by the elements “только недавно... а
уже” (only recently... and already) serves to compress time, denying the manufacturer the evolu-
tionary right to claim high-tech status. The pragmatic effect is then grammatically intensified by
the reflexive verb “мнят себя” (fancy themselves / imagine themselves to be) , which carries an
inherent semantic weight of delusion and unjustified arrogance. Through these lexico-grammatical
choices, the commentator constructs a moral boundary, punishing the brand for violating the “nat-
ural” historical hierarchy of global automotive production.
The negative moral evaluation intensified during COVID-19 pandemic which spike moral
criticism: “А Китай вообще должен понести ответственность, поэтому я призываю бой-
котировать китайские товары (China must be held accountable, so I call for a boycott…)”
(2020). Here the commenter uses political moralization. “Китай (China)” is personified. Modal
verb “должен (must)” denotes a strong obligation. On the other hand consumer acknowledge fast
developing and omnipresent consumer goods: “Они быстро всему учатся. Я дома смотрю уже

Design originality (or “theft” of designs) and production standards further complicated moral

strategy. Reference to the historical examples strengthens normalization. The path metaphor at the
end of the statement portrays copying as a stage of development. The moral legitimation is con -
structed by rationalization mechanisms.
It’s worth mentioning that moral legitimacy demonstrated the slowest improvement over the
analyzed period. When addressing moral legitimacy, discursive strategies should note that moral
framing is predominantly focused on the social meaning of consumption rather than on the product
itself. It is tightly linked to the dichotomy of consumer types. The first clearly pronounced dichoto-
my is “rational vs. foolish”. Some consumers frame themselves as “consciously ignoring Chinese
cars” as an equivalent of rationality, while their opponents, who “dive in headfirst,” are viewed as
an equivalent of foolishness.  On the other hand, some use financial morality framing. They stress
that those who opt for Chinese automobiles have more important life priorities and are seen as
more responsible, preferring “to invest in children’s education or travel”.
All in all, consumers primarily refer to moral evaluation to construct moral legitimacy. At
this point, we observe emotionally colorful metaphors and synecdoche used to present value
judgements. Rationalization mechanisms appear mainly through historical anchors. Mythopoiesis
mechanisms articulate the call for punishment of Chinese consumption. Unlike the pragmatic di -
mension, moral legitimacy relies on effective moral evaluation.
4.2.3. Cognitive legitimacy dimension
Although cognitive legitimacy accounts for only 10.2% of the total corpus, its linguistic man-
ifestations represent the most profound institutional shift in the dataset: the transition from “alien”
to “normal.” Early commenters (2014-2016) exhibited a complete cognitive legitimacy deficit, us-
ing lexemes of incomprehension such as “непонятные” (incomprehensible) or “инопланетные”
(alien), linguistically positioning the brands outside the established taxonomic boundaries of the
automotive market.
By the 2020-2023 period, cognitive legitimation is achieved primarily through the discursive
mechanism of mythopoiesis, specifically realized through normalization narratives. Linguistically,
this is enacted through specific temporal adverbs and generic pronouns. Consider the following
example: “СКОРО ВСЕ БУДУТ НА КИТАЙЦАХ ЕЗДИТЬ” (Soon everyone will be driving Chi-
nese cars) (2023). The temporal adverb “Скоро” (soon) creates a trajectory of inevitability. The
universal quantifier/generic pronoun “все” (everyone) overrides individual consumer choice, es -
tablishing market dominance as a collective, naturalized future reality.
Furthermore, users frequently employ comparative historical analogies to enact cognitive as -
similation: “Когда появились первые японцы, бывалые владельцы лады ухмылялись”  (When
the first Japanese cars appeared, experienced Lada owners smirked) (2017). This specific narrative
structure normalizes current skepticism by framing it as a temporary, repeatable historical phase.

5. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
In this study, which aimed to explore how Russian consumers discursively construct, chal -
lenge, and renegotiate pragmatic, moral, and cognitive legitimacy for Chinese automotive brands
on specialized forums, we examined the evolution of the Chinese automobile legitimacy process in
the Russian market. We randomly selected 1,144 consumer comments from two online automobile
forums published from 2014 to 2023. Drawing on the integrative framework developed based on
Suchman’s legitimacy dimensions and van Leeuwen’s legitimation mechanisms, we performed
content analysis. Our findings allow us to reconstruct a complex, multidimensional legitimation
process.
Rather than correcting Suchman’s (1995) theoretical paradigm, our findings provide critical
empirical evidence of how legitimation sequences unfold in highly transitional, non-Western mar-
ket contexts. In markets where functional evaluation criteria dominate, we observe a distinct empir-
ical asynchronicity: pragmatic legitimacy acts as the vanguard, establishing itself rapidly through
rationalized cost-benefit discourses, with moral and cognitive legitimacy lagging significantly,
weighed down by historically entrenched cultural stereotypes. The findings suggest that pragmatic
legitimation goes along with product quality improvement that triggers consumers’ reevaluation,
while moral legitimacy is linked with value-based judgements deeply rooted in consumers’ minds
and is highly resilient to change.  This sequence appears particularly suited to markets where
functional evaluation criteria dominate and where entering brands confront major stereotyping or
historical reputation challenges (Deephouse & Suchman, 2008).
Drawing on Van Leeuwen’s (2007) legitimation mechanisms, we demonstrate how authoriza-
tion, moral evaluation, rationalization, and mythopoiesis are realized through specific grammatical
and lexical means in Russian-language consumer comments.
By analyzing the specific lexico-grammatical choices (e.g., epistemic modality, cultural -
ly embedded metaphors, the use of the “us vs. them” juxtaposition through pronouns, syntactic
framing), our study demonstrates that consumer legitimation is not merely a shift in numerical
sentiment, but a complex linguistic renegotiation. The transition from using agrarian synecdoche
(i.e., delegitimizing) to utilizing the precise statistical lexicon of automotive professionals (i.e.,
legitimizing) illustrates how macro-level institutional changes in the Russian market are enacted at
the micro-discursive level. This highlights the value of merging organizational legitimacy frame -
works with critical discourse analysis, providing a deeper understanding of how perceptions of the
market are constructed through everyday language.
Then, we offer a dynamic perspective to the evolution of the legitimation process by tracing
legitimation over a decade.

Moreover, we operationalize discursive legitimacy processes at the consumer level. Our find-
ings demonstrate that consumers are active legitimacy constructors rather than mere reflectors (Et-
ter et al., 2018; Palazzo & Scherer, 2006). Thus, we extend digital professional discourse research
by demonstrating how consumer comments operate as a response to professional journalism. This
interplay between professional and lay discourse offers a novel perspective on how legitimation
unfolds interactively in online platforms. The study stresses the increasing importance of the digi-
tal space in the construction of discursive legitimacy. The online forum’s function extends far be-
yond the reflection of consumer sentiments, serving as an active legitimacy platform where market
categories are constructed, challenged, and reconstructed by means of everyday communicative
practices.
From a practical standpoint, the findings are beneficial for marketers, highlighting the suc -
cess of the pragmatic legitimacy strategy prior to implementation. Emphasizing price-performance
advantages, extended warranties, and feature superiority is a practical market-entry approach in
price-sensitive markets with functional evaluation criteria. The outcomes indicate the relevance of
strategic discourse framing in legitimacy acquisition. Given the asynchronous nature of develop -
ment, marketers may strategically prioritize legitimacy dimensions.
By examining the comment sections of professional test-drive overviews, our study highlights
the dialogic nature of digital consumer-to-consumer (C2C) discourse. Though the professional
journalists provide the institutional foundation (e.g., detailing specifications and aesthetics), it is
within the consumer comment sections that these expert evaluations are collectively contested,
validated, or rejected. We demonstrate that ordinary consumers do not merely absorb institutional
legitimacy - they actively engineer it from the bottom up, using highly specific lexico-grammatical
means to restructure market hierarchies in real time.
The study is not without limitations. The data were gathered from two internet forums. Online
forum users tend to overrepresent a more technically informed population. Moreover, male users
outnumber female ones, which may downplay the female consumer perspective. Therefore, the
findings do not aim for generalizability but rather demonstrate the general trend. Online comments
from forums reflect the opinions of more active and adaptable consumers who are better informed
about technological developments in automobiles and may differ from those of the general public.
