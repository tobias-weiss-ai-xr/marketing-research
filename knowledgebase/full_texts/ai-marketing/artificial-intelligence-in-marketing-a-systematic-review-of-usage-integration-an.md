Abstract. In the contemporary market, Artificial Intelligence (AI) is increasingly recognized as a transformative element that
enhances customer engagement and decision -making efficiency while gaining a competitive advantage. However, despite its
rapid adoption, prevailing research on the use and integration of AI in marketing remains fragmented, lacking a solid synthesis
of awareness. This study presents a systematic review following a careful exploration of AI's diverse applications in marketing,
focusing on t hree major dimensions: existing usage patterns across the marketing synthesis, organizational, technical, and
ethical obstacles to implementation, and strategic gaps between AI deployment and long -term marketing objectives in both
academic research and practical integration. The study utilizes the PRISMA 2020 framework (Page et al., 2021) and reviewed
150 peer -reviewed journals (2015 – 2025) collected from databases such as Scopus, ResearchGate, Web of Science,
ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar. Using thematic synthesis (Braun & Clarke, 2006), 45 articles were selected for the analysis
based on the categories, integration barriers, and strategic misalignments. Findings showcase that AI usage is concentrated i n
several aspects, such as personalization, predic tive analytics, and marketing automation. However, major strategic gaps are
identified: gaps dominate: the personalization trap, integration gap, measurement gap, and ethics –strategy gap. This study
contributed to the literature by providing the first structured mapping of AI usage, integration, and strategy gaps in marketing
and proposing a broad agenda for future research and administrative practice.
Keywords: artificial intelligence, marketing strategy, PRISMA, systematic review, strategic gap, AI adoption, personalization,
digital transformation, integration.

INTRODUCTION
The evolution of marketing  has reached distinct
phases and  has been significantly influenced by
technological advancements, with artificial
intelligence (AI) emerging as the most recent
paradigm shift. According to studies, AI will become
an integral part of every marketable entity worldwide
in the long term  [1]. AI technologies such as
Generative AI, Machine L earning (ML), Predictive
Analytics, and Natural Language Processing (NLP)
enable marketers to analyse wide -ranging datasets,
forecast consumer behaviour , and automate data-
driven decision-making with significant speed and
accuracy [2].  This move has been mostly evident in
organizations that depend on data -driven results to
develop customer experiences, as seen in businesses
like Amazon, Salesforce, Adobe, HubSpot, Oracle ,
and Netflix, which employ AI for personalized
recommendations, establishing  new standards for
customer expectations and engagement. According to
Statista (2025), industries have spent more than $60
billion on AI in marketing and sales in 2024, and are
projected to reach $100 billion by 2027.
However, beneath this rapid integration of AI into
marketing lies a more troubling reality: many AI
marketing integrations fail to meet their intended
objectives [3,4]. On the other hand, research on AI in
marketing remains fragmented, with many studies
focusing on developed markets, leaving a gap in
understanding its impact in emerging economies and
in identifying the effects of infrastructure, regulatory
environments, and cultural differences  in marketing
[5]. Existing studies mainly focus on a single
application at a time, such as recommendation
systems or chatbots, without examining their impact
on comprehensive marketing strategies.  Since many
companies have invested heavily in AI without prior
knowledge or research, resulting in chatbot fai lures,
discriminatory pricing, privacy violations, brand
reputation damage , and regulatory sanctions [6-7].
These emphasize the gap of a comprehensive
synthesis of AI literature in marketing that
contributes to enduring strategic objectives such as
brand equity, consumer relationships, and sustainable
competitive advantage. To address the gap in existing
literature and offer a more strategic identification of
AI in marketing, this study is directed by three
interconnected research questions concentrating on
application scope, integration barriers, and the
strategic gap between potential and realized value.
RQ1: To what extent and in which domains is AI
used across the marketing scope, and does its
deployment replicate a strategically balanced
integration or a concentration in narrow, strategic
domains?
RQ2: What technical, organizational, and ethical
barriers hold back  the integration of AI into
intelligible marketing strategies, bringing out its use
as fragmented tools?
RQ3: What strategic gaps emerge between AI’s
potential contribution to long -term marketing
objectives and its comprehended impact in practice,
and are these gaps analytical of correctable
implementation subjects or deeper structural
problems?
By addressing these questions , this systematic
review critically synthesises the peer -reviewed
literature published between 2015 and 2025, going
beyond descriptive analysis. The study's contribution
is a critical evaluation of AI’s role in marketing,
highlighting str ategic integration and long -term
implications while challenging several fundamental
assumptions about its role.
CONCEPTUAL BACKGROUND AND
THEORETICAL FRAMING
DEFINING ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE IN
MARKETING
The term "artificial intelligence" (AI) has become
widely used in marketing, making it vital to establish
a clear definition.  As per [8], AI includes
computational systems that execute tasks typically
requiring human intelligence, such as machine
learning (ML), natural language processing (NLP),
computer vision, and the generation of novel content.
Essentially, this definition eliminates basic rul e-
based automation and focuses on advanced
applications such as predictive analytics,
recommendation systems, chatbots, programmatic
bidding, dynamic pricing, and generative content
creation. AI is defined  as a discipline that involves
the theory, methods, techniques , and application
systems that simulate, encompass, and enlarge
human intelligence [9]. A study by [10] proposed a
hierarchy in which AI is divided into three major
categories: mechanical AI, which focuses on
automating repetitive tasks; thinking AI, which
processes data for vision; and feeling AI, which aims
to understand human feelings.

Many current marketing applications operate at the

The Resource-Based View (RBV)  frames the
understanding of AI in the context of competitive
advantage, viewing businesse s as collections of
assets from which competitive advantage arises from
unique, valuable capabilities  [11]. AI fits into this
structure, but its efficiency needs complementary
assets like data quality, organizational alignment, and

THE MARKETING MIX AS AN ORGANIZING
FRAMEWORK FOR AI USAGE
This study adopts the 7Ps marketing mix
framework, which includes Product, Price, Place,
Promotion, People, Process, and Physical Evidence
[13]. Since people, process, and physical evidence are
more service-related functions, this framework is
preferable to the simpler 4Ps for AI research , where
AI applications like chatbots and process automation
are mostly prevalent.  In th is review, the marketing
mix covers two major segments, which are:
categorizing AI usage in a systematic way under the
scope of marketing activity and identifying
integration gaps.
 INTEGRATION: FROM TACTICAL TOOL TO
STRATEGIC CAPABILITY
In AI marketing research, integration is considered
the center but undertheorized concept . This study
aims to discuss three dimensions of integration based
on organizational ca pabilities literature : Technical
integration, Organizational integration, and Et hical
integration [14]. Ethical integration plays a vital role
in AI integrations as it can produce biased or privacy-
violating results that damage brand trust [15-16].
However, all three dimensions are interdependent;
any one without the others does not yield  the
expected results.
STRATEGIC GAPS
According to the strategy process theory, the
strategic gap is defined  as the distinction betwe en
exploitation and exploration [17]. This study
highlights AI marketing deployments as exploitative,
where the decisions are made based on existing
customer data. As a result, a strategic gap emerges, in
which exploration is limited to the point where long-
term adaptability, brand equity, and sustainable
competitive advantage are sacrificed for short -term
proficiency gains.
RESOURCE-BASED VIEW AND DYNAMIC
CAPABILITIES AS THEORETICAL LENSES
Studies show that the Resource-Based View (RBV)
of the firm  posits that sustainable competitive
advantage is generated by assets that are valuab le,
rare, inimitable, and non -substitutable (VRIN) [18-
19]. Dynamic Capabilities theory is derived from the
RBV [20] which highlights a firm's capacity to
integrate, build, and reconfigure resources to address
internal and external promptly fluctuating
environments [21].  However, AI marketing
capabilities can be considered as dynamic
capabilities when they e nable sensing, seizing and
transforming.

METHODOLOGY
REVIEW DESIGN AND REGISTRATION
This study was conducted in accordance with the
PRISMA 2020 (Preferred Reporting Items for
Systematic Reviews and Meta -Analyses) guidelines
[22], which provide a structured framework for
identifying, screening, and selecting eligible studies.
Further, the thematic synthesis approach was utilized
to integrate findings, which is an established method
to integrate results from both qualitative and
quantitative studies in academic research.
DATA SOURCES AND SEARCH STRATEGY
Several electronic databases were selected: Scopus,
ResearchGate and Web of Science Core , to conduct
the literature search.  These databases were selected
because of their  comprehensive coverage of peer -
reviewed marketing, information systems, business,
and management journals. The search covered the
period from January 2015 to December 2025,
accounting for the initial hype cycle of AI in 2015–
2017.
The search queries were applied to titles, abstracts,
and author keywords related to AI and marketing:
(“artificial intelligence”,  “Machine learning and
consumer behavior ”, “ machine learning”, “deep
learning”, “ Marketing automation ”, “ AI-driven
marketing strategy ”, “generative AI”,  “predictive
analytics”, “large language model”, “ computer
vision”, “neural network”, “ marketing”,
“advertising”, “customer relationship management”,
“branding”, “ pricing”, “retailing”, “omnichannel”,
“social media marketing”, “email marketing”,
“usage”, “adoption”, “integration”, “strategy”,
“implementation”, “deployment”, “application”)
Limitations applied: considered only the peer -
reviewed journal articles in English, focu sing on AI
applications within marketing contexts  with
sufficient details from 2015 to 2025 , and excluded
Conference proceedings, commentaries, book
chapters, non-peer-reviewed articles, editorials,
theses, trade publications, and duplicated records.

SCREENING PROCESS
The screening process was conducted for the 150
studies collected according to the PRISMA
framework, comprising four stages:  identification,
screening, eligibility, and inclusion.
Identification – Using Zotero, the collected research
articles were filtered, duplicates were removed, and
120 unique records remained.
Screening – the 85 studies collected were screened
by reviewing their titles and abstracts to confirm
whether they were  aligned with this study. Some
studies were excluded that did not meet the inclusion
criteria.
Eligibility – The full texts of selected articles after
screening were thoroughly checked against the
eligibility criteria, and 40 articles were excluded for
several reasons, such as being conference
proceedings misclassified as journal articles, lacking
a strategic focus, and a usage focus.
Inclusion - The final amount comprised 45 articles
that reached all eligibility criteria and were included
in the thematic synthesis.

RQ2: BARRIERS TO AI INTEGRATION INTO
COHERENT MARKETING STRATEGY
The thematic synthesis consistently identified three
categories of barriers: technical, organizational, and
ethical, which reinforce each other for the integration
of AI into intelligible marketing strategies.
Technical Barriers
Technical barriers mostly relate to challenges of
data quality, availability, and infrastructure. Many
companies struggle with fragmented data systems,
which limit the deployment of AI through different
marketing segments. This fragmentation : customer
information scattered across CRM, web analytics,
sales, service, and third-party platforms, often results
in isolated AI applications rather than allowing an
organized, enterprise -wide integration  since AI
cannot develop a unified view of the customer
without integrated data  [26]. In addition, there is a
distinguished temporal disparity between the rapid
optimization cycles of AI and the extended timelines
usually accompanying marketing strategies [27].
Organizational barriers
The reason for organizational barriers is due to
misalignment between internal departments of the
organizations, such as marketing, IT , and data
science, which normally operate independently. One
of the most dangerous organizational barriers is the
structural separation barrier, where marketing
decision rights are separated from AI capabilities.
Many marketing teams lack the knowledge of AI
capabilities to inform data scientists regarding their
strategic requirements in algorithmic terms, yet data
scientists produce optimization models based on the
data they have, but marketing controls the company
budgets and strategies ; this is  where the disparity
occurs [28].  This lack of co -operation can impede
strategic integration, undermine the effective use of
AI capabilities, and lead to suboptimal decision -
making, in which short -term advantages undermine
long-term brand value.
These types of encounters highlight the need for
restructuring, in which data scientists must be
embedded in marketing teams and create hybrid roles
that combine marketing knowledge wi th technical
skills [29].
Ethical Barriers
Ethical barriers are also considered a significant
challenge, mostly concerning data privacy,
algorithmic bias, explainability, accountability, and
transparency. Regulatory frameworks, such as the
General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), impose
constraints on data use, which can govern  AI
deployment in marketing contexts. Nevertheless, the
serious issue lies in the fact that most organizations
consider ethical AI as a compliance exercise rather
than a strategic prospect to leverage ethical practices
to build brand trust. However, the studies emphasize
that many organizations implement minimal
protections to escape fines but do not proactively use
ethical AI to build brand trust.  Consumers are
increasingly demonstrating  a willingness to pay a
premium, often cited in the range of 8 –15% for
brands that proactively guarantee that  their AI
systems are transparent, ethical, and fair [30].
Overall, these three barriers are interconnected ,
contributing to the fra gmentation of AI adoption in
marketing. Addressing the interdependencies
amongst technical, organizational, and ethical
challenges is vital for accomplishing a cohesive
integration of AI into marketing strategies.
RQ3: STRATEGIC GAPS BETWEEN AI POTENTIAL
AND REALIZED OUTCOMES
A comparative analysis of numerous studies
identified four significant strategic gaps that clarify
the disparity between the potential of AI in marketing
and its real effect.
The personalization trap
 One of the main strategic gaps is the over-reliance
on personalization. Organisations use AI to provide
personalized interactions for consumers based on
their observed behaviour. However, this tactic
provides short-term benefits , such as higher
conversion and click-through rates; over time, it can
lead to diminishing returns, including  customer
fatigue, fragmentation of brand identity, and erosion
of trust . Tailored messages for each customer can
gradually erode a coherent brand since the brand’s
focus is heavily on optimising engagement rather
than comprehensive marketing objectives [31]. This
focus may limit strategic diversity and overlook
broader marketing objectives, creating a so -called
"personalization trap." This personalization cannot be
considered a failure of technology but a failure of
strategy.
Integration Gap
Many organizations deploy separate AI tools across
different marketing functions, such as promotion ,
product recommendat ions, and customer service,
without ensuring they share data, models, or
objectives. Since different functions work with
different databases, such as the service AI optimizes
for handle time, promotion AI optimizes for click -
through, and the recommendation AI optimizes for
purchase probability. This lack of coordination can
result in inconsistent optimizations that fail to

The studies show that most of the organizations are

The ethics–strategy gap
The ethics-strategy gap in AI marketing highlights
that ethical considerations are often treated as mere
compliance rather than as a s trategic advantage.
Organizations increasingly invest in AI technologies
but little in ethical AI practices, which can perpetuate
biases, infringe on privacy , or make erroneous
decisions while missing out on opportunities to
enhance customer loyalty and tr ust through
transparency. This represents a failure in strategic
vision rather than a limitation of technology [38].
DISCUSSION
The findings of this study highlight a fundamental
inconsistency: even though AI offers transformative
potential, AI’s real use in marketing remains
constrained by both practical and structural
limitations. While the unevenness of AI application
distribution suggests that this is not a fully developed
complementary resource.
INTERPRETATION OF FINDINGS
The focus of AI in promotion and process, the
fragmentation of AI systems, and the governance of
short-term metrics are all centered towards a major
misalignment between AI investments and marketing
strategy. These four major strategic gaps form a self-
reinforcing system that traps firms in short -term
optimization. However, as shown in the conceptual
framework, this pattern replicates th e exploitation
bias. Companies utilize AI to exploit current
customer journeys, acknowledged segments, and
customary messages [39]. Most of these firms do not
use AI to explore new customer bases, new markets,
new value propositions, or new brand significances.
This is the reason for tactical efficiency devoid of
strategic differentiation.
The recognized integration barriers: technical,
organizational, and ethical , reinforce each other
without being independent.  The inte gration of
strategic measurements is hindered by  data silos ,
resulting in a measurement gap. The personalization
traps are formed as a result of a lack of governance
and uncoordinated AI projects. Since companies are
treating AI ethics as compliance rather than a
strategic lens, an ethics-strategy gap emerges.
THEORETICAL IMPLICATIONS
This study provides insights into marketing theory
in several significant ways. Even though prior
research established AI as a tactical tool for existing
marketing functions to optimize . Nevertheless,
modern research recommends that the real strategic
value of AI arises only when it is employed through
the whole marketing mix, known as the 7Ps, and is
integrated across technical, organizational, an d
ethical scopes. This imposes a move in marketing
strategy theory to identify AI as a strategic
orchestrator rather than a n ordinary tactical
facilitator.
In addition, AI algorithms and data have become
generic and reachable to all opponents; the
sustainable benefit derives from the exclusive
organizational abilities developed around AI ,
according to RBV.  The solutions include robust data
integration architecture, effective cross -functional
governance, addressing organizational barriers
simultaneously, ethical AI practices, and well -
established human-AI alliance schedules. However,
according to this study, there are three structural
tensions that cannot be eliminated when proceeding
with AI deployment in marketing but can be
managed.

THE AI MARKETING MATURITY FRAMEWORK
Based on the thematic synthesis, a four-stage
maturity framework is designed that integrates usage
patterns, barriers, and strategic gaps.
Stage 1: Ad Hoc Experimentation
Basically, for promotion and customer service,
firms have launched individual AI pilots without any
infrastructure for data sharing, coordination , or
governance. So, there are se vere strategic gaps , an
emerging personalization trap , less integration , ad
hoc measurem ent, and ignored ethics. Hence, the
priority should be given not to more AI pilots but to
the foundational decisions , such as strategic
objectives for AI, executive sponsorship , and data
inventory.
Stage 2: Functional Optimization
Companies utilize multip le AI tools within
functional silos , such as service has its own AI,
promotion has its own, and processes another without
any data link from one to another. KPIs have been
introduced as functional and short -term, while
personalization is dominating. However, the priority
should be for technical integration to build customer
data platforms , establish data governance , and
encourage shared data access.
Stage 3: Cross-Functional Integration
Firms should redesign their processes and break the
data silos, and generate AI models to coordinate and
inform shared data. Process redesigned while
initiating hybrid roles, cross -functional teams, and
shared KPIs. On the other hand, measurements are
kept deliberate while prioritizing strategic
measurements (customer lifetime value - CLV, brand
equity dashboards).
Stage 4: Strategic AI Maturity
Across all 7Ps, AI is entirely implanted in marketing
strategy, from pricing to physical evidence. Introduce
AI ethics as the brand differentiator, reducing
strategic gaps and prioritizing continuous learning
and adaptation, where AI is considered as an ongoing
strategic capability, not as a one-time investment.
However, the progression won’t be linear  or
automatic. Companies may regress, skip stages later,
and identify that tactical AI tool expenditures fail due
to missing foundational integration.
PRACTICAL IMPLICATIONS
Implications are clear for the market leaders. As the
first step, leaders should  audit their AI portfolio
against the 7Ps. If the audit shows 70% investment in
AI as promotion and process, that means the firm is
trapped in the personalization trap. As the most
important step, data integration issues must be fixed
before investing more in AI tool adoptions. As the
third step, firms should change their metrics to focus
more on long -term strategies, such as CLV impacts
and brand equity , not focusing only on short -term
efficiency gains. The f ourth step is to introduce
hybrid roles such as "marketing data scientists ,"
which will help data interpretations to be more
focused on brand strategy  and conduct rotational
marketers. As the final step , firms should consider
ethics as a strategy without outsourcing AI ethics to
legal or compliance. Establish the brand promise as
fairness and transparency , and inform customers
regarding AI systems certifications.
FUTURE RESEARCH AGENDA

This study identifies several future research
opportunities, building  on the findings of this
systematic review , reflecting both limitations
identified and the structural gaps emphasised through
the conceptual framework.
Since existing studies on AI applications in
marketing mainly focus on promotion, pricing, and
process-related functions, future research should
explore how AI can be applied across the marketing
mix, particularly  in creativity and experiential
branding, to cultivate a more comprehensive
understanding of its strategic role. In other words,
firms should focus  more on how to upgrade from
stage 1 to stage 4 using AI deployments where critical
inflection points, enabl ing conditions, and common
failure modes are identified.
Additionally, a notable gap exists in the literature
where the long -term goals of AI deployments in
marketing are studied, and what reporting structures,
incentive systems, and career pat hs let strategic AI
adoption are studied as well. The pros and cons o f
centralized AI departments and the role of IT in
marketing should be further assessed.
Even though AI can often replace human decision-
making to a certain extent , human involvement is
necessary for creativity, ethics, and relationship

CONCLUSION
This systematic review emphasizes an important
paradox in the marketing setting on the subject of the
adoption of artificial intelligence (AI): while AI is
being extensively used for tactical applications, it
regularly considers short in providing strategic
performance. The present deployment of AI is mainly
focused on promotion and process management,
without utilizing areas such as pric ing, distribution,
and physical . A number of  integration barriers ,
technical, organizational, and ethical, have headed to
the fragmentation of AI into isolated tools rather than
nurturing cohesive strategies.
Four strategic gaps are identified, which are  the
personalization trap, the integration gap, the
measurement gap, and the ethics-strategy gap. These
gaps produce a self -reinforcing system that holds
back firms from moving further than  short-term
optimization. The study proposes the AI Marketing
Maturity Framework as both a diagnostic tool and a
roadmap for organizations to advance from
fragmented adoption to holistic and strategically
strengthen implementation.
