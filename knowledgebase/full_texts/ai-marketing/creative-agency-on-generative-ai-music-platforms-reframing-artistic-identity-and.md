Abstract
Generative AI music platforms promise to facilitate creative production without advanced
technical skills. This study analyzed an approximately 22,000-word corpus of interface texts,
terms of use, marketing materials, and blog posts from Suno, Udio, AIVA, and Soundraw using
Fairclough’s Critical Discourse Analysis and Lakoff and Johnson’s conceptual metaphor the-
ory. It examined how platform texts construct power relations and attribute agency. Three
tendencies emerged. The agency game denotes the tension between the creative agency
attributed to users in interface discourse and the contractual limits imposed through licens-
ing and ownership clauses. The illusion of transparency denotes the backgrounding of algo-
rithmic operation behind metaphors of magic, tool, and partnership. The rhetoric of democ-
ratization reflects the ideological function of the “anyone can be a musician” framing, which
may bear on how professional expertise is valued. Disclosure of training material was as-
sessed at three levels of specificity. Three analytical units on two platforms described in gen-
eral terms where training material was obtained, one under litigation and two as a commercial
guarantee; none identified specific works or datasets, and none disclosed any consent, attrib-
ution, or compensation mechanism for the relevant rights holders. The findings indicate that
these platforms do not eliminate creative agency but render it discursively ambiguous by of-
fering users a symbolic creative subject position while obscuring the distributed agency un-
derlying production.
Keywords: Generative artificial intelligence, music production, discourse analysis,
digital culture, artistic identity

Introduction

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into
music production has moved to the center of both
industrial and academic agendas. Platforms such
as Suno, Udio, AIVA, and Soundraw generate full-
length audio files within minutes from a short text
input, and their reach is considerable: Suno an-
nounced that more than 12 million people had used
the platform by 2024 (Tencer, 2024). Despite com-
peting with one another, these platforms converge
on a strikingly similar language practice in their
publicly available texts. The user is consistently po-
sitioned as a “creator” or “musician”, while the algo-
rithm appears as an invisible and neutral instru-
ment. Suno’s “From idea to hit” and its claim to
make music creation “accessible to all” (Unit S1),
AIVA’s self-presentation as a “personal AI music
generation assistant” addressing beginners and
seasoned professionals alike (Unit A1), Soundraw’s
invitation to “Make Unlimited Music with AI” (Unit
SR1), and Udio’s “Make your music” (Unit U1) draw
on a shared repertoire of access, freedom, and the
democratization of creativity. That repertoire of-
fers the user a symbolic creative subject position
while pushing the role of the algorithm into the
background. Within the corpus examined, these
platforms function not only as technical tools but
also as ideological actors participating in the re-
definition of creativity, musicianship, and artistic
identity.
Academic debates on creativity and artistic
identity have long emphasized that the subject is
less an individual creator than a position shaped by
cultural, economic, and technological forces.
Barthes’s (1977) “death of the author” argument
showed that the subject who produces the mean-
ing of a text is not a fixed and singular creator, while
Foucault’s (1969/1984) conceptualization of the
author function established that authorship is a
historically and institutionally constructed position.
From this perspective, the “anyone can be crea-
tive” framing of AI platforms reads less as a new
narrative of emancipation than as a digital repro-
duction of that historically constituted function.
How the discursive strategies that position users
simultaneously as customers and creative sub-
jects operate, which functions they fulfill, and how
they transform artistic identity constitute the cen-
tral research problem of this study.
Research on the intersection of AI and creativ-
ity has largely focused on technical performance
(Agostinelli et al., 2023; Copet et al., 2023), copy-
right problems (Grimmelmann, 2016; Samuelson,
2023), or user experience (Louie et al., 2020). How
these tools present themselves, and which ideo-
logical functions that self-presentation fulfills, has
received far less attention. The present study ad-
dresses this gap.
The capacity of digital platforms to reshape cul-
tural production has been documented in detail
within platform studies. Srnicek’s (2017) frame-
work of platform capitalism shows that platforms
are not merely technological infrastructures but
economic structures that bring data, labor, and
value production under control, and van Dijck et al.
(2018) demonstrated how they transform public
values and cultural practices. AI music platforms
present a distinctive case: they do not simply dis-
tribute existing content; they carry out production
itself. This difference raises fundamental ques-
tions about who produces, who owns, and who
counts as an “artist”, and those questions concern
not only individual users but also the artists whose
works may have been incorporated into training
datasets.
The study combines Critical Discourse Analysis
(CDA) with conceptual metaphor analysis in order
to examine both the attribution of agency and the
dimensions that platform discourse conceals (Fair-
clough, 1992; Lakoff & Johnson, 1980). Critical
studies of AI discourse have tended toward gen-
eral forms of representation rather than the dis-
course of creativity constructed by generative
platforms (Broussard, 2018; Cave & Dihal, 2019;
Nass & Moon, 2000). For music, Pram and Morre-
ale (2025) have begun to fill this gap, examining de-
veloper rhetoric, product functionality, and user re-
ception through autoethnography and digital eth-
nography with particular emphasis on democrati-
zation. The present study differs by constructing a
bounded corpus of corporate texts, compari ng

Conceptual Framework

Agency, Author, and Subject

For Foucault (1969/1984), the author function is an
institutional mechanism that classifies texts, regu-
lates the production of meaning, and assigns re-
sponsibility to a particular subject. Who counts as
an author, and which forms of production are rec-
ognized as original creativity, is therefore deter-
mined through social negotiation rather than given
in advance; Barthes (1977, p. 142) had already dis-
placed the author’s intention as the source of tex-
tual meaning. Seen from these perspectives, the
image of the “creative user” on AI platforms is a
new digital appearance of a historically constituted
position. The platform attributes the author posi-
tion to the user discursively while transforming the
legal and economic content of that position
through its terms of use.
Four concepts that the platform texts tend to
blur into one another need to be kept analytically
distinct. Legal ownership refers to the holding of
copyright in a generated output; a license is a con-
tractual permission to use that output, which may
be granted by the user to the platform or by the
platform to the user without any transfer of owner-
ship; authorship is the socially and institutionally
recognized attribution of a work to a subject, in
Foucault’s (1969/1984) sense of the author func-
tion; and creative agency, as used in this study, is a
discursive category describing who is represented
as the acting subject of production. Ownership
here refers to the contractual allocation of rights
and claims between platform and user; it does not
constitute a determination that copyright neces-
sarily subsists in a given AI-generated output un-
der applicable law. These categories can diverge: a
user may hold ownership while granting the plat-
form a broad license, or may be addressed as an
author in marketing language while holding neither
ownership nor a license. The findings section
therefore treats “agency retraction” not as a single
legal event but as a family of mechanisms, ranging
from license grants to outright platform owner-
ship, whose common discursive effect is to narrow
at the contractual level what is attributed at the
promotional level.
Latour’s (2005) actor-network theory attrib-
utes agency not to a single subject but to a network
formed by human and non-human actors. On an AI
music platform the creative process involves the
user, the algorithms, the training data, the interface
design, and the economic structure. Platform dis-
course turns this network into a “black box”: dis-
tributed agency is erased by being reduced to the
creative will of the user. The prompt box, the selec-
tion menus, and the one-click production experi-
ence form an interface layer that covers the data
flows, the model architecture, and the licensing in-
frastructure operating behind them. This is a lin-
guistic strategy as much as a technical necessity,
since directing the user’s attention toward the in-
put-output relation reinforces the impression that
agency resides in the user.

Terranova’s (2004) concept of “free labor” ex-

works may have been incorporated into training
datasets undergoes a similar erasure, an issue now

Critical Discourse Analysis and Conceptual Met-
aphor Theory

Fairclough (1992) conceptualizes discourse as a
three-dimensional structure comprising the tex-
tual level (language use), discursive practice (forms
of production and consumption), and social prac-
tice (ideological context). This makes it possible to
examine not only the surface linguistic features of
platform texts but also how those texts are pro-
duced and which power relations they reproduce.
Analysis at the textual level asks who is placed in
the subject position and who in the object position,
which actions are constructed actively and which
passively, and which concepts are foregrounded
and which pushed into the background.
Lakoff and Johnson (1980) show that meta-
phors are the fundamental organizers of concep-
tual thought: mapping a source domain onto a tar-
get domain determines both what is highlighted
and what is hidden. A discourse that conceives of
AI creativity as magic covers the technical, eco-
nomic, and labor dimensions of the process. Com-
bined with CDA, conceptual metaphor analysis
therefore offers a suitable instrument for revealing
both the surface structure and the ideological op-
eration of these strategies.

AI Discourse and Generative AI Studies

Research addressing the communicative and lin-
guistic dimensions of AI systems clusters around a
few main topics: the representation of AI through
the attribution of human-like qualities (Nass &
Moon, 2000), utopian and dystopian positionings
of AI discourse (Broussard, 2018), and popular nar-
ratives of AI agency (Cave & Dihal, 2019). In the
specific context of generative AI, research has
concentrated on the technical problems of music
generation (Agostinelli et al., 2023; Copet et al.,
2023), the aesthetic experiences created by auto-
matic composers (Sturm et al., 2019), and repre-
sentation strategies in symbolic music generation
(Jiang et al., 2020).
Studies that directly address how generative AI
platforms construct their own discourse of creativ-
ity remain very limited. Works such as Briot et al.
(2020) and Herremans et al. (2017), which examine
the technical architecture and creative potential of
generative AI music systems, are key references in
the field but do not address this dimension. In the
adjacent field of AI art, Jiang et al. (2023) show that
artists confronting generative systems report con-
siderable uncertainty and contestation over how
creative contribution and attribution should be as-
signed. This raises a parallel question for music,
which the present study approaches from the
other side of the relation, through the texts pro-
duced by the platforms themselves.

Method

Research Design and Rationale

The study rests on a qualitative research design.
Data were collected through document compila-
tion from publicly available platform texts, and Crit-
ical Discourse Analysis was used as the analytical
method (Fairclough, 1992). The aim is to reveal how
AI music platforms frame creative agency at the
level of discourse and how they position artistic
identity, an aim that requires in-depth qualitative
analysis rather than quantitative assessment. The
object of analysis is textual throughout. No model
architecture, source code, training pipeline, or un-
derlying dataset was examined technically, and
terms such as algorithm, underlying model training
data, and black box are used here to denote how
these technical objects are represented in plat-
form discourse rather than to describe their prop-
erties.

Platform Selection and Rationale

Suno, Udio, AIVA, and Soundraw were selected ac-
cording to three criteria: user base and market vis-
ibility, discursive variety, and text accessibility.
Suno has a large and highly visible consumer base
(Tencer, 2024) and Udio targets a similar audience,
while AIVA stands out in the corporate and profes-
sional segment and Soundraw in the segment fo-
cused on commercial licensing. Together the four
cover both the consumer and the professional
sides of the AI music production market and ad-
dress users ranging from amateurs to film score
composers. Distribution platforms such as Spotify
were excluded because they perform no produc-
tion function. Each platform also represents a dif-
ferent positioning strategy: Suno and Udio lean on
the rhetoric of access and democratization, AIVA
on the language of professional partnership, and
Soundraw on a tool-centered discourse. This vari-
ety makes it possible to identify recurring language
tendencies across the selected cases and within
the sampled market segment rather than patterns
specific to a single platform.

Data Set and Corpus Compilation

Four categories of text were compiled from each
platform: homepage and “about” texts; the provi-
sions of the terms of use concerning creativity, li-
censing, ownership, and user rights; official blog
posts and press releases; and interface guidance
texts and prompt suggestions. This yielded 16 ana-
lytical units containing 20 individual source docu-
ments, since three Suno blog posts, two Udio blog
posts, and two Soundraw blog posts were grouped
into their respective platform-level blog units, with
a total volume of approximately 22,000 words. All
texts were collected from the platforms’ publicly
available pages in May 2025, and original URLs to-
gether with the corresponding Wayback Machine
archive links were recorded. Selection was purpos-
ive: a text was included when it was produced by
the platform itself rather than by users or third par-
ties, when it was publicly accessible without a paid
account at the time of archiving, and when it ad-
dressed creativity, production, ownership, or the
user’s role at least in part. User-generated content
and community forums, third-party reviews and
press coverage, technical documentation without
discursive content such as API references, and lo-
calized non-English versions of the sites were ex-
cluded on these grounds. Each unit was assigned
an identifier (S1-S4 for Suno, U1-U4 for Udio, A1-A4
for AIVA, SR1-SR4 for Soundraw), used when quot-
ing from or referring to the corpus and keyed to the
source list in Appendix A.

Analysis Procedure and Coding

The analysis proceeded in two stages. The entire
corpus was first read through open coding, from
which categories concerning agency attribution,
process representation, and labor visibility
emerged in a data-driven manner. These catego-
ries were then converted into a fixed codebook
and each text unit was reread with them applied
consistently. Second-cycle coding was theoreti-
cally informed by Fairclough’s (1992) CDA frame-
work and by conceptual metaphor theory (Lakoff &
Johnson, 1980), so the overall strategy was hybrid
inductive-deductive. At the same stage, the source
domains with which the platforms match the con-
cepts of creativity, algorithm, and user were identi-
fied.
The coding unit was the clause or sentence:
each unit of the corpus was segmented, and every
segment relevant to creativity, production, or
rights was assigned one or more codes. The final
codebook comprised seven discursive codes in
three thematic groups together with three cross-
cutting disclosure categories. The agency group
contained agency attribution (the user is the gram-
matical subject of a production verb), shared
agency (production is predicated jointly of user and
system), and agency retraction (a  contractual
clause that narrows, redistributes, or withdraws
rights and control over the output through licens-
ing or ownership terms). The process group con-
tained magic/transformation (production is repre-
sented as instantaneous, effortless, or mysteri-
ous), tool (the system is represented as a passive
instrument), and partnership (the system is repre-
sented as a collaborator). The access group con-
tained democratization (production is represented
as newly opened to everyone through the removal
of barriers). Disclosure of underlying model training
data was treated as a cross-cutting concern. Be-
cause the first reading encountered one passage in
which a platform accounted for the origin of its
training material in general terms without identify-
ing any of it, the category was separated into three
levels of specificity: generic training-source disclo-
sure, where the text describes in general terms
where training material was obtained; specific or
traceable training-data disclosure, where the text
identifies particular works, catalogues, or datasets,
or a source that can be independently traced; and
rights-holder consent, attribution, or compensa-
tion disclosure, where the text describes a mecha-
nism through which the holders of rights in that
material consented, were credited, or were remu-
nerated.
The seven discursive codes were applied at the
clause or sentence level, whereas the three disclo-
sure categories were assessed at the analytical-
unit level: a unit was marked present at a given
level if any passage in it met that level’s criterion,
and absent otherwise. For each discursive code the
codebook recorded a definition, an inclusion rule,
the boundary at which the code was not applied,
and an anchor example drawn from the first read-
ing; for each disclosure level it recorded an explicit
presence-absence rule. The codebook is repro-
duced in full in Appendix B, with anchor examples
keyed to the archived captures listed in Appendix
A. The study was conducted by a single researcher
and no intercoder reliability test was performed, a
point addressed in the limitations section.

The Agency Game: The Linguistic Construction
of Symbolic Creativity

The first cluster is described here as an agency
game because the creative subject position it of-
fers is granted in one genre and qualified in an-
other, so that the attribution is reversible rather
than stable. The structure observed most clearly
across the corpus consists of active constructions
in which the user occupies the sentence subject.
Suno’s “Create music that moves people” (Unit S1)
brackets out the existence of the algorithm en-
tirely and assigns the action directly to the user.
Udio’s “Make your music” (Unit U1) similarly con-
structs the user as the creative subject, implying
the platform only as a ground. AIVA’s homepage
addresses the user as the party for whose projects
emotional soundtracks are produced (Unit A1),
subordinating the system’s contribution to the
user’s ends rather than foregrounding it as auton-
omous composition, and Soundraw’s “Create the
perfect song for your needs” (Unit SR1) implies that
the creative outcome corresponds exactly to the
user’s specific needs, veiling the parameters within
which the system operates. In the homepage and
marketing units coded here, the algorithm typically
appears outside the subject position of production
verbs or is relegated to secondary positionings
such as tool, assistant, or partner. AIVA constitutes

The Illusion of Transparency: Backgrounding the
Algorithm through Metaphors

How the operation of the algorithm is represented
forms the second cluster. The label used here is an
analytical characterization of the sampled texts
and not a claim about platform intent: what the cor-
pus supports is the backgrounding of algorithmic
operation in the discourse rather than a deliberate
act of concealment. Across the corpus the tech-
nical process is covered by three distinct metaphor
domains. In the domain of magic and transfor-
mation, Soundraw’s “Music at your Fingertips” and
its promise of unique songs “in just a few clicks”
(Unit SR1), together with Suno’s framing of music
as something within reach from anywhere (Unit S1),
present complex computational processes as in-
stantaneous and effortless. In the tool domain,
Udio’s “A new tool for music makers” and its de-
scription of itself as “a tool for realizing musical
ideas” (Unit U1) and AIVA’s “Your personal AI music
generation assistant” (Unit A1) reduce the algo-
rithm to an instrument at the user’s disposal. In the
partnership domain, AIVA’s discourse of compos-
ing alongside the user present agency as shared
while excluding from the text the asymmetric di-
mensions of that sharing, namely whose labor,
whose data, and whose profit are at stake. AIVA is
also the unit in which the system itself is most visi-
bly credited with composing activity, though this
activity is immediately repackaged through the as-
sistant and collaborator vocabulary, so that even
where the algorithm is granted an active verb it re-
mains subordinated to the user’s project.
Examined through Lakoff and Johnson’s (1980)
conceptual metaphor theory, each domain per-
forms a different backgrounding function. The
magic metaphor directs the user’s attention from
process to outcome and thereby covers the under-
lying computational operations. The tool metaphor
removes the algorithm from the status of an inde-
pendent actor and places responsibility entirely on
the user. The partnership metaphor constructs an
apparently equal and voluntary relationship be-
tween the two parties and thereby cov ers the
asymmetry of power. AIVA’s corporate blog offers
a concrete example of the last two operating to-
gether: the platform states that it optimizes its
models for uses that empower artists to create ex-
ceptional music, “supporting their creative journey
rather than replacing it”, and describes a planned
derivative model that would act as an “arrange-
ment co-pilot” (Unit A3).
The explicit denial of replacement positions the
user as an autonomous creator, while the support
vocabulary reduces the algorithm to an extension
of the user’s will and the co-pilot figure represents
an asymmetric relationship as a voluntary and
equal partnership. The co-pilot description refers
to a model announced as forthcoming rather than
to the product then available, so it registers here as
a projected rather than an operative self-descrip-
tion. Excluded from this discourse are the technical
constraints of the algorithm, the sources of the
data sets on which the model was trained, and the
labor relations involved. In Soundraw’s homepage
texts the tool metaphor is sharper still: editing
functions are presented as “AI -powered tools”
through which the user shortens intros, rearranges
choruses, and personalizes song structure, so that
the system takes over the technical side of produc-
tion while the compositional decision remains at-
tributed to the user (Unit SR1).
The disclaimer of substitution and the division
between the technical side and the creators’ ideas
draw a hard boundary between the creative and
the technical. The boundary attributes creativity to
the user while transferring the entire technical pro-
cess to the platform, yet the scope of that transfer
is never specified: with which data, under which pa-
rameters, with whose musical contribution. Posi-

The Rhetoric of Democratization: Access as a
Discursive Frame

The third cluster is the rhetoric of democratizing
music production. Suno’s claim that it “makes high-
quality music creation accessible to all” (Unit S1)
and AIVA’s address to the user “whether a com-
plete beginner or a seasoned professional in music
making” (Unit A1) present access to the platforms
as a radically positive development. As Table 2
shows, the frame is not evenly distributed: it is rec-
orded for Suno, Udio, and AIVA but not for
Soundraw, whose sampled texts foreground com-
mercial use rather than the removal of skill barriers.
The frame is not limited to a promise addressed to
individual users; it also legitimizes the platforms’
market positioning. The discourse of “anyone can
be a musician” may contribute to the devaluation
of the long-term labor and accumulated skill that
professional musicianship requires, while blending
that devaluation into a narrative of emancipation.
Pram and Morreale (2025), combining autoethnog-
raphy and digital ethnography across four genera-
tive AI music systems (AIVA, Stable Audio, Suno,
and Udio), conclude that the ideology driving their
adoption is individualistic and techno-liberal, with
democratization functioning as marketable rheto-
ric rather than a guiding principle. The present cor-
pus analysis, which approaches an overlapping set
of platforms through a different method, produces
compatible findings.
“Whether a complete beginner or a seasoned
professional in music making, use the power of
generative AI to create your own songs.” AIVA,
homepage, Unit A1 (May 2025)
Udio’s homepage builds the same frame on a
historical ground: music is presented as something
that has always belonged to everyone, and the
platform as the party that removes the remaining
obstacles, with training, instruments, and barriers
listed as things the user no longer needs (Unit U1).
In the units where the frame was recorded, the
democratization discourse is woven around the
concept of the “barrier”. Suno’s claim of accessibil-
ity for all, AIVA’s address to beginners and profes-
sionals alike, and Udio’s enumeration of dispensa-
ble requirements characterize technical compe-
tence, musical knowledge, and instrumental prac-
tice as obstacles, while Udio’s appeal to music’s
having always belonged to everyone positions
those obstacles as a matter of historical and struc-
tural exclusion that the platform has resolved. The
frame shifts attention away from the conditions
that structure access and value distribution, includ-
ing subscription tiers, control over training data,
and the unequal allocation of economic returns.
Two silences are usually attributed to this
frame, but in the present corpus the first is better
described as disclosure without specificity. Three
analytical units, distributed across two platforms,
account for the origin of the training material, and
they do so in opposite rhetorical registers. In a blog
post published in response to litigation, Suno
states that its models are trained on “medium- and
high-quality music we can find on the open inter-
net” and acknowledges that much of that material
is copyrighted, some of it held by major record la-
bels (Unit S3b). The account is general rather than
traceable. No work, catalogue, or dataset is named,
no consent, attribution, or compensation mecha-
nism is described, and the acknowledgement is im-
mediately absorbed into an analogy with human lis-
tening and learning, through which the use of the
material is presented as something other than in-
fringement. What the passage supplies, in other

than a justification for leaving it unspecified.
Soundraw makes the corresponding statement in
the opposite position. Its homepage asserts that
“real producers create original beats in-house to
train our AI model with” and that the platform
never trains its model with other artists’ music or
sounds (Unit SR1), a claim repeated in a blog post

should be described. Provenance is not uniformly
absent from the sampled texts; it becomes sayable
where it can be presented as an assurance and is
deferred where it cannot. What both accounts
withhold is the same. Neither identifies a work, cat-
alogue, or dataset that could be independently
traced, and neither describes a consent, attribu-
tion, or compensation mechanism for the holders
of rights in the material. Soundraw’s formulation
does not describe such a mechanism but asserts
that no external rights holder is implicated, which is
a claim about the scope of the training corpus ra-
ther than an account of how third-party rights were
handled. No comparable statement appears in the
remaining 13 analytical units, and in none of the 16
are specific works or datasets identified or a rights-
holder mechanism described.
The second silence concerns access condi-
tions, since the corpus foregrounds accessibility
while providing limited information about how sub-
scription tiers structure feature availability. Both
patterns hold within the four text categories sam-
pled, as pricing pages and technical documenta-
tion without discursive content fell outside the in-
clusion criteria, a boundary considered further in
the limitations section. They are consistent with
Terranova’s (2004) concept of free labor and with
research on platform stratification (van Dijck et al.,
2018). Tan (2026), drawing on online observation
of Suno-centered music practices, likewise argues
that while AI generation opens the position of mu-
sician to users without conventional training, ac-
cess to and recognition within that position remain
conditioned by economic capital.

Discussion

Three cross-platform thematic clusters emerged
from the analysis of the corpus, although the indi-
vidual codes within them were not observed on
every platform: the agency game, the illusion of
transparency, and the rhetoric of democratization.
This section reads them against the relevant liter-
ature.
The agency game consists in the fact that inter-
face texts recurrently position the user as the cre-
ative subject, whereas the terms of use of all four
platforms narrow that position at the legal and eco-
nomic level, through mechanisms that differ in kind
and should not be treated as equivalent: a broad li-
cense granted to the platform over user content, a
tier-dependent allocation of rights in outputs, and
the retention of rights in the generated track by the
provider. The finding maps onto Fairclough’s
(1992) three-dimensional model: the textual attrib-
ution of agency is sustained through the interac-
tion between promotional and contractual genres
at the level of discursive practice and is condi-
tioned by platform-economic relations at the level
of social practice. Comparable asymmetries have
been documented in studies of how digital plat-
forms conceal user labor (Terranova, 2004; van
Dijck et al., 2018); the present findings carry that
framework into the context of AI music production.
The pattern described here as an illusion of
transparency is an analytical characterization of
the sampled texts and not a claim about intent:
what the corpus supports is that the algorithmic
process is backgrounded rather than deliberately
concealed. It shows, in line with Lakoff and John-
son’s (1980) conceptual metaphor theory, that
metaphors in the domains of magic, tool, and part-
nership structurally cover the algorithmic process,
and Latour’s (2005) black box grounds this theo-
retically: the simple prompt interface offered to the
user covers a complex system relying on large -

scale and largely undisclosed training data. The re-
currence of the strategy across all four platforms
suggests a shared tendency within this market
segment rather than an idiosyncratic choice,
though whether it amounts to an industry -wide
norm is a question a four-platform corpus cannot
settle.
The democratization finding is consistent with
Pram and Morreale’s (2025) ethnographic account
of the individualistic, techno-liberal ideology em-
bedded in generative AI music systems, and with
Tan’s (2026) argument that access to the position
of musician opened by Suno remains conditioned
by economic capital. Read together, the three
studies indicate that the promise of democratiza-
tion reflects a real expansion of access that coex-
ists with new asymmetries organized through sub-
scription tiers and data ownership regimes. This
corresponds to van Dijck et al.’s (2018) conceptual-
ization of the “platform society”, in which platforms
that increase access simultaneously reshape that
access through structural constraints.
The three tendencies operate not as separate
and independent mechanisms but as an articulated
whole. The agency game offers the user a symbolic
creative identity, the illusion of transparency neu-
tralizes the technical knowledge that could call that
identity into question, and the rhetoric of democra-
tization serves as a legitimating frame for the ar-
rangement. This articulation intersects with Fou-
cault’s (1982) account of subjectification, in which
the subject is not forcibly placed into a position im-
posed from outside; power operates through dis-
course by constructing the frame within which the
individual voluntarily recognizes himself or herself
in that position. The interface language of AI music
platforms invites users to identify with the position
of the creator, while the same process conceals
the distributed and infrastructural conditions of
production. Within the limits of a four-platform cor-
pus, this coherence is better read as a logic shared
across the examined segment of the market than
as a set of individual platform choices.
Several practical implications follow. For musi-
cians and producers, awareness of how the sym-
bolic creative subject position constructed by plat-
form interfaces is contractually narrowed in the
terms of use is a safeguard in matters of both cop-
yright and identity politics. For policymakers, the
absence of specific and traceable disclosure con-
cerning the sources of the third-party training rep-
ertoire and the rights of the relevant holders needs
to be addressed through regulation, since a gen-
eral acknowledgement that material was gathered
from the open internet leaves nothing that a rights
holder can act upon; the lack of transparency re-
vealed by the corpus analysis is a structural prob-
lem affecting not only individual users but also the
musicians whose works may have contributed to
these training ecosystems. For researchers,
mixed-methods designs integrating discourse
analysis with audience studies offer a way to un-
derstand how these discourses bear on user expe-
rience and perceptions of identity.
For music education institutions the rhetoric of
democratization requires particular attention. The
frame of “anyone can be a musician” transforms
not only the economic value of professional musi-
cianship but also the process through which musi-
cal competence is acquired: technical skill and
long-term practice cease to be a precondition and
are made to appear almost as an obstacle. Institu-
tions providing education in music technology and
sound arts face this pressure directly, and the ten-
sion between legitimizing the curriculum and incor-
porating AI tools into it from a critical perspective
is becoming a concrete pedagogical problem.

Limitations

Six limitations frame the findings. First, the corpus
was restricted to four platforms and to a specific
time frame (May 2025), leaving the temporal trans-
formation of the discourse outside the scope. The
language strategies of AI music platforms can
change rapidly under legal pressure, competitive
dynamics, and public reaction, so a cross-sectional
approach identifies the language practices of a
given period rather than causal relations. This ap-
plies to the contractual material in particular: the li-
censing and ownership provisions reported here

describe the versions of the terms of use archived
in May 2025 and may since have been revised.
Second, the corpus consists solely of English
corporate texts. Although the platforms examined
operate on a global scale, they conduct their corpo-
rate communication in English, which leaves out-
side the scope any discursive differences aimed at
local markets in languages such as Turkish, Japa-
nese, or Spanish. Inconsistencies or adaptations
between localized interface texts and corporate
English texts constitute a separate research ques-
tion for cultural discourse studies.
Third, the depth of the contractual analysis is
uneven across the four platforms. The terms of
Suno and AIVA are examined at greater length
than those of Udio and Soundraw, for which a sin-
gle supporting clause is cited rather than a pas-
sage-by-passage discussion; the cross-platform
claim about contractual narrowing should be read
with that difference in view. Fourth, the disclosure
patterns reported in the findings hold within the
text types sampled rather than across platform
communication as a whole. Pricing and subscrip-
tion pages were not among the four text catego-
ries compiled, so the limited articulation of access
barriers reflects the boundaries of the sampling
frame as much as the discourse itself, and the ex-
clusion of technical documentation without discur-
sive content leaves outside the corpus any model
cards, help pages, or developer resources that
might carry training-data information. The absence
recorded for specific and traceable training-data
disclosure should therefore be read as an absence
within the sampled texts, not as evidence that the
platforms disclose nothing anywhere in their com-
munication.
Fifth, how users perceive, interpret, and experi-
ence these discourses in practice remains outside
the scope of the study. Closing the gap between
corporate discourse and audience interpretation
requires ethnographic or survey-based audience
research.
Finally, the study was conducted by a single re-
searcher and no intercoder reliability test was per-
formed. Coding decisions in qualitative discourse
analysis inevitably reflect the researcher’s theoret-
ical background, analytical focus, and interpretive
preferences; in the CDA tradition this subjectivity
cannot be eliminated entirely and is addressed in-
stead through the positionality stated in the
method section and through an auditable analytical
procedure (Fairclough, 1992). Three properties
should be distinguished here, since they are often
collapsed. Transparency concerns whether the an-
alytical procedure is stated in enough detail to be
understood; auditability concerns whether a
reader can retrieve the same material and follow
the same decisions; analytical reliability concerns
whether an independent coder applying the same
codebook would reach the same decisions. The
first two were pursued as far as the design allows:
the coding categories were developed during the
initial open-coding stage and finalized as a fixed
codebook before second-cycle coding, the code-
book is reproduced in Appendix B, each finding is
tied to identified corpus units and passages with
unit references, and all source documents are
listed in Appendix A with the archived capture on
which the analysis was carried out. The third was
not established. No second coder was involved and
no separate recoding or audit round was con-
ducted after the original analysis, so no reliability
statistic is reported and none should be inferred
from the transparency measures above. For the
same reason Table 2 reports the number of analyt-
ical units in which a code was recorded at least
once rather than a count of coded clauses or sen-
tences: unit-level presence is what the original
coding procedure yielded, and reporting segment
frequencies would require a recoding round that
was not carried out. Team-based coding protocols
could not be applied, so the findings should be
treated as an interpretive analysis to be read within
the theoretical framework rather than as a defini-
tive result. A second coder, or researchers working
from different theoretical frameworks on the same
corpus, could test the interpretive range of these
findings more strongly.

Conclusion and Recommendations

Academic research in Türkiye that examines gen-
erative AI music platforms through language struc-
ture and ideological function rather than technical
performance or legal aspects remains very limited,
and this study aims to contribute to that area. The
findings show that the platforms examined do not
eliminate creative agency but render it ambiguous
within discourse. The creative subject position
constructed in interface discourse is contractually
narrowed through licensing and ownership clauses
of differing scope, most fully evidenced for Suno
and AIVA, whose terms were examined passage by
passage; the operation of the algorithm is back-
grounded through metaphor rather than fore-
grounded as a technical process; and the origin of
the training repertoire, where it is accounted for at
all within the sampled texts, is described only in
general terms that cannot be independently
traced. The promise of democratization can be
read as a legitimating frame. Whether it in fact nor-
malizes existing access inequalities or diminishes
the economic standing of professional musician-
ship is not something a corpus of platform texts
can establish; these are implications of the dis-
course that further research would need to test.
The study contributes on three levels. Method-
ologically, it sets out an analytical framework com-
bining CDA with conceptual metaphor theory and
demonstrates it on the present corpus. A four-plat-
form study carried out by a single researcher does
not establish a generally validated model, and the
transferability of the framework to other corpora
remains to be shown. Theoretically, it examines the
applicability and limits of agency, the author func-
tion, and subjectification in the context of digital
production; the findings support the applicability of
these classical conceptualizations to AI-mediated
creativity while indicating that they need updating
to account for distributed agency and the specific
dynamics of human-machine interaction. For the
field, it adds to the small number of studies in music
technology and digital culture research in Türkiye
that treat AI music production as a language prac-
tice.
The question of artistic identity and the creative
subject is being reshaped on legal, economic, and
professional grounds. The copyright problems ad-
dressed by Samuelson (2023) and Grimmelmann’s
(2016) framework of “copyright for literate robots”
mark the legal dimension of this transformation,
while the present study places its discursive di-
mension under scrutiny. The legal framework asks
who owns, whereas discourse asks who counts as
creative, and the two questions are mutually condi-
tioning rather than separate. Discursive ambiguity
of the kind described here may contribute to the
normalization of legal precarity. This study anal-
yses how platforms represent training practices
and rights allocation; it does not determine the le-
gal status of those practices, nor can a discourse
analysis by itself indicate which regulatory re-
sponse is appropriate. What follows from the anal-
ysis is narrower: debates on labor rights and copy-
right reform may benefit from attending to how
these arrangements are made sayable, or left un-
said, in the texts through which platforms address
their users.
Future research could compare platform dis-
courses across national and cultural contexts to
test whether these strategies are universal or con-
text specific; analyses conducted in Turkish or in
East Asian languages in particular could expose
the limits of Western-centered corporate lan-
guage. Longitudinal work tracking how the lan-
guage of AI music production changes over time
could document the effect of legal regulation, art-
ist organizing, and market dynamics on platform
language. Mixed-methods designs integrating dis-
course analysis with studies of user perception and
identity could clarify the relation between corpo-
rate discourse and audience experience, and re-
search on how music education institutions inte-
grate AI tools into their curricula would concretize
the pedagogical consequences of this discourse.
