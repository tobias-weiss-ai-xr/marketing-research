Abstract
Artificial intelligence promises to transform marketing, but some relational constants endure across technological ages.
The current research proposes a three-layer hierarchical framework that prioritizes three time-invariant foundations of
cooperative exchange relationships. Specifically, each foundation comprises a universal principle (interdependence, reci -
procity, justice), corresponding psychological construct (trust, gratitude, unfairness), and resulting behaviors that permit,
propel, and protect cooperative exchange relationships, regardless of technological contexts. The authors trace the evolu -
tion of the three foundations and their manifestations across pre-industrial, industrial, and digital ages to demonstrate their
consistency. A projection details how these foundations will shape relationship marketing in the future, guiding market -
ers to focus on constants to establish effective relationship marketing strategies in an evolving AI age. This hierarchical
framework counters the paralysis of uncertainty by offering evidence of stability amid technological disruption. It also
reorients relationship marketing from commitment toward cooperation as the practical, dynamic engine of value creation
in any technological age.

The ultimate why behind cooperation is the pursuit of
value. Our quest is to identify the how, the time-invariant
foundations of cooperative exchange relationships that are
hardwired into human psychology, particularly as they man-
ifest in connection with relationship marketing. We conduct
a comprehensive review of evolutionary psychology (Cos -
mides & Tooby, 2015; Henrich & Muthukrishna, 2021),
anthropology (Glowacki & Lew-Levy, 2022; Sahlins,
1972), economic history (De Roover, 1946), and marketing
(Eyuboglu & Buja, 2007; Palmatier et al., 2006) literature.
On this basis, we derive a hierarchical framework of three
foundations of cooperative exchange relationships. In it, we
delineate three main principles of cooperation, their corre -
sponding psychological constructs, and the behaviors they
evoke.
First, the principle of interdependence is the fundamen -
tal condition of vulnerability to another party. It functions
through trust, the psychological mechanism that underlies
permissioning behaviors  that allow for the risk and vul -
nerability inherent to cooperative exchange relationships.
Second, the principle of reciprocity requires that each party
mutually receives and provides some of the value unlocked
by any cooperative exchange. This prosocial cycle of reci -
procity is powered by gratitude, which functions like an
emotional engine for propulsive behaviors in response to
value received, which ensure value also is granted. Third,
the principle of justice is most saliently enforced by a potent
sensitivity to perceived unfairness, which triggers protective
behaviors of punishment, withdrawal, or reputational dispar-
agement to deter exploitation, coercion, and opportunism.
Together, these three foundations determine whether coop -
eration starts, persists, or collapses. By identifying founda -
tions that underpin cooperative exchange relationships and
that have stood the test of time, we delineate that these three
time-invariant foundations of cooperative exchange are
hardwired into human psychology through evolution and
adapt to changing contexts while also preserving their core
functions, such that they extend to and form the stable basis
for developing successful relationship marketing strategies
in the evolving age of AI.
The evolutionary underpinnings and universal applicabil-
ity of each foundation of cooperation reflects natural human
inclinations to make assessments of reliability and integ -
rity before engaging in interdependent relationships (trust),
reciprocate intentionally rendered benefits (gratitude), and
display strong sensitivity to injustice in social interactions
(unfairness). These inclinations are so fundamental that
their absence characterizes sociopathy (Buck, 2004), and
they appear among even very young children, who are eager
cooperators, with innate capacities for trust instincts, grati -
tude, and fairness concerns that are only later disrupted by
negative experiences (Blake et al., 2015; Glowacki & Lew-
Levy, 2022).
Furthermore, we explicitly separate these hardwired,
time-invariant foundations (principles and drivers) from
their evolving manifestations (institutions and practices),
which reflect technological and cultural contexts. We dem -
onstrate the enduring relevance of these foundations by ana-
lyzing their manifestations across pre-industrial, industrial,
and digital eras, in which they consistently facilitate coop -
erative exchanges. For example, pre-industrial gratitude
mainly involved face-to-face interactions, but industrial -
ization necessitated impersonal expressions (e.g., thanking
customers through mass media and exclusive deals), and
the digital age has supported more personalization at scale,
even if computer mediated, and amplified the reach of grati-
tude expressions. As expressions of gratitude proliferate, the
fundamental psychology underlying this principle suggests
that its intensity diminishes as the efficiency of its commu -
nication increases.
We also project how these constants will continue to
shape relationship marketing in the artificial intelligence
age, as AI systems grow increasingly capable of social and
emotional engagement (Garvey et al., 2023; Huang & Rust,
2024; Zhu & Chu, 2025). As Bezos suggests in the open -
ing quote, constants offer the most reliable basis for build -
ing long-term strategies. Therefore, we apply the proposed
framework to offer a research agenda of testable questions.
Finally, in articulating hierarchical foundations of coop -
erative exchange relationships, this article also seeks to
reorient the very field of relationship marketing (RM), to
shift the focus from commitment toward the more practi -
cal and dynamic concept of cooperation. By focusing on
the principles of cooperation and their core psychological
mechanisms, we argue that it is possible to better understand

Cooperation and its foundations for
relational exchanges
To identify enduring mechanisms that enable successful,
sustained exchange relationships—from preindustrial to
AI ages and beyond—we begin with a brief examination of
cooperation. Whether at microscopic or macroscopic levels,
“cooperation is needed for evolution to construct new levels
of organization.… Genomes, cells, multicellular organisms,
social insects, and human society are all based on coopera -
tion” (Nowak, 2006, p. 1560). Mutually beneficial coopera-
tive relationships entail the principles of interdependence,
reciprocity, and justice, as evident in Fig. 1, including rela-
tionship marketing, which refers to “all marketing activities
directed toward establishing, developing, and maintaining
successful relational exchanges” (Morgan & Hunt, 1994, p.
22). In these relationships, the goal is to move beyond dis -
crete transactions toward ongoing relational exchanges that
foster mutual value. The inherent challenge is when “not
cooperating yields for each individual higher personal out -
comes and cooperating yields higher collective outcomes”
(Van Dijk & De Dreu, 2021, p. 416), such that the parties
are vulnerable to exploitation (Axelrod & Hamilton, 1981).
To limit such vulnerability, viable cooperative relation -
ships build on robust socio-psychological foundations. To
identify them, we consider evolutionary and neurobiological
factors that are hardwired into human cognition, implying
that they remain invariant over time and across environ -
ments. We also required candidate constructs to display uni-
versality across cultures. Finally, they had to be relevant to at
least one of the principles of cooperative exchange relation-
ships (interdependence, reciprocity, and justice) and able to
facilitate cooperative behaviors in marketing exchanges—
defined as complex systems of direct and indirect relation -
ships between parties that involve transfers of tangible (i.e.,
utilitarian) and intangible (i.e., symbolic) entities (Bagozzi,
1975)—and establish performance implications.

field of relationship marketing (RM), to shift the focus

ization affirmed by Palmatier et al.’s ( 2006) meta-analysis.

to account for critical mediating mechanisms, including
gratitude (Palmatier et al., 2009) and unfairness (Samaha
et al., 2011). Commitment, relative to trust, gratitude, or

constructs are not merely adjacent to cooperation but rather
are integral to its emergence and endurance (Table 1). First,
trust entails confidence in the exchange partner’s reliabil -
ity and integrity (Morgan & Hunt, 1994), so it provides a
psychological safety net and allows the parties to accept
vulnerability and engage in uncertain exchanges that unfold
over time (Mayer et al., 1995). By reducing perceived risk,
it functions as a pervasive determinant of human coopera -
tion in both personal and business realms (Gambetta, 1988).
Trust underpins the principle of interdependence and in turn
is permissioning, in that it opens the door to cooperation.
Second, gratitude powers the principle of reciprocity and is
the motivational impetus for prosocial, mutually beneficial
exchanges. The positive emotion of receiving an intention -
ally rendered benefit (Palmatier et al., 2009) creates psy -
chological pressure to repay that benefit (Cropanzano &
Mitchell, 2005), fueling ongoing cooperation. This mecha -
nism is so deeply ingrained that its absence is considered
abnormal (Buck, 2004). Its function is propulsive, such that
gratitude amplifies prosocial cycles once the door is open.
Third, the perception of unfairness, conversely, acts as a
guardrail to the principle of justice. Such perceptions trig -
ger negative emotions and retaliatory behaviors, which can
rapidly unravel relational bonds (Samaha et al., 2011; Van
Dijk & De Dreu, 2021). The absence of an innate sense of
fairness (Blake et al., 2015) threatens cooperation. Unfair -
ness is protective and slams the door on exploitation or
norm violations.
Identified as “pillars of relationship marketing,” gratitude
and unfairness actually were missing from early relation -
ship marketing theory (Palmatier & Steinhoff, 2019, p. 56),
despite their evolutionary roles in rewarding adherence to
and punishing deviance from cooperative social norms.
In addition, the three psychological constructs work both
independently and synergistically. They jointly determine
whether cooperation starts, persists, or collapses. Repeated
gratitude can build benevolent trust; a single salient unfair -
ness shock can dismantle reliability and integrity beliefs and
halt reciprocity cycles.

Structural composition  Trust involves cognitive evalu -
ations, affective responses, and behavioral readiness.
Trustors cognitively assess the trustee’s perceived abil -
ity, benevolence, and integrity (Mayer et al., 1995), infer
intentions, and interpret signals (Doney & Cannon, 1997;
Krueger et al., 2007), then update their judgments through
experience (Palmatier & Steinhoff, 2019). Trust is deeply
imbued with emotion (Hancock et al., 2023; Jones &
George, 1998), including confidence and security, while
distrust evokes suspicion, anxiety, negative reactions, and
avoidance (Engelmann & Fehr, 2013; Krueger et al., 2007;
Mayer et al., 1995). Receiving trust prompts an oxytocin
release that literally and physically feels good and serves
as a psychic reward that motivates reciprocal, trustworthy
behavior (Zak, 2008). It also enables behavioral readiness to
accept vulnerability and risk (Mayer et al., 1995). As an effi-
cient governance mechanism, trust reduces transaction costs
(Adler, 2001) and encourages coordination (Zucker, 1986),
long-term orientations, and relationship-specific invest -
ments (Doney & Cannon, 1997; Morgan & Hunt, 1994).
Evolutionary underpinnings  An evolutionary adaptation
that allows an entity to make itself vulnerable is advanta -
geous when it enables productive cooperation (Krueger et
al., 2007). On a neurochemical level, trust of an unknown
other results from the presence of oxytocin, a molecule with
deep evolutionary roots; its precursor, vasotocin, appeared
in fish around 100 million years ago, facilitating reproduc -
tion by reducing fear associated with social approaches
(Zak, 2008). In mammals, it evolved into oxytocin, which
has a key role in social bonding and the modulation of trust.
The related neurobiology involves distinct brain systems,
including the paracingulate cortex, which predicts social
behavior (Krueger et al., 2007). Unconditional trust cor -
relates with activity in the septal area (social attachment,
oxytocin release); conditional trust, based on calculations of
risk and reward, activates the ventral tegmental area (reward
evaluation) (Krueger et al., 2007). This neurochemical com-
plexity underscores how inherently trust, risk-taking, and
social bonding are linked to human interaction.

people to get into positions where they can, if they choose,
injure what we care about, since those are the same posi -
tions that they must be in in order to help us take care of
what we care about” (Baier, 1986, p. 236). The inherent vul-
nerability of interdependence creates diverse situations that
less important than trust (Palmatier et al., 2006) or gratitude
(Palmatier et al., 2009). Moreover, commitment can be mis-

inertia rather than true allegiance (Henderson et al., 2021;
Watson et al., 2015).
Perhaps even more critically, seeking commitment might
stifle dynamic relationship formation and the capacity of
prosperous economies to advance and leverage innovations.
According to Fukuyama ( 1995), economies that encourage
flexible, trust-based cooperation among strangers outper -
form those limited to rigid, kin- and stubborn attachment-
based networks. Committed customers might protect a seller
from competition, but true allegiance rarely is necessary for
mutually beneficial relationships, a notion echoed by recent
research on relationship ambidexterity (Gu et al., 2025).
In summary, we posit that cooperation, rooted in evolved
human psychology and facilitated by the psychological
constructs of trust, gratitude, and absence of unfairness, is
essential for overcoming the tension between individual
costs and collective benefits to sustain successful relational
exchanges. On the basis of this theoretical grounding, we
examine trust, gratitude, and unfairness in depth, in an
attempt to gain enduring insights into relationship market -
ing effectiveness, even across new technological frontiers.
The principle of interdependence and the
psychological construct of trust
The principle of interdependence and its corresponding psy-
chological construct of trust implies the existence of psycho-
logical safety, allowing partners to accept the vulnerability
inherent in cooperative exchanges. Without calibrated trust,
reciprocity cannot persist, and justice cannot be enforced. In
cooperative relationships, trust refers to “the willingness of
a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based
on the expectation that the other will perform a particular
action important to the trustor, irrespective of the ability to
monitor or control that other party” (Mayer et al., 1995, p.
712). In marketing, Morgan and Hunt ( 1994, p. 23) char -
acterize trust as “confidence in an exchange partner’s reli -
ability and integrity,” Khamitov et al. (2024, p. 8) specify it
as “a consumer’s confidence in [the] reliability and integrity
of the target of trust,” and Moorman et al. ( 1992, p. 315)
define it as “a willingness to rely on an exchange partner in
whom one has confidence.” These definitions converge in
highlighting reliability and integrity, not a desire to monitor
or control. Although related, trust (trustor’s state) is distinct
from trustworthiness (trustee’s characteristics, like ability,
benevolence, and integrity); fostering trustworthiness is

The principle of reciprocity and the psychological
construct of gratitude
The desire to provide reciprocal returns, even when they are
separated in time from the provided benefits, is motivated
by gratitude which is a deeply ingrained human emotion and
social force. Following a benefit received from another’s
intentional actions, this “moral memory” reminds recipients
of their interdependence (McCullough et al., 2001, p. 250).
Philosophers have long recognized its paramount virtue, as
in Marcus Tullius Cicero’s often-cited assertion that “Grati-
tude is not only the greatest of virtues, but the parent of
all others” (McCullough et al., 2001, p. 249). In psychol -
ogy, gratitude constitutes an emotional response to kind -
ness received (Ma et al., 2017) or a “feeling of appreciation
in response to an experience that is beneficial to, but not
attributable to, the self” (Fehr et al., 2017, p. 363). Market-
ing scholars similarly note how benefits received can evoke
feelings of gratefulness or appreciation (Palmatier et al.,
2009). As a vital emotional and social mechanism, gratitude
helps initiate, sustain, and strengthen the cooperative bonds
necessary for flourishing relationships.
Structural composition  Gratitude involves cognitive
appraisals, emotional responses, and behavioral readiness,
which are evident in the three moral functions of grati -
tude that McCullough et al. (2001) identify: as a barometer
(response to moral actions), a motive (for prosocial behav -
ior), and a reinforcer (encouraging future moral actions
by benefactors). Cognitively, it arises from an evaluation
of a benefit as valuable, intentionally given, costly to the
benefactor, and gratuitous (Kerry et al., 2023; Locklear et
al., 2023), such that it tends to be more intense if the ben -
efit meets some personal need and is proffered expressly
to achieve that purpose (Henning et al., 2017). Favorable
appraisals in turn evoke the affective core of gratitude,
which is a positive, universally desirable emotional state
linked to happiness and contentment (Kerry et al., 2023;
McCullough et al., 2001).
The emotional landscape also can be more complex though,
in that gratitude can transition into negative socially ori -
ented feelings, particularly if the ability to reciprocate is
constrained (Peng et al., 2018; Walsh et al., 2023). Feelings
of gratitude also prompt behavioral readiness, including
prosocial behaviors toward the benefactor (direct reciproc -
ity) or others, such as “upstream reciprocity” passed for -
ward to third parties (DeSteno et al., 2010, p. 293). Grateful
recipients aid benefactors even at personal cost (Noles &
demand trust, from personal relationships to international
affairs (Gambetta, 1988; Krueger et al., 2007). Develop -
mental psychology adds that achieving basic trust represents
the initial job required of the ego (Evans & Krueger, 2009).
Major religions and philosophers emphasize the virtues cru-
cial for trust too, including truthfulness (Satya in Hinduism,
right speech in Buddhism, Plato’s description of truth as
fundamental), integrity (Xin in Confucianism), obligation to
be trustworthy (Amanah in Islam), and prohibitions against
false witness (Judaism/Christianity, Hume’s assertion that
reliance on testimony is the most common and necessary
form of reason) (Harris et al., 2018). Such enduring claims
indicate that trustworthiness is a functional, morally virtu -
ous social good.
Relevance to exchange cooperation  Trust is both an input
to and outcome of successful exchanges, creating a virtu -
ous cycle (Morgan & Hunt, 1994) that develops incremen -
tally. Direct experience is key in early stages (Dwyer et al.,
1987), and then repeated positive interactions deepen trust.
Interorganizational trust, as a governance mechanism, can
discourage opportunism in exchanges that feature high lev-
els of uncertainty or dependence (Doney & Cannon, 1997),

The principle of justice and the psychological
construct of unfairness
Concerns for fairness, and potent reactions to its absence,
represent both a fundamental pillar of the social principle of
justice as a critical determinant of the success or failure of
cooperative relational exchanges. While gratitude propels
cooperation, perceptions of unfairness act as a powerful
restrictive agent. Fairness, broadly defined as the notion that
“people deserve or have rights to certain resources or kinds
of treatment” (Hallsson et al., 2018, p. 49), is both subjec -
tive and deeply felt. When a customer perceives unfairness,
due to some unacceptable or inequitable ratio of their out -
comes to inputs, compared with the ratio received by other
customers (Steinhoff & Palmatier, 2016), they often exhibit
negative responses, such as anger or tension (Samaha et al.,
2011), which then can lead to punishment, withdrawal, or
reputational disparagement behaviors. Organizational jus -
tice research distinguishes distributive (outcome fairness),
procedural (process fairness), and interactional (interper -
sonal treatment fairness) forms (Dulebohn et al., 2009;
McColl-Kennedy & Sparks, 2003). In customer–seller mar-
keting relationships, “unfair acts often incite even greater
backlash than other negative activities because of the emo -
tional imperative to punish unfair partners” (Samaha et al.,
2011, p. 99).

Evolutionary underpinnings As an evolutionary adaptation,
gratitude facilitates reciprocal altruism, which is crucial for
survival in social groups (McCullough et al., 2001; Smith et
al., 2017). When environmental pressures grew to require
stable relationships that could balance social and economic
resources over time, the emotional component helped regu-
late and calibrate responses to altruistic acts, in accordance
with their cost–benefit ratio (DeSteno et al., 2010; Trivers,
1971). Feelings of gratitude, including the pleasure of recip-
rocating and guilt for failing to do so, are components of an
evolved system supporting cooperation (Becker, 1986; Triv-
ers, 1985). As noted, its absence constitutes a symptom of
psychopathy (Buck, 2004). Gratitude engages brain regions
like the medial (social rewards and moral cognition) and
dorsolateral (cooperative behaviors during gift exchange)
prefrontal cortexes (Balconi et al., 2019). The mu-opioid
signaling that tends to accompany gratitude implies its posi-
tive effects on social relationships, well-being, and health
(Henning et al., 2017).
Universality As Adam Smith (1790/ 1976, p. 68) famously
stated, “the sentiment which most immediately and directly
prompts us to reward, is gratitude.” It echoes across major
world religions, as a core virtue, manifest in diverse prac -
tices: in Christianity, the Eucharist (derived from the Greek
word eucharisteo, meaning to give thanks to God); in Juda-
ism, with Hakarat HaTov (recognizing the good); in Islam,
through Shukr (thankfulness); in Buddhism, with Kataññutā
(knowing what has been done for one); and in Hinduism,
with Kṛtajñatā (gratefulness). The specific exhibitions
may vary across cultures, but the underlying principle of
acknowledging a benefit received and feeling motivated to
respond positively appears constant. Most cultures have lin-
guistic and cultural devices for its expression (McCullough
et al., 2001).

which mitigates accounting demands (Peng et al., 2018),
but furthermore, gratitude builds goodwill and relational
bonds to support long-term cooperation (Palmatier et al.,
2009), which also indirectly reduces transaction costs (e.g.,
Dyer & Singh, 1998). Customer gratitude in particular

Universality  Specific fairness norms vary, but a fundamen-
tal concern for justice in exchange appears universal, such
that “there is to date no documented case of a culture whose
people does not care deeply about fairness” (Hallsson et al.,
2018, p. 50). Blake et al. ( 2015) identify disadvantageous
inequity aversion (rejecting outcomes in which one receives
less than a peer) as a general element of human behavior but
also note more cultural variability in advantageous inequity
aversion (rejecting outcomes in which one receives more
than peers). Overall though, a deep-seated concern for fair -
ness is evident in major world religions, such that Christian-
ity emphasizes justice (Greek word dikaiosune for justice
or righteousness); Judaism champions Tzedek (righteous -
ness) and Mishpat (fairness, equity, impartiality in justice);
Islam underscores Adl (justice, balance, and fairness as fun-
damental attributes of Allah) and Qist (equity); Buddhism
promotes ethical conduct (Sila) which encompasses fairness
and non-harming, and concepts like Karma, which implies
cosmic fairness; and Hinduism upholds Dharma (righ -
teousness/duty), with its principles of justice and equitable
conduct.
Relevance to exchange cooperation  Perceptions of unfair -
ness are detrimental to cooperative exchanges and per -
formance, leading Samaha et al. ( 2011, p. 99) to describe
perceived unfairness as “relationship poison.” It exerts
direct negative effects on channel relationships, intensi -

and undermines contracts, such that its “negative effect on
relational behaviors … and, subsequently, financial perfor -
mance is similar to or greater than the effects of conflict
and opportunism” (Samaha et al., 2011, p. 100). When cus-
tomers identify a violation of justice, they attribute negative
motives to the source, which can induce punitive responses
like reduced loyalty, switching, or negative word of mouth
(McColl-Kennedy & Sparks, 2003; Steinhoff & Palmatier,
2016). In loyalty programs, Steinhoff and Palmatier ( 2016)
determine that the negative effects of perceived unfairness
among bystander customers (e.g., watching others board an

and the drive to punish transgressions both have deep evo -
lutionary roots that are well aligned to cooperation norms
underlying the justice principle. As Brosnan and de Waal

fairness per se but in order to reap the benefits of continued
cooperation.” From an evolutionary perspective, morality

Evolution of foundational principles across
technological ages
As societies grow bigger and more complex, both chal -
lenges to and opportunities for cooperation evolve. In par -
ticular, new technologies, tools, ideas, and cultural practices
emerge to support cooperation at wider scales. Therefore,
we consider how the foundational principles of coopera -
tive exchange relationships have emerged in three historical
eras: pre-industrial, industrial, and digital. Specific forms of
social exchange and the relative importance of the different
foundations likely vary across cultures and time (Cosmides
& Tooby, 2015; Lund et al., 2013), but we assert that the
underlying human capacity to perceive and react to them
remains constant, because cooperative behavior always
requires key societal elements.
Regardless of the era or its technological and cultural
evolutions, societal norms and social embeddedness facili -
tate cooperation, because assistance to others establishes
a positive reputation that tends to evoke rewards (Nowak,
2006), like expanded access to partners and resources. Such
rewards are more plentiful for actors with reputations for
being trustworthy, grateful, and fair cooperators (Glowacki
& Lew-Levy, 2022). A trustworthy social environment in
turn depends on collective enforcement of fairness norms,
including sanctions in interactions involving power imbal -
ances (Coleman, 1988; Glowacki & Lew-Levy, 2022;
Sznycer et al., 2018). As our historical review reveals
(Table 2), as technologies and institutions evolve, so do the
manifestations of cooperative exchange relationships , yet
the founding principles retain their core functions for shap-
ing social and economic interactions.

Industrial age (mid-19th–mid-20th century)
The Industrial Revolution, driven by technological innova -
tions like steam power, mechanization, and the telegraph,
brought about mass production, urbanization, and the rise
of large corporations (Landes, 2003). As societies expanded
and interactions grew more impersonal, anonymous, and
diverse, the traditional, interpersonal mechanisms of pre-
industrial times proved inadequate (Sunderland, 2007;
Zucker, 1986), prompting profound shifts toward formaliza-
tion, standardization, and institutionalization, including the
mechanisms governing cooperation.
Trust shifted toward impersonal, institutional forms that
required personal identification, beginning with etiquette-
governed calling cards and then expanding to business cards
that could help holders navigate wider social and commer -
cial networks (Davidoff, 1973; Kasson, 1991). Creditwor-
thiness assessments also became systematized by credit
reporting agencies (Lauer, 2022). In parallel, professional
credentials and certifications gained importance as stan -
dardized markers of competence (Larson, 2017). Although
Hume viewed them as inferior to promises among friends,
institutional trust mechanisms such as codified laws and
contracts gained usefulness in societies that had grown large
enough that the promisor does not fear the penalty of “never
being trusted again” (Baier, 1986, p. 245).

structured reciprocity in financial payments to reward
quality sales and service efforts (Powers, 2016). These
more standardized gratitude mechanisms all served to
expand the scale of reciprocity.
Industrial age governments also intervened more
directly in the marketplace to ensure justice, through con -
sumer rights legislation (e.g., Pure Food and Drug Act of
1906) and regulatory bodies tasked with combatting per -
ceived unfair practices (Barkan, 1985; Pappalardo, 2014).
Formal institutions and legal systems evolved to man -
age cooperation and enforce marketplace justice, often
superseding informal norms (Fehr & Fischbacher, 2004).
Nongovernmental organizations (e.g., Better Business
Bureau, founded in 1912) promoted ethical standards
while giving consumers another means to address their
complaints (Balleisen, 2017). This industrial-age aver -
sion to unfairness provoked systematic, standardized,
and formal mechanisms that in turn expanded justice
enforcement beyond community boundaries to match the
growing scale of economic activity.
Digital age (mid-20th–early 21 st century)
Digital technologies, computation, and the internet insti -
gated another profound shift, in which marketplace interac-
tions often are mediated by platforms, algorithmic systems
engage in verification and governance, reputation reflects
quantifiable measures, and distributed cooperation is
possible.
Technology-mediated interdependence rests on algorith-
mic forms of trust and governance. People’s digital identi -
ties are evident in their social media profiles (Van Dijck,
2013) and authenticated by cryptographic digital signa -
tures (McGrath, 2016). Online reputations, established
on the basis of platform review histories (Tadelis, 2016),
produce reputation capital (Botsman, 2012) and influence
transaction willingness, especially in the sharing economy
(Beck et al., 2024). Such reliance on algorithmic systems
and quantified reputation expands the scale of interactions
but also introduces risks of bias, opacity, and manipulation;
vulnerabilities to privacy violations; and concerns about
trustworthiness, given the expanded capabilities for sur -
veillance and social control (Botsman, 2017). For example,
blockchain-based smart contracts aim to automate trust by
embedding agreements in code (Hawlitschek et al., 2018),
and digital currencies within blockchains seek to establish
trust without traditional intermediaries (Sedlmeir et al.,

Yet the incomprehensibility and black box  nature of
advanced AI algorithms, with decision processes opaque
even to creators (Kozinets & Gretzel, 2021), challenges pro-

opacity creates a dilemma for marketers: Their reliance on
powerful AI platforms, controlled by tech giants, may mean
they simply use the technology, rather than leveraging and
exploiting it, such that they become vulnerable to algorith -

& Gretzel, 2021). In such a scenario, consumer trust might
shift from brands to intermediary AI platforms.
The rise of feeling AI introduces even more complexity.
Through empathetic engagement, it can foster emotional
connections (Huang & Rust, 2024), but its reliance on highly
sensitive, emotional data heightens concerns about data pri-
vacy and security. Puntoni et al. ( 2021) highlight how data
enable AI personalization, but insufficient transparency and
control leave consumers feeling exploited, which can trig -
ger reactance. Breaches would be catastrophic for trust. In
the privacy paradox , consumers express concern but still
share data, in an uneasy trust dynamic (Bauer et al., 2019).
Evidence of an initial positivity bias, such that users assume
new automation is perfect, suggests the risk that their ini -
tially high trust will drop precipitously following any errors
(Hoff & Bashir, 2015). This fragility is particularly danger-
ous in relation to autonomous AI; excessive trust and misuse
is likely if it is not balanced and calibrated by users’ experi-
ence and transparent communication about the AI’s capabil-
ities and limitations. Developing trustworthy AI hinges on
fairness, explainability, accountability, and privacy (Kaur
et al., 2022), but achieving appropriate trust calibration—
avoiding both detrimentally excessive trust and insufficient,
innovation-stifling trust—remains a thorny challenge.
In agent–agent interactions, the principle of interdepen -
dence is critical, but trust manifests differently, because AI
agents cannot and do not need to sense trust psychologi -
cally. Instead, trust gets operationalized by mechanisms like
cryptographic verification, immutable ledgers (blockchain),
a predictable algorithmic logic, and shared communication
protocols (Salah et al., 2019). In turn, permissioning behav-
iors reflect verifiable computational certainty, not emotional
confidence. The distinction between psychological trust
(human–AI) and computational trust (agent–agent) repre -
sents a compelling avenue for research.

Research agenda: How the foundations of
cooperative exchange relationships manifest in the
artificial intelligence age
As humanity transitions from the digital age into the artifi -
cial intelligence age, characterized by pervasive, advanced
artificial intelligence (AI), marketing will evolve again.
Massive investments of more than $100 billion in AI-related
startups in 2024 (Glaser & Yao, 2025) and announced plans
to invest half a trillion dollars in the AI infrastructure (Seeth-
araman & Dotan, 2025) already underscore the conviction
that AI is a transformative force. Sophisticated AI agents,
autonomous systems, and feeling AI with the capacity to
process, simulate, and respond to human emotions (Huang
& Rust, 2024), reflecting advances in machine learning,
generative AI, and system autonomy, promise future trans -
formative impacts (Kelly et al., 2023; Puntoni et al., 2021).
Of the 78% of surveyed organizations that use generative
AI in at least one business function already, marketing and
sales represents the leading function (Singla et al., 2025).
Thus, cooperation increasingly must include AI entities
that possess varying degrees of autonomy and simulated
social-emotional intelligence, which enables human–AI
augmentation (Davenport et al., 2020). We apply the pro -
posed hierarchical framework of principles, constructs, and
behaviors to generate a robust research agenda for this new
era, as summarized in Table 3.
Researching trust and interdependence: Navigating
complexity and vulnerability
The principle of interdependence still creates vulnerability
that must be managed to allow for cooperation. In the AI
age, interdependence involves human–AI and even AI–
AI (i.e., agent–agent) interactions, beyond conventional
human-based relationships. The psychological construct
of trust thus extends beyond humans or static technology,
because it must account for interdependence with dynamic,
learning, increasingly autonomous AI systems. As Kelly
et al. ( 2023, p. 3) summarize, “Trust in technology allows
users to believe that using a device will achieve the desired
goal.” In human–AI interactions, perceived trustworthiness
depends more on users’ perceptions of the system’s perfor -

understandability), and purpose (designer’s intent); positive
perceptions and the trust they induce can determine whether
people are willing to turn to AI even when the situation
features substantial uncertainty (Hoff & Bashir, 2015). As

Researching unfairness and justice: Algorithmic bias
and perceived intentionality
The principle of justice, a guardrail of cooperation, gets
enforced by sensitivity to perceived unfairness, which
triggers protective behaviors like punishment or with -
drawal. Considerations of justice already are omnipres -
ent, but they arguably will be amplified in the AI age,
especially in relation to algorithmic governance. Algo -
rithms built with particular objective functions and
trained on human-derived data with reinforcement learn -
ing can amplify societal inequities and discrimination,
leading to detrimental consequences (Bacalhau et al.,
2025; Bar-Gill et al., 2023; Lambrecht & Tucker, 2019;
Puntoni et al., 2021). The previously noted black box
nature of many AI systems complicates any such assess -
ments and attempts to rectify the issues (Davenport et al.,
2020). Actual harms may go unnoticed; injustices may be
imagined where they do not exist. This opacity can lead
to what Puntoni et al. ( 2021, p. 137) call the “Classifi -
cation Experience”: An AI-driven categorization causes
consumers to feel “understood” or “misunderstood,” and
because the latter often signals unfairness or discrimina -
tion, it can erode trust. Furthermore, as AI continues to
disrupt global economies, broader distributive and proce -
dural fairness concerns are likely to alter user perceptions
and behaviors (Starke et al., 2022). Such considerations
suggest the centrality of corporate digital responsibility
concepts that can promote ethical, fair, protective uses
of data and AI technology (Kunz & Wirtz, 2024; Wirtz
et al., 2023). In human–AI marketing interactions, cus -
tomer perceptions of distributive and procedural fairness
are likely to drive their relational responses and coopera -
tive instincts.
Initial evidence suggests that customers judge AI
agents as more fair than human agents, especially when
they encounter negative outcomes (Wu et al., 2024).
According to Garvey et al. ( 2023), AI agents presenting
worse-than-expected offers evoke less negative evalua -
tions, seemingly because customers do not attribute self -
ish intentions to AI. Because they infer that AI agents
lack the capacity for intentional malice, they respond
less negatively to unfavorable outcomes. However, it is
unclear whether this benefit of the doubt  will persist with
assisting robot to other, non-helping robots and humans. On
the other side, AI systems can express gratitude, such as per-
sonalized thank-you messages (Huang & Rust, 2024). In a
fundraising context, gratitude shared by AI chatbots induces
prosocial intentions comparable to those offered in response
to human chats, particularly if a human-like AI signals
social presence (Namkoong et al., 2024).
Still, the extent to which such communications signify
appreciation, in a manner that sparks customers’ reciproc -
ity to an algorithm that does not experience emotions, is
unclear. Even if gratitude expressions reinforce perceptions
of a helper’s self-efficacy and social worth (Grant & Gino,
2010), AI agents that lack intrinsic psychological needs
cannot receive these expressions. In addition, customers
might attribute less benevolence to AI agents delivering
favorable outcomes, due to perceptions of limited AI inten-
tionality (Garvey et al., 2023), which also might lead them
to question if an AI, devoid of independent motivations or
genuine affective capacity, can truly appreciate gratitude. If
humans regard AI as incapable of valuing their gratitude,
the psychological rewards of expressing it diminish, and
they might be unable to relieve the psychological pressures
to reciprocate. Such asymmetry could threaten fairness per-
ceptions and undermine trust if AI seems to operate outside
human principles of mutual interdependence and reciprocity
(Shapiro, 1987). The effect of gratitude as a moral reinforcer
of reciprocity (McCullough et al., 2001) might be diluted if
the recipient is not regarded as a moral agent. The question
that arises is whether AI entities qualify as legitimate bene-
factors, deserving human-like treatment (Swanepoel, 2021),
or if human–human gratitude features distinctive qualities
that AI cannot replicate, such that human intervention is
needed to evoke the full cooperative potential of gratitude
(Tachibana, 2025). Some prior research suggests that cus -
tomers attribute less benevolence to AI due to its perceived
lack of intentionality (Garvey et al., 2023), but the AI in
these studies are relatively less advanced. As AI grows more
autonomous and exhibits more sophisticated reasoning, will
perceptions of its intentionality evolve?
In agent–agent relationships, the principle of reciproc -
ity is fundamental to optimizing for long-term collective
goals, but the underlying construct might not be gratitude.
Propulsive behaviors driven not by emotion but by algo -
rithms, written to reward cooperative actions and persist in
exchanges that yield positive outcomes according to a pre -
defined utility function, suggest a utility calculus. Rather
than a moral reinforcer (McCullough et al., 2001), a com -
putational reinforcer would allow patterns of successful
reciprocation to strengthen the probability of future coop -
eration. If expectations of continued mutual benefit to both
parties do not arise, no propellent force to cooperate exists,
and instead, shorter exchange relationships with a more

Conclusion
Amidst rapid technological evolutions, this research identi-
fies three time-invariant foundations that govern coopera -
tive exchange relationships: interdependence managed by
trust, reciprocity powered by gratitude, and justice enforced
increased exposure to AI decisions and repeated situa -
tions that evoke perceptions of unfairness.
In agent–agent interactions, the principle of justice
may function through the construct of protocol adher -
ence. Perhaps protective behaviors like algorithmic sanc -
tioning, network exclusion, or automated audit trails get
initiated, without requiring any moral outrage, if an AI
agent deviates from established rules. Such a scenario
would require cooperative governance structures that can
ensure fairness and provide redress in a purely computa -
tional environment. Regardless of the type of exchange,
a critical, global question pertains to accountability: How
should accountability be allocated when AI systems,
which lack clear legal personhood or moral agency, pro -
duce unfair outcomes? Should the blame be placed on the
AI, its developers, its deployers, or the data on which it
was trained? Addressing unfairness in AI-driven market -
ing will require integrating AI-based technical solutions
together with human-centric approaches that account for
consumers’ subjective fairness perceptions (Narayanan et
al., 2024), to encourage cooperative practices that pre -
serve perceptions of fairness.
Customers’ sensitivity of AI-driven unfairness (RQ7),
mechanisms to enforce algorithmic accountability (RQ8),
and the safeguarding of justice in agent-agent interac -
tions (RQ9) should be investigated in future research to
understand manifestations of fairness and unfairness in
the age of AI.
Reorientation toward cooperation: Implications for
markets and firm strategy
With this framework, we propose a wholesale reorientation
of relationship marketing. Rather than making commitment
the key mediator of performance (Morgan & Hunt, 1994;
Palmatier et al., 2006), we suggest focusing on founda -
tional principles that enable cooperation. This reorientation
seems particularly critical in the AI age. Historically, com -
mitment—as true allegiance—was advantageous because
it provides continuity, helps capture tacit knowledge, and
gives partners a way to navigate complexity. Today, AI can
fulfill many of these functions. It can serve as a persistent
repository of relational knowledge, capture tacit insights
from vast amounts of unstructured data, and establish con -
tinuity even if human personnel change. The unique value
proposition of sticky, high-commitment, loyal relationships
thus decreases in the AI age.

that economies that foster flexible, trust-based coopera -
tion among strangers outperform those limited to rigid,
kin-based networks—we add that AI may be the ulti -
mate catalyst of such performance, because it can provide

strategy based in time-invariant human psychology.

Our proposed framework challenges traditional relation-

high-commitment relationships. When the foundations of
interdependence, reciprocity, and justice receive systemic
support from AI-driven governance, the kinds of dynamic
cooperation prioritized by Fukuyama ( 1995) can flourish
even without unwavering allegiance. This reorientation
opens critical, empirical questions: Might markets that fea -
ture high systemic trust and robust reciprocity norms, but
lower interpersonal commitment, achieve greater dyna -
mism and performance? If firms consistently deliver value
that engenders gratitude and trust, do they outperform those
investing in cultivating allegiance? What conditions would
allow AI-driven systems to replicate the relational benefits
traditionally accrued through committed relationships?

should explore if and how varying norms shape human–
AI interactions, especially as AI becomes more culturally
attuned (Blake et al., 2015; Ma et al., 2017). Furthermore,
we recognize that advanced AI, with its deep cognitive and
emotional augmentation, arguably could alter the evolu -

dynamics demand scrutiny in this case, because if AI and
its designers create new dependencies, they would funda -
mentally alter how trust, gratitude, and unfairness function
in exchanges (Kozinets & Gretzel, 2021). In institutional
design settings, socio-economic frameworks must account
for how AI systems engage with ingrained human mecha -

AI-driven unfairness (Kaur et al., 2022), as well as whether
AI should mimic human relational signals to elicit more
cooperation.

to change. Their relationship strategies should be founded
