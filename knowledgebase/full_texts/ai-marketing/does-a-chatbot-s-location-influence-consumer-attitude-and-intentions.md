Abstract: Chatbots engage in real-time conversations with customers.
Moreover, they can collect data in a digital landscape that is increasingly
sensitive to privacy. The objective of this study was to help s trategic marketing
decision-makers better understand how and where chatbots can be  u s e d
effectively to gather consumer data. We analyse whether consume rs’ privacy
concerns influence their adoption. Furthermore, we invest if pr ivacy concerns,
and subsequently information disclosure, are significantly infl uenced by the
chatbot’s location. In an experimental study (N = 398) we compa red the
(in)direct effects of the same chatbot in two different locatio ns: Facebook and
its own retailer’s website. Our results show that interacting w ith a chatbot on
Facebook led to significantly lower attitudes towards chatbots and lower
intentions to use chatbots. Both relationships are sequentially  mediated via
higher privacy concerns and less comfort with information discl osure.
Companies therefore need to consider where to deploy their chatbots.
Keywords: chatbot; privacy concerns; information disclosure; Facebook;
Facebook messenger; retailer website; adoption behaviour; consu mer-firm
interaction.

1 Introduction
Online real-time consumer-firm interactions can be facilitated through chatbots, a piece
of software capable of engaging in human-like conversations thr ough the use of artificial
intelligence (Davenport et al., 2020; Moriuchi, 2021). Chatbots  engage in real-time
conversation with (potential) customers. They are increasingly used as a powerful tool for
customer relationship management and for providing information as commerce moves to
the internet (Steinhoff et al., 2019; Thomaz et al., 2020; Hasa n et al., 2021). In the near
future real-time online consumer-firm interactions will become the norm (Appel et al.,
2020). However, for chatbots to be effective prospective custom ers need to share
(personal) data. Privacy concerns may inhibit customers and, th erefore, render chatbots
less efficient. It is, therefore, relevant to analyse whether the chatbot’s location affects the
customers’ willingness to share (personal) data. In particular,  it is important to analyse if
there is a significant difference between the attitude and the intention to use a chatbot on
a social media platform (e.g., Facebook) compared to the retailer’s website.
In an increasingly privacy-centred online ecosystem where data collection and
privacy concerns are heavily scrutinised by consumers (Martin a nd Murphy, 2017),
embracing conversational commerce through chatbot usage has sev eral advantages.
Firstly, it bridges the communication gap between customers and  firms by providing an
effortless instant channel for customers to engage with, fulfil ling the behavioural shift
from ‘clicks’ to ‘conversations’ (Daugherty and Wilson, 2018; D eloitte, 2017; Thomaz
et al., 2020). Secondly, it presents an easily scalable channel  to provide personalised
recommendations to customers (Appel et al., 2020). Finally, eng aging in conversational

commerce can also be used as a convenient way to maintain consu mer data collection in
an increasingly private web ecosystem. Hence, chatbot usage for  conversational
commerce can be considered as a tool to ‘nudge’ consumers to vo luntarily share personal
data with companies (Thomaz et al., 2020). Whereas chatbots are , at least in theory, a
promising tool to engage with consumers and collect online cons umer data, research has
yet to demonstrate whether consumers’ privacy concerns influenc e their adoption of this
technology.
Before a chatbot can provide customer-tailored recommendations,  customers need to
disclose personal information during their interaction with the  bot. As a consequence,
interacting with a chatbot affects one’s privacy concerns as de monstrated by Ischen et al.
(2020). Privacy concerns, in turn, can influence consumers’ onl ine privacy-related
behaviour. Research shows that privacy concerns can negatively influence consumers’
willingness to disclose personal information (Martin and Murphy , 2017). Furthermore,
Acquisti et al. (2015) indicated that the online location can a ffect consumers’ level of
privacy concerns and self-disclosure. Hence, not only customers ’ privacy concerns but
also the online location (a social media platform or a retailer ’s website) can pose a
significant threat to information collection. Therefore, measur ing the influence of
consumers’ privacy concerns and their comfort with information disclosure in
interactions with chatbots is necessary to understand the facil itators and barriers for
adoption of this type of technology.
The objective of this study was to help strategic marketing dec ision-makers better
understand how and where to effectively deploy conversational a gents to collect
consumer data. Therefore, we constructed an experimental setting in two online locations
i.e., Facebook versus the retailer’s own website, where a ficti tious brand deploys a
chatbot. We selected Facebook because this platform is one of t he most popular places
for chatbot builders and Facebook is still the most downloaded app of all time (Threlfall,
2021; Kapoor et al., 2020).
To better understand the motivations of consumers to engage wit h conversational
agents and the extent to which they share information about the mselves during this
interaction, it was crucial to disentangle the interplay betwee n the chatbot’s location,
users’ privacy concerns and their comfort with information disc losure. Therefore, we
analysed if privacy concerns, and subsequently information disc losure, are significantly
influenced by the chatbot’s location and consequently influence consumers’ reactions and
intentions to adopt chatbots.
2 Literature review
2.1 Privacy concerns and online information disclosure
Aiello et al. (2020) define online privacy concerns as consumer s’ anxiety over the
handling of personal information. Ischen et al. (2020) define p rivacy concerns as the
degree to which a consumer is worried about the potential invasion of the right to prevent
the disclosure of personal information to others. Privacy conce rns, hence, act as a proxy
for understanding consumers’ feelings, attitudes and perception s of data privacy (Martin
and Murphy, 2017). Online consumers have increasingly become aware of data collection
practices. Thomaz et al. (2020) argues that consumers’ increase d concerns over data
privacy will lead to a hyper private web where consumers adopt a ‘private-as-default’

behaviour. This change in consumer behaviour poses a significant threat to current digital
marketing practices as marketers could see the golden age of op en data collection and
consumer micro-targeting coming to a halt in the near future (F errel, 2017; Martin and
Murphy, 2017). Online consumers’ emerging privacy concerned beh aviour will probably
force firms to shake-up their strategy, as open data collection  and consumer micro-
targeting will become more difficult. Conversational commerce m ay provide new
opportunities (Facebook, 2018; Thomaz et al., 2020).
Conversational commerce refers to the use of natural language i nterfaces (such as
messaging and chats) by consumers to interact in real-time with  organisations (Thomaz
et al., 2020). Chatbots in particular are a promising new technology capable of facilitating
conversational commerce management. In theory, chatbot usage so unds as a promising
tool to facilitate consumers’ data collection (Thomaz et al., 2 020), however, companies
should consider a privacy-centred approach to increase customer s’ intentions to use this
technology.
Privacy concerns are closely related to consumers’ willingness to disclose personal
information online (Martin and Murphy, 2017). According to Melu mad and Meyer
(2020) self-disclosure can be defined as voluntary communicatio n of thoughts, feelings,
and/or other information deemed to be private and that could ma ke the user vulnerable.
Research has indicated a negative relationship between privacy concerns and people’s
willingness to disclose personal data (Dinev and Hart, 2006; Ma rtin and Murphy, 2017):
the more concerned individuals are about their privacy, the less likely they are to share or
disclose personal information to a third party. Therefore, cons umers’ privacy concerns
can be a barrier for the disclosure of online information. This  suggests that it is important
to focus on privacy concerns within the scope of chatbot interaction.
Research on the role of privacy concerns on chatbot interaction s is scarce due to the
relative novelty of this type of AI technology. Brandtzaeg and Folstad (2018) were
among the first to investigate the topic of chatbot privacy. Ba sed on exploratory
interviews, they found that users are concerned about privacy w hen interacting with a
chatbot. Ischen et al. (2020) investigated the negative impact of privacy concerns on
information disclosure in a chatbot experiment. They found that  chatbot usage
significantly increased privacy concerns and negatively influen ced users’ comfort
regarding information disclosure.
2.2 The influence of chatbots’ location on users’ privacy concerns
Research showed that the online location can significantly infl uence users’ willingness to
disclose personal information (Acquisti et al., 2015). For example, Stutzman et al. (2012)
showed how exogenous environmental factors such as the nature o f the web interface
influenced users’ disclosure of personal information. Furthermo r e ,  w i t h i n  t h e  s c o p e  o f
chatbot research, Ischen et al. (2020) showed how privacy conce rns expressed by users
i n t e r a c t i n g  w i t h  c h a t b o t s  v a r i e d  w i t h  t h e  t y p e  o f  c h a t b o t .  A  h uman-like chatbot, for
example, led to significant lower privacy concerns compared to a machine-like chatbot.
Moreover, Ischen et al. (2020) found that compared to an intera ctive website, a machine-
like chatbot induced lower privacy concerns. Nowadays, companie s deploy chatbots in
two locations: on social media platforms or on their own website. However, it is not clear
how these different chatbot locations influence chatbot usage a n d  w h e t h e r  t h e
relationship between chatbot location and chatbot usage is medi ated through users’
privacy concerns.

Social media platforms have increasingly become an integral par t of our daily lives.
Facebook is the biggest social network worldwide with around 2. 85 billion monthly
active users in the first quarter of 2021 (Tankovska, 2021). Wh en Facebook wanted to
become profitable, it found a way to make its users earn money by partnering with
advertisers. Likewise, brands were keen to sell their products directly to consumers,
especially considering the unprecedented quantity/quality of da ta Facebook could
leverage. In fact, social media has become an essential marketi ng channel to engage with
existing and potential consumers. Brands have relied on this co nsumer data to support
their marketing efforts ever since (Petrescu and Krishen, 2018) . Although social media
can be seen as a stable and mature platform that will continue to grow, the reality shows a
more nuanced picture (Wilson, 2020). Recently, there has been a  shift in the way users
interact with social media having far-reaching implications for  social media marketing.
Xie et al. (2019) found a significant positive correlation betw een privacy protection
behaviours (PPB) and consumer awareness of data breaches. This is emphasised by
Hunter et al. (2020) who found that preference for PPB influenc es social media usage in
general. They also found a significant negative correlation bet ween intimacy
(measured as the desire to disclose personal information, but o nly to intimate and trusted
individuals) and social media usage. According to Alkire et al.  (2019) consequences of
PPB adoption are twofold. Firstly, it impacts users’ likelihood  to restrain their
engagement and, thus, reduce information disclosure on social m edia (for example, on
Facebook). Secondly, it can lead to avoidance of usage of speci fic functions and/or
participation in certain discussions. In this context, research  has yet to show whether
privacy-related behaviour on social media platforms influences users’ intentions to use
the chatbot functionality. Moreover, it is unclear whether the level of privacy concerns on
social media differs from the one seen on the world wide web. T he aim of our study is
therefore to close the gap in the chatbot literature regarding the interplay between a
chatbot’s location, consumers’ privacy concerns, and information disclosure.
The literature shows that consumers express privacy concerns wh en interacting with
chatbots. Moreover, research has shown that privacy concerns ca n be a barrier to
information disclosure. However, the influence of the chatbot’s  location is not clear. As
marketing decision-makers wish to embrace chatbots to maintain data collection in an
increasingly private ecosystem, it is crucial to understand whe ther customers’ privacy
concerns are location dependent (social media versus the retail er’s website), and whether
their privacy concerns negatively impact their comfort with dis closing personal
information. Therefore, the following hypothesis can be formulated:
H1 Interacting with a chatbot on social media leads to less com fort with information
disclosure than interacting with the same chatbot on a retailer’s website. This effect
of the chatbot’s usage location on information disclosure is mediated by users’ level
of privacy concerns.
Furthermore, research reported a negative effect of privacy con cerns on users’ intentions
to use chatbots (Rese et al., 2020) and their attitude toward c hatbots (Ischen et al., 2020).
However, the question whether consumers’ attitude towards chatb ots and their intentions
to use chatbots depend on the chatbot’s location (i.e., the web site in which the chatbot is
embedded) has, by the best of our knowledge, not been studied y et. Moreover, given the
strong relationship between privacy concerns and information di sclosure reported in the
literature, we can formulate our second hypothesis:

H2 Interacting with a chatbot on a social media platform leads to lower attitudes towards
chatbots and lower intentions to use chatbots than interacting with the same chatbot
on a retailer’s website. This effect of the chatbot’s location on users’ attitudes and
intentions is mediated by privacy concerns and users’ comfort with information
disclosure.

5 Discussion and conclusions
This study investigated the direct and indirect effects of a be auty store chatbot embedded
in two different online locations: Facebook Messenger and the c ompany’s own website.
Findings show that the chatbot in Facebook Messenger affected u sers’ privacy concerns
stronger than the chatbot on the retailer’s website. Further, o ur research results show a
significant mediating effect of privacy concerns on users’ comf ort with information
disclosure. Interacting with a chatbot in Facebook Messenger le d  t o  a  h i g h e r  l e v e l  o f
privacy concerns, which in turn affected users’ feelings to dis close personal information
through the chatbot. This finding confirms the relationship between privacy concerns and
self-disclosure reported in the literature (Ischen et al., 2020; Martin and Murphy, 2017).
Interestingly, despite the negative relationship between privac y concerns and
discomfort with disclosing personal information, we noted that all participants disclosed
personal information to the chatbots in our experiment. Hence, our participants
heightened privacy concerns, did not impede their self-disclosure behaviour. This finding
has been previously reported in the literature as the privacy p aradox: people argue they
are concerned about their privacy, but freely disclose personal  information online
(Aguirre et al., 2016; Bleier et al., 2020).
Our findings also revealed a direct effect of a chatbot’s locat ion on users’ attitudes
towards chatbots and their intentions to use chatbots. Besides, we found that these effects
were partially meditated through users’ privacy concerns and th eir comfort with
information disclose. Compared to interacting with a chatbot on  the retailer’s website
interacting with a chatbot on Facebook Messenger led to higher privacy concerns,
resulting in higher discomfort with information disclosure, and  consequently in lower

attitudes towards chatbots. The same mediating effect of privacy concerns and discomfort
with information disclosure was found for users’ intentions to use chatbots. Previous
studies found privacy concerns to be a direct predictor of peop le’s attitudes towards
chatbots and their chatbot usage intentions (Ischen et al., 202 0; Rese et al., 2020). Our
study found that (dis)comfort with information disclosure mediates this relationship.
Our study does not come without its limitations. First, it shou ld be noted that the
research was conducted based on women beauty products only. As a consequence, the
results described in this study cannot be generalised to other products. Therefore, further
research is needed to examine chatbot usage within the scope of  a  d i f f e r e n t  s e c t o r
(e.g., the insurance sector, food products) and gender. Secondl y, the study is limited by
the chatbot design itself. Hence, given that the developed chat bot acts as our independent
variable, it is reasonable to assume that its configuration may  be a limiting factor to our
results. However, we configured the chatbot on an existing exte rnal valid chatbot within
the beauty industry. The conversational agent asked a specific set of questions with
increasing order of intrusiveness and was configured to follow a predetermined flow.
These questions were also restricted to the Facebook GDPR rules . In this regard, future
research could determine if our findings hold with a differentl y configured chatbot on
different platforms. For example, the chatbot could ask a diffe rent set of questions and
users could be granted larger conversational freedom during the ir interaction with the
chatbot.
This research holds several practical implications for marketin g decision-makers.
Specifically, it provides direction on how to effectively deploy conversational agents with
regard to current privacy concerns. As demonstrated by this stu dy, interacting with a
chatbot on Facebook resulted in a higher levels of privacy conc erns compared to a
chatbot on a retailer’s website. If brands wish to encourage cu stomers to disclose
personal information through the use of chatbots, they should t ry to reduce the chatbot
users’ privacy concerns. Martin et al. (2017) demonstrated that  the level of transparency
(e.g., brands being transparent about the way they use the data  they collect) mitigates the
feelings of violation and trust (an antecedent to privacy conce r n s ) .  A s  s u c h ,  b r a n d s
embracing chatbot technology should try to mitigate consumers’ privacy concerns by
making clear what data they collect and for what purpose. Final ly, as Bleier et al. (2020)
indicated, companies can aim to alleviate consumer privacy conc erns by offering
increased ‘trade-in value’. This idea takes advantage of the pr ivacy calculus which posits
that “consumers weigh the risks associated with the release of personal information to a
firm against expected benefits” [Bleier et al. (2020), p.474]. A future research avenue
could therefore investigate whether the privacy calculus holds within the scope of chatbot
interactions.
From a managerial standpoint, managers should take the level of  privacy concerns
raised by the Facebook chatbot into account compared to the retailer’s website chatbot. In
this respect, we argue that privacy concerns should not be rega rded as a decisive point
when deciding on where to implement the chatbot, but rather as a point to be resolved.
Finally, marketing decision-makers should weigh the pros and co ns of both customer
communication locations. For instance, retailer’s website chatb ots don’t need to be
developed as part of Facebook’s application user interface (API ), which gives developers
and companies more freedom for features that they wish to imple ment. However, this
argument can be counterbalanced by the convenience of Facebook,  which represents a
suitable way for brands to reach customers through a channel th ey are already familiar
with.
