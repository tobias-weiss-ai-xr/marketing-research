AUTOMATED CORRECTIVE FEEDBACK
IN TEACHING BUSINESS WRITING

Abstract. This article presents a comprehensive study on the integration of
automated corrective feedback (ACF) systems into t he methodology of teaching
English for Specific Purposes (ESP) for students specialising in economics and
management. The relevance of this research stems from the ongoing transformation of
the educational paradigm in the digital age, which necessitates a shift from traditional
teacher-centred instructional models to autonomous learning environments where the
student acts as an active agent of their own development. Mastery of professional
discourse in today’s global business landscape requires more than just formal linguistic
accuracy; it demands a sophisticated internalised understanding of stylistic and
pragmatic nuances essential for effective corporate communication. The study
addresses the challenge of bridging the gap between mechanical error correcti on and
the strategic intent required in high-stakes professional documentation.
The empirical foundation of the research is based on data collected from 143
fourth-year undergraduate students at the Faculty of Management and Marketing of
Igor Sikorsky Kyiv Polytechnic Institute. The research methodology utilised a
comparative analysis across three distinct genres of business correspondence: a
business inquiry, a letter of complaint, and a cover letter. These genres were selected
specifically fo r their varied communicative demands regarding tone, structure, and
interpersonal sensitivity. The writing process was organised into two distinct stages:
the production of an independent initial draft, followed by an iterative revision process
assisted by artificial intelligence tools. The study distinguishes between the impact of
rule-based linguistic assistants, such as Grammarly, and generative AI models, such as
ChatGPT, on the overall quality and professional effectiveness of the final written
products.
The results of the study indicate that the implementation of automated systems
significantly mitigates the cognitive load on learners by delegating the technical
aspects of "linguistic hygiene" to algorithms. Quantitative findings confirm a notable
increase in grammatical precision scores, rising from an average of 3.6 to 4.8 on a five-
point analytic rubric. However, a persistent "pragmatic gap" was identified; in 82% of
observed cases, AI systems produced outputs that were formally correct but
pragmatically sterile, lacking the contextual flexibility and emotional intelligence

Problem statement. Teaching business writing in English for Specific Purposes
(ESP) programmes has gradually shifted from teacher-centred correction toward more
autonomous forms of learning. In traditional instructional settings, feedback is
predominantly provided by the instructor, which limits students’ active involvement in
revision processes and often positions error  correction as an external rather than
internalised activity.
At the same time, business communication requires not only grammatical
accuracy but also pragmatic adequacy, including appropriate tone, genre awareness,
and the ability to adjust language to specific professional contexts. This combination
of requirements makes business writing particularly sensitive to both linguistic and
discourse-level competence.
Recent research indicates that automated corr ective feedback can support the
development of linguistic accuracy in second language writing and reduce surface -
level errors in student production [1; 2; 3] . However, evidence shows that such
improvements do not automatically extend to pragmatic competenc e, which remains
dependent on contextual interpretation and communicative intent [4].
Analysis of recent research and publications. Automated corrective feedback
(ACF) has been widely discussed in applied linguistics as a tool that supports second
language writing development, particularly in relation to grammatical accuracy and
revision processes [1; 2] . Early research focused primarily on error detection and
immediate correction, while more recent studies have expanded this perspective toward
learner engagement and self-regulated writing development [5; 6].
Empirical findings confirm that ACF contributes to measurable improvements
in linguistic accuracy, especially at the level of grammar, vocabulary, and sentence
structure [3; 4] . Martínez-Carrasco and C habert [3] report that automated feedback
systems can significantly enhance competence development in L2 writing, while Li
and Meng [4] demonstrate comparable outcomes in studies contrasting automated and
teacher-provided feedback.

However, the effectiven ess of ACF becomes less stable when attention shifts
from surface-level accuracy to discourse organisation and pragmatic appropriateness.
Tools such as Grammarly are effective in identifying and correcting grammatical
errors, yet their contribution to genr e-sensitive writing remains limited [5]. This
limitation is particularly visible in ESP contexts, where writing quality depends on the
alignment between linguistic form and professional communicative intent.
The introduction of generative AI systems has f urther expanded the scope of
writing support tools. Recent studies suggest that AI-based applications can assist with
drafting and revision, but their outputs often remain structurally coherent while being
pragmatically generic [7; 8] . Building on this, re search into the integration of
generative AI in language learning highlights its potential as a supportive tool for
drafting business documents, focusing on initial student perceptions and reflective
practices [8]. Poole and Polio [9] emphasise that the integration of AI into task-based
writing environments raises important pedagogical questions regarding how such tools
should be positioned within classroom practice rather than treated as autonomous
writing agents.
From a cognitive perspective, research in dicates that the effectiveness of
automated feedback is closely related to learners’ cognitive load and processing
capacity [11]. Wang and Han [12], using a mixed-methods approach, demonstrate that
different types of feedback (teacher -based and automated) affect not only writing
accuracy but also cognitive and psychological dimensions of language learning. Their
findings suggest that learners’ engagement with feedback is shaped by both cognitive
effort and affective response, which becomes particularly relevant when integrating AI
tools into writing instruction.
In addition, digital literacy and algorithmic competence are increasingly
recognised as necessary conditions for effective use of AI-based systems in education
[13]. Learners with insufficient digit al literacy may rely on automated suggestions
without critical evaluation, which can limit the pedagogical value of such tools.
Comparative studies further indicate that while automated systems are efficient in
improving grammatical accuracy, teachers rema in essential in supporting pragmatic
development and stylistic refinement [6]. Wang and Han [12] also confirm that teacher
feedback continues to play a stabilising role in cognitive and psychological
engagement with writing tasks, especially when automated feedback is used alongside
human mediation.
Despite the growing body of research, a key gap remains. Current studies
provide limited empirical insight into how learners transition from automated
correction to independent pragmatic decision-making in business writing. In particular,
there is insufficient understanding of how students interpret AI-generated feedback in
task-based writing environments and how this interaction contributes to the
development of authorial autonomy in ESP contexts [14; 15].
The aim of this study is to examine the integration of automated corrective
feedback into ESP teaching of business writing with a focus on the development of

student autonomy. The study investigates how rule -based and generative AI tools
function at different  stages of writing development and how students respond to
automated feedback during revision. It also considers the extent to which AI-supported
writing contributes to linguistic accuracy and pragmatic adequacy in business
communication.
Methods and Results. The empirical part of this study is based on data collected
from 143 fourth -year undergraduate students of the Faculty of Management and
Marketing at Igor Sikorsky Kyiv Polytechnic Institute. The participants completed a
series of business writing task s within an ESP course focused on professional
correspondence. The tasks included a business inquiry, a letter of complaint, and a
cover letter, selected for their differing communicative demands in terms of tone,
structure, and pragmatic positioning.
The writing process consisted of two stages. At the first stage, students produced
independent drafts without external assistance. At the second stage, they revised their
texts using automated feedback tools. Rule -based systems (Grammarly) were used
primarily for grammatical and lexical correction, while generative AI (ChatGPT) was
introduced to support rewriting, restructuring, and stylistic adjustment. This stage of
the experiment builds upon the previously established framework for using generative
models as auxiliary tools in professional correspondence [8]. Students were instructed
to critically evaluate all automated suggestions and make final decisions based on
communicative intent and genre requirements.
Assessment of student performance was carried out using a five-point analytic
rubric covering grammatical accuracy, lexical appropriateness, coherence and text
organisation, and pragmatic adequacy. Each text was independently evaluated by two
instructors, with final scores agreed through discussion to ens ure consistency. Error
density was calculated as the number of linguistic errors per 100 words, including
grammatical, lexical, and syntactic deviations affecting clarity and correctness.
Data analysis reveals  a clear improvement in linguistic accuracy aft er the
introduction of automated feedback tools. Rule -based systems were particularly
effective in reducing surface-level errors, while generative AI contributed to improved
sentence variation and structural refinement. At the same time, the data show that
linguistic improvement did not automatically ensure pragmatic adequacy. In several
cases, texts remained formally correct but required further adjustment to match the
communicative expectations of specific business genres.
A recurring pattern concerns the  pragmatic limitations of automated feedback.
In tasks involving interpersonal sensitivity, such as the letter of complaint, AI -
generated suggestions often produced overly neutral or standardised formulations. This
led students to revise outputs manually, restoring contextual specificity and adjusting
tone. These findings align with earlier observations regarding student reflections on
AI-assisted drafting, where learners emphasized the need for human oversight to
maintain professional rapport [8].  Such behaviour indicates that automated feedback
was treated as a draft-level support mechanism rather than a final authority.

Interaction with AI tools also revealed differences in cognitive engagement.
Rule-based correction required limited cognitive effort and supported error awareness
at the surface level. Generative AI, by contrast, increased cognitive demands, as
students had to evaluate the stylistic and pragmatic suitability of suggested revisions.
This evaluative activity contributed to the deve lopment of decision -making skills in
writing and strengthened learners’ ability to critically interpret automated output.
Within the broader pedagogical trajectory, earlier stages of instruction were
characterised by the use of structured corrective tools that reduced cognitive overload
and supported the development of basic writing accuracy. At more advanced stages,
students gradually engaged with generative systems that required higher levels of
reflection and critical assessment. This progression can be summarised as a staged
model of AI integration in ESP writing instruction:
Stage 1  (initial development): rule -based corrective support focused on error
detection and linguistic accuracy (e.g., Grammarly)
Stage 2 (intermediate development): structured revision with increased attention
to coherence and sentence-level improvement
Stage 3  (advanced development): generative AI -supported writing with
emphasis on critical evaluation, pragmatic adjustment, and authorial decision-making
The findings suggest that automated feedback is most effective when it is
embedded within a staged pedagogical  framework. Linguistic accuracy improves
consistently with the use of AI tools, but pragmatic competence and authorial control
depend on the learner’s ability to critically evaluate and adapt automated suggestions
within specific communicative contexts.
Conclusions. The study investigated the role of automated corrective feedback
in ESP business writing, focusing on linguistic accuracy, pragmatic adequacy, and
learner autonomy. The results, based on fourth-year undergraduate students, show that
AI-based tools significantly improve surface -level accuracy and structural coherence
in written business correspondence.
A clear reduction in error density and an increase in rubric -based performance
scores confirm the effectiveness of both rule-based and generative feedback at the level
of linguistic form. However, pragmatic competence remains less responsive to
automated correction and depends on learners’ contextual judgment and interpretative
skills.
The findings suggest that automated feedback should be understood  as a
supportive mechanism rather than an autonomous source of writing quality. Its
pedagogical value lies in its integration within a staged instructional model, where rule-
based tools support early development of accuracy, and generative systems are
introduced at more advanced stages for revision and refinement.
From a pedagogical perspective, the use of AI tools contributes to the gradual
development of learner autonomy by shifting students from passive correction to active
evaluation of feedback. Ethical considerations are also essential, particularly regarding
transparency in the use of generative AI and the preservation of academic integrity in
student writing.
