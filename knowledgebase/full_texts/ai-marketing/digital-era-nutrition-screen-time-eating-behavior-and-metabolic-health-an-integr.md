Abstract
The rapid expansion of digital technology has transformed the contemporary nutrition environment,
influencing food exposure, food choice, ea ting behavior, dietary monitoring, and access to nutrition -
related interventions. Screen exposure has become an important behavioral factor because eating
increasingly occurs alongside television viewing, smartphone use, social media, gaming, and other
digital activities. This integrative review synthesizes current evidence concerning the relationships
among screen time, eating behavior, dietary quality, sedentary behavior, sleep, circadian factors, and
metabolic health, while also examining the emerging op portunities provided by digital nutrition
technologies. Evidence from experimental and systematic -review studies suggests that screen use
during eating may increase acute food intake through distraction and exposure to food -related cues,
whereas prolonged screen-associated sedentary behavior and disrupted sleep may contribute to obesity
and cardiometabolic risk through interconnected behavioral pathways. Digital food environments,
including social media, online food -delivery platforms, and digital marketing , may influence food
preferences and purchasing behavior, while appropriately designed digital interventions can support
nutrition education, self -monitoring, behavioral feedback, and healthier food choices. Emerging
applications of artificial intelligence , wearable technologies, continuous glucose monitoring, and
personalized nutrition may further enable individualized dietary assessment and feedback. However,
limitations related to measurement error, heterogeneity, engagement, algorithmic bias, privacy, digital
literacy, and unequal access remain important. Overall, digital-era nutrition should be understood as a
multidimensional interaction between technology, behavior, environment, and physiology. Future
research should prioritize longitudinal and causal designs, standardized assessment, external validation
of digital and AI -based tools, diverse populations, and health -equity considerations. Digital
technologies are most likely to improve metabolic health when they complement, rather than replace,
evidence-based nutrition science and professional care.

2. Digital Era and the Changing Nutrition Environment
The contemporary nutrition environment is increasingly shaped by digital technologies that influence
how individuals obtain nutrition information, interact with food, select foods, monitor dietary intake,
and engage with health -related behaviors. Smartphones, social media platforms, mobile applications,
wearable devices, online food -ordering systems, websites, and other digital platforms have expanded
the traditional food environment  into a broader digital food environment. In this environment, food -
related information, marketing, recommendations, purchasing opportunities, and social influences can
reach individuals continuously and across multiple settings [1]. Digitalization has also changed the way
individuals monitor and manage their dietary behavior. Mobile applications can support dietary intake
assessment, physical -activity monitoring, weight management, nutrition education, and self -
monitoring. A systematic review of mobile applications identified their potential utility for improving
nutrition-related behaviors and supporting dietary and lifestyle management [6]. Similarly, a systematic
review and meta -analysis of app -based interventions found that mobile interventions can infl uence
nutrition behaviors and selected nutrition -related health outcomes, although considerable variation
exists between interventions and outcomes [7]. The digital food environment is not limited to personal
health applications. Online food -ordering and retail platforms increasingly provide opportunities to
influence food selection at the point of purchase. Digital interventions incorporated into online f ood-
ordering systems have been investigated as a means of encouraging healthier food and beverage choices
[9]. Such interventions may include prompts, nutritional information, product positioning, default
options, or other behavior-change strategies. Consequently, digital technology can influence not only
what individuals know about nutrition but also the immediate context in which food choices are made.
Social media represents another important component of the modern digital nutrition environment.
Individuals are exposed to food -related content through advertisements, influencers, peers, online
communities, recipes, short-form videos, and other forms of user-generated content. This environment
can potentially influence food preferences, perceptions, purchasing intentions, and eating behavior. At
the same time, digital platforms may provide opportunities for nutrition education, peer support, and
dissemination of health -promoting messages. Therefore, the nutritional influence of digital media

3. Screen Time and Eating Behavior
Screen-based activities have increasingly become integrated into everyday eating occasions. Television
viewing, smartphone use, tablet use, computer use, video gaming, and other forms of digital
engagement may occur before, during, or after meals. Consequently, eating is often performed
simultaneously with activities that divert attention away from f ood. This has led to increasing interest
in screen exposure as a behavioral factor that may influence food intake, meal -related attention, and
eating patterns [13]. One of the proposed mechanisms is distracted eating. Eating normally involves
continuous se nsory and cognitive processing, including attention to the appearance, taste, smell,
texture, and quantity of food, as well as recognition of developing satiety. When attention is directed
toward television programs, digital games, social media, or other s creen content, awareness of the
eating episode may be reduced. A systematic review of laboratory -based studies involving children,
adolescents, and young adults reported that screen -based sedentary activities, even in the absence of
food advertising, were generally associated with increased dietary intake compared with non -screen
conditions. Proposed explanations included distraction, interference with physiological regulation of

analysis, and effects were also observed for intake at subsequent meals [14]. This suggests that distracted
eating may influence not only immediate consumption but potentially subsequent eating behavior as
well. Screen exposure may also influence dietary behavior through repeated association between
screens and eating. When individuals regularly consume snacks or meals while watching television,
using smartphones, or engaging in digital entertainment, screen exposure may become a contextual cue
for eating. Such learned associations may encourage eating in the  absence of physiological hunger.
Earlier experimental literature has proposed that screen use can function as a conditioned stimulus for
eating and may interfere with memory of food consumption, although these mechanisms require further
experimental confirmation [13].

4. Screen Time, Dietary Quality and Food Choice
Screen exposure may influence nutrition not only through the quantity of food consumed but also
through the types of foods selected and the overall quality of the diet. The digital environment provides
repeated exposure to food images, advertisements, bran ded products, recipes, influencers, restaurant
promotions, and peer -generated content. Consequently, food choice is increasingly influenced by a
combination of personal preference, social interaction, commercial communication, and digital
exposure. Food-related digital content may affect dietary behavior through visual, emotional, and social
food cues. Highly palatable foods are frequently presented through attractive images, short videos,
advertisements, promotional offers, and influencer content. Such exp osure may increase attention
toward particular foods and influence food preferences and purchasing intentions. A systematic review
examining social media and dietary behavior among children and adolescents found that most included
studies reported associat ions between social -media exposure and behaviors such as increased
consumption of unhealthy snacks and sugar-sweetened beverages, lower fruit and vegetable intake, and
breakfast skipping [10]. The same review identified experimental evidence suggesting tha t exposure to
unhealthy digital food images can increase neural responses in reward - and attention -related brain
regions, providing a potential biological pathway through which digital food cues may influence food
preference [10]. Food advertising represen ts an important component of this environment. Unlike
conventional advertising, digital food marketing can be personalized, interactive, continuous, and
integrated into entertainment and social communication. Individuals may encounter food promotion
while watching videos, browsing social media, playing games, or interacting with influencers. Recent
systematic-review evidence indicates that exposure to digital game -based and influencer food

5. Screen Time, Sedentary Behavior, Sleep and Circadian Rhythm
Screen time is closely intertwined with sedentary behavior because many common screen -based
activities, including television viewing, computer use, smartphone use, video streaming, and gaming,
are performed while sitting or reclining. However, screen time and sed entary behavior should not be
regarded as identical constructs. Sedentary behavior refers to waking behavior characterized by low
energy expenditure while sitting, reclining, or lying, whereas screen time refers specifically to time
spent in screen-related behaviors and may occur during either sedentary or physically active contexts
[21]. Thus, a particular screen activity may contribute to sedentary time, but the two measures are
conceptually distinct. High levels of sedentary behavior have been associated with several unfavorable
health outcomes in adults. An overview of systematic reviews found that greater sedentary behavior
was consistently associated with poorer physical health and that reducing or interrupting prolonged
sedentary periods may benefit b ody composition and cardiometabolic risk markers, although the
certainty of evidence varied from low to very low across outcomes [22]. Similarly, a systematic review
with a meta -analysis component based on objectively measured sedentary time found signific ant
adverse associations between greater sedentary time and fasting glucose, fasting insulin, triglycerides,
high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and waist circumference [23]. These findings provide an important
biological context for understanding why prolonged screen-based sedentary activities may be relevant
to metabolic health.
           Television viewing has historically been one of the most frequently investigated forms of screen-
related sedentary behavior. A dose-response meta-analysis involving more than one million participants
found that greater total sedentary behavior and television viewing were associated with increased risks
of overweight/obesity, type 2 diabetes, and hypertension. For each additional hour per day of total
sedentary behavior, the risk of type 2 diabetes increased by approximately 5%, while each additional
hour of television viewing was associated with an approximately 8% higher risk [24]. These findings
support an association between prolonged sedentary exposure and metabolic  disease, although the
observational nature of much of the evidence means that residual confounding and reverse causation
cannot be excluded. The relationship between screen exposure and physical activity is also relevant.
Sedentary behaviors may displace time that could otherwise be spent in light physical activity or
moderate-to-vigorous physical activity. A systematic review examining sedentary behavior and
physical activity in adults found inverse associations between different forms of sedentary behavi or
and physical activity, although the strength of these associations was generally weak to moderate [25].
                                   Therefore, the metabolic consequences of prolonged screen use may arise partly
from displacement of physical activity rather than from screen exposure itself. Sleep represents another
important pathway linking screen exposure with eating behavior and metabolic health. Screen use
during the evening may delay bedtime, reduce available sleep time, or interfere with sleep initiation. A
recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 21 cohort studies involving 548,338 participants found
that each additional hour of daily screen time was associated with approximately 3–5 minutes less total
sleep duration and a higher risk of  short sleep. Longer screen exposure was also associated with

sleep duration, and altered sleep -wake timing. These changes may subsequently affect appetite
regulation, food choices, meal timing, and energy balance. In this context, screen exposure may
influence metabolic health indirectly thr ough sleep rather than solely through its direct relationship
with eating behavior.

interconnected. Experimental and observational research has associated inadequate or disrupted sleep
with alterations in appetite regulation, food preference, energy intake, and metabolic processes. When
screen exposure contributes to delayed sleep or shorter sleep duration, it may therefore create conditions

eating behavior, and dieta ry intake related to metabolic syndrome [18]. Nevertheless, the evidence
remains heterogeneous, and the extent to which screen exposure independently contributes to these
metabolic pathways remains uncertain. Circadian rhythm provides an additional framewo rk for
understanding these relationships. Human metabolic physiology follows approximately 24 -hour
biological rhythms that regulate sleep -wake behavior, hormone secretion, glucose metabolism,
appetite, and energy utilization. Meal timing is also an important component of circadian organization.
When late -night screen use contributes to delayed sleep and irregular eating patterns, the resulting
mismatch between behavioral timing and endogenous circadian rhythms may potentially influence
metabolic regulation.
                                                  It is important, however, not to overstate this mechanism. A critical
systematic review of television time and cardiometabolic health in adults found that most associations
between television viewing and e ating, movement, affect, and sleep were weak or inconsistent. The
review concluded that there was insufficient strong evidence to establish that television viewing
consistently produces cardiometabolic effects through increased food intake, prolonged inactive sitting,
or subsequent sleep disturbance [19]. This contrasting evidence emphasizes the importance of
distinguishing plausible biological mechanisms from mechanisms that have been conclusively
demonstrated in humans. The relationship between screen exp osure, sedentary behavior, sleep, and
metabolic health can therefore be conceptualized as an interconnected behavioral pathway rather than
as a single causal chain. Increased screen exposure may promote prolonged sitting, displace physical
activity, extend evening activity, delay sleep, and alter the timing of meals and snacks. These behavioral
changes may subsequently interact with dietary intake and energy balance, potentially contributing to
obesity and cardiometabolic risk. At the same time, existing ev idence indicates substantial
heterogeneity, and factors such as age, socioeconomic status, physical activity, baseline health, type of
screen activity, and timing of exposure may modify these relationships.

7. Digital Nutrition Interventions and Opportunities for Metabolic Health Promotion
The increasing use of digital technology in everyday life also provides opportunities to counteract some
of the adverse nutritional consequences associated with excessive or poorly structured screen use.

wearable technologies, digital dietary assessment tools, personalized feedback systems, and other
technology-enabled approaches designed to modify dietary and lifestyle behaviors. These interventions
can incorpor ate self -monitoring, goal setting, behavioral feedback, reminders, nutrition education,
social support, and personalized recommendations. A systematic review of digital interventions among

important limitations.

systematic review of 100 studies evaluating nutrition-support applications found that most applications

cardiovascular conditions. Self -monitoring and coaching were among the most commonly

9. Challenges, Digital Health Inequity, and Ethical Considerations
Despite the considerable potential of digital nutrition technologies, their increasing integration into
dietary assessment, behavior modification,  and personalized nutrition also introduces important
methodological, ethical, and social challenges. Digital nutrition should not be regarded as inherently
beneficial simply because it provides greater quantities of data or more individualized
recommendations. The effectiveness and safety of digital interventions depend on the quality of the
underlying data, accessibility of the technology, usability of the intervention, validity of algorithms,
protection of personal information, and ability of individuals  to understand and appropriately use the
information provided. One major concern is the digital divide. Access to smartphones, reliable internet
connectivity, wearable devices, digital platforms, and technology-enabled healthcare is not distributed
equally across populations. Differences in income, education, geographic location, age, digital literacy,
and technological infrastructure may influence who can access and benefit from digital nutrition
interventions. An umbrella review of social inequalities in digital interventions targeting dietary and
physical-activity behaviors found that age and sex/gender were among the most frequently examined
inequality indicators, whereas income and education were much less frequently evaluated. This limited
assessment may result in an incomplete understanding of who benefits from digital interventions and
who may be left behind [41].

intake through mechanisms involving distraction and exposure to food -related stimuli [2]. More recent

interventions should simultaneously address dietary quality, physical activity, sleep, family routines,
and the broader home environment.
                                                                        Digital interventions can nevertheless be valuable tools
for supporting such multidimensional approaches. Digital self-monitoring of diet and physical activity
has been associated with improvements in weight loss, physical activity, and calorie intake in adults
with overweight or obesity. A systematic review and meta-analysis involving 12 randomized controlled
trials found that digital self -monitoring of both diet and physical activity produced significant
improvements in weight-related outcomes, moderate physical activity, and calorie intake, with tailored
interventions appearing more effective than non -tailored interventions [49]. Personaliz ation may
therefore be an important characteristic of effective digital nutrition interventions.  Technology-
delivered personalized nutrition interventions have demonstrated reductions in energy and fat intake
and modest improvements in fruit and vegetable consumption among adults with overweight or obesity.
However, the certainty of the evidence was rated low or very low, and the authors emphasized the need
for well -designed randomized trials with longer follow -up. This indicates that personalization is
promising but should not yet be assumed to provide superior long -term metabolic outcomes in all
populations [50].

engagement frequently declines over time. A systematic review examining strategies to improve
engagement with digital health interventions found that engagement strategie s may improve
intervention use and user experience, although the certainty of evidence was very low. Social support,
knowledge-related strategies, repetition and substitution, email communication, and SMS -based
approaches were among the strategies associat ed with improved engagement in selected analyses [51].

inequalities. Therefore, scalability should be evaluated together with affordability, accessibility, cultural
acceptability, privacy, and inclusiveness. The emerging evidence from fully digital lifestyle
interventions is encouraging but also illustrates the need for cautious interpretation. A recent systematic

heterogeneity remained, and benefits may vary across settings and populations [52]. This reinforces the
concept that digital delivery is a means of intervention rather than an intervention mechanism in itself.

11. Research Gaps and Methodological Priorities

11.1 Need for longitudinal and causal research
Most available evidence concerning the relationship between screen exposure, eating behavior , and
metabolic health remains observational or is derived from relatively short -term experimental studies.
Longitudinal studies with repeated measurements are needed to establish temporal relationships
between screen exposure, dietary behavior, sedentary behavior, sleep, and metabolic outcomes. Future
research should also incorporate appropriate methods for addressing confounding, reverse causation,
and time -varying exposures. Well -designed prospective cohort studies and randomized or quasi -
experimental interventions will be particularly important for determining whether prolonged or poorly
timed screen exposure independently contributes to unhealthy eating patterns and metabolic risk, rather
than merely occurring alongside other adverse lifestyle factors [19,25].

11.2 Standardization of screen-time and dietary exposure assessment
Future studies should use standardized definitions and objective or validated measures of screen
exposure, eating behavior, dietary intake, meal timing, and sedentary behavior. Screen exposure should
preferably be characterized according to duration, timing, context, device, and type of activity rather
than being treated as a single homogeneous exposure. Greater use of device -derived measurements,
accelerometry, wearable monitoring, and validated dietary assessment methods may reduce recall and
reporting bias. At the same time, researchers should recognize that digital measurement does not
automatically eliminate measurement error and that the validity, feasibility, and population applicability
of each assessment method require systematic evaluation [12,53].

11.3 Integration of behavioral, metabolic, and digital biomarkers
Research should increasingly integrate screen exposure, physical activity, sleep, dietary behavior, body
composition, glucose regulation, lipid profiles, and other metabolic indicators within the same
analytical framework. Wearable technologies, continuous glucose monitoring, and digital dietary
assessment may facilitate multidimensional monitoring under free -living conditions. Integration of
these data streams could help identify behavioral and physiological pathways linking digital lifestyles
with metabolic health and may allow researchers to distinguish the independent contribution of screen
exposure from overla pping factors such as sedentary behavior, sleep disruption, meal timing, and
dietary quality [30,39].

11.4 Need for diverse populations and digital health equity
Future evidence should include diverse socioeconomic, cultural, geographic, age, and educatio nal
populations. Digital interventions must address disparities in device access, digital literacy,
affordability, connectivity, language, cultural acceptability, and algorithmic performance to ensure
equitable benefits. Studies should routinely report dif ferential access, engagement, adherence, and
outcomes across population subgroups rather than assuming that digital interventions have uniform
effects. Particular attention should be given to populations that are historically underrepresented in
digital-health research, because inadequate representation may contribute to both unequal intervention
access and poorer algorithmic performance [41-46].

11.5 Improving intervention design and engagement assessment
Future interventions should combine evidence-based behavioral strategies with appropriately selected
digital tools. Intervention development should move beyond measuring immediate changes in screen
time or dietary behavior and should evaluate sustained engagement, adherence, acceptability, usability,
behavioral maintenance, and clinically meaningful outcomes over longer follow -up periods.
Engagement strategies such as social support, knowledge enhancement, repetition and substitution,
email communication, and SMS -based support may improve interaction with digital interventions,
although the certainty of evidence remains limited [51]. Future studies should therefore distinguish
between initial technology use and sustained engagement and should determine whether improved
engagement translates into durable improvements in dietary and metabolic outcomes.

11.6 Artificial intelligence, personalization, and validation requirements
AI-based dietary assessment and personalized nutrition systems require rigorous validation before
widespread clinical or population -level implementation. Evaluation should include accuracy of food
recognition, portion -size estimation, nutrient estimation, reproducibility, external validation, and
performance across different populations and dietary contexts. A recent systematic review of AI-based
dietary intake assessment identified promising accuracy for several nutrient-estimation approaches but
also found substantial variation in study settings and methodological quality, emphasizing the need for
larger studies and validation across div erse populations [53]. AI-enabled nutrition systems should also
incorporate transparent reporting, appropriate privacy protection, assessment of algorithmic bias, and
clear distinction between predictive performance and demonstrated clinical benefit [37,44,45]. Integration
of dietary, behavioral, wearable, and metabolic data should be clinically meaningful and scientifically
reproducible. Greater quantities of data should not be interpreted as inherently superior evidence.
Measurement error, incomplete datasets, differences in data quality, and algorithmic assumptions may
influence downstream predictions. Therefore, future precision -nutrition systems should demonstrate
not only technical performance but also reproducibility, external validity, interpretabili ty, and
meaningful improvement in patient or population outcomes [12,30,46].

11.7 Future interdisciplinary research priorities
Future research should integrate nutrition, physiology, behavioral science, digital health, sleep science,
epidemiology, and artificial intelligence. Standardized longitudinal frameworks combining behavioral
and metabolic outcomes will be essential for developing effective and equitable digital -era nutrition
strategies. Such frameworks should examine screen exposure together with dietary intake, meal timing,
physical activity, sleep, psychosocial factors, socioeconomic characteristics, and relevant metabolic
biomarkers. Interdisciplinary collaboration will also be necessary to ensure that technological
innovations remain consistent with established principles of nutrition science, clinical practice,
behavioral change, data governance, and public health [12,30,41].

12. Conclusion
Digital-era nutrition is shaped by the interaction of screen exposure, sedentary behavior, eating patterns,
sleep, the digital food environment, and emerging technologies. Screen use during meals may promote
distracted eating and increased food intake, while prolonged sedentary behavior and disrupted sleep
may contribute additional pathways linking digital li festyles with adverse metabolic outcomes [2,3,19].
At the same time, digital technologies offer important opportunities for nutrition education, dietary self-
monitoring, personalized feedback, behavioral support, and metabolic monitoring. Their effectiveness
depends on sustained engagement, appropriate personalization, scientific validation, data quality, and
equitable access [7,8,30,51].
