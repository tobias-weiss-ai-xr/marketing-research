Abstract
As sport business becomes increasingly platformized, trust is no longer a background
condition but a managerial resource shaped by algorithms, synthetic media, and
datafied fan relationships. This bibliometric and thematic review mapped 1,206
Scopus-indexed documents published from 2010 to 2025 to examine how artificial
intelligence, social media, branding, fan engagement, and digital integrity are
reorganizing sport management. Using Bibliometrix/Biblioshiny and VOSviewer,
the analysis identified rapid publication growth, a strong social media foundation,
expanding AI-related themes, and an intellectual structure dispersed across sport
management, communication, marketing, information systems, psychology, and
media studies. Findings suggest that algorithmic sport is not merely a technological
extension of sport business but an emerging trust-sensitive environment involving
authenticity, athlete identity, consumer data, platform power, misinformation,
and organizational legitimacy. Building on these patterns, the review develops an
integrative framework of trust in algorithmic sport business that connects algorithmic
conditions, stakeholder appraisals, trust, governance, and sport-business outcomes,
while offering testable propositions for future empirical research.

  1 Introduction
Sport business is increasingly shaped by platform infrastructures that do more than dis -
tribute sport content [ 25]. Platformization refers to the penetration of digital platform
infrastructures, economic processes, and governance logics into social and cultural sec -
tors, including how visibility and interaction are organized [ 58]. In sport, this means
that clubs, leagues, athletes, sponsors, media actors, and fans are no longer simply com -
municating through digital channels; they are operating within environments that rank
content, measure engagement, extract behavioral data, and condition what becomes

commercially visible. This shift matters because platform systems increasingly transform
sport audiences into data-generating publics whose interactions become part of broader
commercial and organizational processes [ 16]. Social media marketing scholarship has
likewise shown that digital platforms have become central to how organizations build
relationships, circulate content, and sustain consumer attention [ 6]. Then, for sport
organizations, platformized sport business is not merely a communication trend; it is a
managerial condition involving visibility, data dependence, audience access, and com -
mercial relevance [31, 49].
This platform shift also changes the meaning of fan engagement. Fans are still emo -
tionally attached spectators, but they are also measurable and segmentable users whose
likes, shares, comments, viewing habits, purchases, and platform movements become
managerial data. Studies on sport social media engagement show that platform type and
message characteristics can influence consumer engagement actions, suggesting that
fan interaction is increasingly shaped by the technical and communicative conditions of
platforms [3]. At the same time, artificial intelligence and marketing automation make
engagement more predictive, personalized, and commercially actionable by supporting
consumer profiling, automated communication, and data-driven marketing decisions
[14, 57]. This creates a deeper managerial tension because AI can improve sport market -
ing efficiency and fan experience, but it may also intensify opacity, surveillance, over-
personalization, and the commercialization of affective sport loyalty. As AI becomes
more embedded in sport industry labor, communication, and organizational decision-
making, fan engagement must be understood not only as relationship-building but also
as algorithmic relationship management [28, 30].
The same transformation is visible in athlete branding and sport identity work. Digital
platforms have expanded athletes’ ability to communicate directly with publics, culti -
vate visibility, engage in advocacy, and participate in commercial identity-building, yet
this visibility also brings new forms of exposure and risk. Research on athlete visibility
in the name, image, and likeness era shows that branding opportunities are accompa -
nied by heightened scrutiny, safety concerns, and reputational vulnerability [12]. Athlete
advocacy on social media also depends on parasocial interaction and audience involve -
ment, showing that athlete identity is increasingly negotiated through affective and plat -
form-mediated relationships [ 15]. For women athletes and marginalized sport figures,
visibility can produce empowerment but also intensified digital labor, gendered judg -
ment, racialized discourse, and unequal forms of recognition [ 10, 33]. In this context,
authenticity becomes a managerial concern because commercialization, influencer cul -
ture, fan expectations, and platform metrics all shape whether athlete communication is
perceived as genuine or strategically manufactured [51, 70].
These developments make digital trust central to algorithmic sport. AI, content mod -
eration, synthetic media, and deepfakes complicate the ability of sport audiences to
determine whether digital content is authentic, manipulated, automated, sponsored, or
strategically amplified. AI ethics scholarship has identified transparency, responsibility,
fairness, and accountability as recurring concerns in the governance of intelligent sys -
tems [37]. Content moderation scholarship further shows that platform governance is
shaped by questions of scale, judgment, automation, and institutional responsibility [27].
These concerns become more urgent in synthetic media environments because deep -
fakes and AI-enabled disinformation can undermine trust in mediated evidence, blur the

boundary between authentic and fabricated communication, and create new risks for
public credibility [18, 69]. For sport management, this is not only a media problem; it is
an organizational problem involving athlete likeness, brand integrity, sponsorship cred -
ibility, misinformation response, platform accountability, and the protection of fan trust
[45, 60].
The study is theoretically informed by complementary traditions concerned with trust,
technological reliance, relational exchange, and platformized mediation. Organizational
trust theory positions trust as consequential where actors accept vulnerability under
conditions of dependence and incomplete control [48], while trust-in-automation schol-
arship extends this problem to technological systems whose complexity requires users
to determine when reliance is appropriate [ 43]. Research on human trust in AI further
shows that trust develops through both system characteristics and stakeholder interpre -
tations of those characteristics, including judgments surrounding performance, trans -
parency, and representation [ 29]. Within commercial relationships, commitment-trust
theory also establishes trust as central to sustaining durable exchanges between organi -
zations and stakeholders [ 52]. These perspectives intersect with platformization schol -
arship, which demonstrates how digital infrastructures increasingly organize visibility,
interaction, data, and market relations [ 55, 58]. Rather than imposing a single pre-exist -
ing model on a multidisciplinary corpus, these traditions provide the theoretical basis
for examining trust as a connecting problem across AI, synthetic media, platformized
sport business, fan relationships, athlete identity, and organizational legitimacy.
Despite the growing relevance of these developments, the literature remains dispersed
across sport marketing, sport communication, information systems, AI ethics, media
studies, sponsorship, athlete branding, and digital business. Recent work on Sports
Industry 5.0 has already argued that technological innovation in sport must be consid -
ered alongside sustainability and human-centered responsibility rather than efficiency
alone [46]. Sponsorship research likewise continues to demonstrate the importance of
brand image and purchase intention within sport business, although these outcomes are
increasingly shaped by digitally mediated visibility and consumer interpretation [61]. Yet
existing research on social media engagement, digital branding, AI adoption, platform-
mediated communication, synthetic media, and sport integrity has largely developed
through parallel conversations, leaving the relationships among technological media -
tion, stakeholder vulnerability, authenticity, datafication, and trust insufficiently inte -
grated. This fragmentation limits the field’s capacity to understand algorithmic sport as
more than a collection of emerging technologies. A synthesis is therefore needed that
identifies how the intellectual structure of the field has developed, where established
and emerging themes intersect, and which conceptual relationships remain undertheo -
rized. In this regard, this study addresses that need through a combined bibliometric
and thematic review of scholarship published from 2010 to 2025. Its contribution lies in
mapping the conceptual, collaborative, and intellectual development of algorithmic and
platformized sport-business research, integrating previously separated research streams
around trust as a shared relational and managerial concern, and developing an integra -
tive framework with empirically testable propositions connecting algorithmic sport
conditions, stakeholder appraisals, trust, governance, and sport-business outcomes.
Drawing on organizational trust, trust in automation and AI, relationship marketing,
and platformization scholarship, the review provides a theoretical basis for examining

2 Methods
2.1 Research design
This study employed a bibliometric research design integrating science mapping, the -
matic evolution analysis, and interpretive thematic synthesis to examine scholarship on
algorithmic sport and platformized sport business published from 2010 to 2025. The
review covered research related to AI, generative AI, machine learning, synthetic media,
digital trust, authenticity, sport marketing, fan engagement, athlete branding, sport
communication, sponsorship, and platform-based sport business. Bibliometric analysis
provided a systematic field-level account of publication growth, citation patterns, lead -
ing sources, influential authors, institutional contributions, international collaboration,
conceptual networks, and intellectual structures within the corpus [ 20, 36, 44]. Science
mapping further visualized relationships among authors, countries, keywords, sources,
and cited sources, while thematic evolution analysis traced the development and con -
tinuity of major topics across the study period. These quantitative approaches are well
suited to identifying the structure and trajectory of a large body of literature, although
network prominence and bibliographic relationships alone provide limited insight into
the substantive meaning of the patterns they reveal.
To address this limitation, the bibliometric evidence was complemented by inter -
pretive thematic synthesis of the mapped structures and emerging research streams.
This enabled recurring and converging concerns surrounding AI, platformization, fan
engagement, authenticity, synthetic media, digital integrity, and trust to be examined
beyond their frequency or network position. Hence, the combined approach linked
structural mapping with conceptual interpretation. Bibliometric analysis established
where the field is concentrated, connected, and evolving, while thematic interpreta -
tion clarified what these patterns signify for contemporary sport-business scholarship
and provided the basis for the integrative framework developed in the Discussion. The
study followed a criterion-based bibliometric corpus delimitation procedure rather than
a PRISMA-based systematic review protocol because screening was undertaken to con -
struct a thematically coherent corpus for bibliometric and science-mapping analysis
rather than to conduct a study-level evidence synthesis.
2.2 Data source and search strategy
Table 1 summarizes the search strategy and inclusion parameters used in the study.
Bibliographic data were retrieved exclusively from Scopus because it provides broad
multidisciplinary coverage and standardized metadata suitable for bibliometric and
science-mapping analysis, including titles, abstracts, Author’s Keywords, affiliations,
source titles, citations, references, document types, and publication years [ 9]. A single-
database strategy was adopted to maintain consistency in metadata structure, citation
indexing, document classification, and source identification across the corpus. Although
combining Scopus with Web of Science can broaden retrieval, differences in database
coverage, indexing conventions, citation counts, duplicate records, and metadata fields
would require additional harmonization before network construction and comparative
analysis. Therefore, Web of Science was not incorporated into the present corpus. This

2.3 Eligibility criteria and corpus delimitation
Records were retained when their substantive focus connected digital or algorithmic
technologies with sport management, sport business, sport marketing, sport com -
munication, fan engagement, athlete branding, sponsorship, sport media, organiza -
tional practice, digital trust, authenticity, misinformation, or related commercial and
governance concerns. The initial Scopus search yielded 2,520 records. Because the
broad search strategy also retrieved documents in which sport-related or digital terms
appeared only incidentally, the corpus underwent manual relevance screening based on
titles, abstracts, Author’s Keywords, source titles, and available bibliographic metadata.
Records were excluded when their primary focus concerned sports medicine, physical
activity promotion, biomechanics, injury surveillance, coaching analytics, technical per -
formance prediction, PE technology, general computer science, non-sport social media,
or algorithmic applications without substantive relevance to sport management, busi -
ness, media, branding, fan engagement, integrity, trust, or organizational concerns.
Duplicate and incomplete records and metadata inconsistencies were also checked dur -
ing corpus cleaning. Following manual relevance screening and corpus cleaning, 1206
documents were retained across 542 sources, comprising 1002 articles, 175 conference
papers, and 29 reviews. Because screening was conducted as a criterion-based corpus
delimitation procedure, exclusion counts were not recorded separately for each screen -
ing reason and were therefore not retrospectively reconstructed.
2.4 Data cleaning and preprocessing
The retrieved records were cleaned prior to analysis to improve topical precision, meta -
data consistency, and network interpretability. Duplicate entries, incomplete records,
formatting inconsistencies, and off-topic documents were checked and removed through
manual screening of titles, abstracts, author keywords, source titles, and available meta -
data. Records were excluded when they focused mainly on sports medicine, physical
activity, biomechanics, injury surveillance, coaching analytics, performance prediction,
physical education technology, general computer science, non-sport social media, or
algorithmic studies without clear relevance to sport management, sport business, media,
branding, fan engagement, digital trust, integrity, or organizational concerns. Author’s
Keywords were also standardized using a VOSviewer thesaurus file to merge equivalent
terms, remove noisy or overly generic keywords, and improve the clarity of the keyword
co-occurrence, bibliographic coupling, and co-citation maps.
2.5 Bibliometric analysis
The cleaned dataset was analyzed using Bibliometrix/Biblioshiny for descriptive bib -
liometric indicators, trend-topic analysis, and thematic evolution mapping [ 7, 8], while
VOSviewer was used for science mapping and network visualization [ 13]. Descrip -
tive analysis summarized publication growth, source productivity, author citation vis -
ibility, institutional contributions, document types, citation patterns, and collaboration
indicators. Science mapping was conducted through author co-authorship, country

collaboration, Author’s Keywords co-occurrence, source-level bibliographic coupling,
and cited-source co-citation analyses to examine the collaborative, conceptual, and
intellectual structure of the corpus. Trend-topic and thematic evolution analyses were
used to trace how the literature shifted across the study period, particularly from early
social media, sport marketing, and digital fan engagement toward artificial intelligence,
platformized sport business, fan engagement, athlete branding, sport integrity, synthetic
media, and digital trust. For thematic evolution, the corpus was divided into four time
slices: 2010–2014, 2015–2019, 2020–2022, and 2023–2025.
2.6 Network thresholds and visualization parameters
Network thresholds were determined through iterative inspection of the resulting net -
work structures rather than by applying a single arbitrary cut-off across analyses. Dif -
ferent threshold levels were examined during map construction to assess node density,
connectivity, peripheral items, and overall interpretability. Lower thresholds retained
substantially more low-frequency or weakly connected nodes, producing visually con -
gested networks with numerous peripheral items that contributed limited interpretive
value. Conversely, increasingly restrictive thresholds reduced network coverage and
risked excluding meaningful relationships within the corpus. Therefore, the final thresh -
olds represented a deliberate balance between network coverage and interpretive clarity.
These were set at a minimum of 4 documents for author co-authorship, 7 occurrences
for Author’s Keywords co-occurrence, 6 documents for source-level bibliographic cou -
pling, and 20 citations for cited-source co-citation analysis. The thresholds should be
understood as visualization and analytical parameters rather than indicators of substan -
tive importance, and items falling below them were not considered irrelevant to the
field. Across the iterative inspection process, the selected settings provided sufficiently
connected and interpretable structures while limiting excessive peripheral noise. In each
visualization, node size represented relative prominence, link thickness indicated rela -
tionship strength, and colors represented clusters of closely related items.
3 Results
3.1 Descriptive profile and publication growth
The final dataset included 1,206 Scopus-indexed documents on algorithmic sport, digital
trust, artificial intelligence, synthetic media, and platformized sport business published
from 2010 to 2025 across 542 sources, with an annual growth rate of 25.24% and an aver-
age of 15.97 citations per document. The corpus was predominantly composed of arti -
cles (n = 1002), followed by conference papers ( n = 175) and reviews ( n = 29), indicating
that the field is mainly journal-driven while still reflecting contributions from confer -
ence-based digital, technological, and communication scholarship. Authorship patterns
showed 2657 contributing authors, 226 single-authored documents, an average of 2.94
co-authors per document, and 25.95% international co-authorship, suggesting a moder -
ately collaborative and increasingly networked research area. As shown in Fig.  1, annual
scientific production increased substantially across the period, from only 8 documents
in 2010 to 108 in 2020, before rising further to 172 in 2024 and peaking at 234 in 2025.
This upward trend suggests growing scholarly attention to sport as a digitally medi -
ated and algorithmically shaped business environment, particularly in relation to arti -
ficial intelligence, platform-based fan engagement, sport media, branding, authenticity,

4 Discussion
The discussion interprets the bibliometric and thematic patterns identified in the corpus
rather than treating them as evidence of causal or managerial relationships. Publication
structures, keyword associations, thematic evolution, and intellectual clusters indicate
where scholarly attention has concentrated and how concepts have become connected
across the literature. Thus, claims concerning governance, power, stakeholder vulner -
ability, and managerial responsibility are presented as theoretically informed interpre -
tations of these patterns and the literature they represent, not as relationships directly
demonstrated by bibliometric analysis.
4.1 Platformization as the new business infrastructure of sport
The results suggest that the digital turn in sport should no longer be treated as a simple
migration from traditional media to social media. What appears across the corpus is a
deeper structural shift in which sport organizations, athletes, sponsors, media actors,
and fans increasingly operate through platform infrastructures that organize visibility,
interaction, data extraction, and commercial value. Earlier sport social media reviews
already showed that digital platforms altered how sport organizations cultivate rela -
tionships and co-create value with consumers, but the present corpus indicates that
this relationship has matured into a broader business condition rather than a narrow
communication strategy [ 1, 2, 24]. Platformization scholarship helps explain this shift
because platforms do not merely host sport content; they structure how sport content
circulates, how audiences are measured, how attention is monetized, and how organiza -
tional relevance is sustained in competitive digital environments [32, 38, 55].
This interpretation is important because sport management cannot continue to treat
social media as an optional promotional tool placed at the edge of organizational prac -
tice. In platformized sport business, communication, marketing, sponsorship activation,
fan engagement, athlete branding, and media distribution become interdependent man -
agerial activities shaped by platform metrics, recommendation systems, and audience
analytics. The managerial consequence is that sport organizations must now compete
not only through athletic performance, event quality, or brand tradition, but through
their capacity to remain visible, searchable, shareable, and emotionally resonant within
platform-controlled environments. This complicates the meaning of “management” in
sport because organizational effectiveness increasingly depends on managing relation -
ships with both human stakeholders and non-human platform systems that mediate
public attention, commercial exposure, and reputational value [4, 54, 63, 67].
4.2 Algorithmic fan engagement and the datafied sport consumer
The prominence of fan engagement, consumer behavior, online communities, social
media, and artificial intelligence points to a deeper transformation in how sport orga -
nizations understand and manage their publics. Fan engagement is no longer only a
relational or affective construct grounded in loyalty, identification, passion, or commu -
nity belonging; it is increasingly enacted through digital traces, engagement metrics,

Page 19 of 29
Lobo Discover Artificial Intelligence           (2026) 6:1079
predictive analytics, recommendation systems, and automated content environments.
This does not make older relational theories irrelevant, but it changes the conditions
under which relationships are formed and sustained. Sport fans are now simultaneously
emotional supporters, media participants, data producers, brand amplifiers, and algo -
rithmically profiled consumers whose interactions can be measured, segmented, mon -
etized, and strategically targeted [2, 24, 54, 63].
At a deeper level, the convergence of engagement, datafication, and algorithmic rela -
tionship management also brings questions of power into the interpretive frame. When
sport organizations use AI, machine learning, social media analytics, and personalized
marketing tools, they are not merely responding to fan preferences; they are also shaping
the conditions through which fans encounter sport, express attachment, and participate
in digital communities. Marketing scholarship has already shown that AI can reshape
consumer behavior, segmentation, personalization, and service interaction, while con -
sumer research warns that AI-enabled systems also involve data capture, classification,
delegation, and new forms of consumer vulnerability [ 17, 34, 53, 59]. Applied to sport,
this means that fan engagement must be interpreted not simply as evidence of con -
nection, but as part of a datafied relationship in which emotional attachment becomes
commercially actionable. These patterns suggest a managerial tension between engage -
ment optimization and concerns surrounding transparency, fairness, consent, and fan
autonomy.
Recent fan-engagement scholarship further demonstrates that engagement is mul -
tidimensional and cannot be reduced to interaction volume alone. Huettermann et al.
[35] identified both positive and negative components and outcomes of fan engagement
in professional team sport, underscoring that stronger interaction does not necessarily
translate into uniformly beneficial organizational relationships. More recent work on
athlete branding similarly shows that different forms of social-media content can gen -
erate distinct engagement behaviors, with performance- and non-performance-related
athlete content eliciting different patterns of likes and comments [ 47]. These findings
reinforce the need to interpret algorithmically mediated engagement through the quality
and meaning of stakeholder relationships rather than through platform metrics alone.
4.3 Brand visibility, athlete identity, and the problem of authenticity
The findings also suggest that athlete branding and sport branding are no longer confined
to image management, endorsement value, or sponsorship visibility. In platformized
sport business, athlete identity becomes a continuously produced digital asset, shaped
through images, captions, engagement rates, follower communities, algorithmic vis -
ibility, brand collaborations, and audience interpretation. Studies on Instagram, athlete
branding, Olympic athlete endorsement value, and NIL-related athlete influence show
that athletes increasingly function as hybrid figures who are simultaneously performers,
brands, influencers, communicators, and commercial partners [ 4, 11, 21, 26, 65]. The
literature increasingly situates athlete value not only in sport achievement but also in
digitally mediated visibility, audience engagement, and perceptions of authenticity.
However, this same environment creates a serious authenticity problem. The more
athlete identity becomes mediated by platforms, brand partnerships, audience metrics,
and AI-assisted content production, the more difficult it becomes to distinguish between
personal expression, strategic self-branding, commercial performance, and synthetic

representation. This is especially important in the age of generative AI and synthetic
media, where likeness, voice, image, and message can be replicated, manipulated, or cir -
culated outside the athlete’s full control. Deepfake and synthetic media scholarship has
already warned that AI-generated content can destabilize trust by creating uncertainty
about what is real, who authored a message, and whether audiences can rely on medi -
ated evidence [ 39, 50, 66, 68]. From a sport-management perspective, this convergence
positions authenticity as more than a branding concern and connects it conceptually
with athlete rights, sponsorship credibility, reputational protection, and public trust.
4.4 Digital trust as an emerging sport management responsibility
The growing visibility of AI, generative AI, machine learning, misinformation, gam -
bling, sport integrity, and betting-related themes suggests that governance concerns
are becoming more prominent within the literature on algorithmic sport. The manage -
rial question is no longer limited to how digital technologies can increase engagement,
improve marketing, or personalize fan experiences. The more important question is how
sport organizations can govern digital systems that may produce opacity, manipulation,
reputational harm, misinformation, consumer vulnerability, and integrity risks. AI and
algorithmic systems are often adopted for efficiency, prediction, personalization, and
scale, but they also require explainability, accountability, and trust because users may
resist or distrust algorithmic decisions when systems appear opaque or when they make
visible errors [19, 22, 23, 62].
This governance issue becomes sharper in sport because trust is not peripheral to the
product; it is part of the product itself. Fans must trust the authenticity of athlete com -
munication, the fairness of competition, the credibility of sport media, the integrity of
betting environments, and the legitimacy of organizational decision-making. Research
on sport corruption, betting integrity, and gambling advertising shows that sport organi-
zations already operate within fragile trust environments where commercial incentives,
stakeholder interests, and integrity risks can collide [40, 41, 42]. The literature also raises
the possibility that integrity and trust concerns may extend beyond human misconduct
to automated content, synthetic media, data-driven persuasion, platform amplification,
and opaque recommendation systems. Taken together, these developments support
positioning digital trust as an emerging area of sport-management inquiry spanning
marketing, communication, governance, integrity, sponsorship, athlete protection, and
fan welfare.

The bibliometric and thematic structures identified in this review reveal a field con -
nected by common concerns but theoretically dispersed across sport management,
communication, marketing, information systems, media studies, and adjacent disci -
plines. AI, platformization, fan engagement, athlete branding, synthetic media, mis -
information, authenticity, and sport integrity frequently develop as parallel research
streams even though they increasingly intersect within the same digitally mediated busi -
ness environment. Trust provides a productive point of convergence because it becomes
consequential under conditions of uncertainty, dependence, and vulnerability. Mayer et
al. [48] established vulnerability as central to organizational trust, while Lee and See [ 43]
positioned trust as critical to appropriate reliance on automation when technological

4.7 Trust appraisal mechanisms
Authenticity, transparency, fairness, accountability, and data responsibility provide the
appraisal mechanisms through which algorithmic sport conditions may become trust-
relevant. These concepts are not proposed as validated dimensions of algorithmic trust
but as distinct judgments that clarify why technologically similar practices may gener -
ate different stakeholder responses. Authenticity concerns whether identities, messages,
and representations are regarded as genuine and attributable to the actors they appear
to represent, an increasingly fragile judgment when athlete likeness, voice, image, and
communication can be synthetically generated or manipulated [ 39, 50, 66, 68]. Trans-
parency concerns whether technological mediation is sufficiently visible and intelligible,
while fairness concerns whether algorithmic processes distribute visibility, recommen -
dations, opportunities, or consequences in ways perceived as legitimate. Accountability
addresses whether responsibility can be located when automated systems produce ques -
tionable outcomes, and data responsibility concerns whether stakeholder information is
collected, inferred, shared, and monetized within acceptable boundaries. Transparency,
fairness, responsibility, and related principles recur prominently across AI ethics schol -
arship, while explainability has been linked to user judgments of algorithmic trust and
acceptance [37, 62]. Therefore, trust depends not simply on whether an algorithmic sys -
tem functions, but on how its operation is interpreted within relational expectations sur-
rounding legitimacy, authenticity, intelligibility, and responsibility [29, 43].
4.8 Stakeholder trust
Stakeholder trust occupies the center of the framework because algorithmic sport busi -
ness creates relationships in which actors depend on organizations, technologies, plat -
forms, and mediated representations that they cannot fully control. Following Mayer et
al. [48], trust becomes especially salient where dependence entails vulnerability to deci -
sions or actions beyond the trustor’s direct control, although algorithmic sport expands
this vulnerability beyond conventional interpersonal and organizational relationships.
Fans may depend on recommender systems, personalization mechanisms, and organi -
zational data practices; athletes may depend on clubs, platforms, sponsors, and tech -
nology providers to safeguard their identities and likenesses; sponsors may depend on
the credibility and reputational safety of digitally mediated sport properties; and sport
organizations increasingly rely on platform infrastructures whose internal rules, ranking
processes, and data practices remain partly inaccessible. Existing sport scholarship has
established the relational importance of fan attachment, interaction, identification, and

digitally mediated engagement [ 2, 24, 63], but algorithmic mediation complicates these
relationships by inserting automated systems and platform infrastructures between
organizations and their publics. Trust is consequently conceptualized as a stakeholder
judgment produced within specific configurations of technological mediation, depen -
dence, and vulnerability, rather than as a generic attitude toward digital technology.
4.9 Sport-business outcomes
Trust carries potential consequences for the relational, commercial, and institutional
interests through which sport organizations create and preserve value. Relationship
marketing theory has long positioned trust as central to successful and durable exchange
relationships rather than as a peripheral consumer attitude [ 52]. Within algorithmic
sport business, relational outcomes may include engagement, loyalty, and continued
participation, while commercial outcomes include brand credibility, sponsorship con -
fidence, purchase intention, and related marketplace responses. Institutional con -
sequences extend to reputation, organizational legitimacy, and perceived integrity.
Sport-business research already demonstrates the organizational significance of digital
relationship marketing, fan engagement, social media branding, athlete branding, and
platform-mediated consumer interaction [ 1, 4, 21, 56, 67]. The framework adds trust as
a mechanism through which these established outcomes may become sensitive to algo -
rithmic mediation. For example, high engagement cannot automatically be equated with
a durable relationship when interaction occurs through practices perceived as opaque or
manipulative, while athlete visibility and sponsorship exposure may become commer -
cially precarious when synthetic representation or misinformation weakens confidence
in authenticity. Trust thus connects algorithmic practices with sport-business value
without reducing technological success to reach, engagement metrics, or automation
efficiency.
4.10 Algorithmic governance and contextual conditions
Algorithmic governance operates across the framework rather than appearing only after
technological or reputational problems emerge. Disclosure, verification, human over -
sight, data governance, and likeness protection can alter how stakeholders encounter
and appraise algorithmically mediated sport. Disclosure may clarify the use of AI-gen -
erated content, verification may strengthen confidence in athlete and organizational rep-
resentations, human oversight may preserve avenues for responsibility and contestation,
data governance may establish clearer boundaries around profiling and personalization,
and likeness protection becomes increasingly consequential as generative technolo -
gies expand the capacity to reproduce athlete identities without direct participation or
consent. These practices correspond with broader concerns surrounding transparency,
fairness, responsibility, and accountability in AI governance [ 37], although governance
should not be assumed to produce trust automatically. Appropriate reliance requires
calibrated rather than unconditional trust in technological systems [ 43]. Platform power
and information asymmetry further shape these relationships because digital platforms
can exercise substantial influence over visibility, participation, data access, recommen -
dation, and monetization while users and sport organizations often possess less knowl -
edge about the infrastructures governing those outcomes [ 16, 55, 58]. Hence, trust in
algorithmic sport business is likely to vary across stakeholders, platforms, markets,

regulatory environments, and degrees of technological dependence rather than operat -

4.11 Propositions for future empirical testing
The proposed framework translates the interpretive synthesis into relationships that can
be examined empirically without treating the bibliometric findings as evidence of causal
effects. The propositions integrate organizational trust, trust in automation, AI trust,
relationship marketing, platformization, and sport-business scholarship while leaving
their direction, magnitude, and boundary conditions open to empirical evaluation.
P1. Greater perceived opacity in algorithmically mediated sport interactions will be
associated with lower stakeholder trust.
P2. Perceived authenticity will mediate the relationship between exposure to synthetic
or AI-generated sport content and stakeholder trust.
P3. Perceived transparency, fairness, accountability, and responsible data practices
will be positively associated with stakeholder trust in algorithmically mediated sport
environments.
P4. Higher stakeholder trust will be positively associated with relational, commercial,
and institutional sport-business outcomes, including engagement, loyalty, brand cred -
ibility, sponsorship confidence, reputation, and organizational legitimacy.
P5. Algorithmic governance practices, including disclosure, verification, human over -
sight, data governance, and likeness protection, will attenuate the negative relationship
between perceived algorithmic risk and stakeholder trust.
P6. The relationship between algorithmic conditions and stakeholder trust will
become stronger under conditions of greater platform dependence and information
asymmetry.
Collectively, these propositions move the review beyond cataloguing technological
developments by specifying how AI, synthetic media, platformization, and datafication
may become consequential for sport business through stakeholder trust. The proposed
framework locates the theoretical problem in the relationships among technologi -
cal conditions, stakeholder appraisals, governance, and business consequences rather
than assuming that technological adoption carries fixed outcomes. Trust connects
these relationships while preserving the possibility that the same algorithmic practice
may be interpreted differently across stakeholders and contexts. In this regard, the pro -
posed architecture offers an empirically open basis for experiments, surveys, longitu -
dinal studies, organizational case research, qualitative inquiry, platform analysis, and
mixed-method investigations capable of testing, refining, challenging, or extending its
propositions.
5 Conclusion
Algorithmic sport and platformized sport business are no longer peripheral exten -
sions of sport communication, marketing, or digital media practice. They represent an
emerging managerial condition in which sport organizations, athletes, fans, sponsors,
media actors, and platform infrastructures interact within increasingly datafied, auto -
mated, and trust-sensitive environments. Three broader conclusions can be drawn from
the review. First, the literature has moved from early social media and digital engage -
ment concerns toward a more complex platform-centered agenda in which visibility,

computational research, suggesting both interdisciplinary richness and conceptual frag -

5.1 Theoretical and practical implications
Theoretically, this review advances sport-business scholarship by repositioning trust as
a connective construct across research streams that have often developed separately,
including platformization, AI, synthetic media, fan engagement, athlete branding, data -
fication, and digital integrity. The proposed integrative framework extends organiza -
tional trust, trust-in-automation, AI trust, relationship marketing, and platformization
scholarship into an algorithmically mediated sport-business context. Rather than treat -
ing technological adoption as a direct determinant of stakeholder responses, the frame -
work specifies authenticity, transparency, fairness, accountability, and data responsibility
as appraisal mechanisms through which algorithmic conditions may become trust-rele -
vant. It also distinguishes stakeholder trust from its potential relational, commercial, and
institutional consequences, allowing future studies to examine pathways linking techno -
logical mediation with engagement, loyalty, brand credibility, sponsorship confidence,
reputation, and organizational legitimacy. Governance and contextual conditions fur -
ther extend the framework by recognizing that trust may depend on disclosure, verifica -
tion, oversight, data practices, platform dependence, and information asymmetry. The
six propositions developed in this review provide an empirically testable agenda through
which these relationships can be examined, challenged, and refined across different
sports, stakeholder groups, markets, platforms, and regulatory contexts.
Practically, the findings suggest that sport organizations should evaluate AI and plat -
form strategies beyond conventional indicators of reach, efficiency, engagement, or
conversion. Trust-sensitive management requires attention to how algorithmic systems
are experienced by different stakeholders and how organizational practices affect per -
ceptions of authenticity, transparency, fairness, accountability, and responsible data use.
Sport organizations may therefore need explicit procedures for disclosing AI-generated
content, verifying athlete and organizational representations, protecting athlete likeness
and identity, governing fan data, maintaining human oversight of consequential auto -
mated decisions, and responding to synthetic media or misinformation. Fan-engage -
ment strategies should also distinguish between high interaction volume and durable
relational value, particularly where personalization or data extraction may create percep-
tions of manipulation or excessive surveillance. Sponsors and commercial partners may
similarly require clearer safeguards surrounding brand association, synthetic representa-
tion, and reputational risk. These implications do not prescribe a universal governance

model, but they indicate that technological innovation in sport business increasingly
requires parallel investment in trust protection, stakeholder communication, account -

5.2 Limitations
First, the review relied exclusively on Scopus-indexed documents. Although Scopus pro-
vides broad multidisciplinary coverage and structured bibliographic metadata suitable
for science mapping, its indexing does not represent all journals, regions, languages,
or forms of scholarly production equally. Hence, geographic patterns reported in this
review reflect the structure of scholarship visible within Scopus rather than the com -
plete global distribution of research on algorithmic sport business. Apparent concentra -
tions of productivity or collaboration in particular countries may partly reflect database
coverage and indexing visibility. Relevant scholarship indexed exclusively in Web of Sci -
ence, SPORTDiscus, regional databases, local journals, books, technical reports, and
practitioner literature may consequently remain outside the corpus. Second, restrict -
ing the corpus to English-language publications may further privilege Anglophone and
internationally indexed scholarship while reducing the visibility of research produced
within non-Anglophone sport systems. This limitation is particularly relevant because
platformization, AI governance, fan cultures, digital regulation, and perceptions of trust
may differ substantially across national and linguistic contexts.
6 The future of sport media research
Future research should move beyond documenting the adoption of artificial intelligence,
social media platforms, or digital engagement tools and instead examine how algorith -
mic systems reorganize the managerial conditions of sport. More work is needed on how
recommendation systems, generative AI, synthetic athlete content, automated journal -
ism, platform metrics, and data-driven personalization shape fan behavior, sponsor -
ship value, athlete branding, organizational communication, and sport media authority.
Scholars should also investigate how sport organizations define and govern authenticity
in environments where athlete likeness, voice, image, and message can be replicated or
manipulated. This is especially important for athlete branding, NIL markets, influencer-
athletes, women’s sport, para-sport, youth sport, and marginalized athletic identities,
where visibility can create both opportunity and vulnerability.
The field also needs stronger comparative, culturally grounded, and governance-
oriented research. Future studies should examine algorithmic sport beyond dominant
Western, English-language, and commercially powerful sport systems by including
Global South contexts, multilingual sport publics, regional platforms, local fan cultures,
and underrepresented sport organizations. Methodologically, future research should
combine bibliometric mapping with interviews, digital ethnography, platform analysis,
computational media analysis, case studies, policy analysis, and critical discourse analy -
sis. These approaches can explain not only where the literature is growing, but how algo-
rithmic systems are changing the distribution of visibility, attention, data, trust, labor,
and power in contemporary sport business. The next phase of scholarship should there -
fore treat algorithmic sport not merely as a technological trend, but as a managerial, eth-
ical, and organizational challenge that will shape the future legitimacy of sport.
