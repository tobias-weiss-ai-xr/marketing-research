# AI-Powered Personalization In Digital Marketing: How Brands Use Artificial Intelligence to Understand Individual Customers and Personalize What They See

AI-POWERED PERSONALIZATION
IN DIGITAL MARKETING: How Brands Use Artificial Intelligence to Understand
Individual Customers and Personalize What They See
Author: Pankti Chandra
Scholar: Institute of Management Sciences, University of Lucknow, Lucknow India
Abstract
This study examines how brands use Artificial Intelligence (AI) to understand individual
customers and personalize what they see across digital marketing channels. It explores the core
mechanics of AI -based product recommendations, personalized advertising, and pers onalized
emails and notifications, along with the recommendation engines that power platforms such as
Netflix, Spotify, and Amazon. The role of customer data and predictive analytics in enabling this
personalization is discussed, followed by an evaluation of the benefits AI personalization offers to
both businesses and consumers, weighed against the privacy and ethical concerns it raises, such as
data tracking, filter bubbles, and algorithmic bias. Real -world case studies of major brands,
including Amazon, Netflix, Spotify, and Starbucks, along with Indian examples like Nykaa and
Swiggy, are used to illustrate how personalization is applied in practice. The study finds that AI-
driven personalization delivers measurable gains in revenue and marketing efficiency but requires
brands to balance relevance with transparency and consumer trust. It concludes that the future of
digital marketing will depend not on how much data a brand collects, but on how responsibly it
uses that data.
Keywords: Personalized Custome rs, Digital Marketing, AI - Brands, Consumer Behaviour,
Customer Relationship, AI-Personalization.
1. INTRODUCTION
Over the past ten years, digital marketing has shifted from sending the same message to a large
audience towards understanding and targeting indi vidual customers. This transformation has
largely been driven by Artificial Intelligence (AI). From personalised Instagram advertisements
and Netflix recommendations to customised emails and shopping suggestions, AI enables brands
to deliver content based on individual preferences and behaviour.
In digital marketing, personalisation means adapting content, advertisements, product
recommendations, prices, and communication according to the needs and interests of individual
users. AI has expanded this concept by allowing businesses to analyse huge amounts of customer
data and continuously update their understanding of consumer behaviour.
This examination explores how brands use AI to personalise marketing through product
recommendations, targeted advertising, customised emails, and recommendation systems used by
platforms such as Netflix, Spotify, and Amazon. It also examines customer data, predictive
analytics, the benefits of personalisation, privacy concerns, and real-world case studies.

2. UNDERSTANDING AI-POWERED PERSONALIZATION
At its heart, AI -powered personalization operates by gathering data on a user, feeding that data
into machine learning models, and then using the results from the models to determine what
content, product, or message that particular user should see. The pr ocess can be divided into four
main stages.

2.1 Data Collection
Brands collect data from a variety of sources such as browsing history, purchase history, the
amount of time spent on a page, items which have been added to a cart but not bought, search
queries, the type of device used, the user's location, their social media activity, and even the manner
in which they scroll or hover over a page. Additional layers of behavioural and demographic data
are obtained through loyalty programs, mob ile apps, and connected devices  (Kotler et al., 2021;
Chaffey & Ellis-Chadwick, 2022).

On its own, raw data has no value. In order to detect patterns, for example, which products are
often bought together, what kind of content a user interacts with most frequently, or what time of
day a user is most likely to make a purchase, machine learning algorithms are used. At this stage,
techniques including collaborative filtering, content -based filtering, clustering, and natural
language processing are commonly applied (Chaffey & Ellis-Chadwick, 2022).
2.3 Prediction
When patterns have been identified, predictive models are able to estimate what a particular user
is most likely to want next; this might be a product they are likely to buy, a piece of content they
are likely to watch, or an advertisement they are likely to click on. Predictive analytics is the
technology that powers almost every personalization feature mentioned in this investigation
(Kotler et al., 2021).
2.4 Real-Time Personalization
The last stage involves delivery, which means presenting the appropriate content to the correct
person at the right time. Since AI systems are able to deal with signals in real time, personalization
has moved from being static. Therefore, a user's homepage , app feed or email inbox can differ
from one visit to the next depending on their most recent behaviour  (Chaffey & Ellis-Chadwick,
2022)
2.5 Why AI, and Not Just Rules-Based Systems?
Before it became possible to use AI on a large commercial scale, certain  brands tried to offer
personalization by means of simple rules, for instance, displaying a discount banner to anyone who
had visited the website more than three times without having made a purchase. Such rule -based
systems are limited since they require a  human to anticipate every possible situation in advance
and are unable to adjust as customer behaviour changes. On the other hand, AI-based systems learn
directly from data and constantly improve their own understanding of what is effective, without it
being necessary for a marketer to manually set out every possible rule. This is the reason why the
move from rule -based marketing automation to genuine AI -driven personalization is seen as a
major advance rather than just a small improvement.
3. AI-BASED PRODUCT RECOMMENDATIONS

this also bought” or “Recommended for you.” These are examples of product recommendation
systems, which have become an important part of digital marketing. They help customers discover
products that match their interests and preferences, while also encouraging them to explore and
purchase additional products (Table 1).
These recommendations are not manually created by people. Instead, they are generated by
computer programs known as recommendation algorithms, which operate in the background and
continuously analyse customer behaviour. These algorithms examine factors such as previous
purchases and browsing activity to predict products that a cus tomer may be interested in  (Table

3.1 Business Impact
Product recommendation systems can have a significant impact on both sales and customer
experience. By displaying products that are relevant to a customer's interests and previous
behaviour, these systems make it easier for customers to discover products they may be interested
in purchasing. They can also encourage cross-selling and upselling by suggesting complementary
or higher -value products. For businesses, this can lead to higher conversion rates, increased
average order value, and improved customer retention.
Recommendation systems also improve the efficiency of the shopping experience. Instead of
requiring customers to search through a large number of products, AI can identify and display
relevant options based on their individual preferences. As customers rec eive more useful
recommendations, they may be more likely to engage with the platform and return for future
purchases. Therefore, recommendation systems have become an important marketing tool for e -
commerce businesses, helping them improve both customer s atisfaction and overall business
performance.
3.2 Cold-Start Problem
One of the major challenges faced by recommendation systems is the cold-start problem. It occurs
when a system has insufficient information about a new user, a new product, or both. For example,
https://doi.org/10.33774/coe-2026-qdsc6-v2 Content not peer-reviewed by Cambridge University Press. License: CC BY 4.0

when a customer creates a new account, the system may not have enough information about their
preferences or purchasing behaviour to provide accurate personalised recommendations.
To overcome this problem, businesses can initially ask new users to select their interests, preferred
categories, or favourite products. They may also provide general or popular recommendations until
enough behavioural data is collected. For new products, content -based methods can be used by
analysing characteristics such as category, brand, price, or description. As users continue to
browse, purchase, rate, or interact with products, the system gathers more information and
gradually becomes better at generating personalised recommendations.
4. PERSONALIZED ADVERTISING
Personalized advertising uses AI to decide which advertisement a specific user sees, on which
platform and at what time based on that user inferred interests and likelihood of conversion. This
has replaced demographic-only targeting (age, gender , location) with behavioural and predictive
targeting.
4.1 How It Works
Advertising platforms such as Meta Ads Manager and Google Ads use machine learning to build
an audience profile for each user based on the pages they follow , the content they engage with ,
their browsing behaviour across websites (via cookies and tracking pixels), and their past purchase
behaviour. Advertisers can then manually define an audience or increasingly allow the AI system
itself to identify the most likely converters using "lookalike audi ences" and automated bidding
strategies.
4.2 Programmatic Advertising
Programmatic advertising refers to the automated buying and selling of ad space using AI
algorithms that decide within milliseconds, which ad to show to which user on which website. This
real-time bidding process considers hundreds of data points about the user viewing the page ,
calculates which advertiser is most likely to benefit from reaching that specific individual and at
what price.
4.3 Dynamic Creative Optimization
Beyond choosing who sees an ad AI is now also used to decide what the ad itself should look like
for a given user. Dynamic creative optimization automatically changes the image, headline or call-
to-action within an advertisement based on the viewers profile so two differe nt users might see
two versions of the same campaign.
4.4 Retargeting
Retargeting, also called remarketing is one of the common and recognizable applications of
personalized advertising. When a user visits a website and views a product without purchasing i t
a tracking pixel records that behaviour. AI-driven retargeting systems then follow that user across
websites and social media platforms displaying advertisements for the exact product they viewed
sometimes with an added incentive such as a limited -time discount. Retargeting allows brands to

reconnect with users who have previously interacted with their products or websites (Chaffey &
Ellis-Chadwick, 2022).
5. PERSONALIZED EMAILS AND NOTIFICATIONS
Email and push notifications remain among the cost-effective digital marketing channels and AI
has made them significantly more effective by moving them away from a one-size-fits-all approach
5.1 Behavioural Trigger Emails
AI systems monitor user actions such as cart abandonment browsing a product wit hout buying it
or lapsing in activity after a period of engagement. Based on these triggers automated systems send
emails for example a reminder about an item left in the cart often accompanied by a personalized
discount calculated to be just large enough to prompt a purchase witho ut eroding margin
unnecessarily (Chaffey & Ellis-Chadwick, 2022).
5.2 Content and Subject Line Personalization
AI tools analyse which subject lines, images and product recommendations within an email
generate the open and click-through rates for a given user segment and then automatically adjust
future emails accordingly. Some platforms personalize not the content but the send time itself
learning when an individual user is statistical ly most likely to open an email  (Chaffey & Elli s-
Chadwick, 2022)
5.3 Push Notifications
Mobile apps use AI to decide which notifications to send, to whom and when. A food delivery app
for instance may learn that a particular user tends to order dinner around 8 pm and send a
notification with a relevant  offer shortly before that time rather than sending the same generic
notification to all users at a fixed hour.

6.1 Netflix
Netflix uses AI extensively not to recommend which shows or movies a user should watch next
but also to personalize the thumbnail images shown for a title. Two different users may see two
different preview images for the same film, chosen because e ach image is predicted to be more
appealing to that particular viewer based on their past watching history. Netflix’s recommendation
engine also influences the order in which rows and titles appear on a user’s homepage meaning
that no two Netflix homepages look alike.
6.2 Spotify
Spotify’s personalization is built around understanding listening behaviour at a granular level,
including which songs a user skips, replays or saves. Features such as "Discover Weekly" and
"Daily Mix" use filtering combined with audio analysis (tempo, genre, mood) to generate playlists
unique to each listener. Spotify Wrapped, the platforms annual summary feature is itself a
marketing-driven output of the same underlying personalization engine.  (Spotify Engineering,
2021)
6.3 Amazon
Amazon’s recommendation system spans product suggestions on the homepage, related items on
product pages personalized search results and personalized pricing and delivery options in some
markets. Amazon also uses AI to personalize its marketing emails an d to predict purchases so
accurately that its patented "anticipatory shipping" concept proposed dispatching products to
regional warehouses before a customer even placed an order, based pu rely on predicted demand
(Smith & Linden, 2017).
7. CUSTOMER DATA AND PREDICTIVE ANALYTICS
None of the personalization discussed above would be possible without customer data
infrastructure and predictive analytics capabilities.
7.1 Sources of Customer Data
a) First-party data : Information collected directly by the brand, such as purchase history,
website behaviour and loyalty program activity.
https://doi.org/10.33774/coe-2026-qdsc6-v2 Content not peer-reviewed by Cambridge University Press. License: CC BY 4.0

b) Second-party data: Data shared through partnerships with businesses.
c) Third-party data: Data sold by external data brokers though this category is shrinking due
to privacy regulation and the phase-out of third-party cookies.
7.2 Predictive Analytics
Predictive analytics uses real -time data to forecast future behaviour. In marketing this typically
includes predicting the likelihood that a customer will churn (stop using a service) the likelihood
that a customer will respond to an offer the customers expected lifetime value and the next product
category they are likely to purchase from. These predictions allow brands to move from marketing
to proactive marketing reaching out to customers  before a problem or opportunity becomes
obvious.
7.3 Customer Data Platforms (CDPs)
Many large organizations now use Customer Data Platforms, which unify data from sources into
a single customer profile. A unified profile allows a brands website, app, ema il system and
advertising platform to all draw on the understanding of the customer ensuring a consistent
personalized experience across every channel rather than fragmented inconsistent messaging.
7.4 An Illustrative Example
Consider a customer who browse s a pair of running shoes on a retailer’s website but does not
purchase them. A predictive analytics system might estimate the probability that this customer will
purchase the shoes within the seven days if no action is taken and compare that to the probab ility
of purchase if the customer receives a personalized email with a ten percent discount. If the model
estimates that the discount meaningfully increases the probability of conversion without eroding
profit margin beyond a threshold the system can autom atically trigger that email. This kind of
decision made in time and repeated across millions of customers simultaneously is precisely what
predictive analytics enables and what would be impossible to replicate manually at scale.

interests.

c) Efficient marketing spends, since advertising budgets are directed toward users most likely
to convert.
d) Increased average order value through cross-selling and upselling.
e) Better inventory and demand forecasting, supported by analytics.

9. PRIVACY CONCERNS AND ETHICAL CHALLENGES
Despite its benefits AI -powered personalization raises concerns that businesses, regulators and
consumers continue to grapple with.
9.1 Data Privacy
Personalization depends on the collection of personal data often including sensitive information
about a person’s habits, location and preferences. Many consumers are uncomfortable with the
extent of tracking involved particularly when data is collected across platforms without clear easily
understood consent.
9.2 The "Creepy" Factor
There is a documented psychological effect where personalization that is too accurate can feel
invasive rather than helpful. An advertisement that appears immediately after a conversation about
a product even if coincidental, can make consumers feel surveilled damaging trust in the brand
rather than building it.
9.3 Filter Bubbles and Algorithmic Bias
Because recommendation systems show users more of what they engage with they c an create
"filter bubbles" that narrow the range of content or products a person is exposed to. Algorithms
can unintentionally encode bias present in historical data leading to unfair or exclusionary
outcomes for certain groups of users.
9.4 Regulatory Response
Governments around the world have responded with data protection regulations such as the
European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and Indias Digital Personal Data
Protection Act, which place limits on how customer data can be collec ted, stored and used for
personalization. Major browsers and platforms have also begun phasing out third -party cookies
forcing brands to rely heavily on first-party and zero-party data collected with explicit consent.
9.5 The Balancing Act
Brands today fac e the challenge of delivering the convenience and relevance that AI
personalization enables while remaining transparent, about data usage and giving consumers
control over their own information. Companies that get this balance right tend to build long -term
trust while those seen as overstepping risk regulatory penalties and reputational damage.

9.6 Practical Steps Brands Take to Address These Concerns
Brands are adopting several practical measures to address these concerns:
a) Providing clear layered privacy notices instead of long unreadable legal documents.
b) Offering preference centres where users can control what data is used and for what purpose.
c) Aggregating data wherever individual-level detail is not strictly necessary.
d) Conducting algorithmic audits to identify and correct bias in recommendation and targeting
systems.
e) Shifting investment toward party and zero -party data collected through direct consented
relationships with customers.
10. CASE STUDIES OF MAJOR BRANDS
10.1 Amazon
Amazon is widely regarded as one of the pioneers of AI-driven personalization in e-commerce. Its
recommendation engine analyses browsing history, purchase history, items in the cart and the
behaviour of customers to generate the "Customers who bought this item also bought" and
"Recommended for you" sections seen throughout the platform. Amazon also personalizes search
result rankings, marketing emails and even the layout of its homepage based on shopping patterns
contributing meaningfully to its overall sales performance (Smith & Linden, 2017).
10.2 Netflix
Netflix invests heavily in machine learning to personalize every element of the viewing experience
from the order of rows on the homepage to the specific thumbnail image displayed for each title.
The company’s internal research has shown that thumbnail personalization alone measurably
affects whether a user chooses to watch a given title demonstrating how gra nular AI
personalization can be (Netflix, 2026).
10.3 Spotify
Spotify’s "Discover Weekly " "Daily Mix," and "Wrapped" featu res are built on filtering and
audio-feature analysis that considers what a listener plays skips and saves. This has become a
defining part of Spotify’s brand identity and a strong driver of user engagement and retention
distinguishing it from competitors offering a music catalogue (Spotify Engineering, 2021).
10.4 Starbucks
Starbucks uses its app and loyalty program  to personalize product recommendations, offers and
marketing messages for individual customers based on their order history, local weather, time of
day and store inventory. This has helped Starbucks increase transaction value and strengthen
customer loyalty through its rewards program.

10.5 Nykaa and Myntra (Indian Market Examples)
Closer to the market platforms such as Nykaa and Myntra use AI -driven personalization to
recommend beauty and fashion products based on browsing behaviour past purchases and style
preferences while also personalizing push notifications around sales events and restocks. These
examples show that AI personalization is not limited to global technology companies but is now a
standard practice across e-commerce in India as well.
10.6 Swiggy and Zomato (Indian Market Examples)
Food delivery platforms operating in India, such as Swiggy and Zomato rely heavily on AI to
personalize the restaurant and dish recommendations shown to each user based on order history,
cuisine preference, time of day and even local weather conditions, which have been shown to
influence food ordering behaviour. These platforms also use models to person alize delivery time
estimates and to decide which restaurants to feature prominently for a given user illustrating how
AI personalization has become embedded in everyday consumer technology used by millions of
people across the country.
10.7 Key Takeaway Across Case Studies
Across every case study discussed above a common pattern emerges: the brands that lead their
industries treat customer data not as a byproduct of doing business but as a core strategic asset.
Each of these companies has built internal te ams, proprietary algorithms and long -term data
infrastructure specifically to support personalization. This suggests that AI personalization is not
simply a marketing tactic to be adopted overnight but a capability that requires investment,
technical expertise and organizational commitment to execute well.
11. CONCLUSION
AI-powered personalization has fundamentally reshaped marketing moving it away from mass,
undifferentiated messaging toward individualized experiences built on data, machine learning and
predictive analytics. From product recommendations and dynamic advertising to emails and the
recommendation engines that define platforms like Netflix, Spotify and Amazon AI enables brands
to understand customers at a level of detail that was previously impossible.
This capability delivers benefits, including higher conversion rates and marketing efficiency for
businesses and greater convenience and relevance for consumers. However, it also raises concerns
around data privacy, algorithmic transparency and th e ethical boundaries of tracking and
prediction. As data protection regulations tighten and consumer awareness grows, the brands that
will succeed in the long run are likely to be those that treat personalization not merely as a technical
capability but as  a trust-based relationship, with the customer –one where relevance is delivered
responsibly and data is used with consent and respect. Going forward the future of marketing will
likely be defined not by how much data a brand can collect but by how intelligently and ethically
it uses the data it is given.
