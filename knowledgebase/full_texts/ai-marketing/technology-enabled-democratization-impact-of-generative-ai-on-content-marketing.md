ABSTRACT
This study investigates how technology-enabled democratization affects market structures and incumbent firm
strategies, with a focus on generative AI and content marketing agencies (CMAs). While past technologies
augment marketing capabilities, generative AI redistributes core creation, technical, and economic capa-
bilities—challenging traditional agency–client dynamics and prompting strategic reconfigurations among
incumbent firms. Drawing on a qualitative study of 22 professionals from CMAs and their clients, we develop a
framework showing how generative AI enables widespread content marketing capabilities, alters content mar-
keting market structures, and forces CMAs to redefine their value propositions. Our findings reveal a shift from
execution-based services to expertise-driven roles, including creative consulting, hyper-personalization, and AI-
enabled service innovation. We contribute theoretically by extending the concept of technology-enabled
democratization, offering a structured framework that links capability redistribution, market restructuring,
and incumbent response strategies. Practically, we propose a playbook to help CMAs adapt to this transformation
through (1) strategic repositioning, (2) operational integration, (3) ethics, regulation, and compliance, (4) and
market and client engagement. Overall, the study offers conceptual and managerial insights into the evolving
interplay between generative AI, market democratization, and marketing agency transformation.
1. Introduction
Market democratization transpires when the ability to use, consume,
or contribute is distributed to larger market segments and no longer
monopolized by a specific group of consumers or organizations
(Kemming & Humborg, 2010; Shukla et al., 2022). Literature recognizes
that the emergence of a new technology may serve as a trigger for
market democratization (Asmussen et al., 2013), and such technology-
enabled democratization has unfolded repeatedly throughout history.
In the mid-nineteenth century, the emergence of sewing machines
democratized the capabilities to sew, enabling the lower-income classes
to sew clothes at home (Atik et al., 2022). Fast forward to the modern
age, the internet has enabled small businesses to compete globally, and
social media has democratized marketing and content distribution,
allowing individuals and brands to reach mass audiences without
traditional gatekeepers (Hanna et al., 2011; Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010;
Kietzmann et al., 2011; Lynn et al., 1999; Mathews et al., 2021; Prasad
et al., 2001).
Recently, generative artificial intelligence (AI) has triggered a new
wave of technology-enabled democratization (Peres et al., 2023). While
generative AI’s foundation models are developed by a limited number of
large companies (e.g., OpenAI and Google), their availability at rela-
tively low prices (or even zero price) democratizes the ability to create
high-quality digital content based on user-generated prompts (Ramaul
et al., 2024). Consequently, organizations are becoming less dependent
on specialized human skills, which were previously required for content
creation and typically held by a small number of professionals (Cillo &
Rubera, 2025). In this study, we focus on this phenomenon in the
empirical context of content marketing agencies (CMAs). CMAs repre-
sent a business-to-business (B2B) service industry that is expected to be
disrupted and reconfigured by the entering of generative AI models that
can produce texts, images, and videos (Amankwah-Amoah et al., 2024;
Wahid et al., 2023). For example, McKinsey (2023) estimates that
generative AI offers $2.6 trillion to $4.4 trillion in annual economic

Theoretically, the new wave of technology-enabled democratization
driven by generative AI raises several questions that research has yet to
fully address. First, while market democratization research has identi-
fied new technologies as important drivers and investigated their im-
plications (Asmussen et al., 2013; Atik et al., 2022; Chen, 2018; Chen
et al., 2020), the concept of technology-enabled democratization re-
mains undertheorized. Most studies treat it as either an outcome (Berger
et al., 2018; Chang & Sokol, 2022; Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010; Li & Sri-
nivasan, 2019; Zervas et al., 2017) or a background phenomenon
(Asmussen et al., 2013; Atik et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2020), without
offering a cohesive framework that explains how technology-enabled
democratization restructures markets and pressures incumbent firms
to respond. Moreover, prior democratizing technologies (e.g., social
media, digital marketplaces, or marketing automation) primarily
enhanced access, distribution, or task execution. Generative AI differs in
that it automates core creative and communicative capabilities (e.g.,
copywriting, visual design, and ideation), lowering the threshold not
just to participate in the production of marketing materials but also to
compete with professional-level output (Cillo & Rubera, 2025; Peres
et al., 2023; Wahid et al., 2023). This deeper redistribution of capabil-
ities may challenge existing theories of technology-enabled democrati-
zation, which were not designed to account for disruptions posed by
technologies that simulate expert labor.
Second, while emerging research on generative AI-assisted content
marketing highlights efficiency gains and creative acceleration
(Hartmann et al., 2024; Reisenbichler et al., 2022; Wahid et al., 2023), it
tends to overlook how generative AI shifts the organizing logic of the
field. Addressing these gaps is critical for understanding the market-
level implications of generative AI and the strategic recalibrations it
demands from incumbent actors. Third, the marketing agency–client
literature has largely focused on relational dynamics, such as trust,
collaboration, and role clarity, and often assumes that marketing
agencies maintain a stable advantage in creative and technical expertise
(Hughes & Vafeas, 2019; Killian & McManus, 2015; Laurie & Mortimer,
2019; Terho et al., 2022). Yet, as generative AI enables clients to
perform tasks once outsourced to marketing agencies, this foundational
asymmetry is being eroded, calling into question the basis of interde-
pendence in these relationships. Considering these theoretical and
practical gaps in our understanding, our study inquires: How does
technology-enabled democratization affect market structures and incumbent
firm strategies, as seen in the case of generative AI disrupting content mar-
keting agencies?
We address the research question by reviewing and synthesizing
existing literature on technology-enabled democratization to clarify its
key dimensions, including the types of capabilities that are democra-
tized, their broader market implications, and the strategic responses
adopted by incumbent firms. This review forms the conceptual foun-
dation for our empirical inquiry. We then conduct an exploratory and
discovery-oriented study (Zeithaml et al., 2020), drawing on in-depth
interviews with 22 professionals involved in content marketing,
including 13 from CMAs and nine from business customers (i.e., those
firms that choose whether they purchase CMA services). By focusing on
generative AI and the CMA industry, we build theory on technology-
enabled democratization, illustrating how it transforms the market
structures and how incumbent firms reconfigure their strategies to
remain competitive in response to this systemic shift.
This study makes three key theoretical contributions. First, it ad-
vances the concept of technology-enabled democratization by offering a
structured framework that explicates how emerging technologies
redistribute core capabilities, thereby altering market structures and
prompting strategic shifts among incumbent firms. Second, the study
contributes to the literature on generative AI-assisted content marketing
by shifting the focus from task-level enhancements to field-level
disruption, revealing how generative AI transforms the organizing
logic, evaluative norms, and competitive foundations of the content
marketing industry. Third, it extends and challenges the literature on
marketing agency–client relationships by showing that generative AI
disrupts the foundational asymmetry of expertise, prompting a struc-
tural reconfiguration of interdependence between marketing agencies
and clients. This reconfiguration gives rise to more fluid and co-creative
relationships in which tools are shared but expertise must be redefined.
From a managerial perspective, building on the three theoretical con-
tributions, our study offers actionable guidance for CMAs and B2B
marketing professionals in adapting to the technological disruption
caused by generative AI.
2. Literature review
2.1. Technology-enabled democratization
Democracy is a term derived from the Greek words d¯emos, meaning
“people,” and kratos, which signifies “rule or power” (Rosendo-Rios &
Shukla, 2023). It is a foundational idea in political science that accen-
tuates power distribution among people as citizens (Tilly, 2000). De-
mocracy then breeds the notion of democratization (Collier & Levitsky,
1997). Democratization generally entails a transition toward a system
where capabilities (or power) are shared among the broader populations
instead of being concentrated among a few (Atik et al., 2022; Jocz &
Quelch, 2008; Kemming & Humborg, 2010). Relatedly, in management
research the concept of market democratization refers to the increasing
accessibility of access, consumption, and contribution capabilities across
broader market segments, breaking the monopoly previously held by a
select group of consumers, organizations, or industry incumbents
(Asmussen et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2020; Jocz & Quelch, 2008; Shukla
et al., 2022). As a note, democratization is not necessarily about a
perfectly balanced distribution of power, in which everyone has the
exact same amount of power, and social divides are completely extinct
(Atik et al., 2022). It simply means that there is a transition from a less
just to a more just distribution of power and ability (Asmussen et al.,
2013).
Our research aligns closely with the concept of market democrati-
zation, particularly from the technology standpoint. Thus, within this
paradigm, the notion of technology-enabled democratization is espe-
cially relevant to this study, as it captures how technological advance-
ments redistribute capabilities once monopolized by incumbents to a
broader range of market actors. However, it is important to note that
technology-enabled democratization remains undertheorized in existing
literature. To address this, the current study undertook a literature re-
view of prior works that suggest the occurrence of “technology-enabled
democratization” to identify its core themes. The review included the
studies that explicitly used the term “democratization” to describe how
technology transforms market structures (Asmussen et al., 2013; Atik
et al., 2022; Chen, 2018; Chen et al., 2020) and also those that implicitly
discussed the phenomenon without directly labeling it as democratiza-
tion (Berger et al., 2018; Chang & Sokol, 2022; Hanna et al., 2011;
Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010; Kietzmann et al., 2011; Li & Srinivasan, 2019;
Lynn et al., 1999; Mathews et al., 2021; Prasad et al., 2001; Zervas et al.,
2017). As seen in Table 1, our review focuses on the following three key
themes: (1) the capabilities democratized by technology, (2) the impacts
of technology-enabled democratization on the broader market, and (3)
firms’ strategies to cope with technology-enabled democratization. A
more elaborate presentation of the literature review is available in the
Web Appendix.
Based on the prior literature, we formally define technology-enabled
democratization as the process through which emerging technologies

structurally equalize creation, technical, and economic capabilities that were
previously exclusive to experts or well-resourced organizations, thereby
triggering exponential market participation and transforming competitive
dynamics. Most prior research on technology-enabled democratization
has emphasized this capability-focused perspective, examining how
technologies empower new actors to perform tasks or create value in
ways that were once inaccessible (e.g., Atik et al., 2022; Chang & Sokol,
2022; Chen, 2018; Zervas et al., 2017). However, as we indicated pre-
viously, it is important to recognize that democratization does not al-
ways imply decentralization of control (Asmussen et al., 2013). While
technologies may broaden what users can do, the underlying in-
frastructures (e.g., platforms, models, and data systems) often remain
concentrated in the hands of a few powerful providers. In this sense,
democratization is a layered process: it redistributes capabilities at the
user level, even as it consolidates control at the infrastructural level.
This structural tension is important for understanding the long-term
implications of democratizing technologies. Our study follows the
dominant tradition in this literature by focusing on the redistribution of
capabilities while also acknowledging that such redistribution often
occurs within systems of centralized technological control.
Existing research on technology-enabled democratization demon-
strates how emerging digital platforms and advanced technologies
dismantle traditional capability hierarchies across market actors.
Notably, scholars showcase how technologies have collectively driven
the democratization of three capabilities: creation, technical, and eco-
nomic capabilities (Asmussen et al., 2013; Berger et al., 2018; Chen et al.,
2020; Kietzmann et al., 2011; Lynn et al., 1999; Zervas et al., 2017).
Technology-enabled democratization of creation capabilities refers to
the ability to generate products, services, communicative outputs, and
relational networks—forms of participation that historically demanded
specialized resources, organizational access, or institutional legitimacy
(Asmussen et al., 2013; Atik et al., 2022; Chen, 2018; Lynn et al., 1999).
Technology-enabled democratization of technical capabilities encom-
passes access to sophisticated tasks such as real-time data management,
decentralized decision-making, and global market intelligence, which
were once reserved for technologically dominant firms (Chen et al.,
2020; Mathews et al., 2021). Technology-enabled democratization of
economic capabilities involves the capacity to leverage infrastructure,
platforms, and digital tools without prohibitive financial investment
(Berger et al., 2018; Chang & Sokol, 2022; Li & Srinivasan, 2019; Zervas
et al., 2017). Technology-enabled democratization of economic capa-
bilities, therefore, is not merely a matter of lower costs. It reflects a
broader structural shift wherein a much wider range of market partici-
pants can acquire, deploy, and benefit from advanced technologies to
compete in ways that were previously unattainable.
The democratization of capabilities unleashed by technology re-
structures market dynamics across two distinct levels: the broader market
and incumbent firm levels. At the broader market level, technology-
enabled democratization reconfigures the structure of participation
itself. As creation, technical, and economic capabilities spread across a
broader range of market participants, markets can experience an over-
supply of content, products, and services, eroding firms’ ability to cap-
ture consumer attention and sustain differentiation (Asmussen et al.,
2013; Atik et al., 2022; Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010; Kietzmann et al.,
2011). Also, brand narratives, once tightly managed by firms, fragment
across decentralized actors, diminishing organizational control over
meaning and reputation (Asmussen et al., 2013; Hanna et al., 2011;
Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010; Kietzmann et al., 2011). Moreover, although
democratization broadens inclusivity and expands access for underrep-
resented groups, it simultaneously introduces profound ethical and
regulatory challenges, including misinformation, labor exploitation,
environmental degradation, and superficial diversity (Asmussen et al.,
2013; Atik et al., 2022). Therefore, the broader market level reflects a
complex transformation with increased democratized participation
paired with escalating governance, quality, and even ethical dilemmas.
At the incumbent firm level, technology-enabled democratization
deconstructs the structural asymmetries that once insulated dominant
players. By lowering barriers to entry and transferring key capabilities to
emerging actors, technology collapses the protective moats incumbents
historically relied upon (Berger et al., 2018;Chang & Sokol, 2022; Li &
Srinivasan, 2019; Zervas et al., 2017). Incumbent firms must thus
address increasingly volatile and fragmented environments where
traditional sources of advantage (e.g., scale, control, and exclusive
expertise) offer limited defense. Although some scholars acknowledge
marginal benefits for incumbents, such as ecosystem strengthening or
niche innovation (Berger et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2020; Prasad et al.,
2001), these advantages appear increasingly insufficient against the
structural erosion underway. Across industries, democratization forces
incumbents to recalibrate pricing strategies, absorb revenue losses, and
radically reconsider how they create and deliver value (Berger et al.,
2018; Chang & Sokol, 2022; Li & Srinivasan, 2019; Zervas et al., 2017).
Rather than simply expanding markets, technology-enabled democra-
tization often acts as a disruptive force that challenges incumbent power
and reshapes competition.
Confronted with the destabilizing effects of technology-enabled
democratization, incumbent firms cannot rely on legacy advantages to
maintain relevance and competitiveness (Li & Srinivasan, 2019). Rather,
survival demands strategic repositioning across two critical fronts. First,
strategic differentiation through core competencies requires firms to
leverage non-replicable assets (e.g., creativity, brand equity, and rela-
tional capital) to stand out in markets where access to similar tools and
capabilities has expanded across actors (Chang & Sokol, 2022; Li &
Srinivasan, 2019; Zervas et al., 2017). Second, business model innovation
for technological adaptation pushes firms to realign their value creation
and delivery mechanisms with emerging technological architectures,
such as social media ecosystems and blockchain infrastructures (Chang
& Sokol, 2022; Chen et al., 2020; Hanna et al., 2011; Mathews et al.,
2021; Prasad et al., 2001). The literature thus frames technology-

2.2. Generative AI and the democratization of content marketing
capabilities
Generative AI within the domain of content marketing represents a
compelling example of technology-enabled democratization. By making
advanced content creation capabilities (e.g., writing, image generation,
and video production) accessible to a broader range of users (Ramaul
et al., 2024), generative AI has the potential to lower traditional barriers
to participation and reduce reliance on specialized marketing agencies
(Amankwah-Amoah et al., 2024; Hartmann et al., 2024). Tools like
ChatGPT and other foundation models enable firms and individuals to
produce content at scale, with greater speed and lower cost (Cillo &
Rubera, 2025; Pedersen & Ritter, 2024; Peres et al., 2023), raising
important questions about how content production is shifting and who
now holds the capability to create value through content. Importantly,
the major foundation model providers such as OpenAI and Google have
become important bottlenecks for AI infrastructure (for discussion, see
Hern´andez-Tamurejo et al., 2025). In the content marketing landscape,
relying on a particular AI infrastructure provider could lead to “vendor
lock-in.” At the same time, there is major competition between foun-
dation model providers, enabling CMAs and other companies and users
to switch between the models and even develop “model agnostic”
solutions.
While emerging studies have explored the functional benefits and
drawbacks of AI-generated content—highlighting gains in efficiency
alongside concerns about authenticity, emotional resonance, and cred-
ibility (Brüns & Meißner, 2024; Hartmann et al., 2024; Park, 2024;
Reisenbichler et al., 2022; Wahid et al., 2023)—less is known about how
the redistribution of these capabilities alters competitive dynamics and
affects incumbent actors in the content marketing ecosystem. Extending
prior research on generative AI-assisted content marketing, this study
examines generative AI not merely as a content tool, but as a potentially
disruptive force that democratizes core content marketing capabilities
and compels incumbent firms, such as CMAs, to rethink their roles, value
propositions, and strategic responses.
2.3. Technology and marketing agency–client relationship
CMAs are those marketing agencies or consulting firms in which a
part or whole of their offerings provide content marketing services to
clients. They play a central role in enabling firms to execute content
strategies that require technical, creative, and strategic expertise.
Traditionally, clients have relied on CMAs to access capabilities they
lack in-house, especially in response to the growing complexity of digital
media environments (Terho et al., 2022). In recent years, however, the
rise of user-friendly and accessible technologies has enabled many firms
to begin integrating these tools into their content marketing activities
without relying solely on external agencies (Hughes & Vafeas, 2019;
Laurie & Mortimer, 2019). More broadly, this reflects an ongoing shift in
marketing, where firms are increasingly adopting strategies such as
content automation and digital personalization, even though their long-
term value and organizational implications remain underexplored
(Saura et al., 2021).
Existing research has examined the role of technology in the agen-
cy–client dynamics (Hughes & Vafeas, 2019; Killian & McManus, 2015;
Laurie & Mortimer, 2019; Terho et al., 2022; Vieira et al., 2019).
Nevertheless, the effort merely focused on how technology influences
operational workflows, task distribution, and collaboration between
agencies and clients. They do not fully engage with the deeper question
of how emerging technologies may redistribute core marketing capa-
bilities (e.g., creative production, strategic messaging, and content
personalization) that once constituted the primary value proposition of
agencies. In this regard, the concept of technology-enabled democrati-
zation offers a useful but underutilized lens for understanding current
shifts in the agency–client relationship. While previous technologies
often supported or augmented agency tasks (Terho et al., 2022),
generative AI introduces the possibility that clients may now replicate
some of the very capabilities that agencies were traditionally hired to
provide (Hartmann et al., 2024). This shift has the potential to desta-
bilize the foundational asymmetry of expertise and access that un-
derpins agency–client interdependence (Amankwah-Amoah et al.,
2024). Thus, rather than viewing generative AI as a tool for operational
enhancement, this study positions it as a trigger for capability redistri-
bution, prompting strategic questions about how CMAs define their
value, differentiate their services, and respond to changing client
expectations.
3. Methods
Given the novelty of the research topic, we adopted an exploratory
and discovery-oriented approach (Zeithaml et al., 2020) that focuses on
theory building from observations and suits to situations where existing
comprehension of a particular domain is still scant (Corbin & Strauss,
2015). In particular, we chose a qualitative interview study as our
empirical design, rather than, e.g., a single- or multiple-case study, as we
attempted to reach empirical saturation on our research question by
interviewing a broad range of different professionals from both the CMA
side, as well as from the customer side. Furthermore, our chosen
research ontology follows constructivism (Guba & Lincoln, 1994),
where we view reality as being co-constructed through interaction be-
tween individuals and their environments, and researchers participate in
this process by interpreting the perceptions of the informants on their
subjective reality.
3.1. Data collection
Qualitative interviews allow researchers to acquire profound insights
into complex social processes (Kvale, 2007). Grounded on this, we
conducted semi-structured interviews that included themes and pre-
liminary questions, but they were enriched by probing questions to dive
deeper into issues that seemed relevant (see. e.g., Arksey & Knight,
1999).
We used purposeful sampling (Emmel, 2013) to identify suitable
interviewees—focusing on both CMAs and business customers (i.e., the
firms that may or may not purchase CMAs’ services) that have deployed
generative AI in their content marketing. Combining the viewpoints of
both marketing agencies and their clients in the study of the marketing
agency–client relationship has been recommended by previous research
(Hughes et al., 2018) and aided us in gathering a more holistic theo-
retical understanding of the phenomenon. Interviews with business
customers gave us knowledge of what the business customers know, do,
worry, and expect about generative AI. Additionally, interviews with
CMAs shed light on how generative AI has impacted their business ac-
tivities and how CMAs plan their future to stay in the market and suc-
ceed in the era of generative AI-charged content marketing. We followed
the classic theoretical saturation logic in inductive qualitative studies,
where we sought for more informants until new informants did not
provide substantial new insights that would have changed our in-
terpretations or discovery of new theoretical constructs (Saunders et al.,
2018). Specifically, we adopted Guest et al.’s (2020) recommended new
information threshold in data saturation and verified that our last three
interviews produced significantly less than 5 % of new information,
none of which was considered substantial.
We interviewed 22 informants in 2023–2024, comprising nine from
business customers and 13 from CMAs. Twenty interviewees repre-
sented companies headquartered in Finland and two in Indonesia. These
interviewees were sampled in two phases. In the first phase, we con-
tacted employees of business customers and CMAs who had collaborated

(4) interviewees’ strategies to work with generative AI for now and in

3.2. Data analysis
Aligning with our exploratory and discovery-oriented research
strategy, we utilized the inductive coding approach (Corbin & Strauss,
2015). Our coding went through three phases. In the first phase, we
engaged in open coding. Our goal was to identify generative AI’s fea-
tures, strengths, weaknesses, and effects on CMAs’ and their clients’
businesses. Each of the three authors contributed to the open coding,
with the first author taking the lead on the majority of interview
materials.
In the second phase, we engaged in axial coding. We initially iden-
tified the similarities among the initial open codes and then structured
them into more organized and meaningful categories. We grouped these
first-order categories with similar properties and dimensions into
higher-order categories. After this, we mapped the relationships among
the higher-order categories and built a framework with aggregate
themes. All these tasks were highly iterative and involved several coding
meetings and restructuring episodes of the overall coding structure. In
this process, we referred to democratization as a sensitizing concept
(Charmaz, 2014) to structure our codes and juxtapose the emerging
insights with the extant body of literature.
Finally, in the third phase, we further analyzed and distilled the
codes and framework that resulted from the work in the second phase.
Among the three authors, we cross-validated our interpretations of the
data and refined the insights until we all agreed and were satisfied.
Based on the work, we ultimately mapped out the hierarchy and re-
lationships between the aggregate themes, resulting in a grounded
model. Table 3 demonstrates our data structure.
The quality of the study was evaluated by implementing the criteria
of transferability, credibility, dependability, and confirmability. These
criteria are some of the well-recognized criteria for the assessment of
trustworthiness in qualitative research (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). To
attain transferability, we included CMAs and their clients from various
industries. To ensure credibility, we deployed the data- and researcher-
based triangulation. Lastly, to achieve dependability and confirmability,
we exercised two approaches: (1) we documented a detailed account of
our analytical procedures, and (2) we demonstrated cross-case analyses
and direct quotes from our interviews to support our interpretations.

4.1. Democratization of content marketing capabilities
The generative AI was found to democratize content marketing ca-
pabilities in multiple ways. Specifically, the data reveal three different
dimensions of democratization: (1) the democratization of content creation
capabilities, (2) the democratization of technical capabilities, and (3) the
democratization of economic capabilities.
4.1.1. Democratization of content creation capabilities
The strongest driver for democratizing content marketing capabil-
ities stems from the improved ability to create content. Specifically, the
interviewees highlighted that generative AI democratizes copywriting
skills and allows the generation of multimodal and multilingual content
requiring special expertise without generative AI. In other words,
generative AI helps a content creator express oneself in more meaningful
ways and create content in different languages and forms (i.e., multi-
modality: texts, pictures, videos, and audios).
“The ability to express, the ability to explain, the ability to write is not
evenly distributed. So, it [Generative AI] brings more equal opportunities
to use for oneself.”
(CMA2)
“Before, you would use hours on image manipulation and editing. You
don’t really have to do that, especially at the sketching stage anymore.”
(CMA6)
“Our client companies quite often, if we produce the texts, they may have
a translation agency, or we have subcontracted translation from Finnish
to English or the other way around or other languages, so now it is mainly
done by various artificial intelligence applications and then a person only
proofreads and edits it.”
(CMA1)
4.1.2. Democratization of technical capabilities
The democratization of technical capabilities was manifested by
more evenly distributed coding, data analytics, and personalization ca-
pabilities. Importantly, generative AI helps marketers design more
innovative forms of content marketing without coding expertise or
reliance on other parties. The interviewees also mentioned that gener-
ative AI reduces the dependence on human data analysts because the
technology can perform various data analytics tasks, such as analyzing a
firm’s internal data and producing customer intelligence based on the
data. Moreover, generative AI reduces the need for human intervention
and expertise in personalizing content creation and delivery compared
to traditional automation techniques. Taken together, it was expected
that firms with limited coding, analytics, and personalization skills
would have better chances to compete against firms with abundant
resources.
“It gives you the skills of a junior coder. That’s the best thing… Before,
marketing teams haven’t been able to do anything code-based like inter-
activity, generators or something like that, at least in smaller companies.
They have been separate projects where they bring in someone from
another team or hire somebody to do that with a lot of money. So that’s
the best thing to come out of this…”
(CMA8)
“It [generative AI] is a very helpful tool, even starting from the strategy
point of view. That is, when we start to form insights, we can, for example,
dig for a more detailed understanding of the target group’s behavior and
so on without you being a data scientist, and this is significant.”
(CMA3)
“I’m not saying that personalization could not have been done before, but
personalization used to be rigid and code-based in a way. And now we are
able to do that for much smaller target groups and much more precisely
than in the old world where you could maybe afford to have seven seg-
ments and then you do like seven different versions. Content creation was
impossible on such a scale.”
(BC5)

4.1.3. Democratization of economic capabilities
Our interviewees also noted the affordability and easy access to
generative AI tools, which brings down economic barriers to accessing
frontier capabilities. In most generative AI models, the upfront costs are
negligible because the pricing of generative tools is predominantly
subscription-based. Most generative AI providers also offer either a
freemium version or a free trial period. This pricing technique minimizes
the economic risks associated with technology adoption since firms can
test the generative AI tools and just abandon them if proven unsuitable
for their content marketing needs. The affordability of generative AI is
especially important to smaller firms whose financial resources have
been limited for outsourcing or hiring in-house staff for content mar-
keting activities. To conclude, the budget-friendliness of generative AI
and its low adoption risks democratize firms’ economic capabilities for
content marketing, enabling firms of all sizes to create content with
relatively low investment.
“Everyone can do it, and the tools are so accessible, and the marginal cost
of using them is negligibly low. For sure, we are ready to pay for some
licences to use different tools. Because it seems like they are quite
affordable at the moment.”
(BC7)
“We are on the same level. Small companies can produce exactly the same
content in the same way. A single person can do an awful lot of what used
to require a big organization and a large number of content creators, so a
firm does not need them anymore in the future, so it puts all firms in an
equal position.”
(BC5)
4.2. Implications for the content marketing landscape
The democratization of content marketing capabilities has three
major implications for the content marketing landscape. First, there is
mismatch between content supply and demand. Second, as generative AI is
essentially a machine that the masses can easily operate, the technology
can decrease content quality and distinctiveness if utilized without human
supervision and integrity. Third, there are malpractices in content
creation.
4.2.1. Mismatch between content supply and demand
The interviewees broadly considered that one of the key benefits of
generative AI is that it saves time and money from content creation, thus
increasing the efficiency of content creation. Since many individuals and
firms can create more content faster and cheaper, the explosion of content
volumes in digital media is inevitable. While the numbers of content have
grown substantially, the consumers consuming content and the time spent
for content consumption stagnate. This condition suggests that despite
enhancing content marketing efficiency, firms will experience more
competition over content consumption and engagement, which can
eventually hamper the effectiveness of their content marketing.
“Over the last month we [as an industry] have produced twice or three
times the amount of content, but actually, the number of readers has not
grown in proportion, or those who consume the content or how much they
consume.”
(BC2)
“What causes the challenge is that when everyone does the same… The
amount of web content will blow up, be it text-based or images or video…
Even though it might be cheap to produce the text, does it give you the
benefit anymore?”
(CMA7)
“The challenge is, it is now possible to produce enormous amounts of
content at almost no cost, so then it is likely that basic and mediocre
content will explode in quantity, and then who will read them anymore.”
(BC5)

The interviewees were broadly worried that the increasing use of
generative AI decreases the quality or distinctiveness of content. Since

4.2.3. Malpractices in content creation
Besides the homogeneity and lack of creativity in AI-generated
content, the interviewees were concerned that copying and faking abili-
ties in content generation would amplify. Generative AI can emulate
existing content and brand languages effortlessly. The technology can
even mimic certain styles, tones, and voices, which can be done with
simple prompts. Hence, everyone with elementary technology skills can
utilize generative AI to copy or fake content or emulate a particular
communication style and visual appearance. This will further erode the
distinctiveness of content creation but can also lead to various in-
consistencies. In fact, it was suggested that the seamlessness of genera-
tive AI-assisted content generation may further lead to the proliferation
of content containing misinformation, errors, and inaccuracies.
“Copying and faking and things like that, making them is of course
becoming more and more easier, so probably the risk of abusing it and
making something that shouldn’t be made is increasing.”
(BC3)
“AI is better at taking into account the company’s tone of voice. So, if we
write something for [our company] website, and someone wants to
compete with us, they have access to the same database. They can directly
search, or actually, they can already give the prompt. Write, let’s say, five
articles in the same style and pick which ones are the most important and
interesting.”
(BC2)
“The second drawback can be that maybe there is some draft and some
texts by generative AI, but who is confirming the facts to be right? Maybe
it’s populist and nobody is checking the facts. And then there is more
misleading, misinformation on the web.”
(BC6)
“We’ll also be able to produce some really attractive information where
everything is fake and untrue, which nevertheless seems credible as we can
copy the styles of these credible sources”
(BC5)
4.3. Implications for the content marketing services markets
The democratization of content marketing capabilities has major
implications for the content marketing services markets. On the one
hand, business customers are empowered to create content themselves
without outsourcing the work to CMAs. This condition leads to decreased
demand for CMAs and reevaluation of the pricing schemes in content
marketing services. On the other hand, generative AI promotes new ideas
and opportunities, which prompt increased competition among CMAs due
to the emergence of AI-focused CMAs.
4.3.1. Decreased demand for content marketing service providers
Our interviewees expect that generative AI will decrease the demand
for content marketing services. Generative AI eases content generation,
which increases business customers’ preference for creating content inde-
pendently. Subsequently, because business customers have more content
marketing capabilities, they are less reliant on CMAs to improve their
content marketing performance.
“If ChatGPT can produce the insights I will provide to my clients, then the
value of my business will go down. Or even worse, maybe my clients will
not need me anymore. They can solve their own problems using their in-
house team.”
(CMA10)
“We sell marketing services, we sell content creation, so if we go around
telling everyone about it [the use of generative AI] openly, our customers
might see it as something that, well, if they do it with artificial intelligence,
then we can do it ourselves with artificial intelligence too, so we don’t need
these services anymore. The easier it all gets, it might be that we also lose
clients along with that.”
(CMA9)
“The whole [content marketing] industry is indeed going to change
fundamentally… They [clients] are taking control of those processes in-
house, because there are many things they can actually do themselves…
Perhaps the smaller, more production-focused cases will drop out entirely.
Meaning those small campaigns, like ‘could you make a follow-up
campaign where the message is slightly adjusted in this direction,’
which at some point might have been quite a large part of the revenue.
Because those are the kinds of things where AI can make the adjust-
ments… I don’t understand why they would still use an agency for that if it
can be done in a few minutes with some tool.”
(CMA13)
4.3.2. Reevaluation of the pricing schemes in content marketing services
Along with the decreased demand, generative AI inflicts a decreased
willingness to pay for content marketing services. As business customers
realize the power of generative AI for content marketing, they might
question paying a certain amount of money to CMAs for works that
require less effort and resources. Consequently, CMAs experience pres-
sure to lower the prices of content marketing services to stay competitive and
retain their clients. Yet, our informants suggested that so far, the CMAs
have not implemented major changes to their pricing schemes, while at
the same time, they are trying to find the most appropriate pricing
formula to find a balance between price and value for customers.
“The production becomes much faster, so then we no longer can, or can
we still bill customers the same amount of money if we do the work four
times faster than we would’ve done without artificial intelligence?”
(CMA9)
“And if you somehow don’t keep up with the development, at some point,
someone will offer the same services to your customer at a cheaper price,
or more services for the same money, because the productivity is
improved. If each individual’s productivity seems to be improved to some
extent, then the question is how it will work out in invoicing.”
(CMA7)
“It felt like you could get a hundred pieces of content or articles for the
same price as one from us, for example. For a certain type of person,
especially entrepreneur-owners, it was like, ‘Why on earth would I pay
this much?’ So yeah, those kinds of offers were definitely out there… We
have not changed our pricing yet. We rather think that our clients get
better value with the same price.”

4.3.3. Increased competition among CMAs
The interviewees explained that generative AI will promote the emer-
gence of new types of CMAs that intensify the competition for content
marketing services. Some of these new CMAs cater to customers with
limited resources for content creation and can provide cost-effective
content creation for the clients due to their superior prompting skills
and AI application expertise. While such CMAs fulfill the basic demand
for content creation, others were concerned that some CMAs would
innovate a model that integrates AI into agency services in a transformative
way. Some interviewees also envisioned that the competition would be
more global due to the focus on technological know-how rather than local-
ized content marketing expertise.
“Yes, new competitors like AI agencies have emerged… There were quite a
few that popped up, especially around the turn of the year [2024]… If the
target group is, for example, solo entrepreneurs or similar, it’s really easy
to offer AI-driven agency services to them.”
(CMA12)
“There could be actors that start their business in a completely different
way than what we are used to or what the traditional agencies are used to.
It can be a shocking thing too. There could be completely new types of
actors that are utilizing AI in an optimal way. That’s a very possible
scenario.”
(CMA6)
“In the future, the competitive landscape on the production side won’t be
small, new Finnish expert companies, but rather online services coming
mostly from outside Finland that handle these tasks and can be integrated
into that process. The risk, of course, is that they also come closer to our
company’s core expertise and that mentioned ‘crown jewel,’ meaning that
our competitors will increasingly be application-based rather than expert
companies. And of course, entirely new types of expert companies will
emerge—AI-native companies that will undoubtedly be created in the
marketing field as well, building their entire business model from the start
around utilizing these latest tools.”
(CMA13)
4.4. Redefinition of CMAs’ value propositions
Given the market changes induced by generative AI, our in-
terviewees largely agreed that CMAs can no longer position themselves
only as outsourced partners for content production. Instead, their value
propositions must evolve from conventional content marketing tech-
nical works to higher-level value propositions (see Fig. 2). We identified
four different approaches to redefining CMAs’ value propositions in the
age of generative AI: (1) from producers to creative marketing experts, (2)
from traditional to AI-empowered CMAs, (3) from traditional to hyper-
personalization, and (4) from implicit human involvement to explicit per-
sonal branding.
4.4.1. From producers to creative content marketing experts
The interviewees largely agreed that the emergence of generative AI
forces CMAs to transcend their value propositions from standard content
producers toward creative content designers. In this regard, many in-
terviewees considered creative idea generation as the fundamental value
drivers CMAs can provide their clients. Although business customers
may be resistant to paying for content production as such, they still
highly value novel ideas. It was noted that basic informational content in
the age of AI is inadequate for differentiation. Hence, creativity is
imperative to cut through the clutter and get people’s attention. Our
CMA interviewees further argued that creative CMAs will still acquire
clients because this creativity, combined with CMAs’ wealth of content
marketing insights and experiences, is highly valuable for their clients.
“In the so-called creative industry, there is still faith in their own… in the
crown jewel, so to speak, which is human creativity.”
(CMA13)
“I do still think people’s creativity is what customers are ready to pay for.
I don’t think it is likely that customers would start to do things directly on
their own, I do think they still want to work with creative and competent
people and buy the service. But you have to think about what the core will
be. The strategic work and creativity, to use these tools creatively on the

4.4.2. From traditional to AI-empowered CMAs
Generative AI as the core competency is the second identified option
for higher-order value propositions for CMAs. Specifically, CMAs can
step up their game, changing their value propositions from traditional
CMAs to AI-empowered CMAs. In this case, the interviewees stated that
integrating AI into content marketing approaches creates new opportunities
for CMAs, which requires new roles and tasks for creating content
marketing services. To be attractive and eventually hired by their cli-
ents, CMAs must build expertise in the use of generative AI to enhance
content marketing projects. The interviewees also suggested that CMAs
can sell such AI-driven content marketing expertise to their clients via new AI-
driven content marketing services (e.g., consultation, training, and strategy
work). One CMA explicitly proclaimed that they had expanded their
business significantly with generative AI-related service offerings by
implementing such an AI-driven value proposition.
“This is indeed a time of great uncertainty for the [marketing agency]
side… Yes, new approaches [for content marketing services] are emerging
from that. Training and various types of curation, and so on. In a way,
new roles and tasks are emerging where a consultative approach can be
offered to clients.”
(CMA13)
“Content marketers actually need to be better at consulting or strategizing
about how to structure generative AI so it actually captures the latest
story. So that it actually performs as it should. So, you need to be able to
administer the workflow of generative AI.”
(CMA10)
“It has already created a new business for us, clearly, we have been given
commissions we previously couldn’t even dream about, outside of mar-
keting communications. It has brought new business to us, because we
were the first one to try it and did it in an eye-catching manner, and we
have documented it, been transparent, and talked about it a lot.”
(CMA4)
4.4.3. From traditional to hyper-personalization
The third avenue for higher-level value propositions is transitioning
from traditional to hyper-personalization. Although personalization was
practiced long before the arrival of generative AI, it has been costly, and
hence, limited in quantity and quality. Now, generative AI makes hyper-
personalization a reality. Our CMA interviewees mentioned that gener-
ative AI can be used for scalable content creation for granular target groups.
Moreover, generative AI can dynamically adjust the content to individual
customers’ preferences in a given context or situation. As there are only a
few businesses with the expertise to implement hyper-personalization,
CMAs can capitalize on this situation and develop such capabilities.
“It gives a very delicious opportunity to scale the operation and produce
huge amounts of text content for different needs and to optimize it. We
have previously done for example three versions for different target
groups. Now you can do a hundred different versions, so the content
becomes much more personal and curated.”
(CMA1)
“Personalization is definitely emerging as a big trend across the board,
and I can really see it. Even things like tailoring messages for different
target groups, even for quite small ones, are much faster with AI
assistance.”
(CMA12)
“AI takes it [content marketing] to a sphere that dynamically adapts to
your preferences in a given situation. If you don’t like a pushy seller, then
it’s less pushy. If you like a kind seller, then it is kind to you.”
(CMA2)
4.4.4. From implicit human involvement to explicit personal branding
Besides redefining value propositions that align with contemporary
client demands, CMAs must contemplate how to position themselves as
trusted partners for their potential clients as the competition in the
content marketing services market intensifies. According to the CMA
interviewees, branding is becoming increasingly important for CMAs. In
particular, the interviewees stressed that personal branding is a promising
approach to stand out from other CMAs. The interviewees mentioned that
CMAs should incorporate real human experts with their names and faces
visible to their communication. Many interviewees further concluded
that people are more interested in people than machines. To this end,
our interviewees argued that, in the realm of robots, people search for
human connection and dialogue. Thus, the data imply that in the world of
increasing human-machine collaboration via AI, the role of human
bonds between a service provider and client may become more impor-
tant than ever.
“And maybe a megatrend, which I believe we have actually followed at
[name of the agency] right from the start, is that it’s human-centered,
meaning we are people working with people, and focused on under-
standing people.”
(CMA13)
“Many firms will do machine-made content, but then some firms can
counterbalance that, so that we still have a person here and I want our
customers to enjoy the fact that it’s our [name of the content creator] who
is here writing blogs and newsletters from person to person, so it may be
that some people miss this traditional human contact and it is taken as a
conscious choice. It may resonate with the target group so that they like
the fact there is a person there, and not a machine.”
(CMA11)
“Human experts will still be a significant competitive advantage in the
future. It’s also important in content that our own experts are brought to
the forefront, appearing with their own faces and names.”
(CMA12)
“In B2B expert sales, you have to take someone’s idea seriously if you
know that they have bothered to come to the place and you have actually

5. Discussion
This study advances the understanding of technology-enabled
democratization by examining how generative AI transforms the con-
tent marketing landscape and reshapes the strategic positioning of
incumbent CMAs. In this study we theoretically ground the emergence
of generative AI in the content marketing landscape with the broader
phenomenon of technology-enabled democratization (Asmussen et al.,
R. Wahid et al.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  Industrial Marketing Management 131 (2025) 1–16
10

2013; Atik et al., 2022; Berger et al., 2018; Chang & Sokol, 2022; Chen,
2018; Chen et al., 2020; Hanna et al., 2011; Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010;
Kietzmann et al., 2011; Li & Srinivasan, 2019; Lynn et al., 1999;
Mathews et al., 2021; Zervas et al., 2017). Our results indicate that,
similarly to earlier waves of broadly available technologies (e.g., the
internet, social media, sharing platforms, and automation), generative
AI reconfigures market relationships, provides access to new capabil-
ities, and enables new types of strategies, business models, and value
creation approaches to emerge. However, our findings also suggest that
generative AI differs meaningfully from these earlier technologies.
Whereas previous democratizing technologies primarily expanded ac-
cess to audiences (e.g., through social media), monetization channels (e.
g., via platforms), or execution support (e.g., automation tools), they
tended to complement human expertise by enhancing reach, speed, or
coordination. In contrast, generative AI represents a more profound shift
by helping to automate and scale up the production of many creative
functions and processes (e.g., copywriting, design, ideation) that have
traditionally been central to CMA value creation. This deeper redistri-
bution of productive capabilities enables clients not just to participate in
content creation, but to directly compete with CMAs on their core
output. This capability reallocation challenges the conventional asym-
metry in agency–client relationships and compels a reconsideration of
how value is constructed and defended in creative service work.
Further, we find that this technology-enabled democratization un-
folds along three interrelated pathways. First, it affects the broader
market by contributing to a mismatch between supply and demand,
declining in content quality and distinctiveness, and malpractices in
content creation—trends echoed in other democratized markets such as
fashion (Atik et al., 2022), taxi services (Berger et al., 2018), and hotels
(Chang & Sokol, 2022; Li & Srinivasan, 2019; Zervas et al., 2017).
Second, technology-enabled democratization affects CMAs directly as
clients reduce their reliance on CMAs and gradually begin to question
traditional pricing models. This erosion of dependency forces CMAs to
reconsider how they create and communicate value in a context where
technical execution and content generation are increasingly commodi-
tized. Industry forecasts already reflect this shift: for example, a report
from Bain and Company (2024) estimates that generative AI could lead
to price reductions of up to 20 % for marketing agency services. Third,
the effects on CMAs are also indirect, as shifts in the broader market
heighten competition, reshape norms, and alter client expectations,
which in the end, amplifies the pressure on incumbents to adapt and
reshape their approaches.
In response to these pressures, CMAs engage in various forms of
strategic reconfiguration. Some CMAs reposition themselves as creative
consultants who guide and contextualize AI-generated content, effec-
tively modifying the stakeholder expectations and institutional expec-
tations. Other CMAs focus on hyper-personalization, creative
orchestration, or explicit human involvement in content marketing
services, demonstrating an approach that is more adaptive than proac-
tive shaping, highlighting the core capabilities of CMAs and their pro-
fessionals, augmented by generative AI in various ways. Importantly,
these redefinitions signal a broader shift from production-based to
expertise-based value propositions, where the CMA’s role lies not in
exclusive access to tools but in its ability to deliver insight, judgment,
and integration across human and machine capabilities. Technology-
enabled democratization, then, does not eliminate the need for CMAs.
It instead forces a re-articulation of what they are for and under what
conditions they remain essential.
6. Implications
6.1. Theoretical implications
Our study contributes to theory by advancing the concept of
technology-enabled democratization and elaborating its consequences
for markets and incumbent actors in the context of generative AI and
CMAs. In doing so, we engage with and refine three strands of literature:
market democratization, generative AI-assisted content marketing, and
agency–client dynamics in marketing.
6.1.1. Conceptualization of technology-enabled democratization
We offer a novel framework that conceptualizes technology-enabled
democratization as a systemic force that interlinks the redistribution of
key capabilities, market restructuring, and incumbent response strate-
gies. While prior works have described technology-enabled democrati-
zation in terms of the redistribution of capabilities (Asmussen et al.,
2013; Atik et al., 2022; Berger et al., 2018; Chang & Sokol, 2022; Chen,
2018; Chen et al., 2020; Li & Srinivasan, 2019; Zervas et al., 2017), they
often present it as a background phenomenon without treating it as a
structured and theorized construct. Most inquiries stop at noting that
digital technologies reduce entry barriers or broaden participation. Our
study formalizes technology-enabled democratization as a process that
structurally equalizes creation, technical, and economic capabilities
across market actors—capabilities that were historically controlled by
specialized or well-resourced players. Importantly, we move beyond
description to offer a framework composed of three interlinked di-
mensions: (1) the types of capabilities democratized, (2) the effects of
such democratization on market structures, and (3) the strategies used
by incumbent firms in response. By doing so, we build the conceptual
understanding of technology-enabled democratization as a market
force, offering a more structured and analytically robust framework than
prior accounts that have tended to treat it as a diffuse or assumed
outcome of digital transformation.
Our study also advances the concept of technology-enabled democ-
ratization by clarifying that not all technologies democratize capabilities
in the same way or to the same depth. Prior work has shown that digital
technologies can broaden access to creation, technical, and economic
capabilities (Asmussen et al., 2013; Atik et al., 2022; Berger et al., 2018;
Chang & Sokol, 2022; Chen, 2018; Chen et al., 2020; Hanna et al., 2011;
Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010; Kietzmann et al., 2011; Li & Srinivasan, 2019;
Lynn et al., 1999; Mathews et al., 2021; Zervas et al., 2017). However,
many of these technologies operate by supporting, simplifying, or aug-
menting human expertise (e.g., by making tools easier to use, data more
accessible, or processes more efficient). In contrast, the newer class of
generative AI technologies redistributes capabilities by simulating the
expert labor itself (Ritala et al., 2024). Thus, these technologies do not
merely enable more people to participate, but they can potentially
automate the creative, analytical, or technical functions that once
defined professional advantage. This deeper form of democratization
destabilizes markets not simply by expanding who can do the work, but
by eroding what it means to have specialized capabilities in the first
place. Through the case of generative AI in content marketing, we
extend the theoretical understanding of technology-enabled democra-
tization to include this more disruptive form of capability redistribution:
one that reconfigures not just access to expertise, but the meaning and
necessity of expertise itself.
6.1.2. A systemic perspective on generative AI-assisted content marketing
We contribute to the literature on generative AI-assisted content
marketing by extending the prevailing view of generative AI as a task-
enhancing tool to a disruptive force that redefines the organizing
logic, creative norms, and competitive rules of the content marketing
field. Prior studies have primarily emphasized how generative AI sup-
ports existing practices, such as improving copywriting, accelerating
workflows, or enabling personalization (Brüns & Meißner, 2024; Hart-
mann et al., 2024; Park, 2024; Reisenbichler et al., 2022; Wahid et al.,
2023). While these insights are valuable, they frame generative AI as an
additive input within stable systems of content production.
Our study shows that generative AI does more than enhance tasks. It
destabilizes established content marketing practices by flooding the
market with AI-generated material, amplifying sameness, lowering the
threshold for participation, and weakening traditional markers of

originality and distinctiveness. As content becomes easier and cheaper
to produce, the field shifts from valuing technical output to valuing
interpretation, contextual fit, and human signaling. These changes affect
not only service providers but also brand teams, content strategists, and
digital platforms. What counts as “good content” is being renegotiated as
new tensions arise between speed and meaning, automation and
authenticity, or volume and value. By surfacing these structural and
normative shifts, our study contributes a deeper understanding of how
generative AI transforms not only the means of content creation, but the
cultural and strategic foundations of content marketing itself.
6.1.3. Reconfiguration of the agency–client relationships under capability
democratization
We extend and challenge the literature on marketing agency–client
relationships by showing that generative AI disrupts the foundational
asymmetry of expertise and forces a renegotiation of value, roles, and
interdependence between agencies and clients. Existing research has
emphasized relational dynamics such as trust, collaboration, role clarity,
and strategic alignment (Hughes & Vafeas, 2019; Killian & McManus,
2015; Laurie & Mortimer, 2019; Terho et al., 2022; Vieira et al., 2019),
typically assuming that marketing agencies retain a stable advantage in
creative or technical expertise. Even as digital tools (e.g., customer
relationship management systems or marketing automation platforms)
have transformed workflows, these technologies have historically sup-
ported the agency’s role rather than displaced it.
Generative AI introduces a more substantial shift. It enables clients to
perform tasks that were once the exclusive domain of agencies, thereby
eroding the structural asymmetry on which agency–client relationships
have long depended. As our findings reveal, clients increasingly perceive
agency deliverables as “a commodity,” leading them to question the
value of outsourcing and reduce their reliance on external providers. In
response, agencies are compelled to reposition themselves, offering
strategic insight, contextual interpretation, and automation-enabled
services that help clients navigate and apply generative AI effectively.
This shift reveals that when core capabilities become democratized,
agency–client relationships are not just adjusted but restructured. Rather
than being defined by clear divisions of labor or access, these relation-
ships evolve into more fluid, co-creative partnerships where tools are
shared but expertise is differentiated. Our study contributes to the
literature by rethinking agency–client dynamics in light of capability
convergence, and by highlighting how generative AI destabilizes the
very foundation of interdependence that once defined these
relationships.
6.2. Practical implications
This study offers a practitioner playbook to help CMAs adapt to
technology-enabled democratization in the age of generative AI (see
Table 4). The implications fall into four domains: (1) strategic reposi-
tioning, (2) operational integration, (3) ethics, regulation, and compliance,
and (4) market and client engagement. Each domain includes specific
actions CMAs can take, their intended internal outcomes, and the
broader consequences for how they work with clients.
Starting with strategic repositioning, CMAs need to revisit how they
define and communicate their value. As generative AI redistributes ac-
cess to production tools, CMAs can no longer rely on executional capa-
bility as their competitive edge. Instead, they need to reposition around
strategic and creative values. This begins with auditing service offerings
that are now easily replicable (e.g., short-form copywriting or static
visuals) and refocusing efforts on brand storytelling, creative strategy,
and content architecture. By reframing themselves as interpreters and
shapers of brand voice, CMAs shift the client perception from “execution
vendor” to “strategic partner.” This repositioning also demands a change
in pricing models. Rather than charging based on volume or time, CMAs

traditional content or strategy roles, CMAs should update hiring profiles
to seek hybrid talent that blends creative sensibility with AI fluency. This
may include roles like AI Content Architect or Human-in-the-Loop Edi-

human relevance. Doing so not only builds internal adaptability but also
signals to clients that the CMA is investing in future-facing skills and
leadership capacity in a rapidly evolving landscape. Third, internal
capability building remains essential. Training in prompt engineering,
editorial oversight, and human–AI collaboration should be integrated
into onboarding and professional development systems. Distributing AI
expertise across teams ensures that it does not become siloed within a
few “tech champions,” but instead becomes part of the CMA’s collective
intelligence. This enhances creative resilience and improves operational
agility. Taken together, these operational moves allow CMAs to lead by
example. CMAs that institutionalize AI usage and evolve talent strategies
will be best positioned to set standards, shape client expectations, and
sustain competitive differentiation even as generative AI becomes a
common tool across the industry.
The third strategic response relates to ethics, regulation, and compli-
ance. First, CMAs should develop overarching responsible AI governance
frameworks where there are clear rules and practices for the production,
review, and approval of content. Specific examples within this frame-
work may include guidelines on how to handle data (e.g., client data),
disclose AI use, adhere to the government’s AI regulation, and align with
brand books. Second, given the constant development in the field, CMAs
should regularly audit and mitigate risks associated with AI-generated
content. This can be done internally or through external auditing, and
also in collaboration with customers. Finally, CMAs should develop
client-facing transparency practices, as the new market environment
requires CMAs to communicate their differentiation and AI use more
transparently. Rather than hiding or downplaying the use of AI, CMAs
should showcase how human expertise shapes and elevates AI outputs.
This could involve public-facing case studies, team spotlights, or state-
ments of creative philosophy. By doing so, CMAs distinguish themselves
in a crowded market where authenticity, creativity, and ethical use of AI
matter more than ever.
The last strategic response entails market and client engagement. Many
clients are actively exploring or experimenting with generative AI tools,
but they lack guidance on how to integrate them responsibly or effec-
tively. CMAs can meet this need by offering client enablement services:
onboarding programs, prompt workshops, governance templates, and
co-designed workflows. By helping clients build internal AI maturity,
CMAs become long-term partners, not just deliverers of content, but
enablers of capability. Finally, CMAs should proactively engage with
generative AI tool providers. As power users with multiple clients, CMAs
have distinct needs (e.g., tone-of-voice calibration, approval routing, or
multi-brand dashboards). By partnering with flexible platforms or
building proprietary integrations, CMAs can retain control over their
processes and create differentiated service offerings that clients cannot
easily replicate.
Overall, generative AI does not eliminate the need for CMAs. Instead,
it transforms the basis of their value. CMAs that reposition strategically,
operationalize internally, manage ethically, and engage externally will
not only survive the democratization of content marketing capabilities,
but lead within it. These changes also redefine the agency–client rela-
tionship: no longer a simple exchange of service for fee, but a collabo-
rative and capability-driven partnership in a co-created marketing
ecosystem.

7. Limitations and future research directions
This study develops a theory of technology-enabled democratization
through the empirical lens of CMAs navigating generative AI adoption.
While the findings contribute to both theory and practice, several lim-
itations remain. These limitations are contextual, methodological, and
conceptual, and each suggests productive avenues for future research.
The contextual limitations of this study involve both the industry
scope and the scale of participating firms. Our theory building is
grounded in a single industry context (i.e., CMAs operating as B2B ser-
vice firms) and our sample consists entirely of small to micro-sized
CMAs. Although we deliberately sampled across sectors and roles
within this field, our findings may not transfer directly to other service
sectors undergoing technology-enabled democratization, nor do they
capture the perspectives of larger CMAs that may have greater resources,
infrastructure, and strategic latitude to respond to generative AI. Future
research could extend this framework to other industries and organi-
zational sizes, using comparative or multi-case designs to assess its
boundary conditions and identify how structural position influences
strategic response.
The methodological limitation concerns the qualitative and cross-
sectional design, which captures a snapshot of CMA experiences at an
early stage of generative AI adoption. While this approach surfaces rich
patterns and early strategic shifts, it cannot account for how these re-
sponses evolve over time. Key dynamics such as pricing experimenta-
tion, client training, and role transformation are still developing.
Longitudinal research could track how these processes unfold and
whether they lead to stable organizational routines or ongoing disrup-
tion. Additionally, although some participants discussed client enable-
ment and pricing concerns, we did not explore these topics in depth.
Future studies could examine how CMAs institutionalize new service
models or reconfigure their pricing logic in response to AI-related
pressures.
The conceptual and empirical limitations include several areas our
study did not directly theorize. First, this study centered on the market-
level implications of generative AI, particularly how the redistribution
of content creation capabilities affects the strategic positioning of
incumbent service firms such as CMAs. In doing so, we did not examine
the ownership structures or governance mechanisms underlying gener-
ative AI technologies and foundation models. However, this structural
layer is increasingly critical to understanding how democratized tools
are accessed and operationalized. While generative AI expands access to

high-quality content creation, the core infrastructures (e.g., foundation
models, training data, and deployment platforms) remain controlled by
a small number of dominant technology firms (e.g., OpenAI, Google,
Meta). These actors set the technical standards, pricing structures, and
usage policies that ultimately govern how capabilities are deployed. In
this sense, generative AI represents a layered form of democratization: it
broadens use at the application level while concentrating control at the
infrastructural level. This mirrors patterns observed in other digital
sectors, such as online travel, where intermediary platforms consolidate
power over both market access and value appropriation. For example,
Hern´andez-Tamurejo et al. (2025) show how online travel agencies (e.
g., Booking.com and Expedia), while offering enhanced customer-facing
services, simultaneously centralize strategic control and impose struc-
tural dependencies on tourism providers operating within their ecosys-
tems. Similar dynamics may unfold in the generative AI landscape,
where firms reliant on particular foundational models face constraints
not only in pricing and functionality but also in their strategic auton-
omy. Yet, companies such as the CMAs could develop a variety of
workarounds, such as model agnostic solutions or those relying on open-
source models. Future research could build on these ideas to further
examine how platform-controlled AI infrastructure shapes downstream
market behavior, dependency structures, and power asymmetries across
different industries, and what are the opportunities of companies and
customers to navigate these power structures.
Second, although we have outlined how CMAs should act
ethically—by establishing responsible AI governance frameworks,
auditing content risks, and practicing transparency with clients—there
remains an empirical gap in understanding how these practices are
implemented and perceived in real-world settings. This is even more
crucial considering AI regulations continue to evolve. Further studies
could explore how regulatory change and demands, such as data pro-
tection laws, AI transparency mandates, or client-specific compliance
requirements, may not only constrain but also enable new value creation
strategies for CMAs. For instance, corporate clients may prefer to
collaborate with CMAs that proactively manage AI-related risks and can
demonstrate compliance readiness. Thus, it is both a theoretically sig-
nificant and practically urgent task to investigate how regulatory de-
velopments shape the operations, offerings, and differentiation
strategies of CMAs in the age of generative AI.
Finally, while our study relied on technology-enabled democratiza-
tion as the primary lens, we acknowledge that other theoretical per-
spectives (e.g., technological disruption, power asymmetries, the
commodification of creative labor, and market-shaping) could provide
valuable complementary insights. Future research could draw on this
literature to explore how generative AI reshapes professional authority,
redistributes control within business networks, redefines the symbolic
and economic value of creativity, or enables certain actors to actively
influence market structures, roles, and expectations through strategic
shaping efforts.
