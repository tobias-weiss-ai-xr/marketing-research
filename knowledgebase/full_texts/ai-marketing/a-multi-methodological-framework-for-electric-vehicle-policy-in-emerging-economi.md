Abstract

Electric vehicle (EV) adoption in emerging economies is shaped by interacting technical, economic, institutional,
and behavioral factors. In Indonesia, despite substantial fiscal and non -fiscal incentives, EV adoption remains
below 1% of vehicle sales, reflecting persistent misalignment among government targets, industry capabilities,
and consumer acceptance. Existing studies tend to analyze these challenges in isolation and rarely integrate
behavioral and institutional dimensions within a single coherent f ramework suited to such "wicked" policy
problems. This study is conceptual and methodological in nature. Through a systematic literature review (SLR)
of 81 articles, supported by a System of Systems Methodologies (SOSM) analysis and refined by a pilot workshop
with Indonesian experts, it develops an integrated problem -structuring framework. The review indicates that
Problem Structuring Methods, particularly Soft Systems Methodology (SSM), remain underutilized in emerging-
economy transport policy. On this basis, the study proposes a six-stage integration protocol that positions SSM
as the methodological backbone, complemented by Innovation Diffusion Theory (IDT), Principal-Agent Theory
(PAT), and Strategic Choice Approach (SCA), spanning problem exploration, innovation analysis, incentive
mapping, conceptual modeling, uncertainty management, and implementation strategy. The framework offers
a structured way to combine interpretive, behavioral, institutional, and strategic perspectives while
maintaining theoret ical coherence. Its applicability is illustrated through an exploratory application to
Indonesia's EV charging -infrastructure policy, with broader empirical validation identified as a priority for
future research.

INTRODUCTION
 The global transition toward low carbon transportation has placed electric vehicles (EVs) at
the center of efforts to achieve net zero emissions, with projections indicating up to a 90%
reduction in transport related CO₂ emissions by 2050 ( IEA, 2021 ) and life cycle evidence
confirming that EVs emit substantially less CO₂ than internal combustion vehicles ( Kawamoto et
al., 2019). Global EV uptake has accelerated rapidly, reaching nearly 20% of new car sales by 2023
(IEA, 2024). Indonesia has aligned itself with this transition through ambitious national targets of
400,000 electric cars and 2.1 million electric motorcycles by 2030 (Ministry of Energy and Mineral
Resources, 2021 ), supported by a range of fiscal and non fiscal incentives. Actual progress,
however, remains far below these targets, with only about 12,000 cars and 62,000 two wheelers
adopted by 2023, representing less than one percent of vehicle sales ( Antara, 2023 ; Hakam &
Jumayla, 2024). This persistent gap between policy ambition and adoption reality reflects more
than technical or economic constraints. It is shaped by high upfront costs, limited charging

 Research on EV adoption in Indonesia has examined this challenge from several distinct
angles. Economic studies emphasize cost barriers and total cost of ownership (Wibowo & Septiari,
2023; Pirmana et al., 2023 ), infrastructure studies focus on the spatial optimization of charging
stations (Setiawan et al., 2020 ), behavioral studies analyze consumer attitudes and perceptions
(Lazuardy et al., 2024 ), and institutional studies highlight regulatory inconsistency and
coordination failures ( Sasongko et al., 2024 ; Lonan & Ardi, 2020 ). While each stream offers
valuable insight, most rely on reductionist and single method approaches that isolate one
dimension of the problem. Conventional adoption theories such as Innovation Diffusion Theory
(Rogers, 2014), together with expanded behavi oral models such as TPB and UTAUT, explain
individual adoption factors but remain grounded in rational decision making assumptions and
rarely account for Indonesia's institutional and socio spatial complexity ( Abbasi et al., 2021 ;
Lazuardy et al., 2024).
 Building on this body of work, the systematic literature review conducted in this study
(Section 4) confirms that behavioral and institutional barriers are seldom integrated within a single
coherent framework, and that Problem Structuring Methods, particul arly Soft Systems
Methodology, remain underutilized in emerging economy transport policy ( Flores et al., 2024 ;
Franco & Montibeller, 2010). The research gap, therefore, does not lie in a shortage of individual
studies but in the absence of a systematic methodological framework that connects their
behavioral, institutional, and strategic dimensions in a way that reflects the wicked nature of  the
problem. This fragmentation leaves policymakers without an analytical structure capable of
capturing how these dimensions interact.
 To address this gap, the objective of this study is to develop an integrated, stage specific multi
methodological framework for analyzing EV policy in emerging economies, using Indonesia as the
primary context. The framework positions Soft Systems Methodology (Checkland & Poulter, 2007)
as the methodological backbone and combines it with Innovation Diffusion Theory, Principal-Agent
Theory, and Strategic Choice Approach so that behavioral, institutional, and strategic dimensions
can be analyzed within a single coherent structure. The fra mework is developed through a
systematic literature review and refined through a pilot workshop with Indonesian experts.
Guided by this objective, the study addresses four research questions:
1. How has Soft Systems Methodology been applied in public policy contexts, particularly in
transportation and energy transitions?
2. What are the methodological strengths and limitations of Soft Systems Methodology in
addressing wicked problems in these domains?
3. How can Soft Systems Methodology be effectively integrated with complementary
methodologies (IDT, PAT, and SCA) to analyze complex policy challenges such as EV
adoption?
4. What are the key implementation considerations for applying multi methodological
approaches in emerging economy contexts such as Indonesia?

 In answering these questions, the study offers two contributions. Methodologically, it
proposes a six stage integration protocol that combines interpretive, behavioral, institutional, and
strategic perspectives while preserving theoretical coherence, advan cing the way Problem
Structuring Methods can be systematically integrated. Practically, it provides a context sensitive

LITERATURE REVIEW
Indonesia's EV transition illustrates the methodological challenges of policy analysis in
emerging economies. As noted, adoption remains below one percent of vehicle sales despite
substantial fiscal incentives ( Antara, 2023 ; Hakam & Jumayla, 2024 ). This gap stems from
institutional fragmentation, reflected in conflicting priorities among ministries, limited PLN
charging infrastructure of 1,582 stations across 1,131 areas, and regulatory uncertainty for foreign
OEMs, together with socio cultural resistance such as range anxiety, hybrid bias, and the dominance
of two wheelers ( Sasongko et al., 2024 ; Tritto, 2025; Setiawan et al., 2020 ; Lazuardy et al., 2024 ;
Wibowo & Septiari, 2023).
This emphasis reflects a longer shift in Operational Research, which began with the
mathematical optimization developed during World War II for quantifiable problems with clear
objectives, then moved toward interpretive and participatory methodologies as c ritics judged
technique driven analysis to be excessively reductionist (Ackoff, 1979; Rosenhead, 1996). That shift
introduced the distinction between tame problems, which yield to technical solutions, and wicked
problems, which are marked by stakeholder disagreement, interconnected causality, and
irreversible consequences ( Rittel & Webber, 1973 ), and it established the foundation for PSMs,
including SSM, which prioritize dialogue and iterative exploration over the search for a single
optimum (Checkland, 1981; Rosenhead, 1996). Table 1 summarizes this evolution and the rationale
for an SSM based multi methodology in Indonesia's EV transition.

Problem Structuring Methods, Theories, and Their Integration
PSMs are designed for decision contexts marked by complexity, uncertainty, and multiple
stakeholder perspectives, and rather than seeking definitive solutions they rely on iterative
learning, value pluralism, and participatory modeling (Mingers & Rosenhead, 2011). A distinction
often blurred in the literature, and one this study makes explicit, is that the four approaches
integrated here are not of the same kind. SSM and the Strategic Choice Approach (SCA) are problem
structuring methodologies, that is, procedure s for organizing inquiry and decision making under
complexity, whereas Innovation Diffusion Theory (IDT) and Principal -Agent Theory (PAT) are
substantive theories that explain how adoption spreads and how incentives become misaligned.

Because these approaches rest on different assumptions, with SSM and SCA grounded in an
interpretivist and participatory stance and IDT and PAT in more functionalist and rationalist
premises, their integration requires explicit justification rather than si mple juxtaposition.
Following the logic of multimethodology ( Mingers & Brocklesby, 1997 ; Mingers, 2006 ), the
framework manages this in three ways: SSM serves as the overarching methodology that frames
the inquiry and supplies the interpretive structure for the others; IDT and PAT are used not as
competing paradigms but as bounded lenses inserted only at th e stages where their assumptions
hold, IDT where adoption behavior is examined and PAT where incentive structures are diagnosed;
and this stage specific deployment keeps each theory within its zone of validity so that assumption
conflicts are contained. The System of Systems Methodologies provides the meta level rationale for
matching each approach to its context ( Jackson & Keys, 1984 ), developed further in the findings.

contexts, particularly in emerging economies where technocratic optimization still dominates
(Head & Alford, 2015 ). Applying this lens, Table 3 disaggregates Indonesia's EV transition across

Soft Systems Methodology and Stage-Specific Integration
Among PSMs, SSM is especially relevant for the ill structured problems that characterize EV
policy (Checkland, 1981; Checkland & Poulter, 2007 ). Grounded in interpretivist epistemology, it
does not seek an optimal solution but facilitates structured learning among stakeholders with
differing worldviews through rich pictures, root definitions, CATWOE analysis, and conceptual
models, surfacing implicit assumptions and accommodating contextual diversity. Because a single
method rarely captures the technical, behavioral, and institutional dimensions of complex
transitions, SSM benefits from integration with complementary approaches, for which Mingers '
multimethodology framework (Figure 1) provides the conceptual basis by showing how tools from
different methodologies combine across the stages of an intervention ( Mingers & Brocklesby,
1997). This need is especially clear in emerging economies, where conventional analysis assumes
clear objectives, stable preferences, and linear causality that rarely hold, and where PSM
application remains limited, particularly in transportation policy with i ts complex infrastructure,
multi level governance, and behavioral change ( Head, 2019; Flores et al., 2024 ; Georgiou, 2013;
Franco & Montibeller, 2010 ). Indonesia's EV ecosystem displays exactly these features, which
makes SSM a suitable central framework when complemented by IDT, PAT, and SCA (Checkland &
Poulter, 2007; Sasongko et al., 2024).

behavioral and institutional dimensions, and applies SCA to manage uncertainty and evaluate
feasible alternatives. The final stage produces integrated implementation strategies that maintain
systemic coherence while addressing governance fragmentation and coordinat ion challenges in
Indonesia's EV ecosystem (Setiawan et al., 2020; Tritto, 2025).

Scopus served as the primary database because it offers broad, peer reviewed,
interdisciplinary coverage and consistent metadata for advanced filtering ( Mongeon & Paul -Hus,
2016). A single primary database was chosen to keep metadata and filtering consistent, and because
relying on one database limits recall, backward and forward snowballing of the included articles
was added as a corrective (Tranfield et al., 2003). The search was conducted on 16 June 2025 across
the title, abstract, and keyword fields (TITLE -ABS-KEY) using the string (Electric Vehicles OR
Electric Mobility OR Low -Carbon Transportation) AND (Emerging Economies OR Developing
Countries) AND (Adoption  OR Transition OR Policy), which returned 170 records. Duplicate
removal and title and abstract screening then excluded 42 records, leaving 128 for eligibility
assessment, after which full text assessment excluded a further 47 and produced a final corpus o f
81 articles published between 2007 and 2025. Inclusion was limited to English language articles in
final publication stage within the Business, Management and Accounting subject area that
addressed technology adoption or policy in emerging economies, whi le purely technical studies
without policy relevance, non final publications, and records outside that subject area were
excluded, and each retained article was additionally checked for peer reviewed status,
methodological clarity, and relevance to the research questions. Extraction was carried out in NVivo
using a structured coding scheme organized around problem dimensions, methodologies, and
policy implications, with the individual article as the coding unit. Two researchers first calibrated
the scheme on a shared subset of articles and then coded all 81 articles independently, reaching a
Cohen's kappa of 0.86, which indicates almost perfect agreement, with the few remaining
discrepancies resolved through discussion (Landis & Koch, 1977). The complete selection process

Building on the framework derived from this review, a pilot validation workshop with nine
Indonesian experts was conducted using SSM principles to strengthen contextual validity. The
experts were selected purposively on the basis of direct expertise in Indonesia's EV ecosystem and
representation across the relevant stakeholder domains, including government, industry, energy,
and academia. Working through SSM and CATWOE prompts, participants assessed the framework's
stages, the realism of the identified barr iers, and its fit with coordination dynamics, mobility
patterns, and informal governance in Indonesia, and the discussion was recorded with consent and
analyzed thematically so that the resulting themes confirmed and refined the framework rather
than generating new empirical findings.

Across these studies, the review indicates that Indonesia's EV challenge is typically
addressed in separate analytical silos, with economic work centered on cost barriers, infrastructure
work on spatial optimization, behavioral work on consumer attitudes, policy work on regulatory
inconsistency, and institutional work on coordination failures. What the reviewed literature does
not offer is a single framework that integrates these dimensions in a way suited to wicked problems.
In the authors' reading, this f ragmentation helps account for the persistent distance between the
policy ambition of 400,000 EVs by 2030 and current adoption below one percent, and it motivates
the stage specific integration protocol set out earlier.

Framework Operationalization in Indonesian Context
To illustrate how the framework operates, this study applies it to Indonesia's EV charging
infrastructure policy. This application is illustrative rather than a full empirical test, and it draws
on the patterns identified in the systematic review together with the issues raised during the
validation workshop. Applied in this way, the early stages surface competing stakeholder priorities,
with government actors oriented toward technical deployment, private operators toward
commercial viability, and consumers  toward accessibility and convenience. Subsequent stages
bring out adoption barriers such as limited infrastructure, incompatible standards, and low
visibility, alongside incentive misalignments between public and private actors that participants
linked to  underused charging stations. This misalignment was captured in the observation that
current policy provided "no user incentives, only for manufacturing and importers", while the
dependence on imported equipment was summarized in the remark that "all charg ing equipment
is imported" (Lazuardy et al., 2024; Sasongko et al., 2024).

A further set of barriers concerned institutional dependence and geographic inequity. One
recurring observation, that "password communication held by brand owners" can block locally

Discussion
The findings respond to the four research questions by showing that Soft Systems
Methodology has been applied in public policy mainly as a problem structuring device, that its
interpretive strength is matched by a limited prescriptive capacity when used al one, and that this
limitation can be addressed by integrating it with complementary approaches at defined stages.
The central contribution of this study lies in the third point. Whereas the reviewed literature treats
economic, infrastructural, behavioral, and institutional dimensions in separate silos, the proposed
framework connects them within one structure by assigning IDT, PAT, and SCA to the stages where
each is analytically valid, with SSM and CATWOE acting as linking devices that preserve coherence
across the stages (Figure 5).

CONCLUSION
This study addresses a methodological gap in EV policy research for emerging economies by
proposing a stage specific multi methodological framework that integrates Soft Systems
Methodology with complementary approaches, using Indonesia as the primary conte xt. The four
research questions can be answered briefly. On the first, the review indicates that SSM has been
applied in public policy mainly as a problem structuring tool that supports stakeholder
engagement, contextual understanding, and iterative learni ng, including in transportation and
energy transitions. On the second, its strengths lie in handling ambiguity and accommodating
multiple perspectives, while its limitations are its qualitative nature, reliance on facilitation, and
limited prescriptive pow er when used alone. On the third, the study shows that SSM can be
integrated with IDT, PAT, and SCA through stage specific alignment, so that behavioral, institutional,
and strategic dimensions are addressed without losing coherence. On the fourth, the key
implementation considerations identified are stakeholder diversity, institutional fragmentation,
and the need for adaptive and iterative policy processes.
These answers carry two kinds of implication. Theoretically, the study contributes a
structured way to combine interpretive, behavioral, institutional, and strategic perspectives within
a single coherent protocol, advancing how Problem Structuring Methods can be integrated rather
than applied in isolation. Practically, the six stage framework offers policymakers a flexible basis
for analyzing and designing EV policy in settings marked by fragmentation and competing interests.
These contributions should be r ead with appropriate caution. The framework is proposed rather
than fully tested, it has so far received only preliminary validation through a systematic literature
review and an illustrative application refined in an expert workshop, and further empirical testing
is needed before its utility can be established. While it is developed for Indonesia and may inform
comparable emerging economies, its transferability to other institutional and socio economic
settings remains to be examined.

LIMITATION AND FURTHER RESEARCH
This study has several limitations. First, the framework is developed primarily through a
systematic literature review and an illustrative application, so its empirical validation across
different contexts is still limited. Second, the review relied on Scopus as the single primary database
and included only English language, peer reviewed articles, which means relevant work in other
databases, in other languages, or in grey literature such as government and industry reports may
have been missed. Third, the contextual validation rested on a small pilot workshop, whose limited
number of participants constrains the strength of the validation. Fourth, the multi methodology
approach is itself demanding, since combining four approaches requires considerable expertise and
facilitation capacity for practical use. Finally, the study focuses on the Indonesian context, which
limits the generalizability of its findings to countries with different institutional and socio economic
conditions.
Building on these limitations, future research can pursue several directions. The most
immediate is empirical validation of the proposed framework through case studies, pilot
implementations, or longitudinal analysis in different policy settings, supported by validation with
a larger and more diverse group of stakeholders. Comparative studies across countries would help
assess how well the framework adapts to varying institutional environments, while broader
literature coverage that includes additional data bases, non English studies, and grey literature

system dynamics or simulation to complement the framework's qualitative insights, and could
