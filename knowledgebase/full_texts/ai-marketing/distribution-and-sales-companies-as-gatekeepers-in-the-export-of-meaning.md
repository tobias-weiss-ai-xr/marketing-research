Introduction
The media and television landscape was very different compared to today, when
the US television drama series Dallas was introduced in many countries of the
world. It premiered in the USA on April 2, 1978, on CBS. The United Kingdom
was able to enjoy the first international premiere on September 5, the same year.
Australia, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Spain followed one year later. It was
not until 1981 that Dallas was launched in many Western and Northern European
countries, including Germany (June 30), and in Israel (February 14). At that time,
there was no commercial television, not to mention streaming platforms. The series
was scheduled on the public channels: Aruz 1 in Israel on Sunday evening and
on ARD in Germany on Tuesday evening. The market shares were considerable,
especially as there was no competition on other channels. In Germany, the market
share was about 40 per cent which is about 15 million viewers every week (Mikos
1994, 215). The market share in Germany decreased when the second public chan-
nel ZDF started to show Dynasty (USA 1981–1989, ABC) on Wednesday evenings
in 1983. Since then, the television and media landscape has changed significantly.
Developments in politics, economics, and technology enabled the introduction of
commercial television in the 1980s and 1990s, and eventually streaming platforms
in the 21st century. Keywords here are deregulation, digitisation, globalisation, and
marketisation.
At the beginning of the 1980s, technological developments such as cable televi-
sion and satellite television, among others, increased the pressure on politicians to
open up television to commercial interests. However, the deregulation of the broad-
casting system must be seen as a process of “trial and error” (Pauwels and Donders
2013, 22). It should be noted here that television is subject to national regulation,
even if the European Union has introduced various directives such as “Television
Without Frontiers” in 1989 up to the revised “Audiovisual Media Service Directive”
in 2018. The political deregulation of television resulted in a dual system of public
or state and private-commercial television. That said, the development did not take
place simultaneously everywhere, but differently in different countries, “as a result
of their particular histories, cultures, and legal and political traditions” (Franquet,
Richeri, and Hibberd 2020, 257). Commercial television was introduced in most
Western European countries during the 1980s (Iosifidis, Steemers, and Wheeler

The development of commercial television was driven by technological innova-
tions such as digitisation. It enabled cable television, satellite television, the con -
vergence of the media and telecommunications industries, television distribution
via the internet (IPTV), and, finally, streaming. Commercialisation made it pos -
sible for not only national, but above all international media companies to become
involved in the television market, resulting in concentration processes (Arsenault
2012; Evens and Donders 2018; Papathanassopoulos 2002; Straubhaar 2007).
Television was losing its national character not only through satellite channels that
broadcast across borders but also through the business “that is becoming controlled
by large, and often international, corporations in search of the accumulation of
capital, economic power and political influence” (Evens and Donders 2018, 25).
Television was going global (Shimpach 2020). A global market was established. A
market “can be defined as institutions or organisations that make economic deci -
sions, where particular goods or services are bought and sold at prices negotiated
between buyers and sellers” (Lee and Jin 2018, 55). On the global television mar -
ket programmes such as films, television shows and drama series are traded. While
television via satellite already crossed borders, global streaming platforms such as
Amazon Prime Video and Netflix can offer their content anywhere in the world.
This gives television drama series a global reach that did not exist before.
Digitisation knows no national borders, which is why it is making a significant
contribution to the “global shift”. Access to the global market is being simplified,
partly because digital multinational companies are engaging in the television busi-
ness (Chalaby 2023, 25). In addition to the concentration on a few multinational
companies, deregulation and digitisation have led to the rapid growth of television
channels. To name some examples: Since 1990, the number of television channels
in Europe has increased significantly, from 103 in 1990 to 1100 in 2003 (Iosifidis
2012, 9), 5370 in 2015 and finally 9434 in 2023, supplemented by 3269 video-on-
demand platforms (Schneeberger 2024). “In the middle of 2009, there are close
to 470 Arabic language satellite channels” in the Middle East (Kraidy and Khalil
2009, 31). They all broadcast not just a few hours a day, but around the clock, 24/7.

As a result, the demand for content has grown enormously. Good times for content
producers, but also for sales agents and distribution companies.
The television landscape has changed radically since the 1980s. Technological,
economic, political, ideological, and social factors have contributed to this
(Michalis 2013, 51). The multiplicity of channels and streaming providers has led
to a fragmentation of both supply and audience. A series like Dallas, which, to a
certain extent, reached a huge audience around the world on mainly public service
channels without competition, would no longer be possible under these conditions.
The global market for television series has expanded, as the numerous “flows and
contra-flows” show, from Latin American telenovelas (Sinclair and Straubhaar
2013; Straubhaar 2007, 152–66) or from Danish television series (Waade, Redvall,
and Jensen 2020). With global streaming providers, the dominance of US series
has grown even more, even if the platforms produce local original series. Sales
agents and distribution companies have an important role in this global circulation
of drama series.
Transnational Television, International Co-production and the
Distribution of TV Drama Series
From the very beginning, television was international; programmes were
exchanged, copied, or adapted. Havens (2006, 16) has stated that “the international
exchange of television programmes goes back almost as far as television broad -
casting itself.” However, it should be considered that television is always subject to
national legislation. This is also the case in times of global television. Multinational
media groups are confronted with national or transnational, e.g. European, legisla-
tion. Therefore, there is no free flow of television series because they are produced
under local laws, have to conform to trade laws and agreements, have to observe
tax laws in the respective country of production as well as copyright laws. Even
though the European Union is striving to harmonise these laws, the production of
television is subject to the copyright laws of the countries in which it is produced.
The principle of territoriality dominates (Rasmussen 2018). This means that series
that are broadcast in one country cannot be watched in another. Geo-blocking is the
keyword here (Elkins 2021; Rasmussen 2018). Unless a TV channel in the other
country has acquired licensing rights to broadcast the TV series. This is where sales
agents and distribution companies come into play, who deal with such licences.
Transnational television series are always bound to national contexts, on the
one hand to national regulation, but also to the national audiences that watch them.
Television is part of a transnational television culture and
is crossing borders, but anchored in national television cultures, it overcomes
the distinction of the global and the local and can be conceptualized as a
social and cultural process in a transnational arena where agents, institutions
and structures interact with one another.
(Mikos 2020, 76)

Elke Weissmann (2012, 9) has rightly pointed out the following connection: “The
national, then, already includes the transnational, and vice versa”. Television is
always local/national and global/transnational at the same time. This transnational-
ity is evident both in terms of production and distribution, the television series as
cultural texts themselves and the audience (Mikos 2020, 75–81).
Various agents are active in a transnational television culture, from production
and distribution companies to sales agents and audiences. Adrian Athique (2016,
11) therefore speaks of transnational audiences that cultivate a “non-resident mode
of media consumption”. Viewers of television drama series in all parts of the world
form a community – in the 1980s the Dallas community and in the 21st century
communities around series such as Game of Thrones (USA 2011–2019, HBO),
The Lord of the Rings: Ring of Power (USA 2022–, Amazon Prime Video), and
Bridgerton (USA 2020-, Netflix). However, there are only a few TV series that still
reach huge audiences. This is because the market for TV drama series is large and
fragmented. In 2022, for example, “599 English-language scripted shows” were
produced in the USA (Porter 2023). The following year, the number fell to under
500 series. According to the European Audiovisual Observatory, 1435 series were
produced in Europe in 2022 (Schneeberger and Fontaine 2023, 14).
The increase in the number of TV channels and streaming platforms mentioned
above has not only led to a fragmentation of the market with an oversupply of
content such as television drama series, but also to a fragmentation of the audi-
ence. The number of viewers per series is falling, even if they reach a global
audience. This audience is fundamentally transnational, as Adrian Athique (2016,
11) has noted: “In practice, any social body defined through media-use is likely
to be shaped by transnational patterns of cultural consumption and association,
since what most characterizes contemporary media is its multiple sources and its
intertextuality.”
Audiences consume transnational television series that are designed for inter-
national exploitation. The large number of television channels and streaming plat-
forms that need more and more content has led, as we have seen, to an enormous
number of television series being produced and traded internationally. The TV
drama series that are traded are formatted in the sense that they fit into different
genres such as crime, thriller, and others. Their aesthetics and conventions are well
known. This genre framework is filled with local stories that deal with universal
themes. In this way, telenovelas from Latin America have found their way into the
global television market. In the early 21st century, Scandinavian drama series were
also internationally successful (Bondebjerg et. al. 2017; Gamula and Mikos 2014;
Redvall 2013; Waade, Redvall and Jensen 2020). The second decade of the 21st
century saw the international success of Turkish (Berg 2021; Kaptan and Algan
2021; Wagner and Kraidy 2023; Yesil 2015) and Korean TV drama series (Ju
2020; Oh 2018, 38–71; Park, Kim and Lee 2023). "Since 2014, the Turkish TV
industry has maintained its status as the second highest exporter of scripted TV
dramas in the world” (Kaptan and Algan 2021, 2). But the export of American
drama series is still dominant, also due to streaming platforms such as Amazon
Prime Video and Netflix as new players in the market.

In the 21st century the budgets of transnational television drama series pushed
up in the USA. Thus, the production value of many drama series increased. So
that European television series could compete with American ones, they must
also create increased production values – and they need more money for that.
International co-productions are one way to generate a higher budget. Therefore,
the first two decades of the 21st century saw an increasing number of international
co-productions in television. There’s a vivid scene of co-production of television
drama series in the European television industry based on bi-national co-produc -
tion treaties. The pooling of resources and gaining of higher budgets is necessary
for the European television industry to be able to compete with US productions.
Co-productions involve at least two partners from different countries who are
involved in the creative process (Havens 2006, 35). Hammet-Jamart, Mitric, and
Redvall (2018, 11) distinguish between co-productions as “private ventures and
official co-productions”. The former try to generate as much profit as possible as a
joint venture with a co-production and utilise opportunities such as tax incentives
and public funding. Official co-productions not only strive for profit, but also have
a cultural value and pursue cultural goals. There is a certain level of international
co-productions in the global television market. They have numerous advantages
such as access to locations, talent, and information about other production cultures
and the requirements for success in other markets. “Additionally, tax incentives and
cost sharing allow international co-production partners to create programmes with
higher production values that would not be otherwise possible to fund” (Selznick
2008, 176). But there are also disadvantages, especially in the case of official co-
productions, where bureaucratic obstacles in organisation and management are not
uncommon. Bondebjerg et al. (2017, 80–81) distinguish between the production
level, the institutional level, and the content level of international co-productions.
The production level comprises the cultural background of the production, such as
casting or editing. The institutional level is about the organisational cultures of the
partners. The content level encompasses the way in which the partners make their
respective production culture an integral part of the narrative. This can also lead to
regulatory requirements in different countries turning out to be limitations for the
narrative and the design of the series (Selznick 2008, 176).
A legislative and a cultural framework influence international co-productions
from the beginning. The cultural contexts include the experiences of the producers
with certain partners from other countries who are trusted because of a common
production history (Bondebjerg et al. 2017, 79–97). Confidence refers both to the
skills of a producer and to the ability to communicate, because a complex co-
production requires a lot of administrative work and a high degree of coordination,
which requires communication – not only within the production team but also with
lawyers and representatives of banks, funding institutions, and local authorities.
The legislative framework is also important in the planning of the project, as the
opportunities for funding and tax incentives have a significant impact on the budget.
And a high budget is more and more important in a highly competitive market in
which audiences must be found and bound. International co-productions are part
of transnational television cultures in which shared meanings are circulating. In

Sales agents, distribution companies, and buyers also play an important role
in international co-productions. The latter share a common meaning of quality

(Kuipers 2012, 554). The balance of power in the circulation of transnational tel -
evision series has shifted more and more towards sales agents and distribution
companies on the one hand, while the major streaming platforms are competing
with them with their original series on the other. The ownership of licensing rights
to television series has shifted more and more from the producers to the distribu -
tion companies.
Distribution companies that operate internationally are therefore an integral part
of the financing of a series, just as they are in the film industry. Distributors such as
Banijay Entertainment, Beta Film, Fremantle, Lumière, Red Arrow, Studio Canal,
and ZDF Studios (formerly ZDF Enterprises) are involved in the financing pro -

Gomorra (I 2014–2021, Sky Atlantic) in which Beta Film and Lumière co-pro -
duced and co-financed. This shows how the business of sales agents and distribu -
tion companies has changed since the international distribution of Dallas.
Release Windows and Distribution Companies
The global demand for television drama series must not only be realised through
increased production. The finished products must also be sent out into the global
world, they must circulate. The Hollywood studios already knew this. Their power
did not lie in production: “Rather, studios are financing and distribution machines
that bankroll production and then dominate the distribution channels to market and

release the films they finance” (Ulin 2019, 5). This is because production alone does
not generate revenue. Only sales on the domestic and international markets gener-
ate profits. In times of globalisation, studio revenues have shifted more towards
the international market. This does not apply equally to all production countries.
German films continue to earn their mostly modest profits on the domestic market.
The television market, in turn, is structured differently from the film market. State
broadcasters and public service channels must first and foremost serve the domes-
tic, national audience, while private television stations must satisfy their adver -
tising customers, who usually offer local products in their programmes. This has
changed in the globalised and digitised market. On the one hand, the high cost of
producing TV series in the USA, initially mainly in Europe, but then also in other
countries, has caused production costs to skyrocket. One of the consequences of
this was that the costs could no longer be amortised on the domestic market. Selling
the licence rights in as many countries as possible can alleviate this situation.
The trade and distribution of films is different from TV drama series and TV
shows. Although there are some similarities, the differences are still relevant.
Globalisation and digitisation have also led to numerous changes here. Traditionally,
Hollywood studios were vertically integrated, meaning that all stages on the way
to the audience, “from production through distribution and sales” were united in
the company (Havens and Lotz 2017, 35) and were controlled (Albarran 2017,
46–47). The studios had their own distribution departments. The French com -
pany Studiocanal founded distribution companies in other countries (the United
Kingdom, France, Germany, Australia, and New Zealand) (Meir 2019, 4). In times
of the monopoly of state television and public television, there was no compulsion
to sell series internationally for economic reasons. The broadcasters did not have
their own distribution departments. This only changed with globalisation and dig -
itisation in the 1990s. In 1993, the German broadcaster ZDF founded a subsidiary,
ZDF Enterprises, which was responsible for purchasing series and other formats,
distribution, and co-productions. It was renamed ZDF Studios in 2022. In contrast,
the Danish broadcaster DR has its own sales department. In horizontal integration,
the various stages of the exploitation chain for a film or TV series are distributed
across different companies. This is where independent, autonomous distribution
companies such as Beta Film in Germany come into play. In Turkey, there are
independent distribution companies such as Global Agency and ITV Inter Medya
(Yesil 2015, 53–54), which are responsible for the global success of Turkish drama
series. Although these companies buy and sell films and TV drama series as their
main business, they also do marketing, press releases, and advertising for the
products.
Sales agents are important because they have a network of buyers who then
bring the films or TV drama series internationally to cinemas, TV channels, or
streaming platforms where audiences can watch them. However, this is only pos -
sible if the distribution companies have the licence rights for certain territories or
even worldwide. Sales agents and distribution companies are therefore important
intermediaries between producers and audiences who have turned this into a busi -
ness with profits. They also have the power to decide which films or television

series they acquire licence rights to and to which territories they then sell them.
Distribution companies and sales agents are therefore important gatekeepers who
have the power to decide which films or series are worth selling to certain terri-
tories (Crisp 2015, 2021). The circumstances of the market and the cultural char-
acteristics in the target countries must be considered. Smits (2019, 101) has also
stated: “Gatekeeping functions performed by sales agents at different stages of a
film’s life cycle have an immediate impact on cultural flows, the access produc-
ers have to markets and the range of films available to audiences”. This regulates
access to markets (Smits 2017, 120). This is not about the aesthetic or cultural
value of films and television series, but about their economic potential. Cubitt
(2005, 202) has therefore defined distribution “as the management of space-time
flows of product and money”. The territorial licence rights make up the space
aspect of distribution, in which the global networks of the companies are impor-
tant. In addition to the space aspect, the time aspect is reflected in the so-called
release windows in which films and television series are marketed (Doyle 2016;
Gershon 1997, 39; Havens 2006, 13–14; Ulin 2019, 34–56). Film and “televi-
sion programming is sold into what economists call ‘windows’, which are distinct
markets separated by either geography or time, where buyers receive differential
access and variable prices” (Havens 2006, 13). Licence agreements are also lim-
ited in time. Since the 1980s, with the deregulation of television, globalisation and
digitisation, the number of release windows has increased (Gershon 1997; Ulin
2019, 152–406).
In the years before home entertainment became established with the introduc -
tion of Video Cassette Recording (VCRs), there were only three ways of exploit -
ing films: domestic cinema, domestic television, and international exploitation in
cinemas and on television. Television drama series had only two exploitation win-
dows, one on domestic television and the other on international television channels
in other countries. With deregulation and the introduction of home entertainment,
which received a new boost thanks to digitisation with the DVD, exploitation
opportunities increased. In addition, there were more and more television channels
with which licence agreements could be concluded. “Most original linear media
has now found additional sales windows outside of its launch platform” (Ulin
2019, 34), most recently with streaming providers.
Films were first exploited in cinemas (theatrical release) for one to three months,
sometimes longer. The films were then released on video and DVD before being
shown on Pay TV after around six months and on Free TV after two to three years.
Whereas Hollywood films used to be released in cinemas in other countries around
two to three months after their cinema release in the USA, they are now released
at the same time or with a delay of one to three weeks. This is intended to prevent
piracy, i.e. the illegal distribution of films. With online exploitation and streaming
providers, these release windows have changed, and above all shortened. Films
are now released on DVD just four to six months after their cinema release, some-
times in parallel with pay-per-view or the option to download them from online
platforms. A short time later, they are then available on streaming platforms before
being released on Pay TV and two years later on Free TV. The release windows

for online and streaming exploitation have slipped between the windows for DVD

However, streaming providers are exploiting their new market power by not
releasing films in cinemas in the first place but producing them directly for their
platform (Smits 2024, 93–96). The platforms benefited from the fact that cinemas
were closed in key markets during the COVID-19 pandemic. Prior to this, they
had already started to release films on their platforms at the same time as in cin -
emas, which is called “day-and-date release” (Smits 2017, 123). The exploitation
opportunities for films have increased due to the increase in the number of release
windows, and thus also profit opportunities for the distribution companies, as a
separate price is set for each window. In addition to the rights for exploitation in
cinemas, on DVD (rental DVD and purchase DVD), online (rental and download),
Pay TV, streaming platforms and Free TV, ancillary rights are also sold for exploi-
tation in hotels, aeroplanes, clinics, computer games and for merchandising (Ulin
2019, 407–52). While around 60 per cent of revenue used to come from televi -
sion and only around 1–2 per cent from cinema, the revenues from television are
decreasing continuously, partly because the proportion of revenue from sales to
streaming platforms is increasing. The cinema market and the online market follow
different principles: “While the theatrical market is built on an assumed scarcity
of content, the online market is built on abundance of content, and this is shifting
focus from supply to demand” (Smits 2017, 123). The seemingly endless availabil-
ity of films and TV drama series in the catalogues of video-on-demand platforms
leads audiences to become accustomed to the availability of content at any time, in
any place and on any device.
While these largely fixed release windows exist for film, they are not so strictly
defined for television. Originally, television series were part of a so-called output
deal, in which a package of films and series was sold by the studios to television
broadcasters around the world or to distribution companies for several years. TV
drama series were part of a programme package from the studios that also included
films. In Germany, the Kirch Media Group was active with its distribution com -
pany Beta Film. “By the 1970s, Kirch held a virtual monopoly on imported films
and television programmes in Germany” (Havens 2006, 30). The films and drama
series were then sold to the two public television stations that existed in Germany
at the time. In other countries, American series were then released one to sev -
eral years after their initial broadcast. Dallas, for example, could only be seen in
Germany three years after its launch in the USA. In Hungary, the series was not
even shown until 1990, twelve years later. This also had to do with the fact that it
was only after the fall of the Berlin Wall that a cultural openness to Western films
and TV series emerged in the countries of the former Eastern Bloc, including on
state television.
Television drama series were later also released on video and DVDs, offered
for download and streamed on video-on-demand platforms (Ulin 2019, 42–43).
The time frames were not as fixed as for films. However, it took a few months
for a TV channel’s original series to become available on DVD. International
sales played a major role in the release windows for television series before

more porous and overlapping, rights owners are confronted with the problem
that shepherding content through a series of neatly delineated windows is no
longer feasible” (Doyle 2016, 635).
The increase in the number of release windows is also accompanied by increased
opportunities for distribution companies to maximise profits. “In global television
sales, the variety of windows, the prices available in those windows and the rela -
tionship among windows in different locations offer multiple possibilities for dis -
tributors to maximise profit” (Havens 2006, 38). This is also due to the fact that
companies can grant different rights per release window. The licence rights can
be limited or unlimited in time, they can be exclusive, they can be secondary or
third-party exploitation rights, they can allow a television channel to also distrib -
ute the series on another channel of the same media company, they can relate to
individual territories (the standard) or to several territories or to regions or to the
whole world. Streaming rights to TV series are allocated, for example, to the so-
called DACH region, a geo-linguistic market with German language, consisting of
Germany, Austria, and Switzerland. The prices for the rights are based on the size
of the respective market in the territories, i.e. global rights are more expensive than
national rights and rights for a large country such as Germany are more expensive
than those for a small country such as Bulgaria. However, the market share of
the TV channel or streaming platform, its reputation and economic opportunities
also play a role. The online market has also changed a lot here. Netflix usually
holds the rights to its original series for the global market, i.e. all territories in
which Netflix is active, and often for an unlimited time. To date, Netflix has only
rarely released the series on DVD in individual territories. Netflix series have not
yet been sub-licensed for other TV channels or other streaming platforms, as the
company attaches great importance to exclusive rights. The licensing of TV series
has become very complex, and “the rights attached to a TV show are extensive”
(Chalaby 2023, 113–14). On the one hand, it is possible for the distribution com -

distributing the licence rights to the various windows in such a way that they do not
compete with each other has become very complex.
Both dependent and independent distribution companies are active in the licence
market for television series. The dependent companies belong to studios such as
Disney Media Distribution or Studiocanal in Europe. In these cases, it is often
difficult to draw the line between production and distribution, even if “distribu -
tion is understood as a clearly separate activity from production” (Cubitt 2005,
200). Independent companies are not directly linked to studios, even if they some-
times sell films or television series for studios. The market for these companies has
changed considerably since the 1980s. In the days of the public monopoly and the
duopoly with commercial broadcasters, films and TV drama series were bought
and sold via output deals. The distribution companies bought a large package of
content for several years, which they then sold individually to the TV stations.
So-called canned programmes were traded, i.e. films or television drama series that
had already been completed. These were then subtitled or dubbed in the respec -
tive territories. With the increase in television channels and ultimately streaming
platforms in times of digitalisation and digitisation, the demand for content also
increased, as did production costs due to the competition. As a result, produc -
tion companies were looking for partners to help them cope with the high costs.
Independent production companies were the most obvious choice, and from then
on they became involved as co-producers or co-financiers. This experience in turn
led to distribution companies acquiring shares in production companies or buying
them outright. ZDF Studios owns 15 subsidiaries with stakes of between 26 and
100 per cent as well as additional stakes in music, rights and service companies
(ZDF Studios 2024a). In 2021, Beta Film has 47 investments in production compa-
nies with shares of between 25.5 and 100 per cent. The company also holds shares
in investment and management companies (Beta Film 2023). In the competition
for content on the fragmented TV and streaming market, distribution companies
are becoming involved in the creation process of TV drama series at an ever earlier
stage by getting involved in the story development phase. The companies’ activi -
ties have diversified, from trading in licensing rights for canned programmes to
co-productions, co-financing, and story development.
In order to illustrate the role of distribution companies in transnational televi -
sion culture, I would like to look at two examples from Germany: ZDF Studios,
formerly ZDF Enterprises, and Beta Film, formerly part of the Kirch Media Group.
ZDF Studios has the international licence rights for 625 television drama series.
Beta Film’s portfolio comprises more than 4000 titles, including around 402 series
(imdb .c om). Both companies are also active as co-producers and co-financiers.
ZDF Studios was founded in 1993 under the name ZDF Enterprises as a sub -
sidiary of the public broadcaster ZDF, which was and is responsible “for global
programme distribution, realisation of international co-productions, acquisition of
licences and merchandising of successful programme brands under its own name,
for ZDF and third parties” (ZDF Studios 2024b). Until the 2010s, the company
was largely responsible for the global distribution of Danish drama series. It had
“the worldwide distribution rights (excluding the Nordic region) for the majority of

Danish series” and sold series such as The Killing (original title: Forbrydelsen, DK
2007–2012) to the BBC, among others (Eichner and Esser 2020, 194). This was
based on a collaboration of ZDF Enterprises and Danish broadcaster DR (Eichner
and Esser 2020, 193–94; Redvall 2013, 53; Redvall 2020, 127), based on personal
connections. It turned out to work for the broadcaster: “DR benefited from ZDF’s
substantial investment, which allowed for productions values be raised” (Eichner
and Esser 2020, 193). On the other hand, ZDF Enterprises benefited from selling
the DR drama series into the world.
Beta Film was founded in 1959 as part of the Kirch Media Group. Within the
company empire there was a rival company, Taurus Film, for the acquisition of
rights. Kirch’s companies made output deals with Hollywood studios such as
Paramount, RKO, Universal, and Warner (Havens 2006, 31; Radtke 1996, 22).
They then sold the acquired films and TV drama series to the public broadcast -
ers ARD and ZDF. Beta Film also held the licence rights to Dallas, whereby the
Kirch Group and its companies had a virtual monopoly (Havens 2006, 30). "Beta/
Taurus grew into a corporate empire that had no equivalent in Europe in the field
of purchasing and exploiting feature films and television programmes” (Naeher
1989, 26). Business went very well and was further fuelled by the introduction
of commercial television. But when Kirch lost a bet on the exploitation of sports
rights, the company went bankrupt in 2002 (Havens 2006, 31; Mikos 2006). In
2004, Jan Mojto, a former Kirch manager, bought Beta Film from the insolvency
estate and adapted it over time to the changes in the digital and global market.
The company is not only active as a licence trader but also as a co-producer and
co-financier of television series. Collaborations were also established with part -
ners in the Arab region: “Beta Film and O3 Productions, the production arm of
Middle Eastern broadcaster MBC, are partnering on a major multi-series deal to
coproduce scripted projects which bridge the Arab and European worlds. The deal
marks the first major partnership O3 has undertaken with a European company,
and a number of series are already in development under the new agreement” (Beta
Film 2018). MBC (Middle East Broadcasting Centre) was founded in 1991 as a
satellite broadcaster, owned by a Saudi company (Kraidy and Khalil 2009, 20) and
advanced to a leading media company in the Middle East, operating several chan -
nels throughout the region. In 2010 the company launched the streaming platform
Shahid (Al-Shagra 2010). There have also been collaborations of Beta Film with
Brazilian production companies since 2024.
Beta Film is very active when it comes to acquiring the licence rights to signa -
ture series. For example, the company acquired the European distribution rights
to Skam (NOR 2015–2017, NRK). It was also involved in the adaptations of the
series in Belgium, Germany, France, Italy, the Netherlands, and Spain (Sundet
2021, 66–69). In Germany and Italy, Beta Film produced the adaptations via sub -
sidiaries. One of Beta Film’s prestige projects was its involvement in the series
Babylon Berlin. For the first time, there was a series that was a co-production of
the public broadcaster ARD through its subsidiary ARD Degeto, and the Pay-TV
channel Sky, with the participation of Beta Film and the production company X
Filme Creative Pool, in which Beta Film in turn holds a 24.9 per cent share (Mikos

2021, 184–185). The distribution company held the worldwide distribution rights.
The series was sold to more than 140 territories, including the USA, where it was
shown on Netflix and branded as a Netflix Original. This cooperation also had con-
sequences for the release windows. Babylon Berlin premiered on the pay TV chan-
nel Sky and was only shown on the public broadcaster ARD for free a year later.
The first seasons of the series can now also be streamed on Netflix in Germany.
Beta Film was also involved in the production of The Swarm (original title: Der
Schwarm, GER 2023, ZDF). The series was produced by Intaglio Films, a com -
pany in which Beta Film holds a 49 per cent stake and ZDF Studios a 51 per cent
stake (ZDF Studios 2024a). The unique aspect of this cooperation is that both com-
panies have the worldwide distribution rights ( imdb .c om). The new conditions of
the television market in the streaming era are also leading to new business models
for distribution companies, as the example of Beta Film and ZDF Studios shows.
Conclusion
The changes in the international television landscape brought about by deregula -
tion in the 1980s and globalisation and digitisation in the 1990s have had a lasting
impact on the “flows” of television series. The days of Dallas are over, when trad-
ing programmes were very simple. Output deals were negotiated with Hollywood
studios that included both films and TV series as canned programmes. In the 21st
century, trading has become considerably more complex, especially as more and
more players are active in the business. Since the 1980s, not only have the number
of television channels and content increased significantly, but more and more pro-
duction and distribution companies are also active on the global market. As gate -
keepers, the latter help decide which films and television series are worth selling in
certain countries or even worldwide.
In general, distribution can therefore be defined as follows: “By enabling or
inhibiting end user access to cultural forms, distribution contributes to the forma -
tion of mediated publics, communities, and identities. Limiting when and where
media are available, distribution defines the temporal and spatial conditions in
which media exist” (Donoghue, Havens and McDonald 2021, 4). Sales agents and
distribution companies can help to put previously underrepresented countries on
the map of global television trade, as the example of Turkish drama series shows
(Yesil 2015). Release windows play an important role in the global distribution
of television series. These exploitation windows have increased since the 1990s,
when films and series could also be streamed. This has made the business more
complicated and complex for distribution companies and sales agents. It is becom-
ing increasingly difficult to keep track of which release windows are relevant for
which broadcasters or streaming platforms in which country. “But even if window-
ing has become more complex, it is still highly relevant” (Doyle 2016, 641). This
is because it is important for all parties who own the intellectual property rights to
TV drama series to maximise their profits with each release window.
In the digitised and global television market, there are no longer just “flows and
contra-flows” (Thussu 2007), but these flows have diversified. There is a multitude

of flows: global, regional, and national. There is not only a flow of telenovelas
around the world, but also a flow of Turkish drama series to Latin America and
the Arab world, or a flow of Icelandic drama series within Scandinavia and Central
Europe. At the same time, this is linked to exclusion processes. If Danish crime
series are globally popular, crime series of other origins are no longer bought and
sold because the focus of the sales agents and distribution companies is then dif -
ferent, as they only want to deal with series that promise international success.
Havens (2006, 40) has therefore pointed out that “the inequities in profit and pres-
tige among distributors lead almost all television merchants, distributors and buy -
ers alike, to value Western culture above all others”. However, this has changed
since the second decade of the 21st century, when the focus was on Nordic Noir,
Turkish and Korean drama series, even if they were not popular in all regions of
the world.
Distribution companies such as Beta Film and ZDF Studios are very active in
this market, contributing to the global trade in television drama series with their
networks, which extend to more and more regions – and thus making them avail -
able to transnational audiences. However, this trend of diversifying the production
and trade of TV drama series runs counter to another trend, as “content aggregation
is concentrated in American hands. The online services of US-based media con -
glomerates and tech firms largely dominate the global streaming market” (Chalaby
2023, 190). This is particularly true from a Western perspective, as platforms such
as Amazon Prime Video and Netflix are available all over the world, with a few
exceptions.
However, this masks the fact that local streaming platforms play an almost
equally important role in many regions, such as Viaplay in Scandinavia, the Czech
platform Voyo in Central Europe, the Qatari platform OSN (Orbit Showtime
Network) and the Saudi-based Shahid in the Arab world, the Malaysian platform
iFlix and Viu, based in Hong Kong, in Southeast Asia or iQIYI and Tencent Video
in China. This shows that the power of the US platforms is not limitless. In this
diversified market, sales agents and distribution companies are becoming increas -
ingly important because all these streaming platforms, like the countless TV chan-
nels, need more and more content. Even if it is no longer as simple as it was in
the days of Dallas, sales agents and distribution companies will retain their role
as gatekeepers for the export of meaning, even if the entire television trade has
become increasingly diverse and complex.
