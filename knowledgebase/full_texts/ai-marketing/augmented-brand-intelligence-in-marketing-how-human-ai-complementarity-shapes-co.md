Abstract
Artificial intelligence (AI) is reshaping marketing and consumer-brand
interactions through personalization, efficiency, prediction, and scalability. Its
expanding role, however, raises a more fundamental question: what does AI add to
consumer interactions, and where does human involvement remain especially
valuable? Using signaling theory as its principal explanatory lens, this chapter
develops the concept of augmented brand intelligence to explain how human and
artificial intelligence can function as complementary sources of brand signals in
consumer-facing interactions. A structured critical synthesis of research on AI in
marketing, human-AI collaboration, consumer responses to AI, signaling, and
consumer-brand relationships suggests that human involvement can signal
relational capability through empathy, contextual judgment, relational
understanding, and authentic engagement, whereas AI can signal technological
capability through efficiency, personalization, analytical capability, and
innovation. The framework distinguishes signal complementarity from signal
congruence: complementarity concerns whether human and AI signals contribute
distinct, nonredundant information about the brand or the capabilities it offers,
whereas congruence concerns whether those signals convey compatible meanings
about the brand. Complementary signals are expected to reduce perceived
information asymmetry more strongly when congruence is high, because

3.1 Introduction
Artificial intelligence has moved from the background of marketing
operations into the consumer-facing core of many brand interactions. AI-
enabled systems now support consumer insight generation, personalized
communication, recommendation, customer service, content creation, and
relationship management, enabling brands to respond at speeds and scales
previously difficult to achieve (Grewal et al., 2025). Generative and
conversational AI have pushed this shift further by taking part in activities
once associated mainly with human marketers, service employees, and
brand communicators. Consequently, a combination of human and
artificial intelligence, rather than either source alone, produces many
contemporary consumer-brand interactions. That combination creates a
distinct theoretical problem. Consumers usually cannot see how an AI-
supported interaction was produced, how much human judgment shaped it,
or where responsibility ultimately sits (Van Pinxteren et al., 2015). A
personalized recommendation may combine algorithmic analysis with
human-defined strategic priorities; an AI-assisted service encounter may
pair machine-generated guidance with an employee's contextual judgment;
and brand communication may emerge through repeated exchanges
between human creativity and generative AI. Consumers are therefore
asked to evaluate hybrid interactions while inferring the brand's
capabilities, intentions, and relational orientation from the cues that are
visible to them.
 Existing research provides important pieces of this puzzle but not yet a
complete account of the consumer's interpretation of the hybrid
configuration. One stream examines how AI improves automation,

appear together, whether the two provide distinct information, and whether
their meanings fit the brand consistently. This leaves a gap between
understanding human-AI collaboration as a functional arrangement and
understanding it as a set of signals through which consumers interpret what
the brand stands for and how it delivers value.
 Augmented brand intelligence (ABI) is introduced to address this gap.
The conceptualization of ABI builds on the work by Kirkby et al. (2025),
who emphasize it as a consumer-facing brand configuration in which
human and artificial intelligence capabilities are strategically orchestrated
so that their observable or reasonably inferable contributions jointly signal
the brand's technological and relational qualities. It therefore shifts
attention from AI adoption itself to how the interplay between human and
AI capabilities shapes consumers' perceptions of the brand. Human
involvement may convey empathy, contextual judgment, relational
understanding, and authentic engagement, whereas effective AI
involvement may convey efficiency, personalization, analytical capability,
and technological innovation. The argument is not that these qualities
belong exclusively to people or machines. What matters is which source
consumers regard as the more credible signal of a particular capability in
an interaction.
 Signaling theory is especially well suited to this problem because the
central issue is inference under information asymmetry (Connelly et al.,
2011; Spence, 1973). Building on this perspective, the ABI framework
develops two distinct properties of a human-AI configuration.
Complementarity captures whether the two sources contribute
differentiated, nonredundant information, whereas congruence captures
whether the meanings conveyed by those signals are coherent and mutually
consistent. When both conditions are favorable, the configuration should

3.2 Approach and Scope of the Critical Synthesis
The chapter uses a structured, theory-driven critical synthesis rather than a
formal systematic review. The unit of evidence is peer-reviewed journal
research, supplemented by official company materials in Section 3.7 for
illustrative cases. The core literature is primarily from 2015-2026, with
earlier foundational work retained where needed for signaling theory,
technology acceptance, service-dominant logic, brand credibility,
consumer confidence, relationship marketing, and brand trust (Snyder,
2019). Source identification was organized around five concept clusters:
AI in marketing and branding; human-AI collaboration and augmentation;
consumer responses to AI; signaling, information asymmetry, and brand
credibility; and consumer-brand relationships, confidence, and trust.
Targeted combinations of these focal concepts were used to locate relevant
studies, with foundational and review articles used to trace additional
pertinent work.
 Inclusion was based on direct relevance to the focal question: how
consumers interpret observable or reasonably inferable human and AI cues
in brand interactions. Priority was given to studies on AI-mediated
experiences, role allocation, algorithmic judgment, perceived competence,

3.3 Theoretical Foundation and Positioning
3.3.1 Signaling theory and information asymmetry
Signaling theory addresses situations in which parties possess unequal
information and a less-informed receiver uses observable signals to infer
qualities that are otherwise difficult to observe (Connelly et al., 2011;
Spence, 1973). Consumers routinely face this problem. They cannot
directly observe a brand's intentions, technological competence, relational
orientation, or the processes behind a particular interaction, so they rely on
cues such as reputation, warranties, endorsements, communication
practices, and brand credibility (Connelly et al., 2025). A recent systematic
review confirms that signaling theory remains relevant to marketing
questions involving information asymmetry and communication (Shahid et
al., 2024). AI makes this inference problem more complex. A consumer
may receive an AI-supported recommendation or service response without
knowing how the system works, what data informed the output, or how
much human review took place. The presence of an employee does not
necessarily resolve that uncertainty; the employee may be exercising
independent judgment or simply relaying an algorithmic decision (Kaur
Nagpal & Cotte, 2026). ABI is therefore a multi-source signaling
environment in which human involvement can convey relational signals,
while AI conveys technological capability signals. The important question
is not simply whether either signal is favorable, but whether the two make
sense together.
3.3.2 Signaling Theory in Relation to Adjacent Perspectives
Signaling theory is the principal lens here, but adjacent perspectives
remain informative. Technology acceptance research, especially the
Technology Acceptance Model, explains adoption through constructs such
as perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use (Xue et al., 2026). That
perspective is useful when the primary question is whether a consumer will

use an AI-enabled technology. ABI asks a different question: what does a
consumer infer about the brand when human and AI capabilities jointly

 Service-Dominant Logic (S-D logic) contributes a different insight by
emphasizing resource integration, interaction, and value co-creation rather
than locating value solely in products or technologies. More recent work
extends this relational and systemic view to the AI era (Vargo et al., 2026).
S-D logic therefore helps explain how human and technological resources
participate in value creation. Signaling theory is more directly relevant to
the narrower issue examined here: how consumers use observable human
and AI cues to infer hidden brand qualities when information is
incomplete. human-AI collaboration research provides a third foundation
by identifying the capabilities that people and AI bring to tasks and by
distinguishing augmentation from automation (Raisch & Krakowski,
2021). The analysis here shifts the focus downstream, to the consumer.
Rather than combining theories indiscriminately, it uses each body of
literature for the question it handles best: technology acceptance research
for adoption, S-D logic for relational value creation, human-AI research for
capability allocation, and signaling theory for consumer inference from the
resulting configuration.
3.4 Conceptualizing Augmented Brand Intelligence
3.4.1 Defining and bounding augmented brand intelligence
Several adjacent concepts, such as AI-enabled marketing, marketing
automation, collaborative AI, human-AI augmentation, and hybrid
intelligence, illustrate how technological and human capabilities can be
combined. ABI differs in its unit of analysis. It is concerned with the
consumer-facing meaning of that combination: not simply whether humans
and AI work together, but what consumers infer about a brand when both
contributions are visible or can reasonably be inferred from the interaction.
ABI is therefore defined as a consumer-facing brand configuration in
which human and artificial intelligence capabilities are strategically
orchestrated so that their observable or inferable contributions jointly
signal the brand's relational and technological qualities. Four boundaries
sharpen the construct. First, ABI is configurational rather than additive:

merely placing a human and an AI system in the same process is not
enough. Second, it is augmentative rather than purely substitutive: its
relational value depends on whether consumers perceive human and AI
contributions as meaningfully differentiated and complementary. Third, it
is consumer-facing: invisible back-end AI may improve organizational
intelligence without creating the signaling process developed here. Fourth,
it is brand-level: consumers read the configuration as evidence about the
brand, not just about the tool they happen to encounter.
 These boundaries distinguish ABI from adjacent concepts without
claiming that the underlying human-AI combination is itself new. AI-
enabled marketing is the broad use of AI in marketing; human-AI
collaboration focusses on how tasks are shared or improved; and hybrid
intelligence focuses on combined human-machine problem solving. ABI
adds a different unit of analysis: the consumer's brand-level inference from
observable or reasonably inferable human and AI contributions. Thus, ABI
explains what the configuration communicates about the brand, not simply
how the configuration performs. They also avoid a rigid human-versus-
machine classification of capabilities. AI can simulate warmth and
generate creative content, while humans can contribute contextual
judgement and nuanced interpretation. The relevant question is contextual:
which source is perceived as the more credible signal of a given capability?
Evidence of both task-dependent algorithm aversion and algorithm
appreciation supports this more contingent view (Qin et al., 2025). Thus,
ABI does not assume that either human or AI contributions are inherently
more credible; rather, their signaling value depends on the capability being
communicated and how consumers interpret the respective contributions in
context.
3.4.2 Human intelligence as a relational capability signal
Human intelligence remains strategically important because meaningful
human involvement can reveal qualities that consumers may not readily
infer from an automated encounter. In consumer-facing settings, these
qualities include empathy, contextual judgment, relational understanding,
and authentic engagement (Rubin et al.,2025). Huang and Rust (2018)
similarly distinguish intuitive and empathetic intelligence from mechanical
and analytical forms, while research on algorithm aversion indicates that
consumers are less willing to rely on algorithms when a task is perceived
as subjective (Castelo et al., 2019). Human presence, however, is not the

3.4.3 Artificial intelligence as a technological capability signal
AI offers a different source of information. Contemporary systems can
process large datasets, identify patterns, generate content, personalize
recommendations, and support decisions at speeds and scales difficult to
reproduce through human effort alone (Grewal et al., 2025). Within ABI,
however, AI becomes a technological signal only when consumers directly
experience, are informed of, or can reasonably infer its contribution; purely
backstage AI remains outside the construct. In such cases, AI can signal
efficiency, personalization, analytical competence, and innovation. The
presence of AI is not, by itself, a favorable signal; same automation may be
read as efficiency in one setting and as relational withdrawal in another.
Poor recommendations, opaque automation, or inaccurate responses can
signal technological weakness rather than sophistication. The strongest
technological signal arises when AI makes an identifiable contribution that
consumers regard as useful. This interpretation is consistent with evidence
that consumers may resist AI when tasks involve uniqueness or subjective
judgement but become more receptive when AI supports rather than
replaces human decision-making (Zehnle et al., 2025).
 P2: Greater perceived value contribution of artificial intelligence to a
consumer-facing brand interaction strengthens consumers' inference that
the brand possesses technological capability.

3.5 Human-AI Signal Complementarity and
Congruence
3.5.1 Signal complementarity
Consumers usually interpret multiple cues together. Within ABI, signal
complementarity describes the extent to which human and AI signals
provide distinct, nonredundant information about the brand's capabilities. It
concerns difference in diagnostic contribution, not consistency of meaning.
AI may provide rapid information processing and personalization, while
human involvement adds contextual judgment and relational sensitivity;
each can therefore resolve a different part of the consumer's uncertainty (Li
et al., 2024). Complementarity is not the same as duplication. If an AI
system generates a standard response and an employee simply repeats it,
the second source adds little diagnostic information. The reverse is also
true: keeping a person in a routine task already handled well by AI may
increase cost without adding signaling value. Effective augmentation
therefore depends on purposeful role differentiation rather than on
maximizing either human or AI involvement (Fügener et al., 2026).
Crucially, that differentiation must be visible to the consumer.
Managerially distinct roles matter for the model only when consumers can
recognize, experience, or reasonably infer what each source contributes.
This role-differentiation logic is consistent with research showing that
consumer responses to human-AI collaboration depend on how
responsibilities are configured and whether human involvement remains
meaningful rather than nominal (Huang & Rust, 2022).
 P3: The more consumers perceive human and AI capability signals as
differentiated and nonredundant, the greater their perceived human-AI
signal complementarity.
 P4: Greater perceived human-AI signal complementarity reduces
perceived information asymmetry in consumer-brand interactions.
3.5.2 Signal congruence
Complementarity is necessary but not sufficient. Two differentiated signals
can still point in different directions. human-AI signal congruence reflects
whether the meanings conveyed by human and AI contributions are
sufficiently compatible to support a coherent interpretation of the brand.
Congruence is therefore about fit in meaning, not similarity in content.

Recent marketing evidence likewise shows that signals from different

but the second source adds little new information. Complementarity
therefore concerns informational difference; congruence concerns semantic
fit.

3.6 From Signals to Consumer-Brand Relationship
Outcomes
3.6.1 Information asymmetry and consumer evaluative confidence
Signaling matters because it helps consumers make more reliable
inferences under uncertainty, not simply because it increases the amount of
information available. Here, perceived information asymmetry refers to the
consumer's sense that important information about the brand's capabilities,
processes, or interaction intentions is missing or difficult to infer. Brand
credibility research shows that credible signals can lower -uncertainty and
information costs and improve the basis for consumer choice (Guo & Luo,
2023). When human and AI signals are both complementary and
congruent, consumers have a broader and more coherent basis for judging
the brand's technological and relational capabilities. That reduction in
ambiguity should influence both consumer perceptions and their
confidence in the validity of their judgments. We refer to this state as
consumer evaluative confidence: the subjective certainty that available
information provides a sufficiently clear and credible basis for evaluating
the brand and its capabilities. Confidence is about certainty, not
favorability; consumers can be highly confident in either a positive or a
negative judgment. Prior consumer research likewise shows that
confidence can vary independently of the content of judgments and be
shaped by how information is processed and interpreted (Rocklage et al.,
2026).

3.6.2 Consumer evaluative confidence and brand trust
Evaluative confidence and brand trust are related but conceptually distinct.
Evaluative confidence is epistemic certainty that one's judgment is
adequately founded; brand trust is the willingness to rely on the brand
based on expectations of its reliability, integrity, or benevolence, despite
uncertainty or vulnerability. Research on brand trust emphasizes reliability
and brand intentions as foundations of consumer reliance (Hegner &
Jevons, 2016), while relationship marketing identifies trust as central to
successful relational exchange (Badrinarayanan & Ramachandran,2024).
The distinction also clarifies the proposed sequence. A consumer may be
quite certain that an AI-enabled service is technologically sophisticated,
yet still unwilling to trust the brand with sensitive information. Conversely,
a well-established brand may retain trust even when consumers are unsure
about a newly introduced AI feature. Confidence should facilitate trust
because reliance becomes easier once consumers feel that their
understanding of the brand rests on a sufficiently sound informational
basis.
 P7: Greater consumer evaluative confidence in a human-AI-enabled
brand interaction increases brand trust.
3.6.3 Brand trust and relationship outcomes
Brand trust carries the effects of ABI beyond a single encounter.
Consumers who trust a brand may be more willing to continue using its AI-
enabled services, accept personalized recommendations, and sustain the
relationship despite some uncertainty around technologically mediated
exchange. The focus here is trust in the brand, not generalized trust in AI.
Someone can remain cautious about AI as a technology while still trusting
a particular brand to deploy it competently and responsibly. Greater brand
trust should, in turn, support continued relationship intention, purchase
intention, recommendation intention, and brand advocacy. These outcomes
vary in behavioral intensity, but they share the same relational foundation:
consumers are more willing to stay with, buy from, recommend, or
advocate for a brand they feel able to rely on.

congruence. Third, those interpretations shape perceived information
asymmetry, evaluative confidence, brand trust, and relationship outcomes.

3.7 Augmented Brand Intelligence in Practice
The practical value of the ABI framework becomes clearer when
considered within a real-world brand ecosystem where artificial
intelligence is integrated with established forms of human expertise to
augment the brand's capacity for intelligence generation and interpretation.
In this regard, L'Oréal provides a useful illustrative case, as its Beauty
Tech ecosystem brings together AI, including generative AI, with human
intelligence in the form of proprietary beauty knowledge and professional
expertise to support consumer-facing services across different stages of the
customer journey (van Doorn et al., 2017). Importantly, this case is not
presented as empirical validation of the ABI framework, nor does it
suggest that all five dimensions are implemented in the same way. Rather,
it illustrates how different configurations of human and AI capabilities may
generate meaningful signals for consumers. Accordingly, an illustration is
treated as ABI only where consumers directly encounter the AI-enabled
touchpoint or can reasonably infer AI's role from the service or brand
communication. L'Oréal's Beauty Genius, for example, combines
generative AI, augmented reality, computer vision, colour science,
proprietary beauty knowledge, and professional expertise to deliver
personalised consumer advice. From an ABI perspective, the key issue is
therefore how these complementary capabilities are integrated into specific
consumer-facing practices and the signals they convey to consumers.
3.7.1 Customer Service: AI Responsiveness with Human Escalation
L'Oréal's Beauty Genius offers consumers an AI-enabled first point of
contact for beauty-related questions, product information, and sensitive

beauty-care concerns. It provides immediate and scalable assistance, while
more complex needs can be redirected to L'Oréal's customer-care channels
for human support. This interaction illustrates a complementary signal: AI
conveys speed, availability, and information-processing capability, while
human support signals responsibility and care when further assistance is
required. This distinction is consistent with recent research showing that
consumers can respond differently to AI- and human-provided service
(Choi et al., 2024). Within the ABI framework, these distinct contributions
can provide consumers with more information about both the brand's
technological capability and its willingness to retain human support where
needed. In this sense, complementarity may help reduce uncertainty about
what the brand can provide through AI and where human expertise remains
available.
3.7.2 Personalization: AI-Scale Processing Grounded in Human
Expertise
Beauty Genius uses consumers' questions, preferences, and diagnostic
inputs to generate personalised recommendations across skincare, makeup,
hair colour, and haircare solutions. L'Oréal indicates that these
recommendations draw on its accumulated beauty knowledge alongside
scientific and professional expertise from dermatologists and hair
professionals. From an ABI perspective, the AI signal lies in the system's
ability to process individual inputs quickly and at scale, whereas the
human-expertise signal is reflected in the knowledge base underlying its
recommendations. Thus, personalization is not presented simply as
algorithmic prediction; it represents an attempt to combine computational
scalability with accumulated domain expertise. This is closer to the ABI
concept of augmentation, in which AI extends the reach and accessibility
of domain expertise while retaining its human and scientific foundations.
For consumers, the combination can make the basis of a recommendation
more understandable: AI contributes the ability to process personal
information, while human-derived expertise provides a substantive basis
for the advice (Li et al., 2024). This combination is therefore consistent
with the ABI pathway from complementary signals toward lower
perceived information asymmetry and greater confidence in evaluating the
brand's recommendations.

3.7.3 Brand Communication: AI-Enabled Creativity Under Human
and Brand Direction
L'Oréal's GenAI Beauty Content Lab provides a further illustration of
human-AI complementarity, but in a different form. Rather than tailoring
recommendations to individual consumers, the platform supports the rapid
development of brand content at scale while retaining the creative input of
artists, creators, and other human experts. From an ABI perspective, this
combination allows AI to signal speed, scalability, and generative
capability, while human involvement contributes creative judgment,
cultural understanding, and consistency with the brand's identity. This
human role is particularly relevant because recent research shows that
consumers can respond more positively to AI-assisted content when human
control remains visible in the creation process (Haupt et al., 2025). Here,
complementarity can reduce uncertainty not only about how content is
produced, but also about whether AI-generated communication remains
connected to recognisable human and brand meaning.
3.7.4 Recommendation: Computational Diagnosis Combined with
Professional Knowledge
L'Oréal's Beauty Genius can conduct tailored diagnostics and recommend
consumer-care products from a large portfolio of skincare, makeup, and
hair-colour products. Its diagnostic technologies use image-based
processing, while its recommendations draw on L'Oréal's accumulated
beauty knowledge. The AI component therefore signals analytical scale
and processing capability, whereas the professional and scientific
knowledge incorporated into the system signals human domain expertise
and credibility. The combination is particularly relevant for beauty
decisions in which consumers may want both computational convenience
and reassurance that recommendations are grounded in substantive
expertise. In ABI terms, the two signals contribute different but with
complementary information: AI indicates what the system can analyse,
while professional knowledge provides meaning and credibility to the
resulting recommendation (Kaur Nagpal & Cotte, 2026). Together, these
signals can make the basis of the brand's recommendation more transparent
and therefore potentially reduce perceived information asymmetry.

3.7.5 Customer Relationship Management: Extending Human
Expertise Through AI
Finally, L'Oréal's use of AI also extends to maintaining longer-term
interactions with consumers. Beauty Genius is designed to remain
accessible beyond a single interaction, while L'Oréal positions its broader
AI ecosystem as supporting more personalised and meaningful beauty
conversations. From an ABI perspective, this creates a relationship-
oriented signal: AI contributes continuous availability, consistency, and
scalability, allowing consumers to seek assistance when it is convenient for
them. At the same time, human expertise remains central to the knowledge
and brand meaning conveyed through these interactions (Roy et al., 2025).
Thus, the value of AI lies not merely in demonstrating technological
advancement, but in making the brand's expertise more accessible and
available.
 Taken together, these applications illustrate how different AI and human
signals can contribute complementary information across the consumer
journey. In the ABI framework, such complementarity is relevant because
it can help consumers form a clearer understanding of the brand's
capabilities, thereby reducing perceived information asymmetry and
supporting greater confidence in their evaluations.
 The next step is the role of signal congruence; complementary signals
are most meaningful when the different messages conveyed by AI
capabilities, human expertise, and the brand itself fit together consistently.
For L'Oréal, for example, technological sophistication is accompanied by
signals of professional expertise, creative judgment, care, and brand
heritage. When these signals reinforce rather than contradict one another,
they can communicate a more coherent meaning about what the brand
represents and how it operates. Thus,
 "Congruence does not replace complementarity; rather, it determines
how consistently complementary signals are interpreted as part of the same
brand meaning".
 Through this lens, the L'Oréal case illustrates the broader ABI pathway:
human and AI capabilities contribute distinct signals, their
complementarity can make the brand's capabilities more interpretable, and
their congruence can strengthen the coherence of those signals. These
processes provide the basis through which consumers may develop greater
confidence in their evaluation of the brand, which can subsequently
support trust and longer-term consumer-brand outcomes.

3.8 Theoretical Contributions
Four theoretical contributions follow from the model. First, ABI's novelty
lies not in the mere combination of humans and AI, but in the
consumerlevel signaling mechanism applied to that combination.
AIenabled marketing describes the use of AI in marketing; human-AI
collaboration and augmentation emphasize task allocation and joint
performance; and hybrid intelligence emphasizes combined
humanmachine problem solving. ABI instead explains what observable or
inferable human-AI configurations signal to consumers about the brand
itself. The second contribution is to signaling theory. ABI places the
consumer in a multisource signaling environment in which relational
signals associated with human intelligence and technological capability
signals associated with AI can appear within the same interaction. The
meaning of either signal may therefore depend partly on its relationship
with the other, not only on its independent strength (Connelly et al., 2025).
Third, the model separates complementarity from congruence as
independent properties of the same configuration. Complementarity
concerns whether human and AI sources add different, nonredundant
information; congruence concerns whether the meanings of those signals

Augmented Brand Intelligence
CHAPTER 3
71
fit together. This distinction explains why a configuration can be
informative but contradictory, or coherent but redundant, and gives the
four-cell taxonomy a clear conceptual basis (Geng et al., 2025). The fourth
contribution is the role assigned to consumer evaluative confidence.
Complementary and congruent signals should reduce informational
ambiguity and leave consumers more certain that their evaluation is
adequately found. That confidence can then support willingness to rely on
the brand. The sequence provides a plausible bridge from interaction-level
signaling to relationship-level outcomes without collapsing confidence and
trust into the same construct.
3.9 Managerial Implications
For managers, consumer-facing AI becomes a signaling decision only
when consumers can experience, are informed of, or reasonably infer its
role. Otherwise, AI is a back-end capability rather than an ABI signal. The
managerial question is therefore not only what the technology
accomplishes, but also what the visible or inferable human-AI
configuration communicates about competence, relational commitment,
and accountability.
 The practical priority is purposeful augmentation rather than
indiscriminate automation. Routine, objective, and information-heavy
tasks may suit AI; emotionally sensitive, ambiguous, or exception-based
interactions often require substantive human judgment. Managers should
specify the distinct contribution of AI and humans, ensure consumers can
recognize those roles, and check whether the two convey a coherent brand
meaning (Fügener et al., 2026). A personal-care promise paired with rigid
automation and no meaningful escalation path may be efficient, but it is
incongruent with the brand's relational signal. Measurement should reflect
the same logic. Operational indicators such as automation rate, response
time, and recommendation accuracy remain useful, but they should be read
alongside relational indicators such as perceived role clarity, human-AI
complementarity, signal congruence, information asymmetry, evaluative
confidence, and brand trust. Operational success and relational damage can
coexist (Roy et al., 2025). The managerial objective is therefore not
automation for its own sake, but a human-AI configuration that delivers
operational value without weakening the brand relationship. Figure 3.2
summarizes this managerial design logic, and Table 3.4 translates it into a
concise ABI diagnostic audit.

3.10 Future Research Agenda
Several parts of the framework now require empirical development. One
priority is measurement: future studies should develop and validate scales
for perceived human-AI signal complementarity, signal congruence, and
consumer evaluative confidence, while demonstrating that these constructs
are distinct from trust, perceived risk, credibility, and general attitudes
toward AI (Khamitov et al., 2024). Experimental work could then directly
compare alternative human-AI configurations. Factorial designs can
independently vary complementarity and congruence, while role-allocation
studies can compare human-led, AI-led, AI-supported human, human-
reviewed AI, and fully automated interactions. Such studies would provide

direct tests of both the four-configuration taxonomy and the proposed
causal relationships.
 Boundary conditions are equally important. Task subjectivity, perceived
risk, emotional involvement, product category, AI familiarity, privacy
concern, technological readiness, need for human interaction, and prior
brand reputation may all change the relative signaling value of human and
AI capabilities. Cross-cultural studies could add another layer by
examining whether expectations of automation, interpersonal service, and
accountability alter the model across markets (Qin et al., 2025). Disclosure
and responsibility merit separate attention. Consumers may respond
differently when they are told that content was generated by AI, co-created
by a human and AI, reviewed by a human, or produced with no disclosure,
consistent with evidence that AI disclosure can materially alter consumer
responses (Luo et al., 2019). An important empirical question is whether
role transparency increases confidence by clarifying who contributed what
and who remains accountable.
 Longitudinal research should also ask how these signals change as AI
becomes ordinary. Technological sophistication that currently signals
innovation may eventually become a baseline expectation. Repeated
encounters may also alter the pathway to trust; successful interactions can
build confidence, whereas repeated incongruence may erode it. The more
useful long-term question is therefore not whether consumers prefer
humans or AI, but which configurations remain credible and coherent
across consumers, contexts, and time. Table 3.5 summarizes the priority

3.11 Conclusion
AI in marketing is often discussed as a choice between human and machine
intelligence. That framing is becoming less useful as customer service,
personalization, communication, recommendation, and relationship
management increasingly combine both. The more consequential question
is how brands can organize human and artificial intelligence so that
consumers experience the combination as credible, complementary, and
coherent.
 ABI captures that problem as a consumer-facing signaling configuration.
Human involvement can signal relational capability; effective AI
involvement can signal technological capability. The value of the
arrangement depends not simply on having both sources present, but on
whether they contribute different information and whether those meanings

CHAPTER 3
76
fit together. When they do, the configuration should reduce perceived
information asymmetry, strengthen consumer evaluative confidence,
support brand trust, and improve consumer-brand relationship outcomes.
The distinction between technological performance and relational value is
therefore crucial. Efficient or highly personalized AI does not
automatically strengthen a relationship, just as a visible human presence
adds little when the person's role is merely symbolic. Effective ABI
requires substantive human responsibility, valuable AI capability, and
deliberate coordination between the two. The future of consumer-brand
interaction is less a contest between humans and machines than a design
problem involving signals, roles, and relational coherence.
