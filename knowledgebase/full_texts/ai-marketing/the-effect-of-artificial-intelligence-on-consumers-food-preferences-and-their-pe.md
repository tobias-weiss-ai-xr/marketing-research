Foreword
Artificial intelligence has evolved from being merely a subject
within the fields of engineering and computer science into a
phenomenon that is transforming virtually every aspect of daily
life, from healthcare to finance, and from education to marketing.
One of the areas where this transformation is most tangibly
felt is in consumers’ food and beverage preferences. Smart
recommendation systems, personalized menus, voice-activated
ordering assistants, and algorithmic food apps are reshaping
the consumer’s dining experience. In today’s world, where
digital transformation is reshaping nearly every aspect of life,
artificial intelligence technologies are significantly influencing
not only production, communication, and service processes
but also individuals’ daily practices, decision-making patterns,
and consumption behaviors. The food and beverage sector, in
particular, has become one of the areas most intensely affected
by this transformation through personalized recommendation
systems, smart menus, online ordering platforms, AI-powered
nutrition apps, digital assistants, automation systems, and data-
driven consumer analytics.
This book, titled “The Impact of Artificial Intelligence
on Consumers’ Perceptions of Technology Acceptance
Levels Regarding Food Preferences,” was prepared to
examine consumers’ perceptions and behaviors regarding food
preferences in light of artificial intelligence technologies from the

perspective of technology acceptance. The study’s fundamental
premise is that consumers no longer make decisions based
solely on traditional factors such as a food item’s taste, price,
quality , healthiness, or accessibility; it also highlights the fact
that they can be influenced by recommendations from digital
systems, algorithmic guidance, online reviews, and AI-powered
applications.
The increasingly evident impact of artificial intelligence on
consumer behavior is giving rise to new research questions in
the fields of gastronomy and food and beverage. A consumer’s
assessment of an AI-powered system as useful, reliable, easy
to use, and functional can influence not only their adoption
of the technology but also their food choices, either directly
or indirectly . For this reason, the perception of technology
acceptance is considered one of the key explanatory variables
in understanding contemporary consumer behavior.
In this book, the phenomenon of artificial intelligence is
addressed not merely as a technological advancement, but
as a social and economic transformative element that creates
multidimensional relationships among gastronomy , consumer
behavior, food preferences, digitalization, and technology
acceptance. In this vein, * * examines the conceptual development

ushered in a new era in the formation of food preferences. With

can influence which products consumers see, which alternatives

1.1. Research Problem
Technological advancements are fundamentally
transforming consumer culture and consumer behavior. In
particular, the widespread adoption of artificial intelligence
(AI)-based systems has reshaped consumers’ access to
information, decision-making, and purchasing processes. The
food and beverage industry is one of the sectors where this
transformation is most clearly observed. E- -based ordering
platforms, smart recommendation engines, and personalized
nutrition apps have become an intermediary layer that directly
influences consumers’ food and beverage decisions.
However, the mere existence of a technology does not
guarantee its adoption. Whether a technology is actually used
depends on the user’s perceptions, attitudes, and intentions
regarding that technology . The Technology Acceptance Model
(TAM), developed by Davis (1989), is one of the most widely
used theoretical frameworks for explaining this relationship.
According to the model, an individual’s behavior regarding

the use of a technology depends largely on how useful they
perceive it to be (perceived utility) and how easy they find it
to use (perceived ease of use).
In the context of food preferences, however, the situation is
more complex. Eating and drinking are not merely functional
but also cultural, emotional, and social phenomena. Therefore,
understanding the impact of AI-supported systems on food
preferences requires a holistic perspective that encompasses not
only the technical acceptance dimension but also psychological
variables such as trust, attitudes, and future usage intentions.
At this point, the variable of trust stands out in particular, as
consumers are making decisions in an environment where
their personal data is processed and recommendations are
generated by an algorithm.
The research question of this study can be stated as follows:
How and through which mechanisms does the phenomenon
of artificial intelligence influence consumers’ levels of perceived
technology acceptance regarding food preferences?
1.2. Purpose of the Study
The primary objective of this study is to determine the
effect of AI-powered applications on consumers’ levels of
perceived technology acceptance regarding food preferences.
In line with this general objective, the study aims to achieve
the following sub-objectives:
•  To describe consumers’ levels of perceived benefit,
perceived ease of use, attitude, trust, and behavioral intention
regarding AI-supported food applications.
•  To identify the relationships among these variables using
correlation and structural equation modeling.
•  To test the effect of ease of use on attitude; the effect of
perceived benefit on trust; and the effect of trust on behavioral
intention.

This study is significant from both theoretical and practical

From a practical perspective, the research findings

1.6. Definitions
Artificial Intelligence (AI): Refers to computer systems
capable of performing tasks that require human intelligence
(learning, reasoning, perception, decision-making).
Technology Acceptance Model (TAM): This is a
theoretical model that explains individuals’ behavior regarding
the adoption and use of information technology in terms of
perceived utility and perceived ease of use (Davis, 1989).
Perceived Usefulness: This is defined as an individual’s
belief that using a technology will improve their performance.
Perceived Ease of Use: This is defined as an individual’s
belief that using a technology will not require much effort.
Attitude: This is defined as an individual’s positive or
negative evaluation of an object or behavior.
Trust: This refers to an individual’s expectation that a
system will operate in a reliable, impartial, and ethical manner.
Behavioral Intention: This refers to an individual’s
willingness to perform a specific behavior in the future.

2.1. The Concept and Definition of Artificial
Intelligence
Artificial intelligence, in its broadest sense, is the scientific
discipline that studies machines and software capable of
exhibiting behaviors that are products of human intelligence
(such as learning, problem-solving, perception, language
comprehension, and decision-making). The concept
encompasses a field that is too broad and interdisciplinary to be
confined to a single definition. Many fields—from computer
science to cognitive psychology , and from philosophy to
linguistics—have contributed to the development of artificial
intelligence.
According to Russell and Norvig’s (2021) classification,
definitions of artificial intelligence can be grouped under four
headings: systems that think like humans, systems that behave
like humans, systems that think rationally , and systems that
behave rationally . The first approach is based on cognitive
modeling and aims to mimic the functioning of the human
mind. The second approach is based on the T uring Test, a

Rana Şat / Mehmet Sarıoğlan / Nilgün Karaman | 11
behavioral criterion; it is considered sufficient for a machine to
behave in a way that is indistinguishable from a human. The
third approach is based on logical rules and formal reasoning.
The fourth and currently most widely accepted approach is
based on the concept of a “rational agent”; according to this,
an artificial intelligence system is defined as an agent that
perceives its environment and selects the most appropriate
action to achieve its goals.
These four approaches demonstrate that artificial
intelligence is both a scientific endeavor (understanding the
human mind) and an engineering endeavor (building useful
systems). Consumer applications, which are the focus of this
book, are primarily based on the “rational agent” approach;
this is because a recommendation system or chatbot aims
to perceive the user’s data and present the most appropriate
action (recommendation, response) to them.
2.2. The Historical Development of Artificial
Intelligence
The conceptual origins of artificial intelligence are traced
back to Alan T uring’s 1950 paper titled “Computing Machinery
and Intelligence.” T uring (1950) addressed the question “Can
machines think?” and proposed the famous imitation game
(the T uring Test), in which he argued that a machine could
be considered “intelligent” if it could communicate in a way
that was indistinguishable from that of a human. The term
“artificial intelligence” itself was first used by John McCarthy
at the 1956 Dartmouth Conference, which is considered the
official birth of the field.
The development of artificial intelligence has not been
linear; periods of high expectations have been followed by
“ AI winters” marked by a decline in funding and interest.
The period between 1950 and 1970 was an era dominated

by symbolic artificial intelligence and optimism. During this
time, researchers believed that human intelligence could be
replicated in the near future. The program ELIZA, developed
in 1966, was capable of conducting a rudimentary conversation
in natural language and could create the impression of genuine
understanding in users. However, the 1970s and the late
1980s were periods of stagnation (AI winters) due to unmet
expectations and insufficient computing power.
Starting in the 1990s, increases in computing power
and improvements in data accessibility revitalized artificial
intelligence. In 1997, IBM’s Deep Blue chess computer
defeated world champion Garry Kasparov, marking a symbolic
turning point. IBM Watson’s victory over human contestants
in a trivia contest in 2011 and the breakthrough of deep
learning methods in image recognition in 2012 sparked a new
and powerful wave of momentum. In 2016, AlphaGo’s victory
over a master player in the game of Go—long considered
too difficult for computers—and the public debut of large
language models in 2022 mark the contemporary era in
which artificial intelligence has permeated everyday life. This

2.3. T ypes of Artificial Intelligence
Artificial intelligence is categorized into three main types
based on the scope of its capabilities. Narrow Artificial
Intelligence (ANI) refers to systems designed to perform
only a specific and limited task. These systems can often
outperform humans in the task for which they were designed;
however, they cannot perform tasks outside of that scope.

Rana Şat / Mehmet Sarıoğlan / Nilgün Karaman | 15
All AI applications in use today (recommendation systems,
translation tools, facial recognition, voice assistants, chess ,
and Go programs) fall into this category . For example, a food
recommendation system may be very successful at predicting
preferences; however, it cannot carry on a conversation or
interpret an image.
Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) refers to a general
form of intelligence capable of flexibly performing any mental
task a human can perform and of transferring knowledge
from one domain to another. AGI is a theoretical goal that
has not yet been achieved, and there is no consensus among
scientists regarding when or how it might be attained.
Artificial Super Intelligence (ASI), on the other hand, refers
to a purely speculative level that surpasses human intelligence
in every domain (creativity , social skills, general wisdom) and
is primarily the subject of philosophical and ethical debates.

2.4. Core Technologies of Artificial Intelligence
Artificial intelligence is not a single technology; it is an
umbrella term encompassing many interrelated subfields.
The most important of these subfields is machine learning.
Machine learning (ML) is an approach that enables systems
to improve their performance by learning from data without
being explicitly programmed. While rules are written by
humans in traditional programming, in machine learning,
the system discovers patterns in the data on its own.

Machine learning is divided into three main categories
based on the type of learning. In supervised learning, the
system is trained using labeled data consisting of input-output
pairs and performs classification (categorical prediction)
or regression (numerical prediction) tasks. For example, a
model trained on historical order data can predict whether a
customer will order a specific dish. In unsupervised learning,

which groups similar consumers, is a typical example of this
approach. In reinforcement learning, an agent interacts with
its environment to learn the best strategy through reward-and-
penalty feedback; this method is used in sequential decision-
making problems such as dynamic pricing.

2.5. Large Language Models and Generative AI
One of the most notable developments in recent years
has been the rise of generative artificial intelligence (AI).
Traditional AI systems were mostly discriminative in nature;
that is, they were designed to classify data or make predictions
(e.g., “Is this a cat?” or “Is this email spam?”). Generative AI,
however, breaks away from this paradigm by being able to
generate new content (text, images, audio, and even original
recipes and menu suggestions).

Large language models (LLMs) form the foundation of
generative artificial intelligence. These models are trained on
massive text datasets to learn statistical patterns of language
and can generate fluent text appropriate to a given context.
The public debut of these models in 2022 has dramatically
increased the visibility of artificial intelligence in everyday life.

The possibilities offered by generative AI in the context
of food and dining are remarkable. A language model can
suggest original recipes based on the ingredients a user has
on hand, create a weekly menu tailored to a specific dietary
restriction, or provide a natural and fluid customer experience
through a restaurant’s chatbot. Such systems have the potential
to directly influence the perceived benefits and ease of use
for consumers. However, the fact that generative systems can
occasionally produce erroneous or unfounded information
(hallucinations) makes the issue of trust even more critical
in a health-related field such as food.
2.6. Ethical Dimensions of Artificial Intelligence
The widespread adoption of artificial intelligence has
brought with it significant ethical issues. Addressing these
issues is of critical importance for both societal acceptance
and consumer trust. AI ethics is a broad field encompassing
many interrelated dimensions.

Bias and discrimination are among the most significant
ethical issues. AI models learn and can reproduce biases present
in the data used to train them. For example, a recommendation
system trained on insufficient data from certain demographic
groups may provide less accurate recommendations to those
groups. Privacy and data security are prominent dimensions,
particularly in food applications that process personal and
health data. The consumer’s right to know how their data
is collected and used forms the foundation of the trust
relationship.
Transparency and explainability refer to the understandability
of an AI system’s decisions. Being able to explain to the

Trust emerges as a decisive factor, particularly in the
adoption of online and algorithm-based systems. Trust is an
individual’s expectation that a system will operate reliably ,
impartially , and ethically; that it will protect their personal

8.5. Recommendations for the Food and Beverage
Industry
Based on the findings of this study , it is recommended that
food and beverage companies adopt a consumer-centered AI
approach rather than a technology-centered one in their AI
investments. The success of technological systems should be
evaluated not solely based on algorithmic capacity , speed, or
automation levels, but rather on the value consumers derive
from these systems.
First, businesses should make the perceived benefits
of AI-powered systems visible to consumers. The rationale
for using systems that save consumers time, narrow down
options, identify products tailored to individual needs, and
take allergens or dietary preferences into account must be
clearly presented.

Rana Şat / Mehmet Sarıoğlan / Nilgün Karaman | 95
Second, the ease of use of these systems must be improved.
AI-powered menus, kiosks, mobile apps, or online ordering
platforms should be as simple, intuitive, and streamlined
as possible. It is particularly important to develop inclusive
interfaces that allow consumers of different ages and digital
proficiency levels to use the same system with ease.
Third, personalized recommendations should be designed
in a way that guides the consumer without limiting their
freedom of choice. The system can provide recommendations
based on past orders, consumption habits, budget, dietary
preferences, and dietary requirements; however, it must clearly
convey that the final decision rests with the consumer.
Fourth, businesses should adopt the principle of
algorithmic transparency. For example, the reason an app
recommends a specific dish to a consumer could be explained
with statements such as “based on your previous orders,”
“based on your vegetarian preferences,” or “because it is
similar to products you’ve liked before.” This approach can
increase consumers’ trust in technology .
Fifth, data security and personal data privacy must be
fundamental design elements of AI systems. The purposes
for which consumers’ personal information, location data,
order histories, and health-related dietary preferences are used
must be clearly stated.
Sixth, businesses should view AI systems not as a
technology that completely replaces human employees, but
as a decision-support mechanism that complements human
service. Especially in restaurants and hospitality businesses
, since consumers continue to expect human interaction,
developing hybrid “human + AI” service models would
be more appropriate.
