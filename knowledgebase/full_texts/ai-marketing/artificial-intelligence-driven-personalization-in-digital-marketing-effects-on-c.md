Introduction: Artificial intelligence (AI)-driven personalization has become a
central strategy in digital marketing; however, existing studies provide a limited
understanding of how AI simultaneously influences customer experience, con -
sumer behavior, trust, and perceived organizational responsiveness.
Objective: This study examines the effects of AI-driven personalization on cus -
tomer experience, purchase intention, consumer trust, and perceived organiza-
tional responsiveness within the Indonesian digital market.
Methodology: A quantitative approach was employed using survey data collected
from 402 users of e-commerce and fintech platforms in Indonesia, which were
analyzed using Structural Equation Modeling–Partial Least Squares (PLS-SEM).
Results: The findings reveal that AI-driven personalization significantly enhances
customer experience, which subsequently increases purchase intention. AI-based
predictive analytics also demonstrates a significant positive relationship with
perceived organizational responsiveness, indicating that consumers associate
AI-enabled analytical capabilities with more timely and effective organizational
responses to customer and market needs. Furthermore, customer experience
serves as an important mediating mechanism, while perceived privacy risk weak-
ens the positive relationship between AI-driven personalization and consumer
trust.
Conclusion: By aligning the organizational dimension with the perspective of
consumer respondents, this study contributes an integrated understanding of
how AI-driven personalization and predictive analytics shape consumer experi -
ences and perceptions of organizational responsiveness in an emerging digital
economy.

1 Introduction
Over the past decade, rapid advancements in digital technology
have driven a profound transformation in modern marketing prac-
tices. One of the most disruptive innovations is the adoption of artifi-
cial intelligence (AI), which enables firms to manage, analyze, and
leverage large-scale consumer data in real time. AI not only functions
as an automation tool but also serves as a strategic enabler for creating
customer value through more personalized and adaptive approaches.
Recent studies suggest that AI-driven personalization enhances mar-
keting effectiveness, operational efficiency, and the accuracy of con-
sumer behavior insights through data analytics and machine learning
techniques (Dwivedi et al., 2021; Kumar et al., 2019).
In the context of digital marketing, AI-driven personalization has
become an increasingly dominant approach. This technology allows
firms to deliver tailored content, product recommendations, and interac-
tions based on individual consumer preferences. Such personalization
has been shown to enhance customer experience, engagement, and loy-
alty by enabling more relevant and context-aware interactions (Bleier et
al., 2019; Jarek and Mazurek, 2019). Industry reports further indicate
that more than 70% of consumers expect personalized experiences in
their digital interactions (Grewal et al., 2020; Kumar et al., 2019).
Despite its widely acknowledged benefits, the empirical literature
remains inconclusive regarding the mechanisms through which
AI-driven personalization influences customer experience and con-
sumer behavior holistically. Most prior studies tend to focus on direct
outcomes, such as purchase intention or customer satisfaction, with-
out thoroughly examining the role of customer experience as a com-
plex and multidimensional mediating variable (Dwivedi et al., 2021;
Bleier et al., 2019).
Furthermore, a significant research gap lies in the limited integra-
tion of consumer and managerial perspectives within AI marketing
studies. Existing research has largely emphasized the impact of AI on
consumer behavior, while its implications for perceived organizational
responsiveness remain underexplored. In practice, however, AI plays
a critical role in enhancing both the speed and quality of data-driven
decision-making through predictive analytics and real-time informa-
tion processing (Grewal et al., 2020; Shrestha et al., 2019). This sug-
gests that AI influences not only the demand side (consumers) but
also the supply side (organizational management).
In addition, ethical and data privacy concerns represent critical
challenges in the implementation of AI-driven personalization. While
personalization increases relevance and convenience, the intensive use
of consumer data raises concerns regarding security and transparency.
Prior research indicates that perceived privacy risk can reduce con-
sumer trust in AI-based systems, ultimately undermining the effec-
tiveness of digital marketing strategies (Cheng and Jiang, 2022; Martin
et al., 2017). Therefore, the success of AI implementation depends not
only on technological sophistication but also on firms’ ability to
manage ethical considerations and build trust.
In the context of developing countries such as Indonesia, research
on AI-driven personalization is particularly relevant. The dynamic
nature of Indonesia’s digital market, characterized by high adoption
rates of e-commerce and fintech platforms, presents both opportuni-
ties and challenges for AI implementation. However, studies that spe-
cifically examine the integration of AI personalization, customer
experience, and perceived organizational responsiveness within devel-
oping country contexts remain limited. Most existing research tends
to treat consumer and managerial perspectives separately, resulting in
a fragmented understanding of how AI simultaneously shapes cus-
tomer behavior and organizational strategy.
Moreover, prior studies have predominantly focused on the direct
relationship between personalization and behavioral outcomes, with-
out adequately exploring the role of customer experience as a complex
mediating mechanism or the moderating effect of privacy risk. These
limitations highlight a significant gap in the AI personalization litera-
ture. Addressing these gaps, this study proposes an integrative model
that links AI-driven personalization, customer experience, and per-
ceived organizational responsiveness within a comprehensive concep-
tual framework.
It explicitly incorporates customer experience as a mediating vari-
able and perceived privacy risk as a moderating variable—an approach
that remains relatively underexplored in prior research. Accordingly,
this study not only contributes to the literature by integrating con-
sumer and managerial perspectives but also provides a more compre-
hensive understanding of how AI-driven personalization influences
customer experience and organizational decision-making in a
dynamic digital market environment.
Existing research on artificial intelligence (AI) in digital market-
ing has largely evolved along two distinct streams. The first stream
focuses on consumer-related outcomes, emphasizing how AI-driven
personalization influences customer experience, engagement, satisfac-
tion, trust, and purchase intention ( Bleier et al., 2019 ; Becker and
Jaakkola, 2020; Hollebeek et al., 2019; Kumar et al., 2019). These stud-
ies primarily examine how consumers perceive and respond to
AI-enabled interactions and personalized recommendations. In con-
trast, the second stream concentrates on managerial and organiza-
tional outcomes, highlighting the role of AI-based analytics in
improving Service Responsiveness, operational efficiency, strategic
responsiveness, and organizational performance (Grewal et al., 2020;
Shrestha et al., 2019; Mikalef et al., 2019).
While both streams have generated important insights, they have
generally developed independently, resulting in a limited understand-
ing of how AI simultaneously affects consumer behavior and per -
ceived organizational responsiveness within a unified framework.
Consequently, the interaction between customer-facing personaliza-
tion mechanisms and organizational decision-making capabilities
remains underexplored, particularly in the context of rapidly growing
digital platforms in emerging economies. Addressing this limitation,
the present study integrates consumer and managerial perspectives to
provide a more comprehensive understanding of the role of AI-driven
personalization in digital marketing.
Despite the growing adoption of AI-driven personalization across
various industries, empirical evidence remains fragmented regarding
its effectiveness within rapidly developing digital economies. In
Indonesia, the expansion of e-commerce and fintech platforms has
accelerated the implementation of AI-based personalization strategies
aimed at improving customer engagement and supporting perceived
organizational responsiveness. However, limited research has simul-
taneously examined the consumer and managerial implications of
AI-driven personalization within this specific context. Therefore, this
study focuses on users of e-commerce and fintech platforms in
Indonesia, providing an industry-specific investigation while offering
insights that may inform broader discussions on AI-enabled digital
marketing practices in emerging markets.
While the proposed conceptual framework is grounded in estab-
lished theories of digital marketing, customer experience, and AI
adoption, the empirical analysis is intentionally bounded to the

1.1 Industrial context and market
expectations
Digital consumer expectations for tailored interaction have
reached unprecedented levels. According to McKinsey & Company’s
Next in Personalization Report ( N = 5,000 consumer respondents
across North America and Europe, quantitative online survey, 2021),
71% of consumers expect companies to deliver personalized interac-
tions, and 76% express frustration when this does not occur. Similarly,
within emerging South-East Asian markets, Google, Temasek, and
Bain & Company’s e-Conomy SEA Report ( multi-method primary
survey and platform transactional data across 6 countries, including
Indonesia, N >10,000, 2023) indicates that 84% of Indonesian digital
consumers actively prefer platforms that anticipate service needs
through proactive recommendations. Grounding baseline market
expectations in these primary empirical industry benchmarks allows
academic literature to focus on explaining the underlying psychologi-
cal mechanisms rather than substituting for market data.
1.2 Systematic literature synthesis and
identification of residual gaps
Rather than assuming a research gap exists, a systematic review of
contemporary empirical literature on AI capabilities, customer experi-
ence, and organizational responsiveness reveals specific conceptual and
empirical gaps. Prior investigations have predominantly focused on
single-construct dynamics, such as the direct impact of AI-driven rec-
ommendation accuracy on immediate purchasing behavior (e.g., Bleier
et al., 2019; Kumar et al., 2019) or firm-level operational benefits of big
data analytics using managerial data (e.g., Mikalef et al., 2019). As dem-
onstrated, existing studies present three distinct residual gaps:
 • Construct Isolation: Studies evaluating AI personalization (AIP)
rarely integrate back-end operational predictive analytics (APA)
into a unified consumer-perceived framework.
 • Contextual & Methodological Realignment: Prior research exam-
ining organizational responsiveness (POR) relies heavily on man-
ager self-reports or Western B2B samples (e.g., Raisch and
Krakowski, 2021), leaving a gap in understanding how platform
users in emerging Southeast Asian digital markets (e.g., Indonesia)
perceive these internal analytical capabilities through front-end
service delivery.
 • Boundary Condition Integration: While studies acknowledge pri-
vacy concerns (e.g., Martin et al., 2017; Cheng and Jiang, 2022),
few have operationalized Perceived Privacy Risk (PPR) as a first-
stage boundary-condition moderator within a dual-path S-O-R
model that links personalization and predictive responsiveness to
Consumer Trust (CT) and Purchase Intention (PI).
1.3 Theoretical contribution and scope
boundaries
Rather than claiming a multi-level integration between firm deci-
sion-making and consumer behavior, this study offers a calibrated
single-level theoretical contribution focused entirely on consumer
perceptions. Grounded in the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R)
framework, the model delineates how consumers evaluate two distinct
platform-level AI capabilities:
 • Front-End AI Stimuli (AIP): Consumer-facing interactive person-
alization that shapes direct affective and cognitive customer expe-
riences (OCX).
 • Back-End AI Stimuli (APA): Consumer-perceived operational
efficiency and promptness that shape evaluations of organizational
responsiveness (OPOR).
By examining these dual pathways entirely through the consumer’s
observational lens, this study avoids conflating customer perceptions
with actual internal managerial performance, providing an empirically
grounded account of consumer-side outcomes. Rather than evaluating
internal firm decision-making alongside end-user behavior, this study
delivers a calibrated, single-level theoretical contribution that captures
the consumer-perceived interplay between front-end personalization and
back-end organizational capabilities. While prior literature examines
managerial capabilities through internal firm metrics and consumer
behavior through user sentiment in isolation, our model bridges these
streams by evaluating how back-end AI-Based Predictive Analytics
(APA) translates into observable Perceived Organizational
Responsiveness (POR) from the user’s vantage point.
Grounded in the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) para -
digm, this operationalization frames front-end customization (AIP)
as an immediate affective stimulus and back-end analytical agility
(APA → POR) as a structural trust-building stimulus. By anchoring
the evaluation of both operational efficiency and tailored marketing
entirely within the consumer observational lens, the study avoids con-
flating internal managerial self-reports with end-user perceptions,
offering a unified model of how integrated AI capabilities shape con-
sumer trust and purchase behavior.
2 Literature review
2.1 Theoretical differentiation: AIP vs. APA
To eliminate conceptual ambiguity, this study explicitly delineates
AI-Driven Personalization (AIP) and AI-Based Predictive Analytics
(APA) as two distinct, independent antecedent constructs operating
at different operational levels. AI-Driven Personalization (AIP) is
defined as a front-end, customer-facing antecedent (Davenport et al.,
2020; Dwivedi et al., 2021; Grewal et al., 2020). It captures algorithmic
customization tailored to individual user preferences (e.g., real-time
product recommendations, tailored content feeds). In accordance with
the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) framework (Davenport et
al., 2020; Huang and Rust, 2021), AIP acts as an external environmen-
tal stimulus (S) directly enhancing Customer Experience (CX) (O).
AI-Based Predictive Analytics (APA) is defined as a back-end capabil-
ity perceived through platform outcomes (Grewal et al., 2020; Verhoef
et al., 2021). Because data are collected from platform users rather
than managers, APA does not measure internal managerial cognition
or back-office decision speed. Instead, it measures consumer percep-
tions of platform operational efficiency, automated service responsive-
ness, and proactive system adjustments (Wirtz et al., 2018).

2.2 Theoretical integration: the dual role of
consumer trust and privacy risk
To establish structural consistency across the study, Consumer Trust
(CT) is conceptualized as a central sequential mediator rather than an
adjunct outcome variable (Bleier et al., 2019 ; Mikalef et al., 2019 ).
Drawing on Trust-Commitment Theory and the Stimulus-Organism-
Response (S-O-R) paradigm, technological capabilities (AIP and APA)
serve as external environmental stimuli (S). These stimuli shape the con-
sumer’s psychological evaluation, where Customer Experience (CX) and
Consumer Trust (CT) function as parallel and sequential internal states
(O). Trust represents the psychological state of confidence that the plat-
form will deliver promised outcomes without exploiting user vulnerabil-
ity (Jarek and Mazurek, 2019; Kumar et al., 2019 ). As an internal
cognitive-affective state, CT directly drives the final behavioral response
(R), operationalized as Purchase Intention (PI).
Concurrently, Perceived Privacy Risk (PPR) is positioned strictly
as a first-stage boundary-condition moderator grounded in Privacy
Calculus Theory. Privacy Calculus posits that consumers continuously
weigh the anticipated benefits of personalized AI service against the
potential risks of data exploitation. High levels of PPR act as a cogni-
tive friction mechanism that buffers (weakens) the positive translation
of AI-Driven Personalization into Consumer Trust ( Martin et al.,
2017). Consequently, PPR governs the conditional indirect effect of
AIP on Purchase Intention through Trust (moderated mediation).
2.3 Customer experience in AI-driven
personalization contexts
Customer experience is a multidimensional construct encompass-
ing consumers’ cognitive, emotional, and behavioral responses to
interactions with a firm. In digital marketing contexts, it has become
a key determinant of the success of technology-driven strategies
(Becker and Jaakkola, 2020; Hollebeek et al., 2019). Within AI-enabled
environments, customer experience is shaped by a system’s ability to
deliver interactions that are relevant, personalized, and adaptive to
user needs (Bleier et al., 2019). Nevertheless, some studies indicate
that customer experience does not always directly translate into pur-
chase behavior, particularly when trust in the technology is low or
when privacy concerns are salient.
Customer Experience (CX) extends beyond simple satisfaction
and encompasses the cognitive, emotional, sensory, and behavioral
responses that arise from interactions between consumers and digital
platforms. In AI-enabled environments, customer experience is
shaped by users’ perceptions of relevance, personalization accuracy,
convenience, responsiveness, and trustworthiness. AI-driven person-
alization influences cognitive evaluations by providing information
and recommendations that align with individual preferences, thereby
reducing information overload and enhancing perceived usefulness.
Simultaneously, personalized interactions may generate positive emo-
tional responses, including enjoyment, engagement, and feelings of
recognition. Consequently, customer experience in AI-mediated envi-
ronments reflects a multidimensional process through which consum-
ers interpret, evaluate, and respond to personalized digital interactions.
Understanding these underlying mechanisms is essential for explain-
ing how AI-driven personalization ultimately influences consumer
behavior.
This suggests that the relationship between customer experience
and purchase intention is complex and context-dependent.
Furthermore, the role of customer experience as a mediating variable
in the relationship between AI-driven personalization and consumer
behavior remains underexplored. Accordingly, this study positions
customer experience as a central mechanism that links personaliza-
tion to behavioral outcomes.
2.3.1 Theoretical specification of the privacy
paradox
A common misconception in digital marketing literature is equat-
ing continued platform usage despite privacy concerns with an irra-
tional “privacy paradox. ” In accordance with Privacy Calculus Theory
(Martin et al., 2017), consumers do not act irrationally; rather, they
perform a risk–benefit assessment where immediate functional utility
often outweighs delayed or abstract privacy risks.
2.3.2 Theoretical standardization of perceived
organizational responsiveness (POR)
To eliminate construct ambiguity, this study standardizes all ser-
vice-delivery evaluations under Perceived Organizational
Responsiveness (POR), discarding informal variants such as “service
responsiveness” or “system promptness. ” Grounded in IS Service
Quality Theory (Huang and Rust, 2021) and operationalized as an
environmental stimulus ( SPOR) within the Stimulus-Organism-
Response (S-O-R) framework, POR is formally defined as:
The degree to which a platform user perceives that an e-commerce
or fintech provider utilizes platform capabilities to deliver
prompt, proactive, and accurate operational responses to con-
sumer actions, inquiries, and service disruptions.
Unlike front-end personalization (AIP), which evaluates tailored
recommendations, POR measures the consumer-perceived opera -
tional agility powered by back-end predictive analytics (APA).
2.4 AI-based perceived organizational
responsiveness
Beyond its direct influence on consumer interactions, artificial
intelligence functions as a foundational driver of perceived organiza-
tional responsiveness. Through AI-based predictive analytics, digital
platforms process massive streams of customer data in real time,
enabling organizations to anticipate shifts in market demand and con-
tinuously adapt their service environments. However, because internal
administrative structures, managerial cognition, and behind-the-
scenes decision-making processes cannot be directly observed by
users, organizational responsiveness in consumer-facing research
must be conceptualized through the lens of external perception
(Shrestha et al., 2019; Grewal et al., 2020).
From the consumer’s observational standpoint, an organization’s
internal analytical capability is manifested through tangible, down-
stream outcomes—such as the speed of issue resolution, the timeliness
of platform updates, adaptive service delivery, and rapid response to
customer inquiries. While prior literature notes a potential operational
paradox where heavy reliance on predictive algorithms might reduce
human managerial discretion, the primary mechanism evaluating
organizational effectiveness in digital markets rests on whether con-
sumers perceive these data-driven actions as prompt and effective. By
shifting the focus from internal managerial processes to consumer-
perceived organizational responsiveness, this study bridges the gap

2.5 Privacy risk and consumer trust
The implementation of AI-driven personalization is closely asso-
ciated with data privacy concerns, which have become a central issue
in digital marketing. The extensive use of personal data in personaliza-
tion processes often raises concerns regarding information security
and transparency (Martin et al., 2017). Prior studies suggest that per-
ceived privacy risk negatively affects consumer trust, which may ulti-
mately reduce the effectiveness of AI-based marketing strategies
(Cheng and Jiang, 2022). However, the phenomenon known as the
privacy paradox indicates that consumers continue to use AI-based
services despite their privacy concerns. This paradox highlights that
the relationships among personalization, privacy risk, and trust are
complex, non-linear, and influenced by contextual factors that remain
insufficiently explored in the literature. Therefore, this study incorpo-
rates privacy risk as a moderating variable in the research model.
2.6 Hypothesis development
The hypotheses developed in this study align directly with the
structural equation model, grounded in the Stimulus-Organism-
Response (S-O-R) paradigm and Trust-Commitment Theory. The
model delineates dual pathways through which front-end personaliza-
tion and back-end predictive analytics influence consumer evaluations
and behavioral responses.
2.6.1 Front-end personalization and experience
pathway
AI-driven personalization enables digital platforms to tailor prod-
uct recommendations and interactions dynamically to individual user
preferences. By reducing information search friction and delivering
context-aware relevance, front-end personalization serves as an envi-
ronmental stimulus (S) that shapes internal cognitive and affective
customer experiences (O). Furthermore, a seamless, high-quality cus-
tomer experience enhances user engagement and value perception,
driving behavioral purchase intentions (R).
H1: AI-Driven Personalization has a positive effect on Customer
Experience
AI-driven personalization enables digital platforms to tailor prod-
ucts, services, recommendations, and interactions according to con-
sumers’ preferences and behavioral patterns. By providing more
relevant and individualized experiences, AI-driven personalization
can reduce information overload and improve the perceived relevance
and convenience of digital interactions (Bleier et al., 2019; Kumar et
al., 2019). Consequently, consumers are more likely to evaluate their
interactions with AI-enabled platforms positively when the services
and recommendations correspond closely to their individual needs
and expectations (Bleier et al., 2019; Ameen et al., 2021; Verhoef et
al., 2021).
H4: AI-based predictive analytics has a positive effect on per -
ceived organizational responsiveness.
AI-based predictive analytics enables digital organizations to pro-
cess customer and market information rapidly, identify emerging pat-
terns, and generate timely insights that can support organizational
responses (Shrestha et al., 2019; Grewal et al., 2020). From the con-
sumer perspective, these technological capabilities may become
observable through faster responses to customer needs, more adaptive
services, timely problem resolution, and improved responsiveness to
changing market conditions. Therefore, greater utilization of AI-based
predictive analytics is expected to enhance consumers’ perceptions of
organizational responsiveness (Mikalef et al., 2019).
2.6.2 Back-end analytics and trust pathway
Back-end AI capabilities are evaluated through the user’s obser-
vational lens. AI-based predictive analytics allows platforms to antici-
pate service trends and process real-time demand, driving consumer
perceptions of platform responsiveness. When consumers perceive
that an organization operates with operational agility and prompt
problem resolution (POR), their confidence in the platform’s compe-
tence and dependability increases, fostering consumer trust (CT). In
turn, higher consumer trust reduces transaction uncertainty and
drives purchase commitments
H2: Customer Experience has a positive effect on Purchase
Intention
Customer experience represents consumers’ overall cognitive,
emotional, and behavioral evaluation of their interactions with a digi-
tal platform. A positive customer experience can increase satisfaction,
perceived value, engagement, and willingness to continue interacting
with a platform. When consumers experience convenient, relevant,
and enjoyable digital services, they are more likely to develop favor-
able behavioral intentions toward purchasing products or services
through the platform ( Becker and Jaakkola, 2020 ; Hollebeek et
al., 2019).
H3: Consumer Trust has a positive effect on Purchase Intention
Consumer trust plays an important role in reducing uncertainty
in digital transactions and shaping consumers’ willingness to engage
with online platforms. In e-commerce and fintech environments, con-
sumers often face concerns regarding information security, transac-
tion reliability, service performance, and the responsible use of
personal data. Higher levels of trust can reduce perceived uncertainty
and encourage consumers to engage more confidently with digital
platforms. Consequently, consumers who perceive a platform as reli-
able, secure, and trustworthy are more likely to demonstrate a stronger
intention to purchase or continue using its services (Bleier et al., 2019;
Verhoef et al., 2021).
H5: AI-based predictive analytics has a positive effect on Service
Responsiveness (SR).
Beyond supporting managerial actions, AI-based predictive ana-
lytics can improve an organization’s ability to translate data-driven
insights into timely responses to customer needs and changing service
conditions. Predictive information can facilitate faster identification
of customer demands, service problems, and emerging patterns,

2.6.3 Boundary conditions of personalization and
trust
While front-end personalization aims to enhance user trust,
Privacy Calculus Theory suggests that extensive data collection trig-
gers privacy concerns. Perceived Privacy Risk (PPR) acts as a cognitive
friction mechanism. When users perceive high risks regarding data
security and intrusive tracking, the positive influence of front-end
personalization on consumer trust is attenuated.
H6: Perceived Privacy Risk (PPR) negatively moderates the rela-
tionship between AI-Driven Personalization (AIP) and
Consumer Trust (CT), such that the relationship becomes
weaker when privacy risk is high.
Despite the benefits of AI-driven personalization, concerns
regarding data privacy may influence how consumers respond to such
technologies. Perceived privacy risk reflects consumers’ concerns
about the misuse of their personal data (Martin et al., 2017).
When privacy concerns are great, consumers may become skepti-
cal of personalized systems, reducing their level of trust. This suggests
that privacy risk weakens the positive relationship between AI-driven
personalization and consumer trust.
Studies on privacy risk consistently show that concerns regarding
data collection and surveillance reduce consumers’ willingness to trust
AI-enabled systems, even when personalization provides tangible ben-
efits (Martin et al., 2017; Cheng and Jiang, 2022).
H7: Consumer trust has a positive effect on purchase intention.
Trust is a fundamental factor in digital environments, particularly
when interactions are mediated by AI systems. Higher levels of trust
reduce perceived risk and increase consumers’ willingness to engage
in transactions (Cheng and Jiang, 2022).
When consumers trust AI systems, they are more likely to rely on
recommendations and proceed with purchase decisions. Therefore,
trust is expected to influence purchase intention positively.
Consumer trust has long been recognized as a critical antecedent
of purchase intention in digital environments because trust reduces
perceived uncertainty and increases confidence in transactions
(Martin et al., 2017; Kumar et al., 2019).
AI-driven personalization can be conceptualized through the
Stimulus–Organism–Response (S–O–R) framework. Within this
perspective, personalized recommendations, adaptive content, and
AI-enabled interactions function as external stimuli that influence
consumers’ internal psychological states. These internal states
include cognitive evaluations of relevance, perceived usefulness,
decision confidence, and emotional responses such as satisfaction
and engagement. These cognitive and affective processes subse -
quently shape behavioral responses, including purchase intention
and continued platform usage. Therefore, customer experience
represents not merely an outcome of personalization but an under -
lying psychological mechanism through which AI influences con -
sumer behavior.
3 Methodology
3.1 Sample and data collection
This study utilizes primary data collected through an online
survey administered to active users of e-commerce and fintech plat-
forms in Indonesia. The selection of Indonesia as the research context
is justified by its rapid digital transformation, high level of technology
adoption, and the significant expansion of its digital economy, making
it one of the most dynamic digital markets in Southeast Asia. Primary
data were gathered via a structured online survey administered
between July 15, 2025, and September 30, 2025, targeting active users

To evaluate consumer-perceived AI dynamics, this study tar -

Indonesia. A purposive non-probability sampling strategy was
employed. To ensure sample eligibility, respondents were required
to satisfy three mandatory screening criteria on the survey’s entry
landing page: (1) be at least 18 years of age, (2) have an active user
history of at least six months on a major Indonesian digital platform
(e.g., Tokopedia, Shopee, GoPay, OVO), and (3) have completed
past transactions guided by platform personalization or recommen -
dation engines.
3.1.1 Instrument translation and pilot testing
Because the original measurement scales were derived from
English-language literature, a formal back-translation protocol
(English to Indonesian and back to English) was conducted by two
independent bilingual marketing researchers to ensure conceptual
equivalence. The translated instrument underwent a pilot test with
35 digital platform users to evaluate item readability and face valid -
ity; minor phrasing adjustments were made based on pilot
feedback.
3.1.2 Recruitment, screening, and response rate
Participants were recruited across major Indonesian consumer
channels (LinkedIn, Instagram, Telegram digital consumer forums,
and targeted e-commerce/fintech user communities). To ensure
sample eligibility, mandatory screening questions verified that respon-
dents were: (1) at least 18 years of age, (2) active users of e-commerce
(e.g., Tokopedia, Shopee, Lazada) or fintech platforms (e.g., GoPay,
OVO, Dana, Bibit) for a minimum of six months, and (3) had previ-
ously completed transactions guided by AI-driven personalized rec-
ommendations or features.
A total of 540 online questionnaire submissions were recorded.
Data cleaning procedures were applied sequentially:
 • Duplicate Responses: IP-address tracking and device fingerprint-
ing were enabled to prevent duplicate submissions.
 • Missing Data: Mandatory response settings in the online survey
software eliminated missing values across primary measure -
ment items.
 • Quality Exclusion Criteria: 138 submissions were excluded due to

3.2.1 Variable description
All variables in this study were operationalized based on measure-
ment scales that have been validated in prior research to ensure reli-
ability and construct validity.
AI-driven personalization (AIP) was measured using five indica-
tors that capture the relevance of recommendations, system accuracy,
and the ability of AI systems to adapt to users’ preferences (Bleier et
al., 2019). Customer experience (CX) was assessed using six indicators
reflecting the cognitive, emotional, and behavioral dimensions of
users’ interactions within digital environments ( Becker and
Jaakkola, 2020).
Purchase intention (PI) was measured using four indicators rep-
resenting consumers’ likelihood of making a purchase after receiving
personalized recommendations (Verhoef et al., 2021). AI-based pre-
dictive analytics (APA) was measured using four indicators that reflect
the system’s capability to anticipate customer needs based on historical
data and behavioral patterns ( Dwivedi et al., 2021 ). Managerial
Service Responsiveness (MSR) and Service Responsiveness (SR) were
each measured using four indicators capturing the effectiveness of AI
in enhancing the timeliness and accuracy of organizational respon-
siveness (Shrestha et al., 2019).
In this study, perceived organizational responsiveness is opera-
tionalized through two observable organizational outcomes, namely
Managerial Service Responsiveness (MSR) and Service Responsiveness
(SR). Rather than measuring internal organizational responsiveness
processes directly, the study focuses on the extent to which AI-based
predictive analytics supports timely, accurate, and responsive organi-
zational actions as perceived by platform users. MSR reflects the per-
ceived responsiveness of managerial actions supported by
AI-generated information, whereas SR captures the perceived ability
of the organization to translate AI-supported insights into timely
responses to customer and market needs.
This operationalization recognizes that internal organizational
responsiveness processes are not directly observable through a con-
sumer-based survey; therefore, responsiveness provides an externally
observable manifestation of AI-supported decision-making.
Accordingly, references to “perceived organizational responsiveness”
throughout this study should be understood as referring specifically
to AI-supported managerial and service responsiveness rather than to
the complete internal cognitive or administrative decision-making
process of managers.
Perceived privacy risk (PPR) was measured using four indicators
that reflect consumers’ concerns regarding data security, misuse of
personal information, and lack of transparency in data handling.
Consumer trust (CT) was assessed using four indicators representing
the degree of confidence consumers place in AI systems and digital
platforms in delivering reliable and secure services ( Martin et al.,
2017). All constructs were measured using a five-point Likert scale
ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), which is widely
used in behavioral and marketing research to capture respondents’
perceptions consistently.
To maintain conceptual and terminological consistency, this study
distinguishes Managerial Service Responsiveness (MSR) from Service
Responsiveness (SR), although both constructs are influenced by
AI-based Predictive Analytics (APA). MSR refers specifically to the
perceived ability of managers and organizational decision-makers to
respond promptly and appropriately to information generated through
AI-supported analytics, particularly in relation to managerial actions
and operational decisions.
In contrast, SR refers to the perceived ability of the organization
to translate AI-supported insights into timely and effective responses
to customer needs and changing service conditions. Thus, MSR rep-
resents the managerial or decision-oriented dimension of responsive-
ness, whereas SR represents the customer-facing or service-delivery
dimension. This distinction is maintained consistently throughout the
hypothesis development, structural model, results, and discussion to
ensure that the two constructs are not treated as interchangeable or
redundant outcomes.

3.4 Data analysis technique
Data analysis was conducted using Structural Equation Modeling–
Partial Least Squares (PLS-SEM) with the assistance of SmartPLS 4.0
software. The PLS-SEM method was selected due to its capability to
analyze complex models involving latent variables, as well as its flex-
ibility in handling non-normal data distributions (Hair et al., 2019;
Sarstedt et al., 2021). The analysis was carried out in two main stages,
namely the evaluation of the measurement model (outer model) and
the structural model (inner model). The evaluation of the outer model
aimed to assess the validity and reliability of the constructs, using the
criteria of factor loadings > 0.70, Average Variance Extracted
(AVE) > 0.50, and Composite Reliability and Cronbach’s Alpha > 0.70.
Subsequently, the evaluation of the inner model was conducted to
test the research hypotheses by examining the path coefficients, t-sta-
tistics (> 1.96), and p-values (< 0.05). In addition, the R-square value
was used to measure the model’s ability to explain the dependent
variables, while f2 and Q2 values were used to evaluate the effect size
and predictive relevance of the model (Hair et al., 2019).
Figure 2 provides a graphical representation of the estimated
structural relationships derived from the PLS-SEM analysis conducted
using SmartPLS 4. To avoid implying that the figure itself is a direct
software export. The figure summarizes the final structural relation-
ships and displays the empirical results reported in the corresponding
structural model tables. In addition, the graphical presentation
includes the relevant R2 values for all endogenous constructs, ensuring
consistency between the figure and the discussion of the model’s
explanatory power. The complete SmartPLS output and detailed
numerical results are provided separately to support the transparency
and reproducibility of the analysis.
To ensure the accuracy and transparency of the structural model
evaluation, the effect size statistics were re-examined using the origi-
nal SmartPLS output. In PLS-SEM, the f2 statistic was assessed based
on the change in the coefficient of determination of an endogenous
construct when a particular exogenous construct was included and
excluded from the model, rather than being directly equated with the
reported R2 value. Accordingly, all f2 values presented were recalcu-
lated and verified against the SmartPLS effect size output.
The structural model results were also rechecked using the origi-
nal dataset and estimation file to confirm the precision of the reported
path coefficients. To enhance reproducibility and allow independent
verification of the analysis, the anonymized raw dataset and the origi-
nal SmartPLS project/output files have been prepared as supplemen-
tary materials and are submitted alongside, subject to the journal’s
data-sharing and confidentiality requirements.

method variance (CMV) was addressed using both ex-ante procedural
controls and robust ex-post statistical diagnostics.

respondents from inferring hypothesized structural paths.

no right or wrong answers, and encouraged to respond honestly.

4 Results
The structural relationships were evaluated using bootstrapped
path coefficients and their associated inferential statistics. Each
hypothesized relationship was assessed separately using the standard-
ized path coefficient, bootstrap standard error, t-value, p-value, and
confidence interval. The explanatory power of the structural model
was then evaluated at the endogenous construct level using R2 and
adjusted R2 values rather than assigning these statistics to individ -
ual paths.
The relative contribution of each predictor was assessed through
f2 effect sizes, while potential multicollinearity among predictors was
examined using inner VIF statistics. Predictive relevance was assessed
separately for each endogenous construct using the selected predictive
assessment procedure. This analytical structure allows the signifi -
cance, explanatory power, effect size, collinearity, and predictive rel-
evance of the model to be interpreted as distinct but complementary
aspects of the PLS-SEM results.

measurement model.

5.2 Limitations and future research
directions
Several methodological limitations of this study present
promising avenues for future inquiry. First, the cross-sectional
design restricts empirical claims to statistical associations rather
than direct causal inferences. Future research should employ lon -
gitudinal tracking or quasi-experimental field designs (e.g., /AB
testing live AI recommendations) to establish temporal prece -
dence and counterfactual causality. Second, self-reported mea -
sures of platform efficiency and responsiveness reflect consumer
perceptions rather than objective back-end system metrics; inte -
grating objective server logs or firm-level performance metrics
alongside consumer surveys would provide a multi-level
perspective.
5.3 Theoretical implications
The empirical findings contribute to the e-commerce and AI man-
agement literature in three specific ways, without overstating cross-
level integration:
 • Dual-Pathway S-O-R Delineation: We demonstrate that platform
users process front-end customization (AIP ) and back-end effi-
ciency ( APA ) through separate psychological mechanisms.
Personalization primarily drives affective customer experience,
whereas predictive responsiveness builds organizational trust.
 • Consumer-Side Realignment of Operational Capabilities: W e
establish that back-end AI capabilities can be measured meaning-
fully through consumer perceptions of operational agility (speed,

tive to manager-reported metrics.
 • Boundary-Condition Mechanism: W e show that privacy risk acts

within user-platform interactions.
5.4 Critical conceptual delineations in
strategy and practice
To maximize the theoretical and managerial utility of our find-
ings, four critical distinctions must be articulated:
Statistical relevance vs. practical significance
While structural path coefficients demonstrate statistical signifi-
cance (p < 0.05). Their practical significance varies. For example, the
interaction effect of Perceived Privacy Risk (AIP × PPR → CT,
β = −0.142, f2 = 0.042) yields a statistically small effect size. However,
in hyper-competitive e-commerce markets, even a small reduction in
trust can drive substantial customer churn. Thus, a statistically “small”
moderation effect carries high strategic significance for platform
retention strategies.
Perceived vs. objective organizational metrics
Evaluated measures of platform responsiveness reflect subjective
consumer perceptions (perceived agility, prompt resolution) rather
than objective back-end operational metrics (e.g., API latency, order
processing times, server throughput). Consumer perceptions are
shaped by psychological thresholds and expectations rather than raw
technical benchmarks. Managers must recognize that optimizing
objective system speed does not automatically translate into improved
consumer-perceived responsiveness unless communicated effectively
through the front-end interface.
Useful vs. intrusive personalization
Personalization exhibits a dual nature. Useful personalization
provides timely, relevant recommendations that minimize search
friction. Conversely, intrusive personalization occurs when algo -
rithmic recommendations cross into perceived surveillance—uti -
lizing overly sensitive location data or cross-site tracking without
explicit context. When personalization feels intrusive, it triggers
psychological reactance and heightens privacy concerns, under -
mining consumer trust.
Perceived privacy risk vs. effective data governance
Perceived Privacy Risk (PPR) is a subjective consumer perception
of potential loss or data misuse. It operates independently of objective
data governance practices (e.g., ISO certifications, AES-256 encryp-
tion, regulatory compliance). A platform may implement state-of-the-
art security protocols, yet if users lack clear visibility into these
safeguards, high PPR will persist. Effective data governance only miti-
gates consumer risk perceptions when paired with transparent con-
sumer-facing privacy communication.

 • Sectoral Boundaries: The operational context focuses strictly on
B2C e-commerce and consumer fintech platforms. Broadening
these implications to non-consumer sectors—such as B2B enter-
prise software, industrial supply chains, or healthcare manage-
ment—is uncalled for, as operational responsiveness and
personalization operate under different functional mechanisms in
non-retail environments.
Accordingly, all references throughout the discussion have been
re-anchored to consumer-facing digital platforms in emerging mar-
kets rather than generic organizations or global digital markets.
6 Conclusion
This study aims to examine the role of AI-driven personalization
in digital marketing and its implications for customer experience and
perceived organizational responsiveness. The findings indicate that
AI-driven personalization has a positive and significant effect on cus-
tomer experience, which in turn enhances consumers’ purchase inten-
tion. In addition, AI-based predictive analytics contributes to
improving both the speed and quality of perceived organizational
responsiveness.
The results confirm that customer experience serves as a key
mechanism linking technology and consumer behavior. In other
words, the effectiveness of AI-driven personalization depends not only
on technological sophistication but also on the organization’s ability
to create relevant and valuable experiences for customers. On the
other hand, this study also reveals that perceived privacy risk weakens
the relationship between AI-driven personalization and consumer
trust, indicating a trade-off between personalization and data
protection.
From a theoretical perspective, this study contributes by integrat-
ing technological, customer experience, and perceived organizational
responsiveness perspectives into a comprehensive conceptual frame-
work. It also extends the literature by positioning customer experience
as a mediating variable and privacy risk as a moderating variable in
the context of AI-driven marketing.
From a practical perspective, the findings provide important
implications for businesses in optimizing the use of AI in digital mar-
keting strategies while maintaining ethical considerations and data
protection. Organizations need to ensure that personalization is not
only relevant but also transparent and does not create concerns among
consumers. Furthermore, the integration of predictive analytics in
perceived organizational responsiveness can enhance strategic accu-
racy and responsiveness to market dynamics.
However, this study has several limitations. First, the data used are
cross-sectional, which limits the ability to capture the dynamic nature
of consumer behavior over time. Second, the focus on e-commerce
and fintech sectors in Indonesia may limit the generalizability of the
findings to other industries or countries. Therefore, future research is
recommended to adopt longitudinal approaches, expand the research
context, and incorporate additional variables such as digital literacy,
privacy regulation, and technology adoption levels that may influence
the effectiveness of AI-driven personalization.
6.1 Limitations and future research
Although this study provides important contributions to under-
standing the role of AI-driven personalization in digital marketing,
several limitations should be acknowledged.
First, this study employs a cross-sectional approach, which
limits its ability to capture the dynamic changes in consumer
behavior and perceived organizational responsiveness over time.
In the context of rapidly evolving digital marketing, relationships
among variables such as customer experience and consumer trust
may change over time, particularly with the continuous advance -
ment of AI technology.
Second, this study focuses on users of e-commerce and fintech
platforms in Indonesia. Therefore, the generalizability of the findings
to other industries or countries with different market characteristics
should be interpreted with caution. Differences in technology adop-
tion, privacy regulations, and digital literacy may influence the effec-
tiveness of AI-driven personalization.
Third, this study adopts a quantitative survey-based approach that
relies on respondents’ perceptions. While this approach is useful for
capturing general patterns, it has limitations in exploring deeper
insights into customer experience and perceived organizational
responsiveness processes, which are inherently complex and context-
dependent. Based on these limitations, future research is recom -
mended to adopt a longitudinal approach in order to better capture
the dynamic relationships among variables over time. In addition,
future studies may expand the research context across different indus-
tries or conduct cross-country comparative studies to enhance the
generalizability of the findings.
Furthermore, the use of mixed methods combining quantita -
tive and qualitative approaches is suggested to provide a more
comprehensive understanding of customer experience and
AI-based decision-making processes. Future research may also
consider additional variables such as digital literacy, technology
trust, and privacy regulations as potential factors that may moder -
ate the relationship between AI-driven personalization and con -
sumer behavior.
