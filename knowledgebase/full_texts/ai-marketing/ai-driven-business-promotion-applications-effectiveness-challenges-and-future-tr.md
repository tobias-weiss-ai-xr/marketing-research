# AI-Driven Business Promotion: Applications, Effectiveness, Challenges, and Future Trends

Applications, Effectiveness, Challenges, and Future Trends
Author: Krishna Gupta, Scholar Business Administration, Institute of Management Sciences,
University of Lucknow, Lucknow, India

Abstract
Artificial intelligence (AI) is becoming increasingly embedded in the way businesses promote
their products, communicate with customers, and make  marketing decisions. Earlier
applications were mainly concerned with analysing customer data and predicting behaviour, whereas
current systems can also  generate content, recommend products, communicate with  customers, and
support decisions in real  time. This paper  examines the  development of AI -driven business
promotion through  a critical  review of academic and  institutional literature. Particular attention
is given to customer  segmentation and personalization, predictive  analytics, recommendation
systems, chatbots, marketing automation, and generative AI. The literature indicates that AI can make
promotional activities more responsive and  can reduce the  amount of  routine work involved
in analysing information and  producing content. At  the same time, the evidence does  not support
the idea that AI  automatically improves  marketing performance.  Results depend on the  quality of
data, the purpose for  which AI is used, organizational capabilities, and the way  customers perceive
AI-mediated communication. Privacy, security, algorithmic bias, transparency, and authenticity have
consequently become central  concerns. Recent studies on  generative AI are  especially relevant
because they show that consumers may react differently to AI-created communication than to human-
created communication. Looking ahead, multimodal generative  AI, real-time personalization,
predictive customer journeys, an d AI agents are  likely to expand  the range of  promotional
activities that can be automated or augmented. The paper argues that the long-term value of AI-driven
promotion will depend less  on automation alone  and more on  the combination  of technological
capability, human judgement, reliable data, and
responsible governance.

1. INTRODUCTION
The use of artificial intelligence in business has  changed considerably over the last decade.
AI is no  longer limited  to specialized technical departments  or experimental projects.  Businesses
increasingly use forms of  machine learning,  predictive analytics, natural language  processing, and
automated decision systems in activities that are directly connected with customers. Marketing is one
of the areas in which this change is particularly visible. A large part of modern promotion now takes
place through  digital channels,  where businesses can collect  information about  customer searches,
purchases, interactions, preferences , and responses to advertisements. AI  provides a way  of
processing this information at a scale that would be difficult to manage through conventional analysis
alone.
The connection between AI and marketing is not entirely new. Recommendation systems, automated
advertising systems, and predictive models were already  being used before  the recent popularity of
generative AI. Davenport et al. (2020), for example,  identified AI as  a technology capable  of
influencing both marketing strategy and  customer behaviour, particularly through activities such  as
personalization, segmentation and decision support. Huang and Rust (2021) subsequently proposed a
strategic framework  that distinguishes between  AI used for  repetitive tasks, AI  used to
process information and make decisions, and AI used in interactions involving human emotions.
What has changed more recently is the breadth of what AI can produce. Generative AI systems can
create written material, images, audio and video, which means that  AI is now  involved not only in
analysing customers  but also in  producing the  promotional material shown to  them. A
recent systematic review of 104 peer-reviewed studies found that generative AI research in marketing
is developing across several  areas, including psychology, communication, innovation  adoption and
human-computer interaction.
This development has created an interesting  tension. On one  hand, AI makes it possible to  produce
highly targeted  communication, automate repetitive work , and respond to customers  quickly. On
the other hand, the same systems raise questions about the amount of personal data businesses collect,
how decisions are made, whether automated communication feels authentic, and who  is responsible

The issue, therefore, is not simply  whether businesses should  use AI.  A more useful

circumstances under which AI-supported promotion is likely to be useful and the conditions that may
limit its effectiveness.
2. CONCEPTUAL FOUNDATION  OF AI-DRIVEN BUSINESS
PROMOTION
2.1 Artificial Intelligence in Business and Marketing
Artificial intelligence is a broad  term covering systems  that can  perform tasks commonly
associated with human cognitive abilities, including learning from information, recognizing patterns,
processing language, making predictions , and supporting decisions. In  business promotion,
the importance of AI comes largely from its ability to connect large amounts of customer and market
information with  particular marketing actions.  This makes AI  different from simple  automation.
Traditional automation follows predefined instructions. A machine-learning system, by contrast, can
identify patterns from  data and use those  patterns to generate  predictions or  classifications. The
distinction matters in marketing because customer behaviour is rarely completely predictable. People
respond differently to products, prices, messages , and situations, and  their preferences can change
over time.
Huang and Rust (2021) describe three broad forms of  AI capability in marketing. Mechanical AI is
associated with  routine and  repetitive activities, thinking AI  with analytical tasks  and decision-
making, and feeling AI with interaction and the interpretation of human responses. Their framework
is useful because it shows  that AI is  not one single  marketing technology. Different  forms of
AI perform different kinds of work.

Davenport et al. (2020) make a  related argument. They suggest that AI  can influence

how the technology  is incorporated into the  organization.  This is  important because discussions
of AI sometimes focus too heavily on what the technology can technically do and not enough on how
businesses actually use it.
In promotional activities, AI can therefore  be viewed as  a capability  rather than simply
a software product. It can help a business decide which customer group to approach, what message to
use, when  to communicate, which  product to  recommend, and how to  respond to
customer interactions. These decisions can be made separately or as parts of a connected system.
2.2 From Traditional Promotion to AI-Driven Promotion
Traditional promotional practices generally involved  identifying relatively broad customer
groups and developing campaigns  for those groups. A company might  divide its market  by age,
income, location, or other demographic characteristics and then develop advertisements intended for
each segment. This approach remains useful, particularly where businesses have limited information
about individual customers. Digital technologies have changed the amount  and type of  information
available to marketers. Instead of knowing only that a customer belongs to a particular demographic
group, a company may have information about browsing behaviour, previous purchases, responses to
earlier messages and interactions  across different  channels. AI can process  these differe nt
signals together.
The result  is a movement from  relatively static segmentation  towards more dynamic  forms of
targeting. A customer's previous  behaviour can  influence what the system  recommends next.
A campaign can  be adjusted according to  performance. An advertisement  can be  shown to a
narrower group based on predicted interests rather than a broad demographic category.
This does not  mean that AI  has made traditional  marketing obsolete.  Human judgement remains
important because customer data  does not necessarily explain why a  person behaves in  a particular
way. A  model may  identify a correlation  without understanding  the underlying context.
Marketers also have to decide whether a particular form of targeting is appropriate in the first place.
The development can therefore be understood as a shift in the balance between human judgement and
computational analysis. AI allows businesses to process more information and respond more quickly,
but the interpretation of that information remains an organizational responsibility.

2.3 Technologies Supporting AI-Driven Promotion
Several technologies contribute  to AI -driven promotion. Machine  learning is important
for classification and prediction. Predictive  analytics can  be used to  estimate customer behaviour,
demand, or campaign response. Recommendation systems match customers with products, services,
or content. Natural  language processing supports  language analysis and  conversational
systems, while generative AI produces new content.
Chatbots are an  example of whe re several technologies overlap. A  conversational system  can
interpret a customer's question, identify relevant information, and generate a response. In promotional
settings, this may involve product recommendations or assistance before a purchase. Research on AI-
enabled customer engagement  has increasingly examined these  forms of interaction  alongside
recommendation systems and other intelligent technologies.
Generative AI represents  a particularly significant  change because  the system can participa te in
content production itself. Instead of simply telling a marketer which customers are likely to respond
to a  campaign, a generative  system can help draft the campaign  message. This makes  AI relevant
to both the analytical and creative sides of promotion. At the same time, these technologies should not
be treated  as interchangeable. A  recommendation algorithm  and a  generative language model
perform different functions and  carry different risks. A prediction system  can produce
a wrong probability, whereas  a generative system  may produce an apparently  convincing but
factually incorrect statement. Understanding these differences is necessary when
evaluating AI-driven promotion.
3. APPLICATIONS OF  AI IN BUSINESS  PROMOTION
3.1 Customer Segmentation, Targeting and Personalization
Customer segmentation is  one of the  areas where AI  fits naturally  into marketing because
businesses have access to increasingly detailed customer information. A conventional segmentation
exercise might place customers into  a small numbe r of predefined groups.  Machine-learning
approaches can work with many  more variables  and can identify patterns  that may not  have been
specified in advance.

This is useful for targeting. A business does not necessarily want to show every customer the
same promotion. A predictive model can estimate the likelihood that different customers will respond
to a particular offer, product , or communication. The model can  then be incorporated  into an
advertising or customer-relationship system.
Personalization takes this process further.  Instead of deciding  only which  group should receive
a campaign, businesses can  tailor recommendations or messages to  individual users.
Recommendation systems are particularly  important in this  respect because they can  continuously
adjust what is presented to a customer according to previous interactions. Kumar et al. (2019) argue
that AI can support  personalized engagement marketing by helping firms use  customer information
to make interactions more relevant. The significance  of their work is that personalization is treated
as an engagement process  rather than simply an advertising technique. AI can affect  several points
in the relationship between a customer and a business.
There is, however, a point at  which personalization can  become uncomfortable. A highly accurate
recommendation is not necessarily a  positive experience if  customers feel that  they are
being monitored. This is  particularly important because the information used for personalization  is
often collected passively through digital interactions.
Recent research provides evidence that consumers do not always want more  personalization.
Leszczynska and Baltag (2024), for example, examined  consumers' willingness to share data for
personalized services and  advertisements and high lighted the continuing tension between
personalization and privacy.  Springer Link . The implication is fairly straightforward:  businesses
cannot assume that customers will exchange personal information for personalization simply because
the resulting advertisement is more relevant.
AI-driven personalization is consequently most  useful when relevance  and consumer
expectations are considered together. Technical accuracy is only one part of the outcome.
3.2 Generative AI and Promotional Content
The arrival of generative AI has  changed the discussion  about automated marketing. Earlier AI
applications mainly helped marketers understand information or make decisions. Generative systems
can now participate directly in producing promotional material.
A marketing team can use a  generative system to  produce alternative versions  of an email,  draft
social-media material, create product  descriptions, develop advertising  concepts, or produce visual

material. The  attraction is obvious  for organizations that have to  create a large  amount of
content across several channels.
The literature increasingly recognizes this potential. Kshetri et al. describe generative AI as relevant
to content creation, personalization, customer interaction, and other marketing functions. More recent
research continues to map  the expanding field, with Prasanna  and Kushwaha's systematic
review identifying 104 peer-reviewed studies on generative AI in marketing.
The practical advantage is not necessarily that AI produces better creative work than people. Its more
immediate advantage  is that it can produce  alternatives quickly. A  marketer who  would normally
spend several  hours preparing initial  drafts can  use AI to produce  a starting point  and then
spend more time editing and evaluating the material.
That distinction between assistance and replacement is important. Research published in the *Journal
of Retailing  and Consumer  Services* found that  the use of  generative AI  for social-media
content could reduce perceived  brand authenticity. The  negative re sponse was  weaker when
AI assisted human creators rather than replacing them.
A similar finding appears  in the work  of Kirk and  Givi (2024).  Across seven preregistered
experiments, they found that  when consumers believed  emotional marketing communications had
been written by AI rather than humans, positive word of mouth and loyalty were reduced. The effect
was weaker for factual communication and when AI was used for editing rather than producing the
communication from scratch.
These findings complicate the argument that  generative AI will  simply make
promotional communication more efficient. Efficiency is valuable to the organization, but
promotional communication  is ultimately evaluated  by people. If  customers interpret
extensive automation as a loss of authenticity, the organization may gain production efficiency while
losing some of the relational value of the communication.
Another issue is  accuracy. A generated  advertisement may sound  convincing while containing
an incorrect product  specification or an unsupported  claim. Human review  is therefore  not just a
matter of maintaining writing quality. It is also a form of risk control.

3.3 Predictive Analytics, Recommendation Systems and Conversational AI
Predictive analytics allows  businesses to move  from describing  previous customer behaviour
to estimating what may  happen next. Common applications include lead  scoring, churn prediction,
demand forecasting, and campaign-response prediction.  For promotional purposes, this can
help businesses dec ide where  to allocate limited  marketing resources. If  a company has  a large
number of potential customers, a predictive system can help identify those who appear more likely to
respond. The output does not guarantee a purchase, but it can provide information for prioritization.
Recommendation systems operate somewhat differently. They are generally concerned with deciding
what product, service, or content should be presented to a particular customer.
Their widespread use  illustrates how AI can  become part of everyday  promotional activity
without necessarily appearing as  an obvious  AI interaction. Conversational AI has  made the
relationship even more direct. Chatbots can  answer questions, provide information  about products,
and sometimes make  recommendations. Research  into generative  AI chatbots suggests
that characteristics such as  perceived competence, warmth , and authenticity can  affect customer
responses. One recent study  based on 282  participants found  relationships between
anthropomorphic characteristics of generative -AI chatbots and  customer engagement, experience ,
and recommendation behaviour.
Yet a chatbot does not automatically improve customer experience. A simple question can  often be
answered effectively by  an automated system, but  more c omplicated situations  may require
human intervention. Customers  may also become  frustrated when  an automated system
repeatedly fails to understand their request.
This illustrates a broader point about AI-driven promotion: the appropriate question is not whether a
task can be automated, but  whether automation is  appropriate for that particular task and  customer
situation.

4. EFFECTIVENESS AND  BUSINESS IMPACT OF  AI-DRIVEN
PROMOTION
4.1 Efficiency and Automation
The most straightforw ard argument for AI  in promotion  concerns efficiency. Marketing
departments perform many  repetitive tasks, from organizing  customer information  to monitoring
campaign results. Some of these tasks can be automated, allowing employees to spend more time on
planning and judgment.
     Huang and Rust's distinction between mechanical and thinking AI is useful here. Automation can
handle repetitive work, while  more advanced systems  can help process  information and
support decisions. Generative AI adds another dimension because it can reduce  the time needed to
produce initial versions of promotional content. This does not necessarily mean that the total cost of
marketing falls. Businesses still need  people to check  content, manage data,  integrate systems ,
and monitor AI outputs. In fact, the introduction of AI can create new work. Employees may have to
learn new  tools, establish approval  procedures, and check the accuracy of  generated material.

An organization that  measures only the number  of hours saved  may ther efore overestimate the

      AI should consequently be assessed in terms of the complete process. If it reduces routine work
without creating  equivalent verification costs, the efficiency  argument becomes stronger. If the
system produces large amounts of material that require extensive correction, the benefit is less clear.
4.2 Customer Engagement and Personalization
The relationship between  personalization and customer  engagement is  more complicated than
simply assuming that customers prefer individualized messages. AI can help a business respond more
quickly and make recommendations that are relevant to an individual's interests. This can reduce the
effort required to search  through products  or information. In some  situations, it can make the
customer experience feel more convenient.
       At the same time, relevance and intrusiveness can exist together. A customer may recognize that
a recommendation is accurate but still dislike the fact that the business has access to the information
used to generate it. Research on AI-enabled customer engagement has therefore increasingly focused
on trust and perceived value. The important question  is not only whether AI can  predict customer
preferences but whether customers are comfortable with the way those predictions are used.
The issue of  authenticity is particularly  important for  generative AI. Kirk and  Givi (2024)
found that perceived AI authorship could negatively  affect responses to  emotional communication,
while the study by the authors of the social-media research similarly found that disclosure of GenAI
content could reduce perceived  brand authenticity. suggests that  AI may have  different effects
depending on the  kind of promotional communication  involved. A customer  may not
care greatly whether an automatically generated product description contains AI assistance, while a
message intended to  communicate sympathy, gratitude , or a personal relationship  may be
judged differently.
4.3 Business Performance and Marketing Decisions
It is temp ting to connect AI  adoption directly with  sales growth,  but the evidence requires  more
caution. AI can provide  better information for  marketing decisions. It  can identify patterns  in
customer data, compare campaign performance , and help managers make decisions more quickly.
These capabilities may contribute to business performance, but they do not operate independently of
other factors. Suppose a business introduces an AI  recommendation system and  sales subsequently

5. CHALLENGES, LIMITATIONS, AND ETHICAL
CONSIDERATIONS
5.1 Privacy, Data Security and Consumer Trust
Data is central  to AI -driven promotion. Without information  about customers, many  forms of
personalization and prediction  become much less  useful. Yet the  collection and  processing of
customer information creates an important ethical and organizational responsibility.
Businesses may collect  information directly from  customers, obtain  it through transactions ,
or infer characteristics from  digital behaviour. The more  sophisticated AI becomes, the more
possible it is to  combine these differen t forms of information.  The OECD's 2024 work on AI, data
governance and privacy specifically notes that developments in AI, particularly generative AI, have
raised new questions about  data governance and  privacy. It  also emphasizes the need  to consider
AI and privacy frameworks together rather than  treating them as  unrelated policy areas. Privacy is
not simply a legal  issue. It can  influence whether  customers trust the organization. If consumers
do not understand  what information is being  collected or why  it is  being used, even
technically legitimate personalization may be perceived negatively.
      Security creates another  concern. A system  containing large quantities  of customer
information creates consequences if  that information  is accessed improperly. AI governance
therefore needs to include data management and security rather than focusing exclusively on model
accuracy. The OECD AI Principles emphasize privacy and data protection alongside fairness,
transparency, human agency, robustness and accountability. These principles are relevant to business
promotion because promotional AI systems interact directly with individual consumers.

5.2 Algorithmic Bias, Transparency and Authenticity
Bias can enter an AI system through the data used to train it, the variables selected by developers, or
the way  the model's output is applied. In marketing, biased  systems may  influence who receives
certain advertisements, recommendations, or opportunities. The difficulty is that bias is  not always
obvious. A system  can appear technically accurate  while producing  systematically different
outcomes for  particular groups. This  is why testing  needs to consider  more than
average performance. Transparency is related  to this issue. Consumers do not  necessarily need
to understand the mathematical  details of a  model, but they  may reasonably want to  know when
they are interacting with AI or when an important recommendation is being generated automatically.
   The OECD Principles  call for transparency  and explainability  and emphasize providing
meaningful information about AI systems and their limitations. Generative AI creates another aspect
of transparency: authorship. The consumer  may want to know  whether a promotional  message was
written by a person or generated by a machine, especially when the message is personal or emotional.

The experimental evidence discussed earlier is relevant here. Kirk and Givi (2024) found that
perceived AI authorship reduced positive responses to emotional marketing communication in several
experiments. The effect was related to  perceived authenticity. ScienceDirect.  Similarly, research
on GenAI-created social-media content found that AI use could reduce perceived brand authenticity,
particularly when AI  replaced rather than assisted  human cr eators. These findings do  not mean
that consumers reject AI -generated content  in every situation. Instead, they suggest that  the type
of communication matters.  The role of  AI may be  less problematic when it performs an  editing or
supporting function than when it is presented as the source of a highly personal, human-like message.
5.3 Organizational and Measurement Limitations
A business can purchase an AI  tool relatively easily. Building the organizational conditions needed
to use  it effectively  is much harder. One problem is  data quality. AI  models learn from  available
information, so incomplete or inconsistent  data can  affect their outputs. Another  problem is
integration. A marketing  department may have customer  information stored in  several systems
that were not originally  designed to work  together. Skills are another  constraint. Marketing
professionals do not  necessarily need to become  AI engineers, but  they increasingly need
enough knowledge to understand  model outputs, recognize  limitations, and eval uate generated
material. NIST's AI Risk  Management Framework is  useful in this  context because  it treats AI

           Measurement is perhaps  the most difficult  issue. AI may  improve several  parts of the

avoid evaluating AI solely  through short-term performance numbers. Process  quality, customer
experience, data governance, employee productivity, and long-term trust may also matter.

6. FUTURE TRENDS  IN AI-DRIVEN BUSINESS PROMOTION
6.1 Generative and Multimodal AI
Generative AI is  likely to  remain an important area  of marketing  development. Current
systems can  already generate text  and images,  while increasingly capable systems  work across
several types of media. This creates the possibility of producing coordinated promotional campaigns
involving written, visual, audio, and video content.
      The attraction for  businesses is clear. A campaign can  potentially be  adapted for several
platforms without requiring every  version to  be produced manually. A  single product
description could become a social-media post, advertising script, email draft, and visual concept. The
research literature is still catching up with this development. The 2025 systematic review by Prasanna
and Kushwaha demonstrates how quickly the research  area has expanded, while also showing that
much of  the literature remains  concerned with conceptual and  emerging applications rather  than
long-term organizational outcomes.
Multimodal AI should therefore be treated as an emerging direction rather than a guaranteed
improvement. Mor e content does  not necessarily  mean better communication. In  fact, if
organizations can produc e promotional material very quickly, the ability to  check that
material becomes even more  important. There is also  the question of  consistency. A brand  may
want its communication to  have a recognizable  identity across platforms.  Generating content
through several AI systems could make that consistency harder to maintain unless appropriate human
review and brand controls are used.

6.2 Hyper-Personalization, Predictive Marketing and AI Agents
A likely direction  of development is  increasingly real-time personalization. Instead  of
updating customer recommendations periodically, businesses may use  AI systems
that respond continuously to new  information. This could make  marketing more adaptive. A
customer's recent interaction could influence what the customer sees next, which offer is presented,
or whether a particular communication is sent at all.
          The next step may involve AI agents. An agentic marketing system could potentially monitor
campaign performance, interpret customer information, generate alternatives , and recommend
or execute selected actions. Such systems are  still developing, so strong claims about their eventual
commercial impact would  be premature. The more autonomous  these s ystems become, the more
important oversight becomes. An automated system  that changes a  campaign once  is one thing;
a system that continuously makes decisions affecting thousands of customers is another.

6.3 Responsible and Human-Centered AI Promotion
Responsible AI is  likely to become  a more important  part of  marketing practice as
AI becomes more capable.  The OECD AI Principles emphasize human rights, privacy, fairness,
transparency, robustness, security, and accountability. For marketing departments, these frameworks
have practical implications. Businesses need to  know what data  an AI system uses, how its outputs
are evaluated, who is responsible for errors, and when a human should intervene. Human involvement
will probably remain particularly  important in  areas where communication depends  on empathy,
brand identity, or complex judgement. The evidence  concerning AI-generated emotional
communication suggests why. Consumers may judge a machine-generated message differently from
a message they believe came from a person.
Responsible AI therefore should not mean preventing organizations from using AI. It should
mean designing the relationship between humans and AI carefully. Businesses need to decide where
automation is useful, where human  involvement adds value , and how  consumers can retain
meaningful control over their  data and  interactions. The final stage  in the figure  is deliberately
governance rather than  complete automation. As AI  becomes more capable, responsible use
becomes more important rather than less important.
7. CONCLUSION
Artificial intelligence has become  an important part  of business promoti on because it
changes both how marketing  information is processed  and how  customers can be reached. The
technology has developed from relatively  narrow analytical applications toward systems capable of
prediction, recommendation, conversation, and content generation. This has widened the role  of AI
from a back -office analytical tool to  something that can  directly shape the  customer's
promotional experience. The literature reviewed in this paper  points to several  areas in which AI is
already being used. Customer segmentation and  personalization allow businesses to  work with
detailed behavioural information. Predictive analytics can support targeting and campaign decisions.
Recommendation systems can  match products or content  with individual  customers. Cha tbots
provide automated interaction,  while generative AI  can assist  with the production of  promotional
material.

These applications offer genuine advantages, but the evidence does not justify treating AI as
an automatic solution to marketing problems. The effectiveness of AI depends heavily on the quality
of data, the suitability  of the application, the organization's  ability to integrate the  technology, and
the way customers respond to it. In  particular, recent research  has raised important questions about
authenticity. Some consumers respond negatively when they believe that emotional or brand-related
communication has been generated by AI rather than by humans.
The main challenges  are therefore not  purely technical. Privacy,  security, transparency,
algorithmic bias and  consumer trust  are closely connected  with the effectiveness  of AI-
driven promotion. An organization may have a technically sophisticated personalization system and
still damage  customer relationships if people feel  that their data  is being used in ways they
do not understand or accept.
Generative AI makes  this issue even  more relevant. It  can increase  the speed and scale  of
content production, but it also  creates risks involving  accuracy, authenticity , and accountability.
Human review is con sequently likely to remain  important, especially for  promotional
communication that involves  personal claims, emotional messages , or information that  could
materially affect  consumers. Future developments may  take AI -driven promotion toward
multimodal content generation, more continuous personalization, predictive customer journeys, and
AI agents  capable of  carrying out interconnected marketing  tasks. These  developments are
promising, but they should not be treated as inevitable improvements. Greater automation can create
greater consequences when something goes wrong.
The central lesson  from the literature is  therefore relatively  simple: the value of  AI in
business promotion lies not  in automation by  itself, but in  how effectively technology  and human
judgement are combined. Businesses  need reliable information, suitable  technological systems ,
and employees capable  of questioning AI outputs  rather than accepting  them automatically.
They also need governance  arrangements that  address privacy, f airness, transparency , and
accountability. AI-driven promotion is consequently best understood as an evolving form of human-
technology collaboration. Its future success  will depend  on whether organizations can  use the
analytical and creative capabilities of AI without losing the trust, authenticity, and human judgement
on which long-term customer relationships depend.
