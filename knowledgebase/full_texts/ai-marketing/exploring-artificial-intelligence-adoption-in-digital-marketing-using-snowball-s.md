A B S T R A C T
Artificial intelligence (AI) is transforming digital marketing by enabling advanced analytics,
automation, and personalised customer engagement. Despite growing adoption, qualitative insights
into how small retail enterprises in emerging economies adopt AI remain limited. This study examines
AI adoption in digital marketing among small retailers in Cape Town using an interpretivist qualitative
case study design. Grounded in an integrated Technology–Organisation–Environment (TOE) and
Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (U TAUT) framework, data were collected
through semi-structured interviews with 18 participants identified via snowball sampling. Thematic
analysis shows that AI adoption is driven by competitive pressure, customer expectations, and
perceived performance benefits, but is constrained by skills gaps, cost concerns, and data -related
challenges. The study extends TOE–UTAUT theory by demonstrating how platform-based AI tools
reduce technological barriers while amplifying organisational and capability-related constraints in
emerging market contexts. The findings provide actionable insights for policymakers and practitioners
aiming to support sustainable digital transformation in small business ecosystems.

Introduction
Digital transformation has become a strategic imperative for businesses aiming to remain competitive in increasingly dynamic, data-
driven markets (Kringelum, Holm, Holmgren, Friis & Jensen, 2025). Among the technologies driving this transformation, artificial
intelligence (AI) has emerged as a key enabler of advanced analytics, automation, and personalised customer engagement in digital
marketing. AI applications such as machine learning-based analytics, chatbots, recommendation systems, and predictive modelling
enable firms to analyse customer behaviour, optimise marketing campaigns, and improve decision-making in real time (Sagala &
Őri, 2025).
For small retail businesses, AI offers significant opportunities to enhance marketing effectiveness and compete with larg er
organisations that traditionally possess greater resources and analytical capabilities. Through AI-enabled digital marketing tools,
small retailers can personalise communication, automate customer interactions, improve demand forecasting, and optimise inventory
management. These capabilities are particularly relevant in competitive urban environments such as Cape Town, where customer
expectations for digital engagement continue to rise.
Despite the growing accessibility of AI through cloud-based platforms and software-as-a-service solutions, AI adoption among small
businesses remains uneven. Previous research indicates that adoption is influenced not only by technological availability but also by
organisational readiness, managerial perceptions, and environmental pressures (Norling, 2025). However, much of the existing
literature relies on quantitative surveys that measure adoption levels or performance outcomes, providing limited insight into how
small business decision-makers interpret, evaluate, and implement AI in practice (Ali Alqararah, Shehadeh & Yaseen, 2025).

This limitation is especially pronounced in emerging economy contexts, where infrastructural constraints, skills shortages, and
contextual factors shape technology adoption differently from developed markets. Understanding the subjective experiences and
decision-making processes of small retail business owners and managers is therefore essential to developing a holistic understanding
of AI adoption in digital marketing.
To address this gap, this study adopts an interpretivist qualitative approach grounded in an integrated Technology–Organisation–
Environment (TOE) framework and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT). The TOE framework
explains adoption at the organisational level by examining technological, organisational, and environmental conditions, while
UTAUT provides insight into individual-level behavioural intentions and perceptions influencing technology use. Together, these
frameworks offer a comprehensive perspective for examining both structural and human factors shaping AI adoption (Nguyen,
Nguyen & Tran, 2025).
Using a qualitative case study design and snowball sampling, the study explores AI adoption among small retail businesses in Cape
Town. The research aims to answer the following questions: How do small retailers perceive AI in digital marketing? What factors
influence adoption decisions? What are the perceived impacts on business performance? By addressing these questions, the study
contributes context-specific insights to the digital marketing and business ecosystem literature and provides practical guidance for
small businesses and policymakers seeking to support AI-driven transformation.
Literature Review
Artificial Intelligence in Digital Marketing
Artificial intelligence refers to computational systems capable of performing tasks that typically require human intelligence, such as
learning, reasoning, prediction, and decision-making. In digital marketing, AI enables advanced data analytics, automation, and
personalisation, allowing firms to shift from mass marketing to customer-centric strategies (Nalbant & Aydin,2025). Common
applications include customer segmentation, predictive modelling, recommendation engines, chatbots, sentiment analysis, and
marketing automation (Vij et al.,2025).
AI-powered analytics enable marketers to process large volumes of structured and unstructured data from various digital touchpoints,
including social media, websites, and transactional systems. This capability provides real-time insights into customer preferences,
behaviour patterns, and purchase intentions, enabling more accurate targeting and improved campaign effectiveness (Basu, Aktar &
Kumar, 2025).
AI Adoption in Small Retail Businesses
Small retail businesses differ significantly from large enterprises in structure, resources, and decision-making processes. The adoption
of advanced technologies such as AI is often driven by owner-managers, whose perceptions, skills, and risk tolerance strongly
influence strategic choices. Although AI tools have become more accessible through cloud-based platforms and software-as-a-service
models, small retailers still face challenges related to cost, data availability, technical expertise, and organisational change (Syam,
2025).
Research indicates that small businesses adopt AI primarily for operational efficiency, customer relationship management, and
marketing automation (Shoeb & Zillul, 2025). However, adoption is often incremental and experimental rather than systematic. In
emerging markets, additional barriers include limited digital infrastructure, uneven access to skills, and uncertainty regarding return
on investment.
Technology–Organisation–Environment (TOE) Framework
The TOE framework explains organisational technology adoption through three contextual dimensions:
i. Technological context, which includes perceived benefits, complexity, and compatibility of AI tools.
ii. Organisational context, encompassing digital skills, managerial support, financial resources, and strategic orientation.
iii. Environmental context, including competitive pressure, customer expectations, industry norms, and regulatory conditions.
In small retail environments, environmental pressure from digitally advanced competitors and increasing customer expectations often
acts as a catalyst for AI adoption (Loo, Ramachandran & Raja Yusof, 2025).
Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT)
UTAUT explains individual technology adoption through four key constructs: performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social
influence, and facilitating conditions (Chen, Fan & Azam, 2025). In small businesses, owner-managers’ beliefs about the usefulness
and ease of use of AI significantly influence adoption decisions. Facilitating conditions, such as access to training  and digital
infrastructure, also shape adoption outcomes.

Integrating TOE and UTAUT provides a multi-level perspective on AI adoption (Ates & Polat, 2025). TOE addresses structural
feasibility, while UTAUT captures behavioural intentions and perceptions. This integrated approach is particularly suited to
qualitative inquiry in small business ecosystems, where human agency and contextual factors interact dynamically (Nguyen, Nguyen
& Tran, 2025).
Snowball Sampling in Qualitative Technology Research
Snowball sampling is a non-probability sampling technique commonly used in qualitative research to access hard-to-reach or
specialised populations (Ting, Memon, Thurasamy & Cheah, 2025). In studies of emerging technologies, where expertise may be
unevenly distributed, snowball sampling allows researchers to identify knowledgeable participants through referrals (Tasistro-Hart
et al., 2025). Although the method has limitations regarding generalisability, it is well suited to exploratory research focused on depth
rather than breadth (Mandelkow, 2025).
Research Questions and Propositions
Research Questions
i. RQ1: How do decision-makers in small retail businesses perceive AI in digital marketing?
ii. RQ2: Which technological, organisational, and environmental factors influence AI adoption?
iii. RQ3: How do managerial perceptions influence AI adoption decisions?
iv. RQ4: What are the perceived impacts of AI-driven digital marketing on business performance?
Research Propositions
i. P1: Technological compatibility and perceived benefits positively influence AI adoption in small retail digital marketing.
ii. P2: Organisational readiness and managerial digital literacy facilitate AI adoption.
iii. P3: Competitive pressure and customer digital expectations drive AI adoption.
iv. P4: Performance expectancy and facilitating conditions significantly influence managerial adoption intentions.
v. P5: AI adoption positively influences customer engagement, operational efficiency, and business performance.

Research and Methodology
This study employs an interpretivist qualitative case study design to examine AI adoption in digital marketing among small retail
businesses in Cape Town. A snowball sampling strategy was used to recruit knowledgeable participants with practical experience in
AI-enabled marketing.
The sampling process began with five purposively selected seed participants, identified according to the following criteria:
(a) ownership or managerial responsibility in a small retail business,
(b) active use of AI tools in digital marketing (e.g. chatbots, analytics platforms), and
(c) willingness to participate and refer other relevant participants.
These initial participants facilitated access to additional respondents through referral chains, resulting in a final sample of 18
participants. Data collection continued until theoretical saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new themes or
insights emerged from subsequent interviews. Participants represented diverse retail sub-sectors, including clothing, electronics,
grocery, and beauty products. Business sizes ranged from micro-enterprises (fewer than five employees) to small enterprises (up to
50 employees). Participants had varying levels of AI experience, ranging from one to five years, primarily through platform-based
tools such as Google Analytics, Meta Ads AI, and chatbot systems.
Data were collected through semi-structured interviews lasting between 30 and 60 minutes. All interviews were audio-recorded and
transcribed verbatim. Thematic analysis was conducted using a hybrid coding approach informed by the TOE–UTAUT framework.
To ensure trustworthiness, the study applied the following measures:
i. Credibility: Member checking was conducted by sharing summaries of findings with selected participants for validation.
ii. Transferability: Thick descriptions of the research context and participants were provided to support applicability to
similar settings.
iii. Dependability: An audit trail documenting data collection, coding decisions, and theme development was maintained.

Findings and Discussion
Findings indicate that AI adoption is driven by competitive pressure, customer expectations, and perceived performance benefits.
Applications such as chatbots, analytics tools, and personalised marketing enhance customer engagement and operational efficiency.
However, challenges such as limited digital skills and cost constraints remain.
In practice, small retailers widely adopted AI-powered social media analytics and scheduling tools to identify optimal posting times,
analyse audience engagement, and refine content strategies (Murrar et al.,2025). Participants reported improved online reach and
higher engagement levels because of data-driven content decisions. Similarly, chatbots and automated messaging systems were
commonly implemented to provide instant customer support, manage routine enquiries, and guide customers through the purchasing
process. These applications significantly reduced response times and labour costs while improving communication efficiency.
Personalisation emerged as a central benefit of AI adoption. Retailers used AI-enabled email marketing and recommendation systems
to deliver targeted promotions and product suggestions based on customer preferences and behaviour (Aziz et al., 2025). Participants
perceived these personalised marketing practices to enhance customer satisfaction, increase conversion rates, and strengthen long-
term customer relationships. In addition, AI-driven demand forecasting tools played a critical role in inventory management by
enabling retailers to anticipate demand fluctuations, optimise stock levels, and minimise stock-related losses.
Despite these benefits, several challenges constrained the effective adoption of AI. Participants highlighted limited digital skills and
data literacy as key barriers, restricting their ability to fully exploit AI tools. Cost concerns, data privacy issues, and a  lack of
transparency in algorithmic decision-making further influenced adoption decisions. These challenges underscore the importance of
organisational readiness and adequate support structures in facilitating AI adoption.
Managerial perceptions of usefulness and facilitating conditions were decisive in shaping adoption decisions, confirming the
relevance of key UTAUT constructs, particularly performance expectancy and facilitating conditions. At the same time, technological
compatibility, organisational capabilities, and environmental pressures closely aligned with the Technology –Organisation–
Environment (TOE) framework, supporting the integrated TOE–UTAUT model proposed in this study.

Overall, the findings are consistent with existing literature on AI adoption in small businesses while also highlighting the contextual
realities of an emerging market environment. The results emphasise the critical role of human judgement, continuous learning, and
strategic alignment in shaping AI-driven digital marketing outcomes, reinforcing the view that AI serves as a strategic enabler rather
than a replacement for managerial decision-making.
Conclusion
This study shows that AI adoption in small retail digital marketing is influenced by the interplay of technological benefits,
organisational readiness, and environmental pressures. Integrating TOE and UTAUT offers a comprehensive framework fo r
understanding adoption dynamics. The study contributes to theory by extending TOE–UTAUT integration and provides practical
insights for supporting sustainable AI adoption in emerging markets.
