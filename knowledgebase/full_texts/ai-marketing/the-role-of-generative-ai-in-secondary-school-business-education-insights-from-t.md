ABSTRACT

Background/Introduction/Purpose: Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) has entered classrooms
more quickly than the evidence base needed to guide its use, and that evidence base remains
concentrated in higher education. Comparatively little is known about secondary school business
education, a curricular area that brings together accounting, business studies, economics, financial
literacy, marketing, and retail, and in which teachers move between subjects whose epistemological and
procedural demands differ considerably. Existing studies also tend to treat business education as a
single homogeneous domain, or to examine one of its subjects in isolation. This study addresses that gap
by examining how secondary school business education teachers appraise the pedagogical value, the
risks, and the subject-specific limits of GenAI tools such as ChatGPT. The study focuses on teachers’
professional judgment rather than student outcomes, since teachers mediate the introduction,
adaptation, or refusal of such tools in actual classrooms.

Methods/Design: The study adopts an exploratory qualitative design. Eighteen teachers of students
aged 13 to 18, purposively recruited and spanning less than one year to more than twenty-one years of
teaching experience, took part in a structured in-person workshop held in February 2024. The workshop
comprised an introductory input session, a hands-on phase in which participants prompted ChatGPT
with examination questions and teaching resources drawn from their own subjects, and facilitated
group discussions documented on a shared Padlet. Data comprised the Padlet outputs, an anonymous
thirteen-item exit questionnaire, and field notes. Analysis followed reflexive thematic analysis,
proceeding inductively while sensitised by the Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK)
framework.

Results/Findings: Four themes were generated. Teachers valued GenAI for pedagogical enhancement
and efficiency, particularly in lesson preparation, differentiation, and generating examples. They raised
substantial concerns about academic integrity, the erosion of independent thinking, and unequal access
to the technology. They also identified pronounced subject-specific differences: GenAI was seen as
effective for theoretical explanation in accounting but weak in computation, formatting, and the
application of accounting standards; and, in economics, as offering global rather than locally relevant
examples, no current data, and unreliable graphs. Across all themes, teachers identified professional
development and systemic support as preconditions for responsible use.

Conclusions/Discussion/Implications: The pedagogical value of GenAI is not uniform across business
education but tracks the epistemological and procedural demands of each subject, indicating that its
integration is not pedagogically neutral. The findings carry implications for teacher professional
development, assessment design, including Assessment as Learning, and school-level policy on the
responsible use of AI. Further research can examine longer-term effects on learning and directly
compare subjects and educational levels.

The rapid advancement of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) tools, such as ChatGPT, Gemini,
Claude, and other popular language models, has revolutionised education through personalised content
creation, efficient lesson planning, and interactive learning environments (Annuš, 2023; Grassini, 2023).
Research predominantly examines GenAI’s applications in higher education (Geerling et al., 2023; Mihai
et al., 2024; Wood et al., 2023); findings from these studies may not necessarily apply to secondary
school settings, given differences in student demographics and educational goals. For example, in the
area of business education, MBA students are more experienced and typically engage with content
tailored to practical, real-world applications, whereas K-12 students require foundational skill-building
and age-appropriate methodologies (Terwiesch, 2023). This distinction underscores the need for
focused research on K-12 business education. Given the rising demand for AI literacy among younger
students, understanding teachers’ perspectives on integrating GenAI into secondary school curricula
becomes increasingly critical.

Drawing on insights from a workshop with 18 teachers of students aged 13 to 18, specialising in business
education subjects such as accounting, business studies, economics, financial literacy, marketing, and
retail, this study investigates teachers’ perceptions of the potential benefits, challenges, and ethical
considerations of using GenAI tools in secondary school classrooms. The object of the study is teachers’
professional judgment, not student outcomes, and not the measurement of AI literacy. It asks how
practitioners appraise the pedagogical value and the limits of these tools within the subjects they teach.
This paper thus aims to address an important research gap and to provide a basis for curriculum and
policy decisions concerning the responsible integration of GenAI in schools.

Literature Review

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) has rapidly become a significant topic in educational research
and policy. Existing scholarship has largely focused on higher education and broad discussions of
pedagogical opportunities, while comparatively less attention has been paid to school education and
subject-specific curricular contexts. In response to these concerns, this section first reviews the broader
educational literature on GenAI, then examines recent work in business, accounting, and economics
education, and finally outlines the theoretical framework and the study’s relevance.

The Broader Educational Literature on GenAI

This literature identifies a recurring tension between pedagogical promise and educational risk. On the
one hand, GenAI is often described as a tool to support lesson planning, content generation, feedback,
differentiation, and student support (Alfarwan, 2025; Annuš, 2023; Chiu, 2024; Mosaiyebzadeh et al.,
2023). Studies also suggest that, when used as a scaffold rather than a substitute for thinking, GenAI can
support help-seeking, self-regulated learning, and iterative feedback processes (Alfarwan, 2025; Ng et
al., 2024). On the other hand, systematic reviews and conceptual analyses caution that these benefits
are highly dependent on context, task design, teacher mediation, and students’ ability to evaluate AI-
generated outputs critically (Alfarwan, 2025; Yan et al., 2024; Zhang & Tur, 2024). The literature,
therefore, does not support a simplistic view of GenAI as either inherently transformative or inherently
harmful. Rather, it points to a conditional form of educational value shaped by pedagogy, assessment
design, and institutional support.

A second line of literature focuses on the risks and contradictions associated with GenAI integration.
Concerns about hallucinations, inaccuracies, embedded bias, data privacy, plagiarism, and the
outsourcing of academic labour are now well established (Li et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2023; Memarian &
Doleck, 2023; Vargas-Murillo et al., 2023). The literature also extends this discussion by arguing that
GenAI adoption raises not only technical concerns but also ethical and regulatory questions that require
explicit policy attention and stronger AI literacy (e.g., Alfarwan, 2025; García-López & Trujillo-Liñán,
2025). Related work on teacher adoption likewise indicates that teachers’ decisions to use ChatGPT are
shaped not only by perceptions of usefulness and efficiency but also by concerns about student misuse,
diminished learning quality, and uncertainty about the teacher's future role (e.g., García-López &
Trujillo-Liñán, 2025; Li et al., 2024; Memarian & Doleck, 2023; Vargas-Murillo et al., 2023). This is
especially relevant in school settings, where students are still developing evaluative judgement and
disciplinary habits of mind. Evidence generated in schools is comparatively sparse but points in the same
direction. Busuttil (2025a), working with twenty-one secondary informatics teachers, found that they
valued LLMs for engagement and personalised support while identifying assessment reform, students’
critical evaluation of AI outputs, and over-reliance as the conditions on which that value depended. The
literature thus suggests that the central issue is not whether GenAI can be introduced into education,
but rather under what pedagogical and ethical conditions it can be used without undermining the
quality and integrity of learning.

AI in Business Education

Within business education, the literature is growing but remains fragmented. A substantial proportion of
publications focus on higher education and discuss business education in broad terms, often
emphasising administrative efficiency, curriculum redesign, case-based teaching, and the need to
prepare students for AI-shaped workplaces (e.g., Acquah et al., 2025; Blondeel et al., 2025; Cribben &
Zeinali, 2023; Geerling et al., 2023; Mihai et al., 2024; Valcea et al., 2024; Xu, 2024). Recent peer-
reviewed scholarship has sharpened this discussion by indicating that GenAI in business education is
characterised by a series of paradoxes. Valcea et al. (2024), for example, argue that ChatGPT can expand
access to ideas and accelerate participation while simultaneously weakening originality, reflective
judgement, and disciplinary depth when used uncritically. This is an important insight for business
education because the field requires more than mere information retrieval: students need to interpret
data, reason through cases, justify decisions, and communicate context-sensitive arguments.

Research in accounting education helps clarify the subject-specific affordances and limitations of GenAI.
Wood et al. (2023) found that ChatGPT could approximate average performance on some accounting
assessment questions, yet performed less successfully on mathematically demanding tasks and on
discipline-specific problems requiring procedural accuracy. More recent journal studies refine this
picture. Zhou and Luo (2025) report that students perceive GenAI as supportive of learning efficiency,
knowledge acquisition, and engagement in accounting. Blondeel et al. (2025) similarly show that
students often value GenAI as an accessible, low-pressure help-seeking tool for accounting data
analytics, but continue to prefer human support when accuracy and interpretation are critical. These
studies suggest an important contradiction: GenAI appears to be strongest when supporting first-pass
explanation, feedback, and guidance, but weaker when tasks require precision, verification, and
professional judgement. This contradiction is particularly relevant for school accounting, where
conceptual understanding and procedural correctness need to develop together.

Emerging research in economics education points in a similar direction. Acquah et al. (2025) report
positive associations among economics students’ use of ChatGPT, self-efficacy, and perceived academic
performance, suggesting that AI can serve as motivational and cognitive support. At the same time,
Nagel and Zlatkin-Troitschanskaia (2025) demonstrate persistent weaknesses among economics
education students and teacher trainees in critically evaluating online information, implying that access
to AI-generated explanations does not by itself ensure sound judgment. Bröse et al. (2026) further
examine ChatGPT as an economics tutor and explicitly frame its educational value in terms of both
capabilities and limitations. These studies reinforce the broader argument that in economics, the
usefulness of GenAI depends not only on what the tool can generate, but also on whether students and
teachers can interrogate the quality, evidential basis, and contextual fit of its responses. This issue is
especially salient in school economics and the other school business education subjects, where local
examples, graph interpretation, current data, and economically sound reasoning are central to learning.

Across these strands, one consistent limitation in the literature is the dominance of higher education.
Much less is known about the implications of GenAI in secondary school business education, even
though this field often combines accounting, business studies, economics, financial literacy, and retail

Theoretical Framework

This study is guided by the Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK) framework developed
by Mishra and Koehler (2006) and extended for the age of generative artificial intelligence by Mishra et
al. (2023). TPACK is an appropriate lens because the educational value of GenAI in school business
education cannot be understood as a purely technical issue. The significance of tools such as ChatGPT
depends on how teachers assess their pedagogical usefulness in relation to the content they teach,
students' needs, and the aims of school business education. In this sense, the framework allows the
study to move beyond a simple question of whether teachers use GenAI and instead examine how they
evaluate its educational potential, constraints, and implications for practice. The framework has already
been applied at the school level in this way: Busuttil and Calleja (2025) used TPACK to examine
secondary mathematics teachers’ beliefs and practices regarding ChatGPT, finding that their appraisals
of the tool closely aligned with their existing pedagogical philosophies.

At the core of TPACK are three interrelated domains of teacher knowledge: technological knowledge
(TK), pedagogical knowledge (PK), and content knowledge (CK). In this study, TK refers to teachers’
understanding of the affordances and limitations of GenAI tools, including their capacity to generate
explanations, examples, prompts, feedback, and classroom materials. PK refers to teachers’
understanding of instructional strategies, classroom interaction, assessment, differentiation, and the
conditions that support meaningful learning. CK refers to teachers’ understanding of the subjects that
constitute business education in the secondary school context, including accounting, business studies,
economics, marketing, retail, and financial literacy. The framework is valuable not only because it
identifies these domains, but because it foregrounds the professional judgement required to bring them
together in ways that are educationally defensible.

This emphasis on integration reflects Shulman’s (1986a, 1986b, 1987) argument that expert teaching
depends on more than possession of subject knowledge or generic pedagogy in isolation. His concept of
pedagogical content knowledge (PCK) highlights the importance of transforming subject matter into
comprehensible forms for students through examples, analogies, illustrations, explanations, and other
representations. This insight is relevant to business education, where students are often asked to
engage with abstract concepts, technical terminology, and applied reasoning. For example, Mizzi (2024)
discusses that, in school economics, teachers develop PCK by connecting disciplinary ideas to real-life
scenarios, using varied representations, and adapting explanations to students’ needs. Retaining this
insight is important in this study because GenAI may assist with generating examples and explanations,
but teachers need to judge whether these representations are accurate, age-appropriate, and
pedagogically powerful.

The intersections within TPACK are central to understanding teachers’ perceptions of GenAI.
Technological pedagogical knowledge (TPK) concerns how teaching and learning may change when
GenAI is introduced into classroom practice, for example, through differentiated explanations,
brainstorming support, formative feedback, or lesson planning. Technological content knowledge (TCK)
concerns the relationship between GenAI and the specific demands of subject matter, including whether
these tools can explain accounting principles accurately, support economic reasoning, or generate useful
business case material. PCK remains essential because teachers need to interpret the curriculum,
anticipate misconceptions, and adapt representations to students’ abilities and prior knowledge. The
contribution of Swan and Hofer (2011) is relevant here, as their work on technological pedagogical
content knowledge in economics education shows that technology integration is most educationally
meaningful when it is aligned with disciplinary purposes rather than added superficially. Similarly, Ayers
(2016) emphasises that effective teaching in economics requires pedagogical strategies that help
students make interdisciplinary connections, engage actively with content, and develop understanding
rather than merely reproduce information.

Taken together, these perspectives indicate that TPACK provides a suitable framework for the present
study. It enables teachers’ views of GenAI to be interpreted as professionally grounded judgements
about the relationship between technology, pedagogy, and subject matter. It also helps explain why the
value of GenAI is likely to vary across the business education subjects. A teaching tool may be useful for
generating ideas, simplifying explanations, or supporting discussion, yet it may be less effective when
mathematical accuracy, local economic context, or subject-specific conventions are required. This
interpretation is also consistent with Pereira et al. (2018), who argue that business education research
benefits from examining how technological, pedagogical, and content knowledge are brought together
rather than treating technology use as a stand-alone innovation. In this way, TPACK offers a coherent
framework for examining both the promise and the limits of GenAI in school business education, while
keeping in view the continuing importance of teacher expertise in shaping meaningful learning.

The Relevance of the Study and Research Questions

The literature reviewed above underscores the relevance and necessity of the present study. Although
recent scholarship has expanded rapidly, it is still shaped by three main gaps. First, the evidence base is
heavily concentrated in higher education, with limited research focused on school education (e.g.,
Cribben & Zeinali, 2023; Geerling et al., 2023; Mihai et al., 2024; Wood et al., 2023). This gap is
significant given the growing need for AI literacy among younger students to prepare them to become
potential citizens, workers, and employers. Second, studies tend to examine GenAI either in general
educational terms or within individual disciplines, whereas school business education typically brings
together several related subjects within a single curricular area. Third, much of the published work
focuses on student performance, tool capabilities, or broad discussions of ethics, while relatively less
attention has been paid to how school teachers interpret the pedagogical potential, practical challenges,
and ethical implications of GenAI across the business education subjects. This makes teachers’
perspectives especially important because they mediate the introduction, adaptation, limitation, or
resistance of such tools in actual classrooms.

The relevance of this study lies in its capacity to address these gaps by examining how school business
education teachers perceive GenAI in relation to teaching, learning, assessment, and subject-specific
practice. The study is also timely because business education is a particularly revealing context for
examining GenAI: it encompasses conceptual, procedural, ethical, and context-dependent forms of
knowledge, and it prepares students to participate in an economy increasingly shaped by digital
technologies. Understanding how teachers view GenAI in this setting can contribute not only to the
literature on AI in education but also to curriculum development, teacher education, and policy
discussions concerning responsible AI integration in schools.

In light of these considerations, the study is guided by the following research questions:

1. How do secondary school teachers perceive the impact of AI tools such as ChatGPT on teaching
and learning in the business education subjects?
2. What concerns do school teachers have about the use of AI tools in the business education
subjects, and what strategies do they suggest for addressing these concerns?

Methodology

This study adopts an exploratory qualitative design to investigate school teachers’ perceptions of
integrating GenAI tools into business education, using a structured professional development workshop
as the data-collection setting. A qualitative approach was considered appropriate because the study
aims to capture participants’ subjective experiences, interpretations, and meaning-making processes
regarding an emerging educational phenomenon (Merriam, 2009). Workshops have been used in
comparable studies to generate teachers’ perceptions of GenAI in school subject teaching (Busuttil,
2025a; Busuttil & Calleja, 2025).

A purposive sampling strategy was employed to recruit participants with experience in teaching the
business education subjects at the secondary school level. Teachers were invited via a post shared in
closed social media groups for business education teachers. Interested participants completed an online
form providing demographic details, teaching subjects, and years of experience. Informed consent was
obtained electronically prior to participation. The cohort comprised both practising teachers and pre-
service teachers enrolled in initial teacher education, the latter being referred to as student-teachers
where this distinction is relevant to the findings. Including both groups was considered appropriate
because pre-service teachers are entering a profession in which GenAI is already present, and their
appraisals of these tools are formed alongside, rather than after, the development of their pedagogical
repertoire.

Data were generated through a structured, in-person workshop held in February 2024, designed to
provide participants with both exposure to and hands-on experience with GenAI tools such as ChatGPT.
The workshop functioned as a shared experiential context, ensuring that participants’ reflections were
informed by direct engagement with the technology rather than purely hypothetical perceptions.

The workshop sessions adhered to a structured three-phase format, with the authors acting as
facilitators throughout. Phase 1 was an introductory input session. It covered, in non-technical terms,
what LLMs are and how they generate text; the tools then available (ChatGPT, Gemini, Bing Chat);
worked examples of ChatGPT attempting national secondary examination questions, used to illustrate
hallucination and arithmetic error; applications to the business education subjects, including generating
real-world scenarios, brainstorming activities and questions from syllabus learning outcomes, adapting
and differentiating resources, and administrative tasks such as report writing; guidance on constructing
effective prompts; a demonstration of text-to-image generation; and cautions on the exercise of
professional judgement and on student data privacy.

In Phase 2, participants worked hands-on with ChatGPT. They prompted the tool to attempt past
examination questions in their own subject and to generate and adapt teaching resources for classes
they were currently teaching. The facilitators circulated, assisting with prompting and discussing the
outputs obtained.

Phase 3 consisted of group discussions (hereafter G1, G2, G3, G4 and G5). All groups worked from a
common written brief, displayed throughout, which asked them to identify the benefits, challenges, and
pedagogical implications of LLMs for business education as a whole (teaching methods, student
engagement, curriculum development) and for their specific subject, supporting their points with
examples where possible. Each group documented its observations, insights, and suggestions on a
shared Padlet, which served as the primary record of the group's work; the facilitators moved among
the groups to prompt and clarify rather than lead. Each group then presented its main points in plenary,
which opened a whole-cohort discussion. One author took field notes while circulating during the group
work and during the plenary presentations; because the Padlet captured each group’s contributions in
the participants’ own words, these notes supplement rather than substitute for that record. The
sessions were not audio-recorded.

Upon completing the workshop sessions, participants were asked to complete an exit questionnaire
administered anonymously via Google Forms, with completion constituting consent. The instrument
comprised thirteen items arranged in four sections. A single closed-ended item asked whether the
session had provided insights into incorporating GenAI into the respondent’s teaching, with a branch to
a section on strategies for those who answered affirmatively. That section asked participants to describe
the insights or strategies they found most valuable, how they envisaged these enhancing student
learning and engagement in their own classroom, and to supply specific examples or scenarios. A section
on benefits and challenges asked what participants perceived as the main benefits of GenAI in the
business education classroom, prompting consideration of personalised learning, access to information,
and student-teacher interaction; what challenges they anticipated in implementation; and how they
might address them. It also contained the questionnaire’s only scaled item, a five-point rating of how
likely they were to incorporate GenAI into their teaching in the near future, followed by an open request
for the reasons behind that rating. A section on barriers and facilitators asked which factors would
facilitate adoption, prompting consideration of professional development, access to resources, and
administrative support; whether any barriers might hinder adoption; and what additional resources or
support participants would wish to see. A final open item invited any further comments. All items other
than the two noted were open-ended.

Because the questionnaire was anonymous, responses could not be linked to individuals or to the group
in which a participant had worked. Each returned questionnaire was therefore treated as a distinct
respondent and assigned a pseudonym, and all personal names reported in the findings are
pseudonyms.

The qualitative data from the Padlet activity, exit questionnaires, and session notes were analysed using
reflective thematic analysis, following the six-phase framework outlined by Braun and Clarke (2006). The
analysis proceeded through the following stages:

• Familiarisation with the data
• Generation of initial codes
• Searching for themes
• Reviewing themes
• Defining and naming themes
• Producing the report

The analysis was primarily inductive, allowing themes to emerge from the data, while being sensitised
by the TPACK framework. Themes were developed across all data sources and aligned with the research
questions.

To enhance the study’s trustworthiness, findings were compared across the two independent
participant-generated sources, namely the Padlet outputs produced collectively by each group and the
individually completed exit questionnaires, with the field notes providing contextual corroboration
rather than a third independent record. An audit trail was maintained during coding and theme
development to ensure transparency in analytic decisions. Additionally, thick description is provided
through participant quotes to support interpretive validity.

Descriptive analysis of closed-ended questionnaire items was conducted using Python to categorise
participants’ prior use of GenAI tools (McKinney, 2010). These results were used to contextualise the
qualitative findings rather than as a primary analytic component.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Faculty of Education Research Ethics Committee at
the University of Malta (approval number EDUC-2024-00042). Participants were informed of their rights,
including the right to participate voluntarily, remain anonymous, and withdraw without penalty.

Findings

The analysis generated four key themes relating to teachers’ perceptions of GenAI in school business
education:

1. Pedagogical enhancement and efficiency,
2. Risks to academic integrity, independent thinking, and equitable access,
3. Subject-specific affordances and limitations, and
4. The need for professional development and systemic support.

Teachers consistently perceived GenAI tools as enhancing pedagogical practices while improving

Participants emphasised the immediacy of access to information, with one noting that AI provides

feedback, Teacher Fiona explicitly highlighted that AI “saves time for brainstorming ideas.” Participants
from Group 1 (G1) and Group 2 (G2) further underscored the efficiency of these tools, describing them
as “efficient in certain aspects … They are, for example, very fast” (G1). Teachers discussed how AI tools
can offer “examples from worldwide economies, rather than limiting the scope to local contexts,” which
enriches the learning experience for students (G3). It assisted in the development of case studies (G2),

discussions. Through ChatGPT, case studies and examples are provided that can help both students and
lecturers when studying different topics, especially those that are challenging to teach” (Romina, post-

Teachers experienced AI as a “loyal assistant” in developing their pedagogy (Raymond). Carmen

that resulted in enhanced preparation and teaching strategies.” The group participants mentioned that
incorporating real-life examples into business education lessons became easier by asking ChatGPT.

Theme 2: Risks to Academic Integrity, Independent Thinking, and Equitable Access

A major theme emerging from the data concerns the integrity of assessment and the quality of thinking
that assessment is intended to evidence. In the participants’ accounts, the two were united by a single
premise: that AI-generated text cannot be taken at face value. In the post-session feedback, teacher
Tiziana noted that ChatGPT may “lack subject matter expertise and an understanding of the local
context,” while participants in G5 highlighted the risk that students may use the tool uncritically,
without verifying the accuracy of the information it provides. Teachers argued that where output of
uncertain reliability meets students who are still developing the capacity to evaluate it, the outcome is
not simply an inaccurate answer but a superficial form of engagement in which the work of judgement is
never undertaken.

This premise carried directly into concerns about assessment practices. Teachers worried that students
might misuse the tool by copying and pasting generated text into assignments, including dissertations.
Cindy contended that “this necessitates a change in how teachers and lecturers conduct assessments.”
The difficulty was seen as particularly acute for school-based assessment (SBA), where, as Thomas
noted, “the mark may become inflated,” since coursework completed outside the teacher’s supervision
offers the least opportunity to observe the reasoning behind a submission.

A recurring concern among participants was the risk of over-reliance on AI tools, which teachers
believed could weaken students’ critical thinking and problem-solving abilities. Participants warned that
excessive dependence may lead to “non-thinkers” (Liberata), highlighting fears of reduced cognitive
engagement. This concern was echoed across groups. Elaborating on this point, Liberata argued that the
education system “may start producing non-thinkers as students may rely solely on the platform and
tend to become lazy.”

communication, and empathy may be hindered, particularly when younger students use ChatGPT to
take shortcuts rather than engage in analysis and reflection. To address these challenges, teachers in G1

Theme 3: Subject-Specific Affordances and Limitations

Teachers identified important subject-specific differences in how GenAI tools support or hinder learning,
particularly across accounting, economics, and business-related subjects.

In accounting education, ChatGPT was perceived as particularly effective in supporting students’
understanding of core concepts. Participants noted that it can provide “correct answers to the
theoretical questions that do not require the application of accounting ideas” (G3), highlighting its
strength in addressing foundational knowledge. Teachers in G3 and G5 were especially aware of this
capability. They also emphasised ChatGPT’s potential to enhance comprehension by offering alternative
explanations, with one noting that it may “provide students with methods that they understand better
than those explained in class – an assistant that explains better” (G5). This view was further supported
by participants in G3, who similarly recognised the value of ChatGPT in facilitating a clearer
understanding of accounting concepts.

Weaknesses associated with the use of ChatGPT in accounting education were discussed. Participants in
G3 and G5, along with post-session feedback, noted that ChatGPT tends to be weak in the mathematical
aspects of accounting. It also tends to struggle with formatting and the practical application of
accounting standards and concepts. Teacher Lucy mentioned that “assisting in the practical part of
accounting can be time-consuming when using ChatGPT, as presenting a problem may take considerable
time and effort. There seems to be a lack of adaptability for accounting.” Participants also noted the risk
that students could learn incorrect account formats from ChatGPT.

The data suggests that ChatGPT offers particular strengths in teaching and learning across business
studies, economics, marketing, retail, and financial literacy. One notable advantage is its ability to
provide examples from worldwide economies, not just local ones (G3), thereby enriching students’
understanding and broadening their knowledge base. Participants further highlighted its usefulness in
supporting case study analysis and responding to open-ended questions, with this benefit particularly
emphasised by those in G2.

as a significant constraint. Participants elaborated on these concerns with specific examples. Liberata,

Theme 4: The Need for Professional Development and Systemic Support

A cross-cutting theme across the dataset was the need for ongoing professional development and
institutional support to enable the effective integration of GenAI in teaching. Participants indicated that
the motivation to adopt AI tools is driven by the need to keep pace with modern educational demands,
enhance teaching practices, and provide improved learning experiences. This sense of urgency was
strongly articulated by teacher Ramon, who stated: “Modern times. I need to adapt. Adapt or perish.
This is today’s reality, and I must try to exploit all the opportunities it offers.” Another teacher, Kyle,
reported that he has been using AI because “this approach is the way forward … It is a useful and
interesting tool that can enhance the experience of both the teacher and the learner.” Along these lines,
some teachers suggested designing tasks that integrate AI use, emphasise ethical discussions, verify the
accuracy of AI outputs, and use local data and multiple assessment modes to address challenges.

Teachers highlighted the need for professional development to strengthen both their own
understanding of AI tools and their capacity to support students’ critical use of them. This suggests that
the effective integration of GenAI depends not only on technological familiarity but also on pedagogical
adaptation, enabling teachers to better embed AI within teaching and learning processes and to “assist
students in using AI tools critically” (teacher Josette), while also helping them “keep abreast with what
the students are using.” In addition, other teachers highlighted the need for more time to experiment
with AI tools, as well as concerns about maintaining active student engagement and fostering reflection.

Discussion

This study explored school teachers’ perceptions of the use of generative AI in the business education
subjects. The findings reveal a nuanced perspective in which teachers recognise the pedagogical
potential of GenAI while simultaneously expressing concerns about its implications for learning,
assessment, and professional practice.

tools such as ChatGPT can enhance teaching efficiency and effectiveness (Swan & Hofer, 2011).

The findings highlight significant concerns related to academic integrity and the potential erosion of
critical thinking skills. Teachers expressed concern that students may rely on GenAI tools to generate
responses without engaging in deeper learning. These concerns reflect broader debates in the literature
regarding the ethical and pedagogical implications of GenAI in education (e.g., Memarian & Doleck,
2023; Vargas-Murillo et al., 2023). From a TPACK perspective, this suggests a potential imbalance in
which the integration of technological knowledge may outpace the development of pedagogical
strategies that enhance deep learning in business education.

students’ research skills to reduce their over-reliance on AI tools, emphasising ethical use, and verifying
the accuracy of AI-generated content to ensure that students use these tools responsibly. The need for

those grounded in theory, it remains a general-purpose tool rather than an accounting-specific one.
Therefore, its role in accounting education should be carefully managed to ensure it supplements, rather
than replaces, human instruction.

Conclusion

This study aims to bridge the existing gap in the literature, which predominantly emphasises higher
education, by exploring the impacts and implications of GenAI tools at the secondary school level. This
concluding section discusses the contribution to knowledge, considers the study's limitations, and
identifies areas for further research.

Contribution to Knowledge

This study makes four key contributions to the emerging literature on generative AI in education. First, it
addresses a significant gap by focusing on secondary school business education, a context that remains
underrepresented in current research, which is predominantly centred on higher education (e.g.,
Geerling et al., 2023; Mihai et al., 2024; Wood et al., 2023). By examining teachers’ perspectives in this
setting, the study provides empirical insight into how GenAI is perceived in relation to younger learners,
where foundational knowledge development and pedagogical scaffolding are critical.

Second, the study contributes conceptually by demonstrating that the pedagogical value of GenAI is not
uniform across subjects but varies according to the epistemological and procedural demands of different
disciplines. In particular, while GenAI is perceived as effective at supporting conceptual understanding
and idea generation, its limitations are more evident in domains that require procedural accuracy and

Limitations

While this study offers insights into integrating GenAI tools such as ChatGPT into school business
education, some limitations warrant acknowledgement. Firstly, the sample size of 18 teachers is
relatively small, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. A more extensive and diverse
sample can provide a broader perspective and enhance the reliability of the results. Additionally, this
study relies on self-reported teacher data, a common limitation in qualitative research that introduces
potential biases, including social desirability, recall inaccuracies, and subjective interpretations. While
these factors may affect the robustness of the findings, qualitative approaches are uniquely suited to
capturing the subtle, context-specific insights critical to underexplored areas, such as the integration of
generative AI into secondary business education. Future research could complement this study with
quantitative methods to validate and extend the findings.

A further limitation follows from the workshop format itself. Because participants’ reflections were
generated after a facilitated input session, the framing of that session likely sensitised them to particular
issues. The introductory phase demonstrated hallucinations and arithmetic errors using national
examination questions, explicitly raised professional judgement and student data privacy, and these
emphases recur in participants’ subsequent accounts of risk and of subject-specific weakness. The
design therefore secured the benefit of informed rather than hypothetical perceptions at the cost of a
degree of independence between the stimulus and the responses. Relatedly, the workshop took place in
February 2024 and reflects the capabilities of the tools available at that time; several of those tools have
since changed.

The study was conducted in a specific educational context, secondary school business education, which
may not reflect the experiences of teachers and students in other subjects or educational levels.
Comparative studies across different disciplines and educational stages can determine whether the
findings apply more broadly.

Areas of Further Research

Given this study’s findings and contributions, several areas for further research can be proposed to
deepen understanding and improve the integration of GenAI tools into education, particularly in K-12
business education. One promising direction is to investigate the long-term effects of GenAI on
students’ learning outcomes, including academic performance, critical thinking, problem-solving, ethical
use, practical skills, engagement, and motivation. Longitudinal studies can track students over several
years to assess how sustained use of GenAI influences their educational development, achievement, and
readiness for higher education or the workforce. Such research can also provide insights into how GenAI
can enhance students’ interest in business education across diverse learning environments, while
informing targeted strategies to maximise its potential in schools and address the unique challenges of
this educational level.

Research can explore which features of GenAI tools are most effective in maintaining student interest
and how these tools can be tailored to meet the needs of different learner profiles. Another vital area of
exploration is the development of models for teacher education and professional development that
integrate GenAI tools. Future studies can examine how GenAI’s impacts differ across educational levels
and contexts, including investigating the specific pedagogical, technological, and content-related
challenges faced in K-12 business education compared to tertiary-level business education. These
studies can also explore the adaptations required to make GenAI tools more effective for younger
learners.

Additionally, more extensive studies on the ethical implications of using GenAI in education are needed.
This includes examining data privacy concerns, potential bias in AI-generated content, and strategies for
ensuring equitable access to AI tools. Developing guidelines and policies that protect student data and
promote ethical use of AI can contribute to the field. Comparative studies that explore the use of GenAI
tools across different subjects can reveal insights into how the tools’ effectiveness varies by discipline.
Such studies might identify best practices in one subject area that can be adapted to business education
or highlight unique challenges that require subject-specific solutions. Another crucial area for research is
examining how GenAI tools influence student autonomy and their ability to regulate their learning.
Studies can investigate whether these tools foster independent learning skills or if they create a
dependency that hinders the development of self-regulation.

This study highlights the potential of GenAI tools in enhancing school business education. By providing
quick access to information, aiding in lesson planning, and offering diverse perspectives, GenAI tools can
enrich the educational experience. Despite the challenges, the benefits underscore the importance of
integrating these tools thoughtfully and ethically. This research emphasises the need for professional
