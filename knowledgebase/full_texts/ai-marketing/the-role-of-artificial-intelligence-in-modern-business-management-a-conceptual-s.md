Abstract

Artificial intelligence (AI) has moved from the periphery of experimental computing to the centre of
managerial practice, reshaping how organisations make decisions, allocate resources, engage customers
and manage people. Despite the rapid growth of schola rship on the subject, the literature remains
fragmented across functional silos, and the opportunities and risks of AI are frequently examined in
isolation from one another. This conceptual study synthesises existing literature to examine the role of
AI in modern business management and to articulate an integrated understanding of its opportunities
and challenges. Drawing on the technology–organisation–environment framework, the resource-based
view, dynamic capabilities theory and the automation–augmentation perspective, the paper develops a
conceptual model comprising five interrelated components: AI adoption enablers, AI applications
across management functions, the dual effects of AI (opportunities and challenges), organisational
outcomes, and moderating governance mechanisms. The model proposes that AI generates sustainable
value only when technological capability is matched by organisational readiness, responsible
governance, effective human–AI collaboration and continuous workforce development. The discussion
elaborates the implications of the model for strategic decision -making, operations, marketing, human
resource management and finance, and considers the ethical, social and implementation challenges that
accompany adoption. The paper contributes a th eoretically grounded and integrative framework that
can guide future empirical research and support managers who seek to adopt AI responsibly and
effectively.
Keywords: Artificial Intelligence; Business Management; Decision -Making; Human –AI
Collaboration; AI Governance; Conceptual Model; Digital Transformation

Introduction
Few technologies have attracted as much managerial attention in recent years as artificial intelligence. Once confined to research laboratories
and a narrow set of industrial applications, AI is now embedded in credit scoring, demand forecasting, recruitme nt, customer service,
logistics planning, fraud detection and product design. Scholars have described the current moment as a new phase of technological change
in which machines increasingly perform cognitive tasks that were previously regarded as the excl usive preserve of human judgement
(Brynjolfsson & McAfee, 2014; Makridakis, 2017). Industry evidence points in the same direction: global consultancies have estimated that
AI could add substantial economic value across sectors, while surveys of executives indicate that a growing proportion of org anisations
regard AI as strategically important to their future competitiveness (Bughin et al., 2017; Ransbotham et al., 2017).
Artificial intelligence is commonly understood as the capability of a system to interpret external data correctly, to learn f rom that data and
to use what it has learned to accomplish specific goals through flexible adaptation (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2019). Th e field has a long
intellectual history, marked by periods of enthusiasm and disappointment (Haenlein & Kaplan, 2019), but its recent resurgence is attributable
to the convergence of three developments: the availability of very large data sets, substantial  increases in computing power, and advances
in machine learning techniques, particularly deep learning (Jordan & Mitchell, 2015; Russell & Norvig, 2021). For business ma nagers, the
significance of these developments lies less in the technical detail than i n the economic logic they create. Agrawal et al. (2018) argue that
AI can be understood as a dramatic fall in the cost of prediction, and that when prediction becomes cheap, organisations will  use it more
widely and will rethink the decisions, processes and business models that depend on it.
The implications for management are far -reaching. Management has traditionally been described as a set of activities concerned with
planning, organising, directing and controlling organisational resources, and many of these activities rely on the collectio n, interpretation
and communication of information. AI systems can automate routine administrative work, generate predictions that inform plann ing,
monitor performance continuously and personalise interactions with customers and employees at a scale that human managers cannot match
(Kolbjørnsrud et al., 2016; Davenport & Ronanki, 2018). At the same time, AI raises questions that go to the heart of manageri al
responsibility. If an algorithm recommends whom to hire, whom to lend to or how to price a product, who is accountable for th e outcome?
How should organisations respond when systems reproduce historical bias, when their reasoning is opaque, or when their deployment
threatens established patterns of employment? These questions indicate that AI is not merely a technical tool to be installed ; it is an
organisational phenomenon that changes decision rights, skills requirements, power relationship s and ethical obligations (Berente et al.,
2021; Kellogg et al., 2020).
Academic research has responded vigorously. Studies have examined AI in marketing (Davenport et al., 2020), service delivery (Huang &
Rust, 2018), human resource management (Tambe et al., 2019), supply chain management (Toorajipour et al., 2021) and organi sational
decision-making (Jarrahi, 2018; Shrestha et al., 2019). Parallel streams of research have investigated the business value of AI capabi lities
(Mikalef & Gupta, 2021; Wamba -Taguimdje et al., 2020), the ethics of algorithmic systems (Mittelstadt et al., 2016; Floridi  et al., 2018)
and the human consequences of automation (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2019; Frey & Osborne, 2017). However, these streams have developed
largely in parallel. Studies of opportunity tend to be written from a performance perspective, while studies of  risk are written from ethical,
legal or sociological perspectives, and relatively few attempts have been made to integrate the two within a single conceptua l structure that
managers and researchers can use.
This paper addresses that need. Its purpose is to examine the role of AI in modern business management through a conceptual l ens and to
develop an integrative model that explains how organisational enablers, functional applications, opportunities, challeng es and governance
mechanisms interact to shape organisational outcomes. A conceptual approach is appropriate for three reasons. First, the phen omenon is
evolving faster than empirical research can codify, so theoretical synthesis can provide direction wher e large-scale evidence is still limited.
Second, the existing evidence is dispersed across disciplines, and integration requires systematic conceptual work. Third, a well-specified
conceptual model generates propositions that can be tested empirically in s ubsequent research (Dwivedi et al., 2021).
Section 2 reviews the literature, Section 3 identifies the research gap and objectives, Section 4 presents the conceptual mod el, Section 5
justifies its components, Section 6 discusses the opportunities, challenges and implications, and Section 7 concludes  with contributions,
limitations and future research directions.

The literature on AI in business management is extensive and interdisciplinary. This section organises the existing scholarsh ip into four
themes: the conceptual foundations of AI in organisations, the application of AI across management functions, the oppo rtunities attributed
to AI, and the challenges and risks associated with its adoption. The section concludes with a comparative table that summari ses selected
influential studies.

Conceptual Foundations of AI in Organisations
A first stream of research seeks to clarify what AI is and how it differs from earlier generations of information technology.  Traditional
enterprise systems execute rules that have been explicitly programmed by humans. By contrast, machine learning systems  infer patterns
from data and improve their performance through experience, which allows them to handle tasks that are difficult to specify i n advance
(Jordan & Mitchell, 2015). This distinction has important managerial consequences. Berente et al. (2021) argue that AI differs from
conventional technologies because of its autonomy, its capacity to learn and its inscrutability, and that these characteristics make it a distinct
object of management. Autonomy raises the question of how much discretion should be delegated to machines; learning means that systems
change after deployment, complicating oversight; and inscrutability makes it difficult for managers to understand why a system has produced
a particular output.
Researchers have also proposed typologies that help managers to locate AI within their organisations. Davenport and Ronanki  (2018)
distinguish three broad types of business application: process automation, cognitive insight and cognitive engagement. Proces s automation
substitutes for structured back-office activities, cognitive insight uses algorithms to detect patterns in lar ge volumes of data, and cognitive
engagement employs natural language and conversational interfaces to interact with customers and employees. Huang and Rust (2018) offer
a complementary typology based on the kinds of intelligence that AI can display, namel y mechanical, analytical, intuitive and empathetic
intelligence, and argue that the boundary of tasks that AI can perform is moving progressively from the mechanical towards the empathetic.
Taken together, these typologies show that AI is not a single tech nology but a family of capabilities with different organisational
implications.
AI Across Management Functions
A second stream of research documents applications of AI within specific management functions. In strategic decision -making, AI can
process far larger and more varied data sets than human analysts and can identify patterns that are not visible to unaided cognition (Duan et
al., 2019). Jarrahi (2018) argues that AI is particularly well suited to decision contexts characterised by high complexity, whereas human
judgement remains superior in contexts characterised by uncertainty and equivocality, in which goals are ambiguous and interpretations are
contested. This leads to the proposition that the most effective organisational decisions will emerge from a symbiosis between human and
artificial intelligence rather than from either alone. Shrestha et al. (2019) similarly show that the appropriate decision -making structure,
ranging from full delegation to algorithms through to hybr id and aggregated human –AI arrangements, depends on the specificity of the
decision domain, the size of the alternative set and the speed of decision-making required.
In operations and supply chain management, AI supports demand forecasting, inventory optimisation, route planning, predictive
maintenance and supplier risk assessment. Early work by Min (2010) catalogued the potential of AI techniques in supply chain management,
and a recent systematic review by Toorajipour  et al. (2021) confirms that applications have expanded considerably, with the literature
clustering around demand and sales forecasting, supplier selection, logistics and inventory control. In marketing and customer management,
AI enables segmentation, personalisation, dynamic pricing and conversational service. Davenport et al. (2020) propose a multidimensional
framework in which AI shapes marketing strategy, customer interaction and the nature of the marketing task itself, while Huan g and Rust
(2018) examine how AI is reconfiguring service delivery and the division of labour between employees and machines.
In human resource management, AI is used in recruitment screening, talent analytics, workforce planning and employee engagement. Tambe
et al. (2019) provide a balanced assessment, noting that the complexity of HR outcomes, the limited size of many HR data sets, the ethical
and legal constraints on the use of personal data, and employee reactions to algorithmic decisions all create challenges that  are less
pronounced in domains such as consumer analytics. In finance, risk management and compliance, machine l earning has long been used for
credit scoring, algorithmic trading and the detection of fraudulent transactions (Ngai et al., 2011). More recent work extend s these
applications to anti-money-laundering surveillance, regulatory reporting and the automation of audit procedures. Across these functions, the
literature consistently suggests that AI is most effective when it is integrated with existing processes and expertise rather than introduced as
an isolated tool (Fountaine et al., 2019).
Opportunities Associated with AI
The opportunities attributed to AI in the literature can be grouped into efficiency, decision quality, innovation and competi tive advantage.
Efficiency gains arise when AI automates repetitive tasks, reduces error rates and accelerates processes. Kolbjørnsrud et al. (2016) anticipate
that AI will take over a large share of the administrative coordination and reporting work that currently occupies managers, thereby freeing
them to concentrate on judgement -intensive activities such as creative thinking, experimentation and the development of people.
Improvements in decision quality follow from the ability of AI to generate more accurate predictions and to consider a wider range of
information. Agrawal et al. (2018) emphasise that better prediction does not remove the need for judgement but increases its value, because
human decision-makers must determine which outcomes matter and how to trade them off.

Innovation represents a further opportunity. Verganti et al. (2020) argue that AI is changing the way organisations approach innovation and
design, because it enables data -driven problem-solving and expands the range of solutions that can be explored. Empi rical studies of AI
projects suggest that AI -based transformation can enhance business process performance, support new product and service development
and strengthen firm -level outcomes (Wamba -Taguimdje et al., 2020). Mikalef  and Gupta (2021) show, on the basis of survey data, that
organisations with a strong AI capability, composed of tangible resources such as data and technology, human resources such a s technical
and business skills, and intangible resources such as an appr opriate culture and the willingness to take risks, report higher organisational
creativity and performance. These findings support the view that AI creates value when it is treated as a bundle of complemen tary
organisational resources, rather than as a stand-alone technology.
Evidence concerning the human dimension of AI is encouraging but conditional. Wilson and Daugherty (2018) report, from a stud y of a
large sample of companies, that the greatest performance improvements occurred when humans and machines worked together, wit h each
complementing the other’s strengths. This collaborative-intelligence perspective suggests that the primary opportunity for managers lies in
redesigning work so that human skills such as leadership, empathy and creativity are combined with machine strengths such as speed, scale
and consistency.
Challenges and Risks of AI Adoption
A parallel body of research stresses that AI also generates significant challenges. Ethical concerns are prominent. Mittelsta dt et al. (2016)
map the ethical debate surrounding algorithms and identify epistemic concerns, such as inconclusive and inscrutabl e evidence, and
normative concerns, such as unfair outcomes and threats to autonomy and privacy. The risk of algorithmic bias has been demons trated
empirically. Obermeyer et al. (2019) found that a widely used healthcare algorithm systematically underestimated the health needs of Black
patients because it used past healthcare cost as a proxy for need, illustrating how apparently neutral design choices can emb ed structural
inequalities. A review of 84 AI ethics guidelines by Jobin et al. (2019) found convergence around the principles of transparency, justice and
fairness, non-maleficence, responsibility and privacy, although the interpretation and implementation of these principles varied substantially.
Opacity and trust form a second cluster of challenges. Burrell (2016) distinguishes three forms of opacity in machine learnin g systems:
deliberate secrecy by organisations, technical illiteracy among those affected, and the inherent mismatch between high -dimensional
mathematical optimisation and human styles of interpretation. Research on explainable AI seeks to address these problems (Ada di &
Berrada, 2018; Arrieta et al., 2020), but explanation techniques remain imperfect. Trust is equally critical. Glikson and Woolley (2020)
review empirical research on human trust in AI and show that trust is shaped by the tangibility and transparency of the system, its reliability
and the behaviour it displays. Dietvorst et al. (2015) document algorithm aversion, whereby people lose confidence in algorithmic forecasts
more quickly than in human forecasts after observing an error, even when the algorithm is superior on average. Managers must therefore
address both excessive scepticism and excessive reliance.
Workforce and labour market consequences constitute a third cluster. Frey and Osborne (2017) estimated that a large share of occupations
in the United States was susceptible to computerisation, a finding that has stimulated both alarm and critique. Acemogl u and Restrepo
(2019) offer a more nuanced framework in which technology both displaces labour from existing tasks and reinstates labour thr ough the
creation of new tasks, so that the net effect depends on the balance between these forces. Within organisat ions, Kellogg et al. (2020) show
that algorithms can become instruments of managerial control, recommending, restricting, recording, rating, replacing and rew arding
workers, thereby altering the balance of power in employment relationships. Raisch and Krakowski (2021) frame the underlying tension as
the automation –augmentation paradox: automation and augmentation are interdependent, and attempts to pursue one in isolation may
undermine the other over time.
Finally, privacy, data governance and implementation challenges deserve attention. AI systems depend on large volumes of data , much of
it personal, which raises concerns about surveillance, consent and security (Zuboff, 2019) and requires compliance with regulatory regimes
such as the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (Voigt & Von dem Bussche , 2017). Practical obstacles include data
quality, integration with legacy systems, scarcity of skilled personnel and the cost of development and maintenance. Fountaine et al. (2019)
observe that organisations often fail to realise the benefits of AI not b ecause the technology is inadequate but because of cultural and
organisational barriers, including siloed structures, risk aversion and a lack of leadership commitment. Brock and von Wangen heim (2019)
similarly caution that unrealistic expectations and insufficient attention to data foundations are common causes of failed AI initiatives.

Research Gap and Objectives
Research Gap
The preceding review reveals a substantial and growing body of knowledge, but also several gaps that justify the present stud y. The first
gap concerns fragmentation. Research on AI in management is typically organised by function (for example marketing, human resources or
supply chain) or by discipline (for example information systems, ethics or labour economics). As a result, there is limited u nderstanding of
how developments in one area relate to those in another and how the organisational conditions that enable AI in one function may constrain
it in another. Managers, however, confront AI as an enterprise -wide phenomenon and must make decisions about investment, governance
and skills that cut across functions (Benbya et al., 2020).
The second gap concerns the separation of opportunities and challenges. Performance-oriented studies tend to emphasise the benefits of AI,
whereas critical studies concentrate on its risks. Few frameworks treat opportunities and challenges as two simultaneous and interdependent
effects of the same adoption process, even though the evidence suggests that they are closely connected. For example, the sam e data -
intensive capability that enables personalisation also creates privacy risks, and the same automation that ra ises productivity may displace
workers (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2019; Zuboff, 2019). A model that treats these effects as separate risks understating the trade -offs that
managers actually face.
The third gap concerns the role of moderating mechanisms. Much of the literature acknowledges that governance, ethical framew orks,
human oversight and workforce development matter, but these factors are often treated as background conditions or normative
recommendations rather than as explicit components of an explanatory model. Consequently, little theoretical guidance exists o n how such
mechanisms influence the relationship between AI adoption and organisational outcomes (Berente et al., 2021; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021).

The fourth gap is methodological: much of the evidence is based on case studies, practitioner reports or cross -sectional surveys, and there
are few integrative conceptual frameworks from which testable propositions can be derived (Dwivedi et al., 2021). Fi nally, much of the
existing research has been conducted in advanced economies, and the conditions facing organisations in emerging markets, whic h may
include different infrastructural constraints, regulatory environments and labour market structures, remai n comparatively under-theorised.
Research Objectives
In light of these gaps, the study pursues the following objectives:
1. To examine the conceptual foundations of AI and its role in the major functions of modern business management.
2. To identify and synthesise the principal opportunities that AI creates for organisations.
3. To identify and synthesise the principal challenges and risks associated with AI adoption in business contexts.
4. To develop an integrative conceptual model that explains how enablers, functional applications, opportunities, challenges and
moderating mechanisms jointly influence organisational outcomes.
5. To justify the model theoretically and to derive implications for managers, policymakers and future research.
Conceptual Model
Figure 1 presents the conceptual model developed in this study. The model is organised as a left -to-right causal flow supported by an
overarching layer of governance. It consists of five components. Component A, AI adoption enablers, comprises the technolo gical,
organisational and environmental conditions that determine whether and how an organisation can adopt AI. Component B, AI appl ication
in management functions, represents the concrete domains in which AI is deployed: strategy and decision -making, operations and supply
chain, marketing and customer engagement, human resource management, and finance, risk and compliance. Component C, the dual effects
of AI, distinguishes the opportunities and challenges that result from such deployment. Component D, organisatio nal outcomes, captures
the ultimate consequences in terms of sustainable competitive advantage, organisational performance and long -term stakeholder trust.
Component E, moderating and governing mechanisms, comprises responsible AI governance, human –AI coll aboration and change
management, which condition the relationship between AI application and its effects.

Justification of the Conceptual Model
Each component of the model is grounded in established theory and supported by the literature reviewed above. This section ju stifies the
components in turn and highlights the subthemes that constitute each.
Component A: AI Adoption Enablers
The inclusion of enablers is grounded in the technology–organisation–environment (TOE) framework (Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990), which
holds that the adoption of technological innovations is influenced by the characteristics of the technology, the internal fea tures of the
organisation and the external environment. Three subthemes follow directly from this framew ork.
The first subtheme is technological readiness, which includes data availability and quality, computing infrastructure, intero perability with
legacy systems and access to appropriate algorithms and tools. AI systems are only as good as the data on which they are trained, and Brock
and von Wangenheim (2019) emphasise that weak data foundations are a principal cause of unsuccessful AI projects. The second subtheme
is organisational readiness, which encompasses leadership commitment, technical and managerial sk ills, culture, financial resources and
structural flexibility. The resource-based view (Barney, 1991) supports this subtheme by suggesting that sustainable advantage arises from
resources that are valuable, rare, difficult to imitate and organised for expl oitation. Mikalef and Gupta (2021) operationalise this logic by
showing that AI capability is a composite of tangible, human and intangible resources. Fountaine et al. (2019) reinforce the point by arguing
that culture and organisational capability, rather than technology, are the decisive barriers to scaling AI.
The third subtheme is environmental pressure, which includes regulation, competitive intensity, customer expectations and the  availability
of external expertise and partners. Regulatory regimes such as data protection law shape what organisations may do with data (Voigt & Von
dem Bussche, 2017), while competitive dynamics may compel adoption even where internal readiness is limited. Individual -level adoption
research, notably the technology acceptance model (Davis, 1989) and the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (Venkatesh et
al., 2003), adds that employee perceptions of usefulness, ease of use and social influence affect whether adopted systems are  actually used.
These considerations support proposition P1: the stronger the technological, organisational and environmental enablers, the broader and
more effective the application of AI across management functions.
Component B: AI Application in Management Functions
The second component reflects the observation that AI is deployed not in the abstract but within concrete managerial activiti es. The five
functional domains included in the model were selected because they are the most extensively documented in the literat ure and because
together they span the principal value -creating and control activities of the firm. Strategy and decision -making is included because AI
changes how information is processed and how choices are made (Duan et al., 2019; Jarrahi , 2018; Shrestha et al., 2019). Operations and
supply chain management features because predictive and prescriptive analytics have been shown to improve forecasting, logist ics and
inventory decisions (Min, 2010; Toorajipour  et al., 2021). Marketing and customer engagement is included because AI is transforming
personalisation, pricing and service interaction (Davenport et al., 2020; Huang & Rust, 2018). Human resource management is i ncluded
because AI is increasingly used in  recruitment, performance evaluation and workforce analytics, and because these applications raise
particularly sensitive ethical questions (Tambe et al., 2019). Finally, finance, risk and compliance is included because it is among the earliest
and most mature domains of AI use, notably in fraud detection and credit assessment (Ngai et al., 2011).
Component C: The Dual Effects of AI
The distinctive feature of the model is that opportunities and challenges are presented side by side as simultaneous effects of the same
application. This design responds directly to the second research gap and is supported by the literature in several way s. On the opportunity
side, the model identifies efficiency and cost reduction (Kolbjørnsrud et al., 2016), improved decision quality (Agrawal et a l., 2018),
personalisation and innovation (Verganti et al., 2020) and predictive insight and organisational agility (Wamba-Taguimdje et al., 2020). On
the challenge side, it identifies algorithmic bias and ethical risk (Mittelstadt et al., 2016; Obermeyer et al., 2019), opaci ty and trust deficits
(Burrell, 2016; Glikson & Woolley, 2020), data privacy and security concerns (Zuboff, 2019), workforce displacement (Acemoglu &
Restrepo, 2019; Frey & Osborne, 2017) and skills gaps and implementation costs (Fountaine et al., 2019).
Treating the two sets of effects as coupled rather than independent is justified because they share common causes. Greater us e of personal
data enhances personalisation but heightens privacy exposure. Greater automation of tasks improves efficiency but may  erode skills and
reduce employee autonomy (Kellogg et al., 2020; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021). Greater algorithmic autonomy accelerates decisions  but
weakens human oversight. This coupling supports proposition P2: the application of AI simultaneously increas es both opportunities and
challenges, and the two cannot be separated analytically without distorting the picture available to managers.

Component D: Organisational Outcomes
The fourth component captures the outcomes that matter to organisations and their stakeholders. Sustainable competitive advan tage is
included on the basis of the resource -based view (Barney, 1991) and dynamic capabilities theory (Teece et al., 1997). The l atter holds that
advantage in turbulent environments derives from the ability to sense opportunities, seize them and reconfigure resources acc ordingly. AI
contributes to each of these processes, since it enhances environmental scanning, supports faster dec isions and enables the reconfiguration
of processes, but only if the organisation can integrate it into routines and learning systems. Organisational performance, c omprising
financial and operational measures as well as innovation and creativity, is suppor ted by empirical evidence linking AI capability to firm
outcomes (Mikalef & Gupta, 2021; Wamba-Taguimdje et al., 2020).
The inclusion of long-term value and stakeholder trust distinguishes the model from purely performance-oriented frameworks. Organisations
that deploy AI in ways that employees, customers and regulators perceive as unfair or intrusive risk reputational damage, legal liability and
loss of legitimacy, even where short -term efficiency improves. The inclusion of trust as an outcome therefore reflects both the ethical
literature (Floridi et al., 2018; Jobin et al., 2019) and the empirical evidence on the fragility of trust in algorithmic systems (Dietvorst et al.,
2015). This supports proposition P4: net organisational outcomes depend on the balance between realised opportunities and unm anaged
challenges, rather than on the mere fact of AI adoption.
Component E: Moderating and Governing Mechanisms
The final component is the most important for explaining why organisations with similar technologies obtain divergent results . Three
mechanisms are identified. The first is responsible AI governance, which includes ethical principles, accountability struct ures, risk
assessment, auditing and explainability practices. Floridi  et al. (2018) propose that a good AI society should rest on the principles of
beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, justice and explicability, and Jobin et al. (2019) show that similar principles recur across a wide
range of guidelines. Governance conve rts such principles into organisational routines, and explainability techniques (Arrieta et al., 2020)
provide some of the technical means through which transparency and accountability can be achieved.
The second mechanism is human–AI collaboration. The augmentation perspective (Jarrahi, 2018; Wilson & Daugherty, 2018) holds that AI
performs best as a complement to human capability. Shrestha et al. (2019) show that decision structures should be designed de liberately,
with clear allocation of authority between humans and machines. Raisch and Krakowski (2021) caution that augmentation and automation
are interdependent, so that managers must plan for both. The third mechanism is change management, reskilling and learning. B ecause AI
changes tasks, skills and roles, organisations must invest in training, communication and participation if employees are to accept and use
the technology effectively (Tambe et al., 2019; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Dynamic capabilities theory (Teece et al., 1997) fur ther suggests
that organisations need systematic learning processes to adapt as technologies and contexts evolve.
These mechanisms are placed as moderators because they are expected to influence the strength of the relationships in the model rather than
to serve as stand -alone outcomes. Specifically, proposition P3 states that stronger governance, collaboration and ch ange management
enhance the translation of AI applications into opportunities and reduce the translation of AI applications into harmful chal lenges. The
feedback loop completes the model by recognising that outcomes reshape enablers over time, consistent w ith the view of organisational
capabilities as cumulative and path-dependent (Teece et al., 1997).
Detailed Discussion
AI and Strategic Decision-Making
The most profound managerial implication of AI concerns the quality and structure of decision -making. AI augments human cognition by
processing volumes of data that would be impossible for individuals to analyse, thereby supporting more evidence -based strategic choices
(Duan et al., 2019). Yet the model implies that the contribution of AI is not uniform across decision types. Jarrahi  (2018) argues that AI
excels where complexity arises from the sheer number of variables, whereas uncertainty and equivocality require human intuition and sense-
making. Consequently, managers should avoid the temptation to apply algorithms indiscriminately. Instead, they should assess each decision
domain according to the criteria proposed by Shrestha et al. (2019), namely the specificity of the decision space, the size o f the alternative
set and the need for speed and scalability, and then choose between f ull delegation, sequential human –AI collaboration or aggregation of
human and machine recommendations.
Leyer and Schneider (2021) suggest that managers perceive AI both as a threat and as an opportunity, and that its effect on managerial roles
depends on whether it is used to automate or to augment decisions. The present model supports the augmentation interpretation: the greatest
strategic value arises when AI expands the range of options managers consider and sharpens their predictions, while humans re tain
responsibility for defining objectives, weighing trade -offs and interpreting context (Agrawal et al.,  2018). It follows that the strategic
capability that AI makes scarce is not computation but judgement, and organisations should invest accordingly in the developm ent of
managerial judgement alongside technical capacity.

Operational Efficiency and Supply Chain Management
In operations, AI delivers some of the most measurable benefits. Predictive models improve demand forecasts and reduce invent ory costs,
optimisation algorithms improve routing and scheduling, and condition -monitoring systems anticipate equipment failure be fore it disrupts
production (Toorajipour et al., 2021). Such applications generally fall within the cognitive insight and process automation c ategories
described by Davenport and Ronanki (2018) and typically involve lower ethical sensitivity than applications that evaluate individuals. This
helps to explain why many organisations begin their AI journey in operations. Operational applications nonetheless depend on integrated,
high-quality data across organisational boundaries and can create fragile dependencies if models fail under conditions that differ from th eir
training data, so algorithmic recommendations should be combined with scenario planning and human review.
Marketing, Customer Engagement and Service
AI has transformed the way organisations understand and interact with customers. Recommendation systems, dynamic pricing and
predictive customer analytics allow firms to tailor offers at the level of the individual, while chatbots and virtual assista nts extend service
availability (Davenport et al., 2020). Huang and Rust (2018) argue that as AI acquires analytical and, increasingly, intuitive and empathetic
capabilities, the allocation of tasks between employees and machines in service encounters will shift, with employees concent rating on the
most complex and emotionally demanding interactions. These dev elopments create clear opportunities for personalisation and efficiency,
but they also intensify the tension between personalisation and privacy that the model identifies. Customers may welcome rele vant
recommendations but resist forms of profiling that th ey perceive as intrusive or manipulative (Zuboff, 2019). Organisations must therefore
be transparent about data use, offer meaningful consent and avoid exploitative design practices. Trust, once lost, is difficu lt to rebuild, and
the model positions trust explicitly as an organisational outcome for this reason.
Human Resource Management and the Future of Work
Human resource management illustrates the dual effects of AI more vividly than any other function. AI can reduce administrati ve burden,
identify talent more efficiently and support personalised development. At the same time, algorithmic screening and evaluation can reproduce
historical bias, undermine perceived fairness and diminish employee autonomy (Tambe et al., 2019; Kellogg et al., 2020). The evidence
from other domains that biased proxies can produce discriminatory outcomes at scale (Obermeyer et al.,  2019) is a reminder that human
resource applications require rigorous validation, regular auditing and transparent communication with those affected. Lee (2 018) shows
that people’s perceptions of algorithmic decisions depend on the task: algorithms are ju dged less favourably when they perform tasks that
are perceived as requiring human skills, such as hiring and work evaluation, than when they perform mechanical tasks. This finding implies
that the acceptability of AI in human resource management cannot be assumed and must be actively earned.
At a broader level, AI raises fundamental questions about the future of work. Estimates of the susceptibility of occupations to automation
have been influential (Frey & Osborne, 2017), but the task -based analysis of Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) indicates t hat the net
employment effect depends on whether new tasks are created at a pace sufficient to offset displacement. For organisations, th e practical
implication is to plan workforce transitions deliberately by identifying roles at risk, investing in reskilling and creating new roles that exploit
the complementarity between human and machine capabilities. This is the role of the change management mechanism in the model: without
it, the efficiency gains of AI may be purchased at the cost of employee morale, i nstitutional knowledge and social legitimacy.
Finance, Risk and Compliance
Financial services and corporate finance functions were early adopters of machine learning, using it for credit scoring, frau d detection and
algorithmic trading (Ngai et al., 2011). These applications highlight the importance of the governance component of  the model. Credit and
insurance decisions affect individuals’ life chances, and regulators increasingly require that such decisions be explainable and non -
discriminatory. The opacity of complex models (Burrell, 2016) therefore creates direct compliance ri sks and strengthens the case for
explainable AI techniques ( Adadi & Berrada, 2018; Arrieta et al., 2020). Model risk, the possibility that a model is wrong, misapplied or
manipulated, therefore requires continuous monitoring, validation and documentation across the model life cycle.
Ethical, Social and Governance Challenges
Across all functions, ethical and governance issues emerge as cross -cutting concerns. The convergence of ethics guidelines identified by
Jobin et al. (2019) suggests a growing consensus on principles, but also exposes a gap between principle and practice. Organisations
frequently adopt statements of values without establishing the processes, roles and incentives required to implement them. Ef fective
governance requires clearly assigned accountability for AI systems, impact assessments before deployment, mec hanisms for contesting
algorithmic decisions, and independent auditing. It also requires technical measures, including bias testing, documentation o f data
provenance and explainability tools. Mittelstadt et al. (2016) emphasise that responsibility for algo rithmic outcomes is distributed across
designers, deployers and users, which makes it essential for organisations to specify who is answerable at each stage.

Implementation and Organisational Capability
The discussion of implementation underscores the importance of the enabler component. Many AI initiatives fail to progress be yond the
pilot stage because organisations underestimate the data, process and cultural changes required (Brock & von Wangenheim, 2019; Fountaine
et al., 2019). Benbya  et al. (2020) suggest that organisations should approach AI as a long -term organisational learning agenda, not as a
series of discrete technology projects. Practically, this means building cross-functional teams that combine technical and domain expertise,
investing in data governance, adopting incremental deployment strategies that allow learning and adjustment, and aligning AI initiatives
explicitly with business strategy. Davenport and Ronanki (2018) recommend beginning with applications that have clear business value and
moderate risk and then progressing to more ambitious projects as capabilities mature, advice that is consistent with the dynamic capabilities
logic of the model.
Implications for Practice and Policy
For managers, the model has four main implications. First, AI strategy should be treated as an integral part of business stra tegy and not as
a separate technological programme. Second, investment in enablers, particularly data foundations, skills and leadership, should precede or
accompany investment in algorithms. Third, governance should be built into AI initiatives from the outset, with explicit attention to fairness,
transparency, accountability and privacy. Fourth, organisations should redesign work ar ound human –AI collaboration and invest in
reskilling so that employees participate in, rather than merely experience, the transformation. For policymakers, the model h ighlights the
importance of regulatory frameworks that protect individuals without stifli ng innovation, of education and training systems that prepare
workers for evolving task structures, and of support for small and medium -sized enterprises and for organisations in emerging economies,
which may lack the resources to build AI capabilities independently.
Conclusion
This paper set out to examine the role of artificial intelligence in modern business management and to develop an integrative  conceptual
model of its opportunities and challenges. The review of the literature showed that AI is reshaping management across strategy, operations,
marketing, human resources and finance, offering significant gains in efficiency, decision quality and innovation, while simu ltaneously
raising serious concerns about bias, opacity, privacy, employment and trust. It also showed that re search on these themes has remained
fragmented and that few frameworks integrate benefits and risks within a single explanatory structure.
The conceptual model proposed in this paper responds to these gaps. By linking adoption enablers, functional applications, du al effects,
organisational outcomes and moderating governance mechanisms, the model explains why organisations that deploy similar technologies
may achieve very different results. Its central argument is that the value of AI is not determined by the technology itself b ut by the
organisational and governance context in which it is embedded. Organisations that invest in data and skills, design decision processes around
human–AI collaboration, institutionalise responsible governance and manage workforce transition are more likely to realise the
opportunities of AI while containing its risks. Those that do not may find that efficiency gain s are offset by reputational, legal and human
costs.
The study makes three contributions. Theoretically, it integrates the TOE framework, the resource -based view, dynamic capabilities theory
and the automation –augmentation perspective within a single model and derives four testable propositions. Conceptually , it treats
opportunities and challenges as coupled effects rather than as separate literatures. Practically, it offers managers a struct ured way to think
about AI adoption as an organisation-wide capability-building and governance undertaking.
The study also has limitations. As a conceptual paper, it is based on the synthesis of existing literature and has not been t ested empirically;
the propositions therefore require validation. The literature reviewed is selective, and the rapid pace of techn ological change, including the
diffusion of generative AI systems, means that new evidence will continue to emerge. The model is also general, and its appli cation may
need to be refined for particular industries, firm sizes and national contexts. Future re search could test the propositions using quantitative
and mixed-methods designs, examine the moderating role of specific governance practices, investigate the experience of small firms and
organisations in emerging economies, and explore how generative and  autonomous AI systems alter the balance of opportunities and
challenges identified here. Longitudinal studies would be especially valuable in tracing the feedback processes through which the outcomes
of AI adoption reshape organisational capabilities over time.
In conclusion, AI is neither a panacea nor an inevitable threat. It is a powerful general -purpose capability whose consequences are shaped
by managerial choices. By adopting an integrative and responsible approach, organisations can position AI as a means of enhancing human
judgement, creating durable value and sustaining trust among their stakeholders.
