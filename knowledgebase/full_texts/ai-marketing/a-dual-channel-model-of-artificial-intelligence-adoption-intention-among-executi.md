Abstract: Whether a small or medium-sized firm in a traditional industry takes up artificial intelligence is
usually settled by the chairman or the general manager alone, without an information -systems department
to consult. Existing adoption research draws its evidence mainly from firms that have already completed
basic informatisation, so how external conditions reach the executive remains unclear. This study sur-
veyed 526 SME executives in four traditional industries across four Chinese regions and estima ted a struc-
tural equation model linking technology-side and environment-side conditions, perceived value, perceived
implementation risk and adoption intention, with digital foundation as a boundary condition. External
conditions split into two mediating pathways, the two channels of the model. Performance expectancy and
competitive pressure raise perceived value, while effort expectancy and perceived institutional support
leave it unchanged and instead lower perceived implementation risk. Competitive pressu re carries oppo-
site signs across the two pathways and its total effect on adoption intention is indistinguishable from zero,
as is that of institutional support. Digital foundation weakens the suppression that perceived risk exerts on
intention, the slope falling from −0.284 to −0.125, although the two product terms add only 0.007 to the
explained variance in intention. Peer demonstration and subsidies do not move these firms, and the esti-
mates locate the reason in a risk they cannot absorb rather than in a ny shortfall of perceived value; build-
ing the data and systems base before the application layer fits the way these firms decide. The design is
cross-sectional, so the estimates are associational and the ordering among the constructs rests on theory
rather than on temporal precedence.
Keywords: Artificial Intelligence Adoption, SME Executives, Perceived Value, Perceived Implementation
Risk, Digital Foundation, Upper Echelons Perspective, Structural Equation Modelling

digital technologies, with the gap against large firms widening at the level of artificial intelligence. The bibliometric accoun t of
digital transformation research by Kraus et al. (2021) shows empirical evidence concentrated in firms that have already compl eted

basic informatisation; Wamba-Taguimdje et al. (2020) trace the business value of AI transformation projects to process automation,
decision support and customer interaction, all three of which presuppose existing data and systems. The tests of AI adoption in
manufacturing and production firms by Chatterjee et al. (2021) and the analysis of AI -based B2B practices among SMEs by
Baabdullah et al. (2021) both restrict observation to firms with some informatisation already in place, which excludes by con struc-
tion the traditional firms whose production data have never been digitised. Cubric (2020) assembles drivers, barriers and social
considerations for AI in business and management in a tertiary study, listing skill shortages and weak organisational implementation
capacity among the main barriers; Dwivedi et al. (2021) place SME access to artificial intelligence on the multidisciplinary agenda
as an open question.
The decision structure of traditional -industry SMEs differs in substance from these samples. Such firms have no separate
information-systems department, no formal technology review procedure, and no diversified business lines over which the cost of
experimentation can be spread. Whether a technology is taken up is decided by the chairman, the general manager or a single
functional executive, typically without internal expert advice. The upper echelons perspective of Hambrick and Mason (1984) treats
organisational behaviour as a reflection of the cognition and values of top managers, and that reflection is far stronger in SMEs than
in bureaucratised large firms. Liang et al. (2007) test the effect of institutional pressures on enterprise system assimilati on and
conclude that ex ternal pressure has to pass through the attention allocation of top management before it becomes organisational
action, with the top-management stage carrying the greater part of the mediation. Moving the unit of analysis from “the firm” down
to “the adoption intention of the individual executive ” is not a simplification in the SME setting; it locates the study where the
decision is actually made.
Individual-level frameworks such as the technology acceptance model (Davis, 1989) and the unified theory of acceptance and
use of technology (Venkatesh et al., 2003) attribute adoption intention to performance expectancy and effort expectancy. Organisa-
tional-level frameworks such as the technology –organization–environment (TOE) framework (Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990) and
diffusion of innovations theory (Rogers, 2003) attribute adoption to technological attributes, organisational conditions and environ-
mental pressure. The comparison by Oliveira and Martins (2011) identifies what each family omits. Individual -level frameworks
leave out environmental variables and organisational-level frameworks leave out the cognitive processing stage. The joint between
them lies in how external conditions are translated by the decision maker into subjective assessments. Kim et al. (2007) place benefit
perceptions and sacrifice perceptions inside a single construct of perceived value, which absorbs all antecedents; Featherman  and
Pavlou (2003) decompose perceived risk into performance, financial and privacy components and conclude that risk and benefit act
on adoption intention independently rather than as two ends of one dimension. Read together, the two lines suggest that exte rnal
conditions may not reach adoption intention through a single pathway but split into two parallel assessments of value and of risk
that can work against each other. A further stream explains why firms hold back even when they judge a technology worthwh ile.
Cenfetelli (2004) separates inhibitors from enablers of system usage and shows that the two are not opposite ends of one scal e, so
what suppresses adoption need not be the absence of what promotes it. Polites and Karahanna (2012) trace the hold of inc umbent
habit, switching cost and inertia on the acceptance of a new system, and Kim and Kankanhalli (2009) attribute user resistance  to
status quo bias rather than to a low estimate of benefit. At the organisational level, Piderit (2000) and Ashforth et al . (2014) argue
that attitudes towards change are routinely ambivalent, the positive and the negative component being held at the same time, while
Talke and Heidenreich (2014) and Claudy et al. (2015) show that decision models which omit the resistance side overstate adoption.
This literature is the reason for keeping the risk assessment as a construct of its own rather than treating it as the negati ve tail of
perceived value.
This split takes a testable form in traditional -industry SMEs. Local subsidies for intelligent upgrading, matchmaking events
run by industrial parks and demonstration by competitors are all designed to raise adoption intention; if these same conditions raise
the executive’s fear of implementation failure, the net effect need not be positive. Gan and Xiao (2025), analysing how generative
AI reconstructs the innovation systems of SMEs, argue that algorithm -participative knowledge creation lands only where the  firm
already holds data an algorithm can read. Gan et al. (2026), in a systematic review of anxiety, resistance and governance tra nsfor-
mation among low-skilled workers under artificial intelligence, note that the internal organisational resistance triggered by technol-
ogy entry has long stayed outside the field of view of adoption research. Yue et al. (2026) treat technology anxiety as a bou ndary
variable in fresh-food e-commerce and test its effect on the link between perceived service quality and continua nce intention. To-
gether these results point to a proposition that has not been tested directly in SME artificial intelligence research: a firm’s own digital
condition is not only a precondition for adoption but may also change how hard risk perception presses on intention.
Three questions follow. Does each of the four technology-side and environment -side antecedents reach adoption intention
through perceived value or through perceived implementation risk ? Do the two pathways point the same way, o r do they cancel?
And what boundary role does a firm’s digital foundation play between risk perception and intention?
The contribution is bounded deliberately. This study does not propose a new theory of adoption. It takes constructs already
established in the technology acceptance, technology –organization–environment and perceived risk literatures and specifies where

2. Theoretical Background and Hypothesis Development
2.1. Bridging the TOE Framework and the Upper Echelons Perspective
The technology–organization–environment framework sorts the determinants of an adoption decision into three domains (Tor-
natzky & Fleischer, 1990). Zhu and Kraemer (2005) test the framework on cross -country retail data, where the positive effect of
competitive pressure on the depth of e -business use holds; Lin (2014) works with manufacturing firms and finds effects from all
three domains on electronic supply chain management system adoption. What the framework explains is organisational behaviour,
and the stage at which the decision maker processes these conditions is absent. The unified theory of acceptance and use of tech-
nology supplies that stage by setting performance expectancy and effort expectancy as direct antecedents (Venkatesh et al., 2 003),
and Venkatesh et al. (2012) bring price value and hedonic motivation into the consumer version, writing the trade -off between
benefit and sacrifice formally into the model.
The upper echelons perspective supplies the missing stage in a form specific to this setting. Hambrick and Mason (1984) hold
that organisational outcomes reflect the cognitive base and the values of the dominant coalition, whose members act not on th e
objective situation but on a construed reality filtered through a limited field of vision, selective perception and interpretation. Ham-
brick (2007) states the condition under which that filtering matters most, namely managerial discretion, which is greatest where the
situation is unstructured, the firm small and the decision concentrated in few hands (Hambrick & Finkelstein, 1987). An SME i n a
traditional industry facing an artificial intelligence decision meets all three conditions at once. Ocasio (1997) and Liang et al. (2007)
supply the mechanism: an external condition becomes organisational action only after it captures the attention of top managem ent,
so the executive stage decides the effect of an environmental variable rather than merely relaying it. The perspective is put to work
here in three specific places. It fixes the level of analysis at the individual executive rather than the firm. It identifies perceived value
and perceived implementation risk as the interpretation stage through which the technol ogy and environment domains must pass,
so that the two constructs are not free-standing mediators but the content of the executive’s construed reality. And it treats executive
age and education as observable proxies for the cognitive base, whose coefficien ts are reported and read in Section 5.3 rather than
absorbed silently as controls.
For an SME executive, conditions in the technology and environment domains are externally given information inputs; per-
ceived value and perceived implementation risk are the outputs after processing; adoption intention is the behavioural tendency that
follows. The four antecedents are defined as follows. Performance expectancy is the executive ’s estimate of how far operating
indicators would improve after artificial intelligence is introduced, following Venkatesh et al. (2003) and Chatterjee et al.  (2021).
Effort expectancy is the executive’s estimate of the learning cost and the volume of process rework the firm would have to absorb.
Competitive pressure is the perceived requirement to keep up technologically, coming from competitors and downstream customers,
defined after Zhu and Kraemer (2 005). Perceived institutional support is the perceived availability of resources from local govern-
ment, public service platforms and industry bodies, drawing on the distinction Liang et al. (2007) and Bag et al. (2021) ma ke
between the normative and the supportive components of institutional pressure.
The two intermediate constructs follow the value –risk pairing. Perceived value is the executive ’s net benefit judgement after
weighing inputs against outputs (Kim et al., 2007; Zeithaml, 1988). Perceived implementation risk is the subjective estimate of the
likelihood of sunk expenditure, data leakage, absence of qualified personnel and disruption of production order during introduction
(Featherman & Pavlou, 2003). Digital foundation is the firm’s existing degree of data digitisation, information system usage, system
maintenance manpower and cross -departmental data accessibility, defined with reference to the firm -level AI readiness of
Alsheibani et al. (2018) and the AI capability measure of Mikalef and Gupta (2021).
The four bodies of work enter the model in distinct roles rather than side by side. The technology –organization–environment
framework settles which external conditions are admitted; the upper echelons and attention -based arguments settle who processes
them and why that stage is decisive in an SME; the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology settles the evaluative dimen-
sions on which the processing runs; and the value –risk pairing settles the form of the output, two assessments rather than one. Th e

2.2. Splitting the Technology-Side Antecedents
Performance expectancy describes the benefit end. An executive who expects artificial intelligence to shorten delivery cycles,
cut unit labour cost and reduce rework enters that expectation directly on the credit side of the net benefit calculation. Ch atterjee et
al. (2021) in manufacturing and production firms and Pillai and Sivathanu (2020) in human resource management both test the
effect of perceived usefulness on AI adoption intention, and in each model the estimate reaches statistical significance and  ranks
among the leading antecedents. Performance expectancy also carries an implicit judgement about technological maturity: an exe c-
utive who believes the technology delivers a definite improvement worries less about implementation failure. That negative link
should be weaker than the positive link to the value side, since performance expectations answer whether benefits will arrive  and
say little about whether the process will go wrong.
Hypothesis 1 (H1a): Performance expectancy positively predicts perceived value.
Hypothesis 1 (H1b): Performance expectancy negatively predicts perceived implementation risk.
Effort expectancy describes the process end. Executives of traditional-industry SMEs are rarely the ones operating the system.
Their judgement of how easy a tool is to use converts poorly into any figure on the profit and loss account, yet it maps directly onto
worries of the form “the staff will not take it up after go-live” and “there will be nobody to maintain it”. Davis (1989) reports in the
original test that the effect of perceived ease of use on usage runs largely through perceived usefulness, its di rect link being the
weaker of the two; the AI project value sources traced by Wamba -Taguimdje et al. (2020) likewise sit at the process and decision
level rather than at ease of operation. Transposed to an executive sample, effort expectancy should link weakly to the value end and
strongly to the risk end.
Hypothesis 2 (H2a): Effort expectancy positively predicts perceived value.
Hypothesis 2 (H2b): Effort expectancy negatively predicts perceived implementation risk.
2.3. The Double-Edged Form of the Environment-Side Antecedents
Competitive pressure has long been treated in adoption research as a one -directional driver. The mimetic isomorphism of
DiMaggio and Powell (1983) supplies the source of that drive: when outcomes are uncertain, organisations copy what their peer s
do. The empirical results of Zhu and Kraemer (2005), Lin (2014) and Sun et al. (2020) all support a positive effect of competitive
pressure on adoption. For an SME this drive carries a by -product: a competitor’s earlier move compresses the firm’s own decision
window. Compression is itself a source of risk, since following without adequate assessment raises the chance of sunk expenditure
and of disruption to the production order. The barriers to AI adoption that Kar and Kushwaha (2023) extract from public opini on
text include insufficient implementation capability and expected project failure, and both are amplified under pressure to keep up.
Competitive pressure therefore takes a double-edged form: it raises the value judgement and the risk judgement together.
Hypothesis 3 (H3a): Competitive pressure positively predicts perceived value.
Hypothesis 3 (H3b): Competitive pressure positively predicts perceived implementation risk.
Perceived institutional support works the other way round. Local fiscal funds, technical guidance from public service platforms
and matchmaking run by industrial parks change the fallback conditions surrounding implementation, not what the technology itself
can earn for the firm. A subsidy reduces the severity of sunk expenditure and platform guidance eases the shortage of personn el;
neither changes the executive’s answer to the question of what the technology will do for orders and costs. Bag et al. (2021) separate
institutional pressures from resources as two sets of antecedents of big -data-powered AI adoption, locating the contribution of the
resource side in the firm’s implementation capability and that of the pressure side in internal organisational conditions. Read against
the three pillars of Scott (2014), the construct sits on the regulative pillar, where rules, funds and monitoring set the con ditions of
compliance, and on the supportive part of the normative pillar carried by trade associations and industrial parks; the cultural-cogni-
tive pillar, which governs what counts as a taken -for-granted way of running a firm, is not measured here. Oliver (1991) adds that
organisations respond to institutional processes strategically, from acquiescence through to avoidance, and that the response turns

Hypothesis 4 (H4a): Perceived institutional support positively predicts perceived value.
Hypothesis 4 (H4b): Perceived institutional support negatively predicts perceived implementation risk.
2.4. From the Two Pathways to Adoption Intention
Perceived value is the composite net benefit judgement, and its link to adoption intention has been tested repeatedly in valu e-
based adoption models. The value -based adoption model of Kim et al. (2007) makes perceived value the sole direct antecedent of
adoption intention, with benefit and sacrifice components netted out inside the value construct before they act on intention. Perceived
implementation risk describes the expectation of adverse consequences during introduction, and its suppressive effect on i ntention
holds in e-service adoption (Featherman & Pavlou, 2003) and in artificial intelligence  adoption (Cubric, 2020). The two pathways
are conceptually independent: a firm can hold that artificial intelligence is worth the money and at the same time doubt that  it can
pull the project off. That combination is what the organisational literature calls ambivalence, a positive and a negative evaluation of
the same object held together rather than netted into one position (Ashforth et al., 2014; Piderit, 2000). Forcing the two assessments
onto one dimension pushes the ambivalent case towards a middle score that misrepresents both components, which is the specifica-
tion tested as M3 and rejected in Section 4.6.
Hypothesis 5 (H5): Perceived value positively predicts adoption intention.
Hypothesis 6 (H6): Perceived implementation risk negatively predicts adoption intention.
Four antecedents mediated through two pathways give eight indirect effects. The form of each antecedent’s net effect depends
on how the signs combine across the two pathways: performance expectancy and effort expectancy act in the same direction on both
pathways by raising value and lowering risk, competitive pressure raises value and raises risk at once, an d perceived institutional
support works mainly through the risk pathway.
Hypothesis 7 (H7a–H7d): The effects of performance expectancy, effort expectancy, competitive pressure and perceived institu-
tional support on adoption intention are mediated by perceived value.
Hypothesis 8 (H8a–H8d): The effects of performance expectancy, effort expectancy, competitive pressure and perceived institu-
tional support on adoption intention are mediated by perceived implementation risk.
2.5. Digital Foundation as a Boundary Condition
Digital foundation carries two identities in artificial intelligence adoption. As a precondition, machine -readable data stock, an
information system already running and staff able to maintain it constitute the feasibility base for introduction. Alsheibani  et al.
(2018) group such conditions under firm -level AI readiness; the AI capability instrument of Mikalef and Gupta (2021) covers tan-
gible resources, human skills and intangible organisational elements, and its positive effect on organisational creativity  and firm
performance holds in their firm sample. Xiao and Gan (2026), in an integrative review of operations reconfiguration under big data,
argue that the conversion from data possession to operations reconfiguration depends on internal data accessibilit y rather than on
data volume. This identity corresponds to a direct effect of digital foundation on adoption intention.
The second identity is that of a buffer on risk perception. Perceived implementation risk has specific content: a system that
never goes live, no one available to maintain it, disruption of the production order. Firms already running ERP or MES, alrea dy
staffing system maintenance and already able to move data across departments absorb part of that content through conditions the y
hold. The technology readiness index of Parasuraman (2000) sets optimism and innovativeness as contributor dimensions and dis-
comfort and insecurity as inhibitor dimensions, the four jointly governing how an individual responds to new technology. Yue et al.
(2026) test the boundary role of technology anxiety on the link between perceived service quality and continuance intention in fresh-
food e-commerce, providing consumer-side evidence that the strength of an inhibitory psychological variable varies with external
conditions. On the firm side, the higher the digital foundation, the weaker the suppression that risk perception exerts on  adoption
intention. Whether digital foundation amplifies the value pathway in the sam e way has no corresponding theoretical basis: a net

3. Materials and Methods
3.1. Sampling and Data Collection
The sampling frame was restricted to four traditional industries: manufacturing in textiles and apparel, food processing, met al
products and furniture and building materials; wholesale and retail; transport, warehousing and postal services; and construc tion
and installation. Firms were screened to 20–300 employees, corresponding to the small and medium bands of the relevant industries
under the Provisions on the Standards for Classifying Small and Medium -sized Enterprises issued as MIIT Joint Enterprise [20 11]
No. 300, excluding micro firms below 20 employees and large firms above 300. Coverage spans the Yangtze River Delta, the Pearl
River Delta, the Chengdu–Chongqing area and the central provinces of Henan, Anhui, Hunan and Hubei, taking in regional differ-
ences in digital infrastructure and industrial structure.
Questionnaires were distributed through three routes: membership rosters of local federations of industry and commerce and
trade associations, enterprise service desks in industrial parks across the four a reas, and referrals from firms the research team had
worked with previously. Respondents were restricted to the chairman, general manager, deputy general manager, or the executiv e
in charge of informatisation or production, with one valid questionnaire accepted per firm. Between September and December 2025,
680 questionnaires were distributed and 592 returned, a return rate of 87.06%. Three exclusion rules applied: identical respo nses
across all items of a scale; completion time below 180 seconds; firm size or industry outside the sampling frame. The final set

3.3. Procedural Control of Common Method Bias and Analytical Strategy
Self-reported data from a single source carry a risk of method variance. Four procedural controls follow Podsakoff et al. (2003):
the questionnaire carries no statement of the research topic or hypothesised directions; antecedents, intermediate variables and the
outcome variable sit in separate blocks with unrelated filler items between them; a reverse -worded attention check appears in the
perceived implementation risk scale; and respondents received a written undertaking of anonymity and of statistical use  only.
Analysis follows the two -step procedure of Anderson and Gerbing (1988): the measurement model is tested by eight -factor
confirmatory factor analysis, and the structural model is estimated once it passes. Parameters are estimated by maximum likelihood.
Fit is evaluated against the criteria of Hu and Bentler (1999): χ²/df < 3, CFI and TLI ≥ 0.90, RMSEA ≤ 0.08, SRMR ≤ 0.08. Indirect
effects are tested with 5,000 bias -corrected bootstrap resamples, judged by whether the 95% confidence interval excludes zero
(Preacher & Hayes, 2008); the full measurement and structural model is re -estimated on every resample, which avoids the under-
statement that comes from resampling point estimates alone. Moderation follows Aiken and West (1991), standardising the variables
before forming the product terms, and is judged by the Δ R²  of a hierarchical regression and by the interaction coefficients, with
simple slopes computed at ± 1 standard deviation; product-indicator estimation of latent interactions is more sensitive to sample size

Business and Management Perspectives Volume 01 (2026) 1‒28 9
https://doi.org/10.67728/bmp.2026.016
and distributional assumptions (Marsh et al., 2004) and was not adopted as the main approach. Moderated mediation is tested w ith
the index method of Hayes (2015). Group differences in path coefficients are tested with the z statistic for the equality of regression
coefficients given by Paternoster et al. (1998).  Two decision rules are fixed in advance and applied throughout. All tests are two -
tailed and evaluated at the .05 level. A directional hypothesis counts as supported when the estimate carries the hy pothesised sign
and reaches that level, whatever its magnitude; an estimate that reaches the level but is small in size is flagged as small i n the text
rather than reclassified as a failure.

4.4. Common Method Bias
Three tests were run in parallel. Harman’s single-factor analysis extracts eight factors with eigenvalues above 1 in the unrotated
solution, the first accounting for 28.00% of the variance, below the 40% cut -off. Adding a common latent factor loaded by all 32

The sample was split by employee count into 210 firms with 20 to 49 employees and 316 with 50 or more, and the structural

5. Discussion
5.1. The Shape of the Split between the Two Pathways
The four antecedents distribute unevenly across the two pathways, and that asymmetry is the main result of the estimation.
Performance expectancy is the only antecedent with a coefficient of 0.382 on the value pathway that also retains an effective direct
path of 0.160, in the same direction as the findings of Chatterjee et al. (2021) in manufacturing and production firms and Pi llai and
Sivathanu (2020) in human resource management. The question “what will the technology do for me?” is answ ered consistently
across differences in firm size and in the economy in which the firm operates.
The result for effort expectancy diverges from established findings on individual users. Davis (1989) and the large body of
tests on end users that followed treat perceived ease of use as an antecedent of perceived usefulness. In this estimation, ef fort
expectancy has a coefficient of 0.051 with p = .247 on perceived value and −0.398 with p < .001 on perceived implementation risk,
the second of which is the largest antecedent coefficient in the model. The divergence originates in who the respondent is. End users
bear the operating cost themselves, so ease of use enters their benefit calculation directly. An SME executive does not opera te the
system: ease of use contributes nothing directly to a profit -and-loss judgement, yet maps directly onto implementation  risks of the
form “the staff will not take it up after go -live” and “there will be nobody to maintain it ”. A model that keeps effort expectancy as
an antecedent of benefit misplaces its point of action in an executive sample.
The suppressed total effect of competitive pressure is the result among the four with the most direct bearing on practice. Th e
value pathway contributes 0.160 and the risk pathway −0.041, opposite in sign, and the total effect of 0.064 has an interval of
[−0.037, 0.161] that contains zero. Mimetic isomorphism (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983) accounts for the source of the value pathway
but does not cover the compression of the firm ’s own decision window by a competitor’s earlier move. The barriers of insufficient
implementation capability and expected project failure that Kar and Kushwaha (2023) extract from public opinion text acquire a
parameterised counterpart here: a one standard deviation increase in competitive pressure raises perceived implementation ris k by
0.200 standard deviations. The one-directional positive effect of competitive pressure reported by Zhu and Kraemer (2005) and Lin
(2014) on samples of medium and large firms does not reproduce in the present sample of traditional -industry SMEs. Whether that
difference is attributable to firm size, to industry or to the institutional setting cannot be settled with one cross -sectional sample;
what the estimates establish is that the positive total effect is absent here and that two pathways of opposite sign account for its
absence. The reading proposed is that the difference lies not in whether pressure exists but in whether the firm holds the or ganisa-
tional buffer to absorb it.
The result for institutional support forms the counterpart to competitive pressure. Its coefficient on perceived value is 0.0 55
with p = .252 and is indistinguishable from zero, while its coefficient on perceived implementation risk is −0.162 with p = .003 and
reaches the .01 level. The total effect of 0.071 has an interval containing zero and the total indirect effect of 0.061 has a n interval
excluding it. Fiscal funds, platform guidance and park matchmaking change the conditions under which failure can be borne, not
the earnings outlook of the technology. Bag et al. (2021), who locate the contribution of the resource side in implementation  capa-
bility rather than in adoption intention itself, point the same way.
5.2. The Two Roles of Digital Foundation
Digital foundation obtains a direct path of 0.147 with p = .003 and a moderating effect of 0.080 with p = .011, and the moder-
ation appears only on the risk pathway. The risk slopes of −0.284 in the low digital foundation group and −0.125 in the high group
differ by 56.18%, every index of moderated mediation for the four indirect effects on the risk pathway reaches the .05 level, and the
three simple slopes on the value pathway differ by less than 0.005. This asymmetry says that what digital foundation buffe rs is the
fear of not pulling the project off, not the judgement of whether it is worth the money. ERP or MES already running, staff assigned
to maintenance and data that can move across departments correspond directly to the capacity to bear the three consequences named
in the perceived implementation risk scale: a system that never goes live, no one available to maintain it, disruption of pro duction.

Whether artificial intelligence can improve delivery cycles and labour costs is judged from market and order information, whi ch
has no counterpart in the firm’s internal system conditions. The moderation should be weighed at its measured size. It is consistent
across the four indices of moderated mediation and legible in the simple slopes, yet the two product terms add only 0.007 to R² , so
digital foundation is best described as a condition that changes how heavily risk weighs on the decision rather than as a variable that
reorganises the decision.
Alsheibani et al. (2018) treat firm -level AI readiness as a precondition for adoption and Mikalef and Gupta (2021) treat AI
capability as an antecedent of performance, both remaining at the level of main effects. Xiao and Gan (2026) argue that the conver-
sion from data possession to operations reconfiguration depends on internal data accessibility; the present result gives that argument
a counterpart at the level of decision psychology, in that data accessibility matters not only for technical feasibility bu t because it
removes more than half of the pressure risk perception exerts on the decision. The mean of digital foundation in the sample i s the
only one of the eight below the scale midpoint and its standard deviation is the largest, so traditional -industry SMEs are sharply
divided on this condition and one and the same subsidy policy will differ in its effect through the risk pathway by a factor of about
two across firms.
5.3. The Executive as the Locus of the Decision
The upper echelons argument set out in Section 2.1 can be held against the coefficients on the executive characteristics, which
were entered as controls and are read here as observable proxies for the cognitive base rather than as nuisance terms. Executive age
raises perceived implementation risk by 0.144 ( p = .001) and lowers adoption intention by 0.121 ( p < .001); executive education
contributes 0.055 (p = .095) to intention and does not reach the .05 level. The pattern matches the mechanism Hambrick and Mason
(1984) describe. Age indexes accumulated commitment to an established way of running the firm and a narrower field of vision for
an unfamiliar technology, and it acts here on the risk assessment rather than on the value assessment, which is where the  upper
echelons account would place it. Two qualifications go with this. The estimates were not hypothesised in advance and are read after
the fact, so they are offered as a reading consistent with the perspective and not as a test of it. And demographic indicators are coarse
stand-ins for the cognitive base, a limitation Hambrick (2007) states directly; measuring the executive’s technology experience, risk
propensity and prior project history would put the argument on firmer ground. The practical reading is correspondingly narrow :
where an older executive decides alone, the risk pathway is the one that carries the weight, and an outside technical opinion changes
the decision conditions more than a further demonstration of benefit.
5.4. What Three Kinds of Actors Can Change
For the executives themselves. The sample mean of perceived implementation risk, 5.002, exceeds that of perceived value,
4.858, and their coefficients on intention are −0.206 and 0.495. A one standard deviation reduction in risk perception improv es
intention by 0.206, less than the 0.495 gained from a one standard deviation rise in value perception, yet the room to act is larger on
the risk side: the coefficient of −0.398 from effort expectancy to risk means that choosing a lightweight tool with a low lea rning
cost that fits into existing processes changes the decision conditions more than waiting for a more powerful product. The coefficient
of executive age is 0.144 on risk perception with  p = .001 and −0.121 on intention with  p < .001, which indicates that firms where
an older executive decides alone need outside technical opinion to balance that tilt.
For technology vendors. Concentrating the sales pitch on functional strength acts only on the value pathway. The indirect
effect of effort expectancy through the risk pathway is 0.082 against 0.025 through the value pathway. For an SME executive, three
questions therefore change the purchase decision more than performance specifications do: who will maintain the system after go -
live, how long the staff will take to learn it, and how much of the existing process has to be rebuilt. Gan and Xiao (2025), analysing
how generative AI reconstructs SME innovation systems, argue that algorithm -participative knowledge creation lands only where
the firm already holds usable data; a vendor who extends the delivery scope from software to data preparation and maintenance
hosting is in effect supplying the customer with the moderating variable.
For local authorities. The total effect of institutional support is 0.071, near zero , and its entire effective contribution of 0.061
runs through the risk pathway, which places the point of action of the current subsidy -centred policy toolkit at lowering the conse-
quences of failure rather than at raising adoption intention. The total effe ct of competitive pressure is 0.064 and likewise sits near
zero: publicity built on peer demonstration and benchmark cases raises the value judgement and the risk judgement  together, and
the two offset each other. The moderating effect of digital foundation carries a direct implication for policy sequence. Putt ing
resources first into data digitisation, coverage by basic information systems and system maintenance manpower removes more than
half of the suppression the risk pathway exerts on intention, after which the same money spent on AI application subsidies will work
differently through that pathway. Gan et al. (2026), reviewing anxiety and governance transformation among low-skilled workers
under artificial intelligence, note that the internal resistance provoked by technology entry requires an institutional response; writing

5.5. Theoretical Contribution
Three points can be read from the results. Each is an increment to an existing framework rather than a new theory, and each is
stated at the scale the evidence supports. First, once environmental variables from the TOE framework are joined to cognitive
processing at the individual level, the form of their effect changes: the one -directional positive effect of competitive pressure in
organisational-level research decomposes at the executive cognitive level into two pathways of opposite sign, with the net eff ect
falling back near zero. The gap between the two families of frameworks identified by Oliveira and Martins (2011) appears in t he
traditional-industry SME setting as external pressure being translated by one and the same decision maker into an opportunit y and
a threat at once.

Second, perceived value and perceived implementation risk are not two ends of a single dimension. The correlation between
their composite scores is −0.033 with  p = .451 and the latent correlation is −0.054, and the four antecedents differ clearly in their
coefficient patterns across the two pathways. M3 in the present comparison treats risk as the reverse of value and keeps a si ngle
assessment pathway, which rai ses SRMR from 0.038 to 0.041 and lowers CFI from 0.970 to 0.967. The independence of risk and
benefit proposed by Featherman and Pavlou (2003) in the e-service setting holds equally in firm technology introduction decisions.
Third, a firm’s digital condition has long been treated in adoption research as a main -effect variable or a control. The present
result supplies parameterised evidence for its role as a boundary condition:  the moderation appears only on the risk pathway, and
the three simple slopes on the value pathway differ by less than 0.005. That asymmetry narrows the loose claim that the digital base
determines adoption into the narrower claim that the digital base conditions how hard risk perception presses on the decision, within
the modest incremental variance reported in Section 4.8.
5.6. Boundary Conditions and Generalisability
The estimates come from one institutional environment at one point in time, and separating what is likely to travel from what
is not belongs to reporting them. Three results rest on mechanisms that are not jurisdictional. The separation of the value assessment
from the risk assessment follows from the dual-factor argument of Cenfetelli (2004) and the ambivalence argument of Piderit (2000),
neither of which depends on a national setting. The displacement of effort expectancy from the value side to the risk s ide follows
from who the respondent is, an executive who does not operate the system, and should reappear in any executive sample. The
buffering role of digital foundation follows from the content of the risk items, which name conditions a firm either hold s or lacks.
Two results are tied to the setting. Perceived institutional support is measured here as application -based subsidies, public service
platforms, park matchmaking and an explicit policy category, a package characteristic of Chinese industrial pol icy (Peng et al.,
2008); where public support reaches SMEs through tax credits, innovation vouchers or advisory networks, the content of the co n-
struct changes and the near-zero total effect reported here cannot be assumed to carry over. The near -zero net effect of competitive
pressure depends on how quickly a competitor’s move becomes visible, which in the dense clusters of the Yangtze and Pearl River
Deltas is quicker than in dispersed industries elsewhere; Zhu et al. (2006) report cross -country variation of exactly this kind in the
strength of environmental effects on assimilation. Replication in another institutional environment is the direct test, and i t requires
no change to the instrument beyond the wording of the institutional support items.
A second boundary concerns what the evidence can carry. The eleven hypotheses are propositions derived from the literature
reviewed in Section 2; the coefficients in Section 4 are associations estimated on cross -sectional data. Where the text states that an
antecedent raises or lowers an assessment, it reports the sign and size of an estimated association under the specification stated, not
an observed change over time. The competing model comparison in Section 4.6 narrows the set of specifications consistent with the
covariance structure; it does not establish temporal order, and the design that would is named among the limitations in Secti on 6.
6. Conclusions
Structural equation estimation on 526 SME executives across four regions and four traditional industries yields three conclu-
sions. Performance expectancy and competitive pressure reach adoption intention through perceived value at 0.189 and 0.160, while
the effects of effort expectancy and institutional support on perceived value are indistinguishable from zero and their contrib ution
concentrates on lowering perceived implementation risk at −0.398 and −0.162. Competitive pressure is the only antecedent whos e
two pathways carry opposite signs, the risk pathway cancelling 25.71% of the value pathway, with a total effect of 0.064 whose 95%
interval contains zero; the total effect of institutional support, 0.071, likewise contains zero. Digital foundation weaken s the sup-
pression that perceived implementation risk exerts on adoption intention, the slope falling from −0.284 to −0.125, an attenuation of
56.18%, with no counterpart effect on the value pathway.
Six limitations bound these conclusions. Firs t, the data are self -reported and cross -sectional at a single point in time, so the
ordering among perceived value, perceived implementation risk and adoption intention is set by theory rather than confirmed by the
data, and reverse linkage cannot be exclu ded, since an executive who already intends to adopt may be rationalising that decision;
two-wave tracking at six-month intervals or a scenario experiment would separate the directions. Second, the dependent variable is
adoption intention rather than adoption behaviour, and budget approval, vendor matching and staff acceptance intervene betwee n
the two; the intention–behaviour gap identified by Ajzen (1991) may be wider in resource -constrained SMEs, and actual ad option
records at twelve months would settle the question. Third, the sample covers four industries in four regions and excludes ass et-
heavy industries such as mining and chemicals as well as the north -west and north-east; the industry coefficient of −0.03 3 with p
= .318 does not warrant extrapolation to all traditional industries. Fourth, the questionnaire uses “artificial intelligence” as a collec-
tive term and does not separate machine vision inspection in production, intelligent scheduling and demand forecasting in operations,
and intelligent customer service at the customer end; the three differ markedly in investment scale and implem entation difficulty,
and the differences in organisational response across technology forms noted by Gan et al. (2026) cal l for re-estimation on scales

estimates involving them, including the moderation, are less precise than the others; a fuller digital foundation instrument covering
data quality and system integration would tighten them. Sixth, the moderating effect, although consistent across the  four indices of
moderated mediation, adds only 0.007 to the explained variance in intention, so the boundary condition should be read as a ch ange
in the weight the risk pathway carries and not as a large reorganisation of the decision. These conclusions a lso come from a single

and a replication where public support reaches SMEs through tax credits or advisory networks is t he direct test.
