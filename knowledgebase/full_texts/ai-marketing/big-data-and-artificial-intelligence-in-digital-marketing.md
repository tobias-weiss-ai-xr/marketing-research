Big Data and artificial intelligence are changing digital marketing through
consumer behavior analysis, personalized communication, and automation. This
paper examines their role in marketing processes, performance measurement, and
the conditions for application in Serbia. A narrative review of selected literature is
combined with a descriptive analysis of published statistics on ICT use, e -
commerce, and enterprise AI adoption. The analysis ide ntifies widespread
consumer participation in digital channels alongside more limited enterprise
adoption of AI. Serbia remains below the EU average in enterprise AI use, although
above some member states. The paper’s contribution lies in linking data resources,
analytical capabilities, and managerial competencies to explain why technology
adoption alone is insufficient for effective digital marketing. Practical implications
emphasize employee development, responsible data management, and the
evaluation of m arketing outcomes. The findings should be interpreted within the
limits of aggregate indicators, which do not directly measure marketing-specific AI
use or its effectiveness. Future research should examine these applications and their
outcomes at the enterprise level.
Keywords: Big Data, artificial intelligence, digital marketing, marketing metrics,

follow new trends face increasing challenges in
maintaining their position in the market. One of the first

activity, including marketing, where digital platforms, Big

However, the availability of data does not automatically
ensure better marketing decisions. Its practical value

apply the resulting insights in planning and implementing
marketing activities. In this context, artificial intelligence
provides support for marketing research, segmentation,

Digital marketing encompasses strategies and
technologies aimed at promotion, communication, and
sales in a digital environment. It is closely connected with
the development of electronic commerc e, one of the key
forms of digital exchange of goods and services. In e -
commerce, digital marketing utilizes tools such as
personalized ads, search engine optimization, social
media marketing, automated recommendation systems,
and user behavior analytics, all aimed at enhancing
customer experience and increasing sales. E -commerce
experienced accelerated expansion during the Covid -19
pandemic, becoming an increasingly important segment
of the global economy. Its main advantages include
continuous access to o nline stores and the possibility of
reaching consumers beyond local markets, although
delivery and other operational conditions still affect the
availability of products and services.

The concept of Big Data, combined with the application
of artificial i ntelligence, extends the capabilities of
traditional market research methods in market
segmentation, consumer targeting, and content
personalization. While traditional campaigns often
address broader groups of consumers with a common
message, digital technologies enable communication to be
adapted to individual interests and behavior. One of the
greatest advancements in digital marketing is the ability to
personalize content based on predictions of consumer
preferences and purchasing behavior. The role of A I
therefore goes beyond the automation of individual tasks
and includes support for marketing strategies and a better
understanding of consumers (Davenport et al., 2020).
Recent research additionally emphasizes that digital
marketing should be considered a  component of strategic
management, since digital channels, personalization, and
interaction with consumers can support competitive
positioning and business performance (Dobričanin &
Aleksić, 2025).

Along with the advantages that these technologies bring
to marketing and communication, their accelerated
development and widespread application also raise
numerous questions and controversies. Davenport et al.
(2020) emphasize that the development of AI in marketing
should be considered together with issues of privacy, bias,
and ethics. Regulations are therefore of particular
importance in establishing conditions for the responsible
use of AI solutions, including the protection of users from
discrimination, respect for their privacy, and transparency
in the co llection and use of data. These issues are
especially relevant in personalized recommendations,
targeted advertising, and consumer profiling (Vukmirović
et al., 2025).

The application of these technologies also depends on the
digital capacities of compan ies and the characteristics of
the market in which they operate. In Serbia, data on the
use of ICT, e -commerce, and artificial intelligence
provide a basis for considering the conditions for further
development of digital marketing. However, general
indicators of technology adoption need to be distinguished
from indicators of its specific application in marketing.
This distinction is important for assessing the
opportunities and limitations of using Big Data and AI in
communication with consumers.

The aim of this paper is to examine the role of Big Data
and artificial intelligence in digital marketing, with
particular attention to personalization, marketing metrics,
and the conditions for their application in Serbia. The
paper combines a narrative revie w of selected literature
with a descriptive analysis of published statistical data on
the use of ICT, e -commerce, and AI in Serbia. The
discussion focuses on the relationship between the
possibilities offered by these technologies and the
capacities of com panies to apply them. Its practical
contribution lies in connecting the theoretical discussion
with the Serbian market context and identifying areas in
which further development of digital capabilities could
support marketing activities.

2. Literature review

2.1. From big data to artificial intelligence

The concept of Big Data is commonly explained through
three dimensions, or 3V: volume, velocity, and variety
(Zikopoulos et al., 2012). Volume refers to the huge
amount of data generated daily through transaction
activities, electronic communications, movement, and the
use of electronic devices such as cameras, sensors,
scanners, and smart devices. Velocity refers to the sp eed
at which data is generated and the need for its timely
processing, while variety points to numerical, textual,
audio, visual, biometric, and other heterogeneous data
forms.

In digital marketing, these dimensions are reflected in the
continuous genera tion of data on website visits, product
searches, purchases, responses to advertisements, and
interactions on social networks. Such data can provide
information about consumer interests and behavior, but its
volume alone does not guarantee useful insights.  The
marketing value of Big Data depends on the quality and
relevance of the available data and the ability to connect
its analysis with specific business objectives.

In this process of comprehensive digitization, decision -
makers may have access to large  amounts of data while
still lacking reliable and timely information. This creates
a need for business analytics and tools that support the
identification of relevant patterns. Big Data and artificial
intelligence therefore represent related but different

concepts. Big Data refers to the characteristics of data and
the challenges of its management and analysis, while
artificial intelligence encompasses systems capable of
performing tasks that require abilities such as learning,
reasoning, and decision -making. Machine learning, as an
area of AI, enables systems to learn patterns from data,
while deep learning uses multilayer neural networks.
These approaches can support the analysis of large and
complex datasets, although not every AI application
requires Big Data (Russell & Norvig, 2020).

Recent research further distinguishes analytical AI from
generative AI. Analytical AI primarily supports the
identification of patterns and the prediction of outcomes,
while generative AI produces content such as text, images,
and video. In digital marketing, these capabilities can
complement each other: analytical insights can inform the
selection of audiences and offers, while generative tools
can assist in preparing communication adapted to those
audiences. Grewal et al. (2025) emphasize that
implementation requires decisions about data inputs and
the extent of human involvement in reviewing outputs.
Chan and Choi (2025) similarly identify applications
across consumer adoption, services, advertising, and
innovation, showing that the role of generative AI extends
beyond content creation.

The development and application of AI draw on a number
of principles and disciplines, including mathematics and
statistics, logic, cognitive science, psychology,
neurobiology, linguistics,  philosophy, ethics, computer
science, robotics, economics, and sociology. Artificial
intelligence is therefore an interdisciplinary field that
combines different approaches to developing systems that
imitate or complement aspects of human intelligence. An
intelligent agent acts to maximize the expected
achievement of its objectives based on available
observations and knowledge (Russell & Norvig, 2020).

Marketing is also studied from multiple perspectives. On
one hand, it is viewed as a scientific discipl ine that
develops theoretical models of market behavior and value
exchange. On the other, marketing has an engineering
dimension because it involves the application of research
methods, metrics, analytical tools, and technologies in
shaping market strategies. At the same time, marketing is
an essential business function within companies,
responsible for creating, communicating, and delivering
value to consumers (Kotler & Keller, 2016; Solomon,
2018).

The connection between Big Data and AI becomes
particularly visible when consumer data is used to support
marketing decisions. Data on previous purchases,
browsing behavior, and responses to promotional
messages can be analyzed to identify consumer
preferences and estimate future responses. These insights
can support segmentation, the selection of target groups,
and the adaptation of offers and communication. Huang
and Rust (2021) connect the application of AI with
marketing research, marketing strategy, and marketing
actions, showing that its role extends acro ss different
stages of the marketing process.

The role of artificial intelligence in the behavioral aspect
of marketing is also becoming increasingly significant, as
it supports the understanding and prediction of consumer
behavior. However, predictions based on observed
patterns do not necessarily explain the motives behind
individual decisions. Consumer behavior theories
therefore remain important for interpreting analytical
results and understanding the context in which purchasing
decisions are made (Solomon, 2018). In this sense, AI can
complement the knowledge and experience of marketing
managers rather than remove the need for their judgment
(Davenport et al., 2020).

Understanding consumer behavior also requires attention
to emotions and interperso nal communication. Goleman
(1995) emphasizes the importance of emotional
awareness and empathy in human relationships. These
concepts provide a broader basis for considering the
quality of communication with consumers, while the
application of AI to custom er interactions requires
separate consideration of technological capabilities and
limitations. Personalized communication should therefore
combine analytical insights with an understanding of the
human context in which purchasing decisions are made.

At t he operational level, AI can support automated
communication, recommendation systems, and the
processing of consumer requests. Davenport and Ronanki
(2018) distinguish applications related to process
automation, the development of insights from data, and
engagement with customers and employees. These
applications provide a useful basis for understanding how
AI can support different marketing activities. In
hospitality, contemporary applications similarly
emphasize automation, personalized services, and data -
driven management, which are relevant for digital
marketing in service industries (Zrnić, 2026).

The importance of adapting digital communication to the
characteristics of a particular market is also visible in
luxury branding. Krsmanović (2026) discusses how
luxury brands use digital marketing, personalized AI
experiences, and social media while seek ing to preserve
exclusivity. This illustrates that the application of digital
technologies needs to reflect brand positioning and
consumer expectations, rather than follow the same
approach in all industries.

Accordingly, the relationship between Big Data and AI in
digital marketing can be understood as a connection
between data resources, analytical capabilities, and
marketing decisions. Big Data provides a potential source
of market information, while AI offers tools for
identifying patterns and supporting responses to consumer
needs. Their practical value depends on how companies
interpret the resulting insights and apply them in
communication, service delivery, and the evaluation of
marketing results.

2.2. Key aspects and metrics in digital marketing

Digital marketing is based on communication with the
market via the Internet and other digital channels. The
main advantages of the Internet as a platform for
marketing communications are the ability to direct
multimedia content to selected audiences and t he
interactivity between companies and consumers. Unlike
communication limited to a particular advertising space or
broadcasting schedule, digital channels allow users to
access content at different times and interact with
companies throughout the purchasing process.

However, the possibility of reaching a selected audience
does not automatically ensure that a message will attract
attention or produce the desired response. The
effectiveness of digital communication depends on the
relevance of the content, the characteristics of the
audience, the choice of channel, and the objectives of the
campaign. Measurement is therefore an important
component of digital marketing because it connects
communication activities with observable user behavior
and business results (Chaffey & Ellis-Chadwick, 2019).

Marketing as an engineering discipline relies on metrics
and on the principle that effective management requires
reliable, relevant, and timely information. Digital
platforms provide opportunities for collecting suc h
information, but the selection of indicators needs to follow
the purpose of the activity being evaluated. Kaushik
(2009) emphasizes the importance of connecting web
analytics with business objectives, experimentation, and
an understanding of customer exp erience. A campaign
aimed at increasing brand awareness therefore requires a
different emphasis from a campaign intended to generate
inquiries or direct sales.

Website and campaign indicators can be considered
through several related groups. Traffic indi cators include
visits, users, pageviews, and traffic sources. Audience and
technical characteristics include geographical location,
devices, and browsers. Engagement indicators describe
interactions with content, while conversion indicators
record the comp letion of desired actions. Financial
indicators, such as customer acquisition cost, customer
lifetime value, and return on marketing investment,
connect marketing activities with their economic effects
(Chaffey & Ellis -Chadwick, 2019; Kotler & Keller,
2016).

The conversion rate represents the proportion of users or
interactions that result in a defined action, such as a
purchase, registration, inquiry, or subscription. Its
calculation requires a clearly specified action and
denominator, which may refer to  users, sessions, or
advertising interactions. Comparisons are meaningful
only when the same definitions and measurement
conditions are used. A higher conversion rate can indicate
more effective communication, but it does not
independently demonstrate prof itability because costs,
transaction values, and subsequent customer behavior also
affect business results.

The practical relevance of conversion measurement is also
reflected in industry benchmark publications. Bendasyuk
(2025) and First Page Sage (2023 ) present conversion
benchmarks across industries, while Red Stag Fulfillment
(n.d.) discusses differences associated with product
categories, devices, and measurement approaches. These
publications illustrate why a single average conversion
rate cannot se rve as a universal performance target. The
interpretation of a benchmark requires attention to the
type of conversion, the characteristics of the observed
businesses, and the period covered by the data. Such
sources can provide practical orientation, but their figures
should not be treated as directly comparable findings from
a common research design.

Artificial intelligence can support the use of marketing
metrics by identifying behavioral patterns, estimating the
probability of a response, and assisting  the adaptation of
messages and offers. Huang and Rust (2021) connect
analytical applications with marketing decisions, while
Davenport et al. (2020) emphasize the broader
implications of AI for marketing strategies and customer
behavior.

Commercial and practitioner publications also describe
the use of AI for personalized recommendations, customer
engagement, and conversion optimization (Agentive AIQ,
2025; Girvan, 2025; Tilawat, 2025). However, reported
performance improvements depend on the application ,
baseline conditions, and method of evaluation. These
publications provide examples of expected or reported
business benefits, rather than sufficient evidence that AI
produces the same increase in conversion rates across
companies and industries. The cont ribution of an AI tool
therefore needs to be assessed against a defined objective
and an appropriate comparison.

The development of generative AI also broadens the
assessment of marketing performance. Alongside
conversion and financial indicators, compan ies need to
consider whether generated content is accurate,
appropriate to the brand, and suitable for its intended
audience. Kshetri et al. (2024) discuss both opportunities
and barriers associated with generative AI in marketing,
while Kumar et al. (2025) connect its business and societal
effects with organizational resources and capabilities.
These perspectives suggest that efficiency gains should be
considered together with content quality, human
oversight, and responsible implementation.

Performance measurement also needs to consider the
conditions under which consumer data is collected and
processed. Personalization and algorithmic targeting raise
questions of privacy, transparency, and consumer trust
(Vukmirović et al., 2025). Accordingl y, the evaluation of
digital marketing should connect traffic, engagement,
conversion, and financial indicators with responsible data
practices. This provides a more complete basis for
assessing whether Big Data and AI contribute to
marketing objectives.

Artificial intelligence provides opportunities to develop
digital marketing through personalized communication,

automated customer support, and the analysis of consumer
behavior. Companies in Serbia can cons ider these
applications through available platforms and services,
without necessarily developing their own AI systems.
However, the availability of technology does not
automatically ensure its effective use. Its application
requires appropriate data, emplo yee competencies, and
clearly defined marketing objectives.

The Statistical Office of the Republic of Serbia publishes
annual data on ICT use based on Eurostat methodology.
The 2024 enterprise survey covered enterprises with ten
or more employees in sele cted economic activities, while
the individual survey covered persons aged 16 –74.
According to the enterprise results, 85.0% had a website,
7.0% used AI technologies, and 25.2% employed ICT
specialists. The same publication reports that 28.4% of
enterprises sold products or services online during 2023
(Statistical Office of the Republic of Serbia, 2024).
Among individuals, 87.7% had used the Internet in the
preceding three months. Among recent Internet users,
81.5% participated in social networks and 79.1% searched
for information about goods and services. The publication
also reports that 51.8% of Internet users bought or ordered
goods or services online in the preceding three months
(Statistical Office of the Republic of Serbia, 2024).

A comparison with the European Union provides
additional context for assessing AI adoption in Serbia. In
2024, 13.5% of EU enterprises with ten or more persons
employed used AI technologies, compared with 7.0% in
Serbia, a difference of 6.5 percentage points. However,
considerable differences also existed among EU member
states. The highest adoption rates were recorded in
Denmark (27.6%), Sweden (25.1%), and Belgium
(24.7%), while Romania (3.1%), Poland (5.9%), and
Bulgaria (6.5%) recorded lower rates than Serbia
(Eurostat, 2025; Statistical Office of the Republic of
Serbia, 2024). Serbia therefore remained below the EU
average, although its position was more favorable in
comparison with some member states. These figures
concern AI use across business functions and should not
be interpreted as direct measures of its application in
digital marketing. Nevertheless, they provide a useful
benchmark for considering the wider technological
conditions in which AI-supported marketing can develop.

The Serbian indicators suggest favor able conditions for
reaching consumers through digital channels, alongside
more limited adoption of advanced technologies in
enterprises. Nevertheless, website ownership, online
sales, and AI use represent different dimensions of
business activity. A websi te can support information
provision and customer communication even when
transactions are completed offline. Similarly, the reported
use of AI refers to business applications generally and
does not establish the proportion of enterprises using it
specifically in marketing.

The relationship between these indicators can therefore be
interpreted as a potential gap between basic digital
presence and more advanced use of digital tools.
However, the data do not demonstrate that consumers are
uniformly digitally mature or that all enterprises without
online sales are technologically unprepared. The
relevance of e -commerce and AI depends on the sector,
customer characteristics, and business model. The
employment of ICT specialists also provides only a partial
view of enterprise capabilities because companies may
rely on external service providers.

For digital marketing, the practical question is how
available technologies can support specific business
needs. A company receiving frequent customer inquiries
could consider an automated assistant, while an online
retailer could examine recommendation tools or audience
segmentation. These examples represent possible
applications rather than evidence of their current
prevalence in Serbia. Their value should be assessed
through indicators such as response time, conversion rate,
customer acquisition cost, and customer satisfaction.

The opportunities and limitations identified in this
discussion can be summarized through an indicative
SWOT framework. The framework repres ents an author
interpretation of the selected indicators and literature
rather than a separately validated empirical assessment.

Strengths include widespread Internet use and consumer
participation in digital communication and product
searches. The estab lished online presence of many
enterprises provides a potential starting point for
developing more advanced marketing activities.
Weaknesses include the limited reported adoption of AI
and possible constraints in internal technical capacities.
However, the available indicators do not directly measure
the quality of marketing analytics, data integration, or
employee readiness to use AI tools.

Opportunities include the gradual introduction of
personalized communication, automated support, and
more systemati c analysis of campaign results.
Cooperation with technology providers and educational
institutions could support the development of the
competencies required for these activities. Threats include
dependence on external platforms, competitive pressure,
data security risks, and inappropriate use of personal
information. Ethical concerns associated with profiling
and targeted advertising also require attention, particularly
where consumers have limited understanding of how their
data is processed (Vukmirović et al., 2025).

Overall, the Serbian context provides opportunities for
further development of digital marketing, but the effects
of Big Data and AI cannot be inferred from adoption
indicators alone. A practical approach would connect
technology selection with employee development,
responsible data management, and the measurement of
marketing results.

The development of digital technologies is changing the
ways companies communicate with consumers, collect
market information, and evaluate marketing activities. Big
Data and artificial intelligence have an important role in

this process because they support the analysis of consumer
behavior, the personalization of communication, and the
automation of selected tasks. Their practical value,
however, depends on the ability of companies to connect
technological possibilities with clearly defined marketing
objectives.

The discussion in this paper connects data resources,
analytical capabilities, and marketing decisions. Big Data
provides a potential source of information about consumer
interests and interactions, while AI offers tools for
identifying patterns and supporting decisions. Marketing
metrics help companies assess whether these applications
contribute to desired results. The use of advanced
technology should therefore be accompanied by the
evaluation of customer responses, acquisition costs, and
the longer-term value of customer relationships.

From a theoretical perspective, the discussion supports an
integrated understanding of Big Data, artificial
intelligence, and digital marketing. Big Data provides
information resources, AI supports their analys is, and
marketing metrics connect analytical insights with the
evaluation of marketing activities. This interpretation is
consistent with Huang and Rust’s (2021) framework,
which links AI with marketing research, strategy, and
actions. It also follows Dave nport et al. (2020) in
emphasizing that AI can complement managerial
judgment. More recent research reinforces this
interpretation by identifying infrastructure, skilled
personnel, data access, and adaptive leadership as
conditions shaping the contribution  of generative AI to
marketing capabilities and business transformation
(Kumar et al., 2025).  The theoretical implication is that
the role of AI in digital marketing should be considered
through its integration with marketing processes and
human competenci es, rather than through technology
adoption alone. In the Serbian context, this distinction also
explains why general indicators of AI use provide only a
partial basis for understanding its marketing applications.

The selected indicators for Serbia show widespread use of
digital channels among consumers and an established
online presence among enterprises covered by the survey.
At the same time, the adoption of AI remains more
limited. The comparison with the European Union places
Serbia below the EU avera ge in enterprise AI use,
although above some member states. These findings
suggest room for further development, but they do not
directly establish the extent or effectiveness of AI
application in digital marketing.

For companies in Serbia, a practical a pproach would
involve the gradual introduction of tools that address
specific business needs. Automated customer support,
personalized recommendations, and campaign analysis
represent possible starting points. Their implementation
should be supported by em ployee training, appropriate
data management, and cooperation with external
specialists where necessary. The choice of technology
should reflect the characteristics of the company and its
customers, while expected benefits should be evaluated
against implementation and operating costs.

The application of Big Data and AI also requires attention
to privacy, transparency, and consumer trust. More
detailed knowledge of consumer behavior creates
opportunities for relevant communication, but also
increases resp onsibility for the collection and use of
personal information. Responsible implementation is
therefore an integral part of the development of digital
marketing.

The paper has several limitations. It is based on a narrative
review of selected literature a nd a descriptive analysis of
aggregate statistical data. The enterprise indicators cover
selected activities and enterprises with ten or more
employees, and therefore do not represent all businesses
in Serbia. Furthermore, general AI adoption indicators do
not isolate marketing applications or establish their effects
on business performance. The SWOT framework should
accordingly be understood as an interpretative summary
rather than an independently validated assessment.

Future research could examine the specific AI tools used
in marketing by Serbian enterprises, the barriers to their
adoption, and differences across sectors and company
sizes. Studies connecting these applications with
campaign results and consumer responses would provide
a stronger basis for assessing their contribution. The
further development of digital marketing therefore
requires a connection between technology, employee
competencies, responsible data practices, and measurable
business objectives.
