ABSTRACT
This research develops a nonlinear mediation model to investigate the relationship between real earnings
management, agency costs, and ESG performance. This analysis is based on panel data from 107 non -financial
companies listed on the Egyptian Exchange from 2013 to 2021. The results show a U -shaped relationship
between real earnings management and ESG performance, with sustainability performance declining at low
and moderate levels of manipulation and recovering only beyond a high threshold located in the upper tail of
the distribution. Real earnings management also exhibits a U -shaped effect on agency costs, initially reducing
them by stabilizing reported performance. Agency costs display a corresponding U-shaped association with ESG
performance and partially mediate the relationship between manipulation and sustainability outcomes,
although the direct pathway remains dominant.  This study reveals the nonlinear interdependencies between
reporting behavior, governance efficiency, and sustainability engagement, providing theoretical insights and
practical implications for ESG regulation and corporate governance in emerging markets.
I. INTRODUCTION
Corporate earnings quality and sustainability performance have become central pillars of modern capital
market architecture, as investors, regulators, and development finance institutions increasingly demand
transparency in both financial reporting and non -financial disclosure (Chowdhury & Eliwa, 2021; Muhmad et
al., 2021) . In emerging markets, where institutional frameworks are evolving and information asymmetry
remains pronounced, the intersection of managerial discretion, governance deficiencies, and sustainability
outcomes shapes the allocative efficiency of capital and the credibility of listed firms (El-Bassiouny et al., 2018).
Egypt, as the largest economy in the MENA region by population and a strategic recipient of sustainable
development finance, presents a distinctive laboratory for examining these relationships: the Egyptian
Exchange (EGX) has witnessed progressive ESG integration through the S&P/EGX ESG Index and mandatory
FRA Decree No. 107 and 108 of 2021, yet corporate governance mechanisms remain less formalized than in
developed markets, permitting managerial rent extraction under conditions of weak oversight (El-Deeb &
Ezzat, 2026; Salah, 2026) . Real earnings management (REM) —the distortion of reported earnings through
deviations from normal operating activities such as abnormal production, aggressive sales discounts, or cuts to
discretionary expenditures —consumes firm resources and amplifies the separation between ownership and
control (Dichev et al., 2013; Gunny, 2010; Roychowdhury, 2006) . While prior research documents that strong
ESG performance constrains accrual -based earnings manipulation (Aqabna et al., 2023) , the reverse causal
pathway—whereby REM erodes the governance foundations necessary for substantive sustainability
investment—remains under -theorized, particularly in emerging markets where ESG disclosure is often
symbolic rather than substantive. Agency theory posits that the divergence of interests between principals and
agents generates agency costs manifested as asset underutilization and operational inefficiency (Jensen &
Meckling, 2019) . In the Egyptian context, where ownership concentration is high and minority shareholder
protection is still developing, REM exacerbates these agency costs by obscuring true economic performance and
distorting investment efficiency (Abdou et al., 2021).
Furthermore, the greater importance of environmental, social, and governance practices  as a basis for assessing
business legitimacy and sustainable development potential adds another dimension of precision. Under
growing institutional requirements, companies modify their practices via environmental enhancements, social
responsibility initiatives, and governance reforms; , however, they can also respond by broadening disclosures

This research highlights three gaps in existing literature. First, the relationship between earnings management
and sustainability has mainly been explored within developed markets and using accrual -based manipulation
(Choi & Pae, 2011; Kim et al., 2012; Velte, 2019) , with few findings regarding the impact of real activities
manipulation on sustainability results in emerging markets, where disclosure is largely motivated by
legitimacy. Secondly, while agency costs have been recognized as a mechanism by which reporting behavior
affects corporate results (Muñoz Mendoza et al., 2021) , a mediation  role of specific agency -cost dimensions in
the manipulation -sustainability relationship has not been empirically examined using formal mediation
analyses. Third, the potential for this relationship to be non -linear is strongly indicated by the conflicting
indicators observed in previous research  (García‐Sánchez et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2017) ,
although the level at which the impact reversed has not been determined. I address  such gaps by integrating
mediation via disaggregated agency -cost indicators, non -linear modelling, and threshold detection within a
singular empirical framework.
I contribute to the literature in four ways. First, existing earnings management –sustainability studies rely
heavily on single -dimensional manipulation proxies or on accrual -based measures alone. We construct two
composite real manipulation indices that integrate abnormal production costs, abnormal cash flow from
operations, and abnormal discretionary expenditure, thereby capturing distinct manipulation channels within a
common metric. Second, while previous literature on reporting quality and sustainability focuses heavily on
developed markets, I offer systematic micro -level evidence for 107 Egyptian listed non -financial firms across
fifteen industries over 2013 –2023, thereby filling an important empirical gap. Third, I dissect agency costs into
expenditure-side and cash -flow-side sub -dimensions and formally test their mediating roles using Sobel,
Aroian, and Goodman procedures. I show that the two dimensions operate through heterogeneous channels of
markedly different magnitudes that are masked in single -proxy specifications. Fourth, we present evidence of a
non-monotonic relationship between real manipulation and both sustainability performance and agency costs
and identify the critical thresholds at which each effect changes sign. This result has direct implications for the
design of differentiated disclosure and assurance requirements across firms with different reporting profiles.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews prior literature and the theoretical
foundations and develops the research hypotheses. Section 3 describes the research design, including sample
construction, variable measurement, and empirical models. Section 4 presents and discusses empirical results.
Section 5 concludes.
II. LITERATURE REVIEW AND HYPOTHESIS DEVELOPMENT
Real Earnings Management, Agency costs, and ESG Performance in the Egyptian Context
The consequences of the separation of ownership and control in firms are mainly reflected in information
asymmetry and the possibility of managers harming shareholders’ interests. Managers aim to maximize their
benefits and compensation and avoid various risks, while shareholders aim to achieve greater wealth at lower
costs. When managers and shareholders follow inconsistent goals and interests, managers may manipulate or
adjust the earnings, adopting earnings management practices to maximize their own benefits, thereby harming
shareholders’ interests.
Earnings management, which involves the manipulation of reported accounting results to deceive stakeholders
or affect contractual outcomes, has been demonstrated to have an adverse effect on earnings quality and firm
value (Dechow et al., 1996; Ghosh & Olsen, 2009) . Compared to accrual earnings management (AEM), real
earnings management (REM) imposes higher long -term costs on firms' shareholders because it changes actual
transactions and occurs all year long, making it harder to detect and less costly than AEM (Ewert &
Wagenhofer, 2005; Zang, 2012) . According to Cai et al. (2020), managers in religious regions have incentives to
engage in REM instead of AEM due to a risk -aversion perspective, rather than an ethical one. Managers employ
REM to reduce the risk of job insecurity, enhance the perceived firm value, and improve management
credibility. Therefore, managers may use REM as a low-risk option for earnings management.

Research has shown that managers in religious areas have incentives to manipulate real activities instead of
accrual earnings management due to a risk aversion perspective rather than an ethical one (Cai et al., 2020) .
Thus, managers may manipulate real earnings as a low -risk option for earnings management. Prior research
suggests that ESG sustainability disclosures may serve as a tool to demonstrate compliance with society's
religious standards and provide stakeholders with essential information to analyze the firm's risk profile
(Terzani & Turzo, 2021) . According to the social norm theory, ESG performance enhances societal goals and
reduces reputational risk. Thus, ESG performance provides firms with a mechanism to satisfy stakeholders'
demands and manage risk. Managers can use non -financial disclosure to advance their careers or other
personal goals, improve their reputation, and reduce information asymmetry (Barnea & Rubin, 2010; Cui et al.,
2018; McWilliams et al., 2006).
Although the idea that non -financial disclosure is linked to earnings management is not new. Conflicting
findings have been found in prior research on the association between non -financial disclosure and earnings
management. Two opposing viewpoints have been employed to illustrate the relationship between voluntary
disclosure and earnings management: complementary and substitutive relationships (Francis et al., 2008) . The
substitutive relationship argues that firms that use more earnings management are making more voluntary
disclosures because there is an information asymmetry between managers and shareholders, which motivates
EM practices (Jo & Kim, 2007) . On the other hand, according to the complementary relationship, firms
implementing CSR practices tend to manipulate their earnings less, supporting the view that CSR activities are
driven by managers' desires to be honest and ethical in their decision -making (Choi & Ryu, 2021; Choi & Pae,
2011; Kim et al., 2012; Velte, 2019).
Looking at the REM technique, some studies investigated whether a greater commitment to CSR activities is
associated with a lower level of REM. For example, Kim et al. (2012)  found evidence supporting the ethical
perspective that socially responsible companies might be less engaged in REM. However, Liu et al. (2017) ,
García‐Sánchez et al. (2020) , and Velte (2019)  found no significant association between CSR disclosure and
REM. Moreover, Kim et al. (2019) suggested that firms in institutionally developed regions and those controlled
by the state are more likely to engage more in REM while participating in CSR. 1 As a result, it is worth
investigating the effect of real earnings manipulation on ESG sustainability disclosure in Egypt. Accordingly, our
hypothesis as a test of social norm theory is as follows:
H1. Firms implementing more real earnings management will have a U -shaped effect on ESG performance in
long run in Egyptian listed firms.
The relationship between earnings management and agency costs is central to corporate governance. Existing
studies yield diverse conclusions regarding the differentiated effects of AEM versus REM.
Muñoz Mendoza et al. (2021) , using panel data on 478 on -financial firms in the Latin American Integrated
Market (MILA) from 2000 –2016 and GMM estimation, find that AEM dynamically moderates agency costs:
upward (income -increasing) discretionary accruals can window -dress performance and conceal true agency
conflicts, reducing shareholders’ monitoring demand and lowering agency costs in the short run; conversely,
downward (income-decreasing) discretionary accruals function as a tool for managerial wealth expropriation,
significantly raising agency costs. This study provides key evidence that earnings management does not merely
aggravate agency problems; its effect on agency costs depends on the direction and motive of manipulation,
illuminating a non-monotonic relationship between the two.

In the relationship between REM and ESG performance among Egyptian firms, agency costs play a mediating
role through a short -term pathway chain”. This mediation mechanism is highly integrated into Egypt's family -
firm-dominated institutional framework. To maintain the stability of family control and ensure short -term
operational safety, managers in Egyptian family enterprises typically implement moderate real earnings
management (REM) strategies, such as lowering discretionary expenditures and maximizing manufacturing
capacity to stabilize earnings, thereby signaling business financial stability to the market. This signal lowers the
need for shareholders to monitoring management  and temporarily decreases minority shareholders' worries
of family power , which lowers agency costs in the short term (Achleitner et al., 2014; Muñoz Mendoza et al.,
2021).
Contrary to previous studies findings, this short-term decline in agency costs will not improve ESG performance
in Egypt; in fact, because of how the market works, it makes ESG performance decline. Egypt's ESG regulation is
mostly about following the rules (mandatory disclosure didn't start until 2022), and there are no limits on
important investment. Family businesses, on the other hand, focus on short -term cash flow and leaving the
business down to the next generation. As a result, ESG investments (like on environmental equipment and
employee training) is seen as non -essential because it has high short -term costs and delayed long -term
benefits. When agency costs decrease and resources become available for allocation,  managers in Egyptian
firms focus on short-term profit activities like capacity growth and family compensation instead of ESG (Assad
et al., 2023; Ed‐Dafali et al., 2025) . At the same time, lower agency costs mean less monitoring, which makes it
easier to cut ESG investments, which leads to decreased ESG performance.
H3. Agency costs mediate the relationship between REM and ESG performance in Egyptian listed firms.

3.2.2 ESG performance
In this study, the researcher used annual data on ESG ratings from independent sources for the period between
2013 and 2023 to measure the level of ESG sustainability performance. These ratings aim to offer investors
dependable reference points for managing their ESG investment portfolios and encourage standards of
transparency, disclosure, and reporting. The index measures the performance of the Egyptian firms based on
their environmental, social, and corporate governance scores. The ESG index was extracted from qualitative,
quantitative, and narrative information to extract quantitative, qualitative, and composite scores. The
qualitative score is calculated for each firm based on information derived from websites, news stories, and CSR
filings which are used to evaluate the actual performance of the firm in the range from 5 to 1. The quantitative
score is derived from three factors: the transparency and disclosure of environmental practices, social
practices, and corporate governance. Finally, a composed score is calculated by combining quantitative and
quantitative scores 2. The Egyptian ESG data rates have been successfully utilized in previous CSR research
conducted in Egypt (Abdelfattah & Aboud, 2020; Mohmed et al., 2020).
3.2.3 Agency costs
In academic research, there are numerous indicators for measuring agency costs, but no unified measurement
exists.  Currently, the main proxies used to measure agency costs include the administrative expense ratio,
asset turnover ratio, and selling, general, and administrative expenses (SG&A) expense -to-revenue ratio. In this
study, the SG&A expense ratio and free cash flow ratio are used as the proxy’s variable for agency costs
(Kusumadewi & Wardhani, 2020; Muñoz Mendoza et al., 2021).
3.2.4 Control variables
To more accurately capture firms’ financial characteristics and to mitigate the interference of omitted variable
bias on the empirical results, this study introduces a series of control variables. The selection and measurement
of these variables largely draw on previous studies. Specifically, the following variables are addressed: firm size
(FS), measured as the natural logarithm of total assets, which reflects the potential influence of resource
endowment and market presence; return on assets (ROA), calculated as the ratio of operating income to total
assets, which represents profitability; leverage (LEV), expressed as the ratio of total debt to total assets, which
captures the effect of financial structure; asset growth (AG), measured by the annual change in total assets
scaled by lagged total assets, which reflects growth capacity; loss (LOSS), a dummy variable equal to one if net
profit is negative and zero otherwise, to control for financial distress; and free float (FF), measured as the
proportion of freely tradable shares to total shares, which indicates ownership dispersion. In addition, industry
and year dummy variables are included to control industry and time effects.

4.3 Test of hypotheses results
Tab. 5 reports the impact of real earnings management (REM) on ESG performance among Egyptian firms using
panel-corrected standard errors (PCSE). The findings provide strong evidence of a nonlinear (U -shaped)
relationship between REM and ESG performance. Specifically, the coefficient of real earnings management
based on production manipulation (REMP) is negative and significant (β = –0.496, z = –5.334, p < 0.01),
indicating that at low to moderate levels, earnings manipulation reduces ESG performance. However, the
squared term (REMP²) is positive and significant (β = 0.276, z = 3.066, p < 0.01), supporting the existence of a
U-shaped association.  The inflection point (0.92) suggests that once firms exceed this threshold of REM, the
relationship reverses, and further manipulation is associated with improvements in ESG scores.
Similarly, when REM is measured through cash flow manipulation (REMC), the coefficient is again negative and
significant (β = –0.543, z = –5.172, p < 0.01), while the squared term (REMC²) is positive and significant (β =
0.355, z = 2.403, p < 0.05). The estimated turning point (0.77) confirms the U -shaped relationship. These results
suggest that while moderate REM harms ESG performance, high levels of REM may incentivize firms to
strengthen ESG practices as a legitimacy-enhancing mechanism.
Regarding control variables, firm size (FS) exerts a consistently positive and significant effect across
specifications (β = 0.171, z = 13.322, p < 0.01), highlighting that larger firms are more capable of engaging in
ESG practices. Profitability (ROA) is positive but insignificant, while leverage (LEV) shows a weak positive effect
in some models (β = 0.074, z = 1.952, p < 0.1), suggesting that moderately leveraged firms may enhance ESG
efforts to mitigate creditor concerns.
Interestingly, LOSS is positive and significant (β = 0.070, z = 2.846, p < 0.01), indicating that loss -making firms
disclose more ESG information, possibly to reduce negative market perceptions. Asset growth (AG) is positive
but statistically insignificant, implying that expansion activities do not systematically influence ESG outcomes.
By contrast, ownership dispersion (FF) becomes marginally positive in later models (β = 0.069, z = 1.807, p <
0.1), suggesting that when shares are widely held by small shareholders, firms may feel greater pressure to
align with ESG practices due to dispersed monitoring. Finally, audit size (AUSZ) consistently exerts a strong
positive effect (β = 0.070, z = 4.171, p < 0.01), emphasizing the role of high -quality auditors in strengthening
ESG credibility.

Tab. 6 offers strong support for agency theory by confirming a nonlinear association between real earnings
management and agency costs. Results show that the negative coefficients of REMP (B = –0.078, p < 0.05; B = –
0.116, p < 0.01) and REMC (B = –0.121, p < 0.01; B = –0.136, p < 0.01; B = –0.223, p < 0.01; B = –0.252, p < 0.01)

pressure to manipulate outcomes. These results demonstrate that both managerial discretion in earnings
management and ownership dispersion via free float play central roles in shaping agency costs, with significant
implications for governance in emerging markets. Fig. 3  highlights a U -shaped relationship between real
earning management and agency costs.

The mediation analysis in Tab. 7 reveals important insights into the role of agency costs in shaping the nexus
between real earnings management and ESG engagement. Panel A  shows that agency costs (AGS) partially
mediate the relationship between real earnings management (REMP) and ESG. The indirect effect ( β = –0.021, p
≈ 0.085) indicates that opportunistic manipulation of sales to manage earnings heightens agency costs, which in
turn undermines ESG performance. However, the direct effect ( β = –0.348, p < 0.001) remains the dominant
pathway, with only 5.7% of the total effect mediated. This suggests complementary partial mediation, where
sales-related agency costs reinforce but do not replace the direct detrimental impact of REMP on ESG. Panel B
extends this perspective by examining agency costs (AGF) as a mediator of the relationship between real
earnings management (REMC) and ESG. Here, the indirect effect ( β = –0.034, p = 0.043) is statistically

costs, which subsequently translates into weaker ESG performance. Nevertheless, the direct effect (β = –0.312, p
< 0.001) remains highly significant, again confirming a complementary partial mediation pattern.
The findings suggest that earnings management damages ESG both directly and indirectly by amplifying agency
costs. While agency costs based on sales play a modest mediating role, agency costs derived from cash flows
exert a stronger influence, highlighting that managers’ cash-based manipulations are particularly detrimental to
sustainable practices. This evidence aligns with agency theory, which emphasizes the self -serving behavior of
managers, and with stakeholder theory, which underscores how heightened agency costs crowd out resources
and incentives for ESG engagement.

V. SUMMARY
This study investigates the relationship between real earnings management, agency costs, and ESG
performance among non -financial firms listed on the Egyptian Exchange. The findings reveal that REM
significantly increases agency costs by distorting operational efficiency and amplifying information asymmetry,
which in turn exerts a U -shaped effect on ESG performance. ESG scores decline as agency costs rise from low to
moderate levels, consistent with managerial entrenchment and sustainability underinvestment; however, at
very high agency cost levels, firms engage in symbolic ESG activities that produce a legitimacy -seeking rebound,
causing ESG scores to recover partially. The bootstrapped mediation analysis confirms that the indirect effect of
REM on ESG performance through agency costs is significantly negative at moderate agency cost levels but
weakens and becomes directionally positive at extreme high -agency-cost regimes. The paper contributes to the
emerging market corporate governance literature in three ways. First, it extends the REM framework to
sustainability outcomes, demonstrating that operational earnings manipulation has consequences beyond
financial reporting quality that propagate through agency inefficiency to distort ESG investment. Second, it
enriches the ESG literature by providing emerging market evidence from Egypt, where regulatory mandates
(FRA Decrees 107 and 108 of 2021) are reshaping disclosure practices but where governance quality remains

explore the mechanisms underlying the legitimacy -seeking rebound in ESG at high agency cost levels, enriching
