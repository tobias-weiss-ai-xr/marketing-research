ABSTRACT
There is an increasing tendency in marketing practices of using artificial
intelligence (AI) for message personalization while neuromarketing can provide
data on consumer behavior that are impossible to obtain from self -reports.
Despite similarities, the f ields have been developing separately. This paper
presents the Neuro -Algorithmic Consumer Insights (NACI) model where AI -
based personalization leads to physiological responses (arousal, motivation for
an approach, attention), which influence the results fr om self -reports of
emotional response to purchase execution. The results can be further used as
predictors of purchase behavior. For the verification of the proposed model, an
experiment was conducted with 600 subjects (289 personalized messages and
311 ge neric). The analysis included Welch tests with Holm correction,
hierarchical regression, bootstrapped mediation analysis, and multiple logistic
regression. It was demonstrated that personalization leads to arousal (d = 0.45),
greater left frontal alpha asy mmetry (d = 0.431), attention (d = 0.24),
engagement (d = 0.40), and intention to purchase (d = 0.37). It was found that
physiological variables can account for 24.1% of variability in intention to
purchase over demographic and personalization variables (F (4, 590) = 52.11, p
< .001). It is estimated that attention and engagement accounted for 59 percent
of the overall effect. Incorporation of physiological factors resulted in the
increase in cross -validated AUC for purchase from .61 to .69 according to
logistic regression. The results indicate that the designed pipeline reveals
previously confirmed effects but does not yield novel insights. The article also
reviews limitations of the research and deals with ethical issues raised by it.

1. Introduction

The main purpose of consumer research is based on what customers say in their responses to interviews and surveys. Most of the time,
consumers make their decisions based on randomness (Kahneman, 2011). That is why neuromarketing has developed a set of methods
of neuromarketing (fMRI, EEG, eye movement, measuring electrical activity on the skin, etc.) to solve marketing problems (Fug ate,
2007; Lee et al., 2007). Studies conducted using brain scans reveal that brain reactions measured on small samples of participants can
predict the behavior of the whole population (Berns & Moore, 2012; Boksem & Smidts, 2015; Falk et al., 2012).
Meanwhile, artificial intelligence has become the most important tool for personalized marketing. Such systems as machine lea rning
personalize consumers' marketing messages and offers (Davenport et al., 2020; Huang & Rust, 2021; Kumar et al., 2019). Moreover ,
using digital footprints provides information about consumers’ preferences (Kosinski et al., 2013; Matz et al., 2017). Behavi oral data
can show what consumers did but providing the reason for the success of a particular marketing technique is difficult.  Critiques of
neuromarketing center on issues such as limited sample sizes, limitations of laboratory conditions, and the inability to conduct large-
scale tests (Ariely & Berns, 2010; Plassmann et al., 2015). AI’s advantages (size and real-time optimization) match neuromarketing’s
disadvantages, while neuromarketing’s advantage (the ability to measure implicit response) coincides with the shortcoming of AI
personalization. Thus, their complementary nature becomes a reason for an integrated approach.

1.1 Gap and Contribution
Earlier research has looked into the results of the personalization of AI or the physiological repercussions of advertising, but there has
not been much research into the connection between these two aspects or testing if physiological signals can strengthen predi ction
accuracy of the algorithms being used. This paper presents (a) the NACI,” which is the conceptual model; (b) five hypotheses that can
be tested; (c) a two -step analysis plan involving explanatory (regression and mediation) and predictive (cross -validated machine
learning) methods; and (d) an ethical and a research plan.

1.2 Questions of research
• RQ1. Will the AI-driven personalization lead to greater physiological and emotional responses compared to generic
promotional messages?
• RQ2. Will these responses serve as mediators for the impact of personalization on purchase intentions?
• RQ3. Are the physiological characteristics a better predictor of the purchase intentions than behavioral and demographic
indicators?

2. Literature Review and Hypotheses
2.1 AI and Customized Marketing

In marketing, one can encounter various applications of AI that fall into three different categories: mechanical processes (automation),
cognitive functions (analytics and forecasting), and emotional responses (recognizing and responding to feelings) (Huang & Ru st,
2021). Personalization solutions choose information based on the user’s characteristics by using techniques like collaborative filtering
and tree ensembles (Breiman, 2001; Friedman, 2001). Studies show that personalization yields better rates of engagement and
conversion if the messages are in accordance with psychological profiles (Matz et al., 2017) and if consumers see them as meaningful
rather than intrusive (Aguirre et al., 2015; Kumar et al., 2019). At the same time, behavioral traces are not sufficient as t hey provide
only outcome data and do not reflect inner experiences responsible for clicks.
2.2 Neuromarketing and physiological measures
Three categories of measures are informative. Electrodermal activity (EDA) provides a measure of sympathetic arousal (Boucsei n,
2012). Frontal alpha asymmetry (FAA) obtain ed from EEG has gained acceptance as a measure of the approach -versus-withdrawal
motivation, although some debate its interpretation (Harmon-Jones et al., 2010). Eye movement recording provides a measure of visual
attention. Neural activity in the brain re gions related to reward and valuation systems is linked to acquisition and later success in the
market (Falk et al., 2012; Knutson et al., 2007). Neuro -physiological measures can provide additional insights relative to traditional
measures of advertising ( Venkatraman et al., 2015). Brain data provide evidence for effects that may be missed by self -reports
(Plassmann et al., 2012).
2.3 The Neuro-Algorithmic Loop
The operation of a neuro -algorithmic system involves a loop: (1) an algorithm selects a specific stimulus; (2) sensors record arousal,
attention, and approach; (3) these parameters determine emotional engagement; (4) engagement predicts intention and behavior;  and
(5) predictions alter the algorithm. Only a limited number of studies have examined the process of emotional engagement separately.

3. Research Methodology
3.1 Design

This study employs a between -subjects approach, where subjects are assigned randomly to conditions featuring either an AI -
personalized advertisement (containing content, images, or offers determined by means of a recommendation model based on subjects’
profiles) or a generic advertisement of the same type. In this setup we thus get 289 observations in the personalized condition and 311
observations in the generic condition (total N = 600). With these number of subjects, the power to detect d = 0.25 at α = .05  (two-
tailed) is .86, while the minimum detectable effect with 80% power is at d = 0.23; power for d = 0.20 is only .69.

3.4 Analytical Plan
H1: There will be Welch t -tests with Cohen’s d, as well as Holm (1 979) corrections for the five outcomes, and a two -proportion test
for purchase.
H2: There will be OLS regression of attention and engagement on arousal, approach, and condition.
H3: A hierarchical OLS regression will be performed on intention after controlling during Step 1, then adding personalization in Step
2, and finally introducing the four physiological/affective variables in Step 3, finally performing a parallel mediation test  with 2000-
resample percentile bootstrap intervals (Hayes, 2022).
H4: There will be logistic regression of purchase on intention while controlling for condition and covariates in the analysis.
H5: There will be logistic regression studies using random forest (Breiman, 2001) with gradient boosting (Friedman, 2001) met hods
for predicting purchase; the method will perform 10 times of stratified 5 -fold cross -validation; 5 features will be used in
behavioral/demographic studies, and augmented studies will use 9 features; AUC will be used in these studies.
Assumption checking for th is analysis will include variance inflation factors, residuals from Breusch –Pagan and Shapiro–Wilk tests
during testing.
Software: The software to be used in this study will include Python with NumPy, pandas, SciPy, statsmodels and scikit -learn
(Pedregosa et al., 2011).

5. Discussion and Conclusions
What the simulation shows. The application of the analytical procedure allows obtaining positive results even when the sample size is
not big (in our case N=600). Moreover, the procedure yields meaningful effect and estimation results. Furthermore, it has sho wn
several practical conclusions: 1) the attention effect is right at the edge of detection; 2) the mediation effect is extremely small; 3) the
gain from predictive ability obtained in the case of single train -test split is overstated in comparison to the one obtained when the
model is verified by repeated cross-validation.
What the simulation does not show. Despite the fact that the simulation yielded some promising results, it does not allow cla iming
that AI-generated personalization has impact on physiology of people in reality, do es not prove that physiological indexes work as a
means of mediation and does not show that the use of physiological data allows getting any real predictive ability. Each of t he
“positive” results is predetermined and does not allow achieving any reliable data.
Theoretical and managerial importance of validation of the results. The NACI framework provides the theoretical model for
understanding why personalization works, connects “feeling AI” (Huang & Rust, 2021) with measurable indicators and suggests t hat
When it comes to training and calibration, it should be noted that sensors are seldom present in real campaigns. Any project should
regard the trade-offs of privacy and trust (Aguirre et al., 2015).

5.1 Limitations
1. Data was simulated and not obtained empirically; 2. Effect sizes were assumed, possibly in optimistic terms compared to noisy real
signals; 3. Linear generation model is fit for linear estimators; 4. There is one stimulus and one exposure; 5. Nothing affec ted the
results, no missing data o r individual differences; 6. Mediation and prediction results were based on the assumption of the causal
framework; 7. Consensus not reached about the meaning of FAA (Harmon -Jones et al., 2010).
6. Future Research Directions
1. Validation with actual data. A protocol has been established for experimentation with the use of EEG, EDA, and eye tracking
involving a set of experiments that are pre -registered using a conservative estimate of effect sizes (e.g., d = 0.20 which requires a
sample of 400 subjects). It would involve a mixed-model design utilizing various stimuli, as well as cross-validation with an untouched
hold-out set.
2. Simulation protocols. Designing the study to account for errors in measurement, noise, nonlinearity, and missing data.
3. Forms of scalable approximators. Exploring whether or not the information from web cameras and portable devices such as
EDA monitors and heart rate monitors or voice activity can be treated as sources of signals obtained in the laboratory under standard
controlled conditions.
4. Closed-loop Adaptation. Bandit and reinforcement learning systems coupled with physiological feedback instead of
optimizing pure behavior.
5. Heterogeneity and fairness. Performance distinction based on age, sex, culture, and neurodiv ersity, with potential bias.
6. Personalization paradox. Physiological indicators of reactance and privacy concern.
