ABSTRACT
The growing availability of customer and market data, together with advances in data analytics and artificial
intelligence (AI), is transforming how manufacturing firms organize marketing. Drawing on a management
innovation perspective, this study develops a process theory explaining how manufacturing firms progressively
become data-centric through the continuous innovation of marketing practices. We employ an embedded lon-
gitudinal case study of a European manufacturing SME, Industrial Corp., and examine 16 data-centric marketing
practices as embedded units of analysis. Our findings show that data-centric marketing evolves through three
cumulative phases: data-enabled marketing practices, analytics-driven marketing practices, and integrated
customer intelligence practices. A distinct innovation process characterizes each phase: adopt–implement–refine,
explore–experiment–scale, and reframe–integrate–institutionalize—and by evolving configurations of internal
and external change agents. As complementary bundles of marketing practices accumulate and become insti-
tutionalized, they progressively strengthen operational, dynamic, and transformational marketing capabilities,
enabling increasingly sophisticated operational, strategic, and enterprise-wide marketing decisions. By inte-
grating the management innovation, marketing practice, and marketing capability literatures, this study explains
how manufacturing firms transform customer and market data into sustained marketing capabilities through the
continuous innovation of marketing practices. The findings also provide managers with a process model for
systematically developing data-centric marketing practices and capabilities.
1. Introduction
The increasing availability of customer and market data, together
with advances in data analytics, machine learning, and artificial intel-
ligence (AI), is fundamentally changing how manufacturing firms un-
derstand markets, generate customer insights, and engage with
customers. Rather than relying on periodic market analyses or managers'
intuition, industrial firms increasingly exploit customer, market, and
operational data to sense market changes, anticipate customer needs,
identify business opportunities, and support marketing decision-making
(Huang & Rust, 2021). Marketing analytics capabilities have conse-
quently become an important source of competitive advantage by
strengthening firms' abilities to sense, seize, and reconfigure market
opportunities (Hossain et al., 2022). Likewise, AI increasingly augments
these capabilities by enabling firms to redesign customer-facing
processes and develop dynamic marketing capabilities (Mikalef et al.,
2021).
These developments are accelerating the transition from experience-
based marketing toward data-centric marketing. Rather than merely
implementing digital technologies or analytics platforms, firms
increasingly redesign how marketing activities are organized, coordi-
nated, and executed through new managerial routines and decision
processes that systematically collect, integrate, analyze, and apply
customer and market information. Such changes represent a form of
management innovation because they fundamentally alter how mar-
keting is managed rather than simply introducing new technologies
(Birkinshaw et al., 2008). As firms progressively develop these practices,
marketing evolves from a function supporting sales toward a strategic
capability that generates, coordinates, and disseminates customer in-
telligence across organizational functions (Day, 2011). Recent research

Despite the growing importance of data-centric marketing, relatively
little is known about how these marketing practices emerge, evolve, and
become institutionalized over time. Existing research has established the
strategic importance of marketing capabilities (Morgan, 2012; Vorhies
& Morgan, 2005) and increasingly explains how analytics and AI
improve customer understanding, market sensing, and marketing
decision-making (Hossain et al., 2022; Itani et al., 2026). However,
these studies primarily explain what digital technologies enable firms to
achieve rather than how organizations progressively develop the mar-
keting practices through which these technologies create value. Like-
wise, although recent studies emphasize digital marketing capabilities
(Herhausen et al., 2020), marketing analytics capabilities (Hossain
et al., 2022), and data-centric marketing (Morgan & Feng, 2024), they
provide limited insight into the organizational innovation processes
through which complementary marketing practices evolve into
increasingly sophisticated marketing capabilities. Recent research
further suggests that business value depends on firms' ability to combine
technological resources with organizational and managerial capabilities
rather than treating technology as an isolated resource (Eriksson &
Heikkil¨a, 2023). Consequently, we still know relatively little about
how manufacturing firms evolve from isolated data applications toward
enterprise-wide data-centric marketing.
To address this gap, we adopt management innovation as our theo-
retical lens. Management innovation concerns the introduction, imple-
mentation, adaptation, and institutionalization of new managerial
practices, processes, structures, and routines that fundamentally change
how organizations are managed and coordinated (Birkinshaw et al.,
2008; Damanpour, 2014). Rather than viewing data-centric marketing
as the outcome of technology adoption, this perspective explains how
organizations continuously develop and institutionalize new marketing
practices that transform marketing decision-making while simulta-
neously strengthening marketing capabilities. It further emphasizes that
successful management innovation depends on cumulative experimen-
tation, contextual adaptation, organizational learning, and the com-
plementary roles of internal and external change agents (Mol &
Birkinshaw, 2014; Peeters et al., 2014).
We investigate these processes through an embedded longitudinal
case study of Industrial Corp., a European manufacturing SME that
transformed its marketing activities between 2010 and 2025. We
examine 16 data-centric marketing practices as embedded units of
analysis, including both successful and less successful initiatives. This
design enables us to explain how manufacturing firms progressively
become data-centric while avoiding success bias by examining practices
that were discontinued alongside those that became institutionalized.
Our findings show that data-centric marketing develops through three
cumulative phases: data-enabled marketing practices, analytics-driven
marketing practices, and integrated customer intelligence practices.
Across these phases, firms follow distinct innovation processes and
employ different internal–external change-agent configurations. As
complementary bundles of marketing practices accumulate, they pro-
gressively develop operational, dynamic, and transformational market-
ing capabilities, enabling increasingly sophisticated operational,
strategic, and enterprise-wide marketing decisions.
This study contributes to the literature on data-centric marketing,
marketing capabilities, and management innovation in four ways. First,
we develop a process theory of data-centric marketing by identifying
three cumulative phases through which manufacturing firms progres-
sively become data-centric. Second, we demonstrate that each phase
follows a distinct innovation process. Specifically, data-enabled mar-
keting practices evolve through an adopt–implement–refine process,
analytics-driven marketing practices through an explor-
e–experiment–scale process, and integrated customer intelligence
practices through a reframe–integrate–institutionalize process. These
findings extend research on digital marketing capabilities and AI-
enabled marketing, which has primarily emphasized the technological
foundations of marketing transformation (Herhausen et al., 2020;
Hossain et al., 2022). Third, we explain how internal and external
change-agent configurations evolve across these innovation processes.
Data-enabled marketing practices rely on internal leadership with
external support, analytics-driven marketing practices on external
exploration with internal translation, and integrated customer intelli-
gence practices on internal–external co-creation and institutionaliza-
tion, thereby extending research on change agents in management
innovation (Birkinshaw et al., 2008; Mol & Birkinshaw, 2014) and
complementing the marketing literature on cross-functional collabora-
tion and capability development (Day, 2011; Morgan & Feng, 2024).
Finally, we demonstrate how complementary bundles of data-centric
marketing practices progressively develop operational, dynamic, and
transformational marketing capabilities, enabling increasingly sophis-
ticated operational, strategic, and enterprise-wide marketing decisions
that improve marketing efficiency, marketing effectiveness, and ulti-
mately marketing transformation (Barrales-Molina et al., 2014; Morgan,
2012; Shanka, 2026).
2. Theoretical background
2.1. From experience-based to data-centric marketing
Manufacturing firms have traditionally relied on experience-based
marketing, in which marketing decisions are primarily guided by
managerial judgment, tacit knowledge, customer relationships, and
accumulated market experience rather than by systematic analysis of
customer and market data (Day, 1994). This approach has been partic-
ularly important in industrial markets, where close customer relation-
ships and market orientation have historically provided the foundation
for superior customer value (Narver & Slater, 1990). Increasingly,
however, manufacturing firms are transforming marketing into a data-
centric function that systematically generates, integrates, and lever-
ages customer and market data to strengthen customer understanding
and value creation (Wedel & Kannan, 2016). Digitalization, connected
products, and advances in analytics and artificial intelligence (AI) are
fundamentally changing how industrial firms understand markets and
compete (Huang & Rust, 2021). Rather than merely supporting sales and
customer communication, marketing is increasingly responsible for
sensing customer needs, anticipating market developments, and coor-
dinating customer-facing activities across organizational functions (Day,
2011). Recent industrial marketing research demonstrates that firms
increasingly combine digital channels, marketing analytics, AI,
customer platforms, and product usage data to improve customer un-
derstanding and customer engagement (Herhausen et al., 2020). At the
same time, AI and marketing analytics enable firms to sense market
changes, identify new opportunities, and continuously adapt marketing
activities in competitive manufacturing environments (Hossain et al.,
2022). These developments shift competitive advantage away from ac-
cess to customer data alone toward firms' ability to organize, interpret,
and leverage data through new marketing capabilities (Morgan & Feng,
2024). Recent research further suggests that successful data-driven
innovation depends not only on technological capabilities but also on
marketing capabilities, particularly customer understanding, customer
involvement, and value proposition development (Eriksson & Heikkil¨a,
2023). Consequently, becoming data-centric cannot be achieved simply
by acquiring digital technologies or customer data. Instead, firms must
develop organizational practices that continuously acquire, integrate,
analyze, and apply customer and market information to support mar-
keting decisions and build marketing capabilities (Hossain et al., 2022).
These practices increasingly require marketing to collaborate with sales,
service, product development, and IT, fundamentally changing how
marketing work is organized across organizational functions (Mikalef
et al., 2021). Typical examples of such data-centric marketing

2.2. Data-centric marketing practices and marketing capabilities
Marketing capability research argues that firms create competitive
advantage through organizational capabilities rather than marketing
resources alone (Day, 1994). Marketing capabilities comprise organi-
zational routines that enable firms to acquire market knowledge, coor-
dinate marketing activities, and consistently create superior customer
value (Vorhies & Morgan, 2005). As manufacturing firms become
increasingly data-centric, these capabilities are progressively strength-
ened through new data-centric marketing practices (Morgan & Feng,
2024). Customer analytics, customer segmentation, campaign manage-
ment, pricing analytics, demand forecasting, and marketing dashboards
primarily strengthen operational marketing capabilities by improving
customer information management, market monitoring, campaign
evaluation, and customer prioritization. These capabilities support more
consistent operational marketing decisions and improve marketing ef-
ficiency (Morgan, 2012). As firms increasingly adopt competitive in-
telligence, predictive customer management, AI-supported pricing,
customer journey analytics, and marketing strategy simulations, they
can develop dynamic marketing capabilities. These capabilities enable
managers to sense changing customer needs, integrate customer and
market intelligence, evaluate strategic alternatives, and continuously
adapt marketing decisions (Mitręga, 2020). Hossain et al. (2022) simi-
larly demonstrate that marketing analytics capabilities strengthen firms'
sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring capabilities, thereby improving
competitive advantage in manufacturing industries. At a more advanced
stage, enterprise customer platforms, integrated customer intelligence
environments, AI-assisted collaboration, and enterprise-wide decision--
support systems contribute to transformational marketing capabilities.
Rather than supporting individual marketing activities, these practices
integrate customer intelligence across marketing, sales, service, product
development, and business development, enabling coordinated
enterprise-wide marketing decisions and ultimately marketing trans-
formation (Mikalef et al., 2021). Eriksson and Heikkil¨a (2023) likewise
argue that successful data-driven innovation requires firms to combine
technological capabilities with strong marketing capabilities centered
on customer understanding and value creation. Importantly, these
higher-order marketing capabilities do not emerge from isolated
analytical applications or digital technologies alone. Rather, they
develop through the accumulation, coordination, and institutionaliza-
tion of complementary data-centric marketing practices. Organizational
learning, experimentation, and cross-functional capability renewal,
therefore, become central mechanisms through which firms transform
customer and market data into increasingly sophisticated marketing
capabilities (Morgan & Feng, 2024). While the marketing capability
literature explains the organizational abilities that emerge from these
practices, it provides limited insight into how new data-centric mar-
keting practices are motivated, developed, implemented, and institu-
tionalized over time. To explain these organizational processes, we draw
on the management innovation literature, which conceptualizes the
development of new managerial practices as a process of motivation,
invention, implementation, and theorization (Birkinshaw et al., 2008).
This perspective provides the theoretical foundation for explaining how
manufacturing firms progressively develop data-centric marketing
through the continuous innovation of marketing practices.
2.3. Data-centric marketing practices as management innovations
Management innovation refers to the generation, adoption, and
implementation of management practices, routines, processes, struc-
tures, or techniques that are new to the organization and intended to
further organizational goals (Birkinshaw et al., 2008; Damanpour,
2014). It concerns changes in how managerial work is performed,
including how decisions are made, information is used, activities are
coordinated, responsibilities are allocated, and performance is evalu-
ated (Hamel, 2006; Vaccaro et al., 2012). Importantly, management
innovations need not be novel to the state of the art but may involve
established practices that are adapted to a firm's specific context (Mol &
Birkinshaw, 2009). Unlike technological innovations, management in-
novations reshape the social and organizational systems through which
technologies and knowledge are applied. Consequently, they are often
intangible, difficult to codify, and highly context dependent (Birkinshaw
et al., 2008; Peeters et al., 2014).
Management innovation can be understood at two interconnected
levels. The first comprises management ideas that articulate general
principles for organizing and managing firms, such as Lean Manage-
ment, Customer Orientation, or Market Orientation (Birkinshaw et al.,
2008; Volberda et al., 2014). In this study, data-centricity represents
such an overarching management idea. The second level comprises
concrete management practices that translate these ideas into concrete
routines, processes, structures, and decision-making techniques. Their
novelty lies less in the underlying technology than in how they reshape
managerial work.
Birkinshaw et al. (2008) conceptualize management innovation as a
recursive process comprising four interrelated phases: motivation, in-
vention, implementation, and theorization and labeling. Motivation
arises when organizational actors recognize deficiencies in existing
practices or opportunities created by changing technologies and mar-
kets. Invention involves experimenting with new managerial practices
by combining internal learning with external knowledge. Implementa-
tion embeds these practices into everyday work through iterative
testing, refinement, and adaptation rather than straightforward rollout.
Because management innovations affect organizational routines and
social systems, implementation typically requires experimentation
(Peeters et al., 2014). Finally, theorization and labeling articulate what
the new practice is, how it creates value, and why it should be adopted,
thereby building legitimacy and facilitating broader diffusion.
Although these phases are analytically distinct, management inno-
vation unfolds through recursive rather than linear cycles. Imple-
mentation frequently reveals new challenges that stimulate further
invention, while theorization develops alongside experimentation as
organizations mobilize support and institutionalize emerging practices
(Scarbrough et al., 2015). Subsequent research further shows that
implementation barriers evolve over time and differ across organiza-
tional actors, highlighting the importance of organizational learning and
continuous adaptation (Peeters et al., 2014; Robert et al., 2019).
Altogether, the management innovation lens can help to explain the
process through which data-centric marketing practices are motivated,
invented, implemented, and institutionalized and necessary change-
agent configurations (see Table 1). The marketing capabilities perspec-
tive explains how the repeated coordination of these practices creates
more enduring organizational abilities. Integrating the two perspectives,
therefore, connects practice-level change with capability development
and provides a basis for examining how firms progress from isolated
data applications toward a data-centric marketing.

Our empirical setting is the European manufacturing industry, in
which firms operate in complex and highly competitive business-to-

business markets. This industry is characterized by changing technolo-
gies, customer requirements, and market conditions. To respond to these
developments, manufacturing firms invest in digital technologies to
strengthen their innovation, production, and marketing capabilities.
These investments increase the availability of customer and market data,
enable the use of increasingly sophisticated analytics, and support the
integration of customer intelligence across organizational functions.
Becoming data-centric, therefore, involves more than adopting indi-
vidual technologies. It requires firms to develop and institutionalize new
marketing practices for collecting, analyzing, sharing, and incorporating
data into managerial decision-making.
Against this background, we employed an embedded longitudinal
single-case design in which individual data-centric marketing practices
constituted the embedded units of analysis. This design enabled an in-
depth examination of the organizational context while allowing sys-
tematic comparisons across multiple marketing practices within the
same firm (Yin, 2018). We selected Industrial Corp., a small Swiss high-
precision manufacturer employing between 40 and 80 people during the
study period, as a revelatory case. The company provided longitudinal
access to the gradual development of 16 data-centric marketing prac-
tices between 2010 and 2025. This setting was particularly suitable for
examining how increasing data availability, advances in data analytics,
and greater enterprise-wide data integration progressively changed the
firm's marketing activities.
Table 2 presents Industrial Corp.'s development over the study
period and illustrates how external conditions and internal responses
evolved across three broad periods. During the first period of techno-
logical expansion, from 2010 to 2014, the firm repositioned itself and
invested in advanced manufacturing technologies to meet increasingly
demanding customer and technological requirements. During the sec-
ond period, from 2015 to 2017, currency shocks and intensified inter-
national competition prompted a stronger emphasis on cost
optimization, digitalization, market diversification, and the systematic
collection and use of customer and market data. During the third period,
from 2018 to 2025, the company pursued international expansion,
managed periods of crisis and recovery, strengthened organizational
resilience, and increasingly integrated customer intelligence across its
commercial and operational activities.
Across these periods, we identified 16 data-centric marketing prac-
tices. Eleven were successful and became fully institutionalized, whereas
five remained were less successful, partially implemented, or were
eventually discontinued. This variation was theoretically important
because management innovation rarely unfolds as an uninterrupted
progression toward successful institutionalization (Birkinshaw et al.,
2008; Hamel, 2006). Including both successful and less successful
practices also enabled us to examine not only how data-centric mar-
keting practices became embedded in the organization, but also why
some practices failed to gain sustained acceptance or use. Table 3 de-

3.2. Data collection
We collected longitudinal data on the development of the 16 data-
centric marketing practices through semi-structured interviews, partic-
ipation in workshops, and extensive archival material, following
established recommendations for qualitative case and process research
(Eisenhardt, 1989; Langley, 1999; Yin, 2018). Between 2010 and 2025,
we conducted 33 semi-structured interviews with executives and other
organizational actors closely involved in the firm's marketing, sales,
digitalization, and strategic development activities (see Table 4). The
interviews followed a narrative format and lasted approximately one
hour on average.
The first part of each interview examined Industrial Corp.'s transition
toward data-centric marketing and the development of specific mar-
keting practices over time. Respondents were asked to explain why a
practice was initiated, how the underlying idea emerged, how it was
developed and adapted, and how it became incorporated into organi-
zational activities. Attention was given to how changes in data avail-
ability, analytical sophistication, and the integration of customer
intelligence across functions shaped each practice. The second part
explored the process through which each practice emerged. To facilitate
temporal reconstruction, respondents were asked to provide concrete
examples of important events, decisions, turning points, implementation
difficulties, adaptations, and outcomes.
The interviews also examined the roles of internal and external
change agents. Respondents described how senior managers, marketing
and sales employees, operational personnel, consultants, technology
providers, customers, and other external actors contributed to the
development of individual practices. We deliberately sought accounts of
both successful and less successful initiatives to avoid reconstructing the
firm's development as a linear progression toward greater data
centricity.
Questions also addressed differences among the 16 practices,
including their novelty, organizational reach, data requirements,
analytical sophistication, technological dependencies, frequency of use,
and need for cross-functional coordination. Respondents were invited to
discuss outcomes beyond immediate financial performance, including
improvements in customer understanding, decision quality, marketing
efficiency, organizational learning, cross-functional collaboration, and
marketing capability development. Questions were phrased in an un-
obtrusive and nondirective manner to encourage respondents to explain
developments in their own terms (McCracken, 1988). Each interview
concluded with questions about the respondent's professional back-
ground and involvement in the practices under investigation.

3.3. Data analysis
To analyze the longitudinal development of the 16 data-centric
marketing practices, we combined practice-level chronologies with
Langley's (1999) temporal bracketing strategy. We first reconstructed
the evolution of each practice and then compared the practices to
identify cumulative changes in data availability, analytical sophisticat-
ion, and enterprise-wide customer intelligence integration. This
approach enabled us to move between developing individual practices
and the broader transformation of Industrial Corp.'s marketing
activities.
Our analysis proceeded in four steps, following recommendations for
longitudinal process research (Gioia et al., 2013; Langley, 1999; Langley
et al., 2013). First, we reconstructed a detailed chronology for each of
the 16 data-centric marketing practices. We identified the initial moti-
vation for each practice, the emergence and development of the un-
derlying idea, implementation activities, adaptations, setbacks, and
outcomes. Interview, workshop, observational, and archival data were
triangulated to develop a longitudinal timeline for each practice (Yin,
2018). These chronologies allowed us to examine how practices
emerged, evolved, and either became institutionalized or were
discontinued.
Second, we compared the practice-level chronologies and grouped
them into analytically distinct but cumulative phases of data-centric
marketing development. Temporal bracketing was therefore used to
identify changes in the dominant form of management innovation
without implying that the practices of one phase ended when the next
began (Langley, 1999).
Third, we conducted within-phase analyses of the individual prac-
tices. We examined five dimensions: innovation characteristics, inno-
vation processes, change-agent configurations, implementation
outcomes, and capability development. The analysis of innovation
characteristics considered the novelty, uncertainty, organizational
reach, data requirements, analytical sophistication, and integration de-
mands of each practice. The analysis of innovation processes examined
how practices were motivated, invented, implemented, and institu-
tionalized. We also analyzed how different configurations of internal
and external change agents contributed to these processes. Constant
comparisons across the 16 practices enabled us to identify recurring
mechanisms and differences between institutionalized and discontinued
practices (Eisenhardt, 1989; Gioia et al., 2013).
Finally, we compared the identified cumulative phases to explain
how earlier developments enabled or constrained later practices. We
examined how data resources, analytical knowledge, organizational
routines, legitimacy, governance mechanisms, and cross-functional re-
lationships accumulated and were recombined over time. This analysis
showed that data-enabled marketing practices primarily strengthened
operational marketing capabilities, that analytics-driven marketing
practices contributed to dynamic marketing capabilities, and that inte-
grated customer intelligence practices developed transformational
marketing capabilities. Throughout the analysis, we iteratively moved
between the empirical evidence and the theoretical concepts of man-
agement innovation, marketing practices, and marketing capability
development to construct an inductive process model (Gioia et al., 2013;
Langley et al., 2013).

4.1. Data-enabled marketing practices
The first phase of Industrial Corp.'s transformation began in 2010.
This phase comprised eight data-enabled marketing practices (see
Table 2). Five practices—including the customer analytics cockpit,
automated market and technology intelligence, customer demand
forecasting, customer segmentation analytics, and lead scoring and op-
portunity prioritization—became successful and institutionalized. In
contrast, three practices—marketing performance dashboard, customer
lifetime value analytics, and marketing campaign analytics—were less
successful and were ultimately discontinued. The initiatives typically
unfolded within two years and focused on improving marketing effi-
ciency by systematically collecting, organizing, and using customer and
market data to solve localized operational problems. Rather than
introducing fundamentally new analytical approaches, managers
recombined existing data sources and established analytical tools to
support routine marketing decisions.
4.1.1. Adopt–implement–refine process
Data-enabled marketing practices followed a specific management
innovation process. They followed an Adopt–Implement–Refine process
in which managers selected established data solutions, embedded them
into existing marketing routines, and progressively refined them
through everyday use. Rather than developing fundamentally new data
technologies, management innovation centered on adapting proven data
tools to Industrial Corp.'s operational marketing context.
Adoption began as managers recognized recurring operational
problems, including fragmented customer information, delayed report-
ing, limited visibility of sales opportunities, and inconsistent customer
prioritization. Because these challenges were widely shared across the
organization, managers quickly developed a common understanding of
the need for improvement. As the CEO explained, “Everyone could see
the same problem (…) there was no debate, just urgency.” Rather than
inventing new data approaches, managers deliberately searched for
established data solutions that could be readily adopted and adapted to
the firm's marketing processes. As one interviewee summarized, “The
real work is not invention (…) it's figuring out how to connect the pieces
of existing data solutions so they actually deliver value in our context.”
Successful initiatives, therefore, selected data solutions that aligned
with existing customer data, organizational routines, and decision-
making processes. By contrast, customer lifetime value analytics relied
on assumptions and data structures that did not fit the firm's sales pro-
cesses, limiting its practical relevance from the outset.
Implementation focused on embedding the adopted data solutions
into everyday marketing work through limited piloting and gradual
adaptation. Rather than extensive experimentation, managers intro-
duced the practices on a small scale, gathered user feedback, and
adjusted them until they fitted established workflows. Learning
occurred primarily through repeated use and close user interaction.
Early improvements—including greater customer transparency, faster
reporting, improved opportunity prioritization, and earlier identifica-
tion of commercial risks—reinforced organizational acceptance and
accelerated adoption across marketing, sales, key account management,
and IT. Practices that failed to demonstrate immediate operational value
gradually lost support. As one sales manager explained, “The data tool
looked impressive (…), but it didn't really fit how we work (…). In the
end, we trusted our own experience more than what the system
suggested.”
Refinement occurred as managers continuously adjusted the prac-
tices based on operational experience until they became routine ele-
ments of daily marketing activities. Rather than formal change
programs, repeated use and incremental improvements strengthened
users' confidence in data-supported decision-making. Over time,
customer and market data became the common reference point for
operational marketing decisions, replacing fragmented information and
individual interpretations. As one business developer observed, “No one
called it an innovation (…) it just became the way we work.” The suc-
cessful practices were gradually institutionalized because employees
consistently experienced their practical value, whereas practices that
failed to demonstrate a clear operational contribution were eventually
discontinued.
4.1.2. Internal leadership, external support
Data-enabled marketing practices reveal a distinct change-agent
configuration on internal leadership and external support. The
Adopt–Implement–Refine Process was led by internal change agents,
while external change agents primarily provided technical expertise and
implementation support. Marketing, sales, key account management,
and IT managers recognized recurring operational problems, identified
opportunities for improvement, and initiated the adoption of established
analytical solutions. As the CEO explained, “The real push didn't come
from outside (…) it came from our own people recognizing where we
were falling short.” Internal managers, therefore, determined which
solutions addressed the firm's operational needs and how to incorporate
them into existing marketing processes.
External change agents, mostly software providers, consultants, and
peer firms, supported this process by introducing proven data tools,
implementation experience, and examples of best data practices. How-
ever, their contribution remained complementary. Internal managers
evaluated these external data solutions, adapted them to Industrial
Corp.'s customer data, marketing routines, and decision processes, and
translated them into practical marketing practices. As one business
developer explained, “Consultants brought many ideas (…) our role was
to adapt them to our context.”
Internal middle managers subsequently led implementation and
refinement by coordinating user involvement, simplifying data tools,
and integrating them into everyday marketing work. Through repeated
interaction with users, they continuously refined the practices until they
generated visible improvements in customer transparency, reporting
quality, and opportunity prioritization, thereby legitimizing data-
supported decision-making across the organization.

4.1.3. Building operational marketing capabilities
The five institutionalized data-enabled marketing practices primarily
strengthened operational marketing capabilities. They became repeat-
able routines for collecting, organizing, and using customer and market
data. These routines improved customer information management,
market monitoring, demand forecasting, customer segmentation, op-

more consistent and evidence-based operational marketing decisions.
The strengthening of operational marketing capabilities translated
into higher marketing efficiency. Managers gained greater visibility of
customers and market developments, produced reports more quickly,
prioritized sales opportunities more consistently, and coordinated
marketing and sales activities more effectively. As customer and market
data became the primary basis for routine operational decisions, man-
agers relied less on fragmented information and individual intuition,
leading to faster, more standardized marketing processes.
Although the three discontinued practices did not become institu-
tionalized, they generated important learning regarding data quality,
contextualization, and user involvement. More broadly, this first phase
established the organizational routines, data resources, and legitimacy
for data-supported operational decision-making, providing the founda-
tion for the subsequent development of analytics-driven marketing
practices. Supplementary Material A provides additional evidence sup-

4.2. Analytics-driven marketing practices
The second phase of Industrial Corp.'s transformation began in 2016.
It comprised four analytics-driven marketing practices (see Table 2):
strategic competitive intelligence, customer opportunity management,
marketing strategy simulations, and strategic pricing optimization.
Three practices became successful and institutionalized, whereas stra-
tegic pricing optimization was discontinued. These initiatives typically
lasted 2 to 4 years and aimed to improve marketing effectiveness. They
transformed increasingly available customer and market data, using
more sophisticated analytics, into strategic insights. Compared with the
first phase, managers moved beyond organizing data toward interpret-
ing analytical outputs to anticipate customer needs, identify market
opportunities, improve competitive positioning, and support strategic
decision-making. Consequently, these practices required considerably
greater experimentation, managerial interpretation, and contextual
adaptation before they became institutionalized.
4.2.1. The explore–experiment–scale process
Analytics-driven marketing practices followed another distinct
management innovation process. They follow an explor-
e–experiment–scale process in which managers search for new analyt-
ical possibilities, test alternative approaches, and progressively embed
successful practices into strategic marketing decision-making.
Exploration began as managers recognized the limitations of the
earlier phase. Although data-enabled marketing practices had improved
operational efficiency and reporting, they no longer adequately sup-
ported increasingly complex strategic decisions. Rather than focusing on
operational transparency, managers sought analytical approaches that
could improve customer growth, competitive positioning, pricing de-
cisions, and market expansion. As one senior manager explained, “It
wasn't a single trigger (…) over time, market pressure and internal goals
came together, and it became clear that data had to play a much more
strategic role.” Successful initiatives explored analytics as a source of
strategic insight rather than another reporting tool. Managers evaluated
multiple analytical approaches, drew on external expertise, and com-
bined emerging analytical methods with their own market knowledge.
As one sales manager described the development of customer opportu-
nity management, “We explored several options (…) some worked
better, others didn't (…), but we kept iterating until one option fit our
strategic needs.” By contrast, strategic pricing optimization focused
primarily on a technically sophisticated pricing model without suffi-
ciently exploring how it would support managerial decision-making in
Industrial Corp.'s specific competitive context. This explained why
strategic pricing optimization became less successful and was, finally,
discontinued.
Experimentation involved iterative cycles of piloting, evaluation,
refinement, and organizational learning. Rather than implementing
analytical solutions directly, managers tested alternative market sce-
narios, customer segmentation models, pricing assumptions, and
competitor analyses before incorporating them into broader strategic
marketing processes. As one key account manager explained, “We didn't
roll out our marketing strategy simulations all at once (…) we first tested
market scenarios (…) then refined them before integrating them into our
broader strategic planning process.” Throughout this process, managers
continuously interpreted analytical outputs, compared them with their
own market knowledge, and adapted the practices to changing customer
and competitive conditions. Successful experimentation, therefore,
combined analytical evidence with managerial judgment. In contrast,
the discontinued pricing initiative generated technically robust recom-
mendations that remained disconnected from relationship-specific
knowledge and tacit market experience.
Scaling occurred once managers had developed sufficient confidence
in both the analytical approaches and their strategic relevance. Rather
than replacing managerial expertise, successful practices became
embedded because they consistently supported strategic discussions,
opportunity evaluation, and market planning. As one business developer
explained, “Strategic competitive intelligence worked best when it
supported our strategic thinking (…) not when it tried to replace it.”
Managers gradually developed a shared understanding of how analytics
should inform strategic marketing decisions, enabling the diffusion of
strategic competitive intelligence, customer opportunity management,
and marketing strategy simulations across the organization. By contrast,
strategic pricing optimization never developed a comparable strategic
narrative or organizational legitimacy. Without a shared understanding
of how analytical recommendations should complement managerial
judgment, the practice remained peripheral and was ultimately
discontinued.
4.2.2. External exploration, internal translation
Compared with the first phase, analytics-driven marketing practices
exhibit another distinct configuration of change agents. This configu-
ration is more of an external exploration with internal translation. It
means that analytics-driven marketing practices relied more heavily on
external change agents to broaden the firm's search for new analytical
approaches. In contrast, internal change agents translated these oppor-
tunities into strategically relevant marketing practices. Marketing, sales,
business development, and strategy managers jointly coordinated this
translation process by evaluating alternative analytical approaches,
experimenting with their application, and aligning successful initiatives
with Industrial Corp.'s strategic objectives. Senior executives legitimized
experimentation, allocated resources, and connected individual initia-
tives to broader business priorities.

providers, universities, technology partners, and customers, expanded

4.2.3. Building dynamic marketing capabilities
The three institutionalized analytics-driven marketing practices
primarily strengthened dynamic marketing capabilities by enabling
managers to sense market changes, identify emerging customer oppor-
tunities, and continuously adapt marketing strategies. Through strategic
competitive intelligence, customer opportunity management, and mar-
keting strategy simulations, managers developed stronger capabilities to
interpret customer and market information, evaluate strategic alterna-

competitive conditions.
These dynamic marketing capabilities enabled more informed and
proactive strategic marketing decisions. They translated into greater
marketing effectiveness. Managers identified customer opportunities
earlier, responded more rapidly to market developments, improved
competitive positioning, and made more consistent strategic marketing
decisions that supported customer growth and market expansion.
Although strategic pricing optimization did not become institution-
alized, it clarified the limitations of relying exclusively on analytical
models and reinforced the importance of contextualization, managerial
judgment, and human–AI complementarity. More broadly, this second
phase established the analytical capabilities, cross-functional routines,

integrated customer intelligence practices. Supplementary Material B

4.3. Integrated customer intelligence practices
The third phase of Industrial Corp.'s transformation began in 2020. It
comprised four integrated customer intelligence practices (see Table 2):
an enterprise customer intelligence platform, an AI-assisted customer
co-creation platform, a customer knowledge graph, and analytics-
powered customer journey orchestration. The first two practices
became successful and institutionalized, whereas the last two were less
successful and discontinued. These four initiatives typically unfolded
over four years and sought to integrate customer intelligence across
marketing, sales, service, product development, business development,
and IT. Unlike earlier phases, the objective was no longer to improve
individual marketing activities or strategic decisions, but to establish
shared customer intelligence to coordinate decision-making across
organizational functions. Consequently, these practices required sub-
stantial organizational integration, governance development, and cross-
functional collaboration.
4.3.1. Reframe, integrate, and institutionalize process
Integrated customer intelligence practices followed another, distinct
type of management innovation process. This management innovation
process was a recursive reframe–integrate–institutionalize process in
which managers continuously redefined the role of customer intelli-
gence, integrated organizational and technological resources, and
embedded new practices into enterprise-wide decision-making.
Reframing began as managers increasingly recognized the limita-
tions of the earlier phases. Data-enabled marketing practices had
improved operational efficiency, while analytics-driven marketing
practices had strengthened strategic decision-making. However,
customer knowledge remained fragmented across systems, departments,
and decision processes. Managers realized that the central challenge was
no longer generating more customer data or developing better analytics
but creating a shared organizational understanding of customers. As one
sales manager explained, “The need for transformative change didn't
appear overnight (…) it grew as we realized our previous data initiatives
couldn't support what we needed to do in the future.” Successful ini-
tiatives, therefore, reframed customer intelligence as an enterprise-wide
organizational resource rather than a functional marketing asset. The
enterprise customer intelligence platform, for example, was positioned
as a mechanism for coordinating customer knowledge across marketing,
sales, service, and product development. In contrast, the discontinued
customer knowledge graph and customer journey orchestration initia-
tives were initially framed around technological possibilities rather than
clearly articulated managerial problems. Their technological ambition
exceeded the organization's readiness, weakening commitment across
functions.
Integration involved developing technologies, governance mecha-
nisms, organizational routines, and cross-functional collaboration
simultaneously rather than sequentially. Instead of implementing
comprehensive solutions from the outset, managers progressively con-
nected customer data, analytical tools, organizational roles, and deci-
sion processes through repeated piloting and organizational learning. As
one IT manager explained, “Every time we used our new enterprise
customer intelligence platform, we changed something (…) sometimes
the tool, sometimes even the way we defined the problem.” Industrial
Corp. deliberately introduced the enterprise customer intelligence
platform in modular stages, gradually connecting additional data sour-
ces, business functions, and customer processes. Similarly, the AI-
assisted customer co-creation platform evolved through repeated
workshops in which users jointly refined how AI should support
customer dialogue, requirement analysis, and collaborative innovation.
By contrast, the discontinued initiatives pursued comprehensive inte-
gration before Industrial Corp. could establish common data definitions,
governance arrangements, and organizational routines, creating
complexity that exceeded the firm's absorptive capacity.
Institutionalization occurred as managers developed shared in-
terpretations of customer intelligence and embedded these practices into
everyday organizational routines. They progressively established com-
mon customer data definitions, clarified ownership responsibilities,
introduced governance principles, and integrated customer intelligence
into cross-functional decision-making. As one senior manager reflected,

4.3.2. Internal–external co-creation and institutionalization
Integrated customer intelligence practices revealed another type of
change-agent configuration. They require an internal–external co-
creation and institutionalization of change agents. It means that inte-
grated customer intelligence practices emerged through continuous
collaboration between internal and external change agents. Rather than
contributing sequentially, both groups jointly reframed customer intel-
ligence, integrated organizational and technological capabilities, and
institutionalized new enterprise-wide marketing practices. Senior ex-
ecutives established a shared transformation vision, mobilized organi-
zational commitment, and positioned enterprise-wide customer
intelligence as a strategic priority. Cross-functional leadership teams
brought together marketing, sales, service, product development, busi-
ness development, IT, and data governance to jointly redesign customer
management processes that no single function could address on its own.
Various external change agents, including AI providers, platform
vendors, consultants, universities, and research partners, contributed
emerging technologies, data architectural concepts, governance ap-
proaches, and implementation experience. Rather than prescribing
ready-made solutions, they worked closely with internal change agents
to explore how these technologies could support Industrial Corp.'s
customer management challenges. So-called internal transformation
champions complemented this expertise by contributing organizational
knowledge, coordinating cross-functional collaboration, and ensuring
that technological possibilities evolved alongside business processes and
governance arrangements. As one senior manager reflected, “It was our
people who turned the early confusion about customer-data architec-
tures into something others could trust and support.”
Co-creation continued throughout implementation and institution-
alization. Internal and external change agents jointly refined customer
data definitions, governance mechanisms, organizational roles, and
decision routines through repeated experimentation and organizational
learning. Internal champions sequenced implementation according to
organizational readiness, facilitated cross-functional learning, mediated
possible resistance and/or skeptics, and progressively embedded
customer intelligence within everyday decision-making. External part-
ners simultaneously adapted technological solutions in response to user
feedback and evolving organizational requirements. Consequently,
technologies, governance mechanisms, and marketing practices co-
evolved rather than being developed independently.
The discontinued customer knowledge graph and customer journey
orchestration initiatives illustrate the importance of this collaborative
relationship. In these cases, technological development progressed more
rapidly than the joint development of governance, organizational rou-
tines, and shared understandings. As a result, technically sophisticated
solutions failed to become embedded in everyday marketing practice
because internal and external actors were unable to align technology
with organizational readiness jointly. These findings suggest that
transformational management innovations depend not simply on
combining internal and external expertise, but on co-creating and
institutionalizing enterprise-wide customer intelligence practices
through sustained collaboration, shared learning, and joint governance
development.
4.3.3. Building transformational marketing capabilities
The two institutionalized integrated customer intelligence practices
primarily strengthened transformational marketing capabilities by
enabling the organization to integrate customer intelligence across
marketing, sales, service, product development, business development,
and IT. These practices established shared customer data, common
governance mechanisms, and cross-functional routines that enabled the
organization to develop a unified understanding of customers and co-
ordinate activities around enterprise-wide customer intelligence. As the
CEO explained, the enterprise customer intelligence platform “con-
nected strategy, innovation, and operations (…) enabling more antici-
patory and coordinated decisions through enterprise-wide customer
knowledge.”
These transformational marketing capabilities enabled more coor-
dinated enterprise-wide marketing decisions. Rather than optimizing
decisions within individual functions, managers increasingly aligned
strategic, commercial, and operational decisions through shared
customer intelligence. Customer knowledge became an organizational
rather than departmental resource, enabling managers to jointly eval-
uate customer opportunities, coordinate innovation initiatives, priori-
tize investments, and respond consistently to changing customer needs.
Consequently, managerial work shifted from isolated decision-making
toward collective sensemaking, cross-functional collaboration, and co-
ordinated action supported by enterprise-wide customer intelligence.
The strengthening of transformational marketing capabilities resul-
ted in marketing transformation. Customer intelligence became
embedded within enterprise-wide organizational routines, governance
mechanisms, and decision processes, fundamentally changing how the
firm generated, shared, and applied customer and market knowledge.
Although the two discontinued initiatives did not become institu-
tionalized, they revealed important limitations in data integration,
governance, and organizational readiness. These insights informed the
refinement of subsequent practices and reinforced the importance of
jointly developing technology, governance, and organizational routines.
Overall, integrated customer intelligence practices completed Industrial
Corp.'s transition toward data-centric marketing by embedding
customer intelligence into enterprise-wide organizational processes and
developing transformational marketing capabilities that enabled sus-
tained marketing transformation. Supplementary Material C provides
additional evidence supporting these findings.
5. Discussion
5.1. Theoretical contribution
This study contributes to the emerging literature on data-centric
marketing by developing a process theory that explains how
manufacturing firms progressively build data-centric marketing through
the continuous innovation of marketing practices. Rather than treating
data availability, analytics, or AI as direct drivers of marketing trans-
formation, we show how firms transform customer and market data into
superior marketing capabilities through cumulative management inno-
vation processes, evolving change-agent configurations, and the insti-
tutionalization of new marketing practices. In doing so, the study makes
four theoretical contributions.
First, we contribute to the data-centric marketing literature by
conceptualizing data-centric marketing development as a cumulative
process of marketing practice innovation. Recent research has demon-
strated how digital technologies, analytics, and artificial intelligence
improve customer understanding and marketing decision-making
(Herhausen et al., 2020; Huang & Rust, 2021), while other studies
have highlighted the importance of marketing analytics capabilities and
data-driven innovation for competitive advantage (Hossain et al., 2022;
Eriksson & Heikkil¨a, 2023). Our findings complement these studies by
providing insight into how firms become more data-centric over time.
Our findings identify three cumulative phases—data-enabled marketing
practices, analytics-driven marketing practices, and integrated customer
intelligence practices—that reflect increasing levels of data availability,
analytical sophistication, and enterprise-wide customer intelligence
integration. Rather than replacing one another, these phases accumulate

96

as firms progressively embed customer and market data into operational
routines, strategic decision-making, and enterprise-wide customer in-
telligence. This process perspective shifts attention from technology
adoption toward the organizational evolution of marketing practices
through which firms become increasingly data-centric.
Second, we contribute to management innovation and marketing
theory by showing that distinct innovation processes give rise to
different forms of data-centric marketing practices. Existing research has
largely emphasized the technological foundations of digital marketing
capabilities and AI-enabled marketing (Mikalef et al., 2021; Morgan &
Feng, 2024), while offering considerably less insight into how these
practices are developed, adapted, and institutionalized within organi-
zations. Drawing on management innovation theory (Birkinshaw et al.,
2008), we demonstrate that data-enabled marketing practices evolve
through an adopt–implement–refine process, analytics-driven market-
ing practices through an explore–experiment–scale process, and inte-
grated customer intelligence practices through a
reframe–integrate–institutionalize process. This extends the manage-
ment innovation literature by showing that the underlying innovation
process changes as firms move from implementing established analytical
solutions to experimenting with novel analytical approaches, and ulti-
mately redesigning enterprise-wide marketing systems. It also comple-
ments recent work showing that data-driven innovation requires the co-
evolution of technological and marketing capabilities (Eriksson &
Heikkil¨a, 2023).
Third, we contribute to the literature on organizational change by
explaining how internal and external change-agent configurations
evolve as firms become increasingly data-centric. Prior research recog-
nizes the importance of external partners, customers, technology pro-
viders, and cross-functional collaboration in digital marketing
transformation (Day, 2011), while recent studies emphasize AI-specific
organizational capabilities and cross-functional orchestration (Mikalef
et al., 2021). However, limited attention has been devoted to how the
role of different change agents has evolved across successive forms of
marketing practice innovation. Our findings identify three comple-
mentary configurations of change agents. Data-enabled marketing
practices rely on internal leadership with external support: internal
managers lead implementation, and external actors contribute estab-
lished expertise. Analytics-driven marketing practices depend on
external exploration with internal translation, in which external part-
ners broaden the firm's search for analytical opportunities and internal
managers translate these opportunities into strategically meaningful
marketing practices. Finally, integrated customer intelligence practices
emerge through internal–external co-creation and institutionalization.
Internal and external actors jointly develop technologies, governance
mechanisms, and organizational routines that embed customer intelli-
gence across the enterprise. These findings extend research on change
agents in management innovation (Birkinshaw et al., 2008; Mol &
Birkinshaw, 2014) by demonstrating that change-agent configurations
systematically evolve as innovation complexity increases.
Finally, we contribute to the marketing capability literature by
explaining how complementary bundles of data-centric marketing
practices progressively build higher-order marketing capabilities that
enable increasingly sophisticated marketing decisions and organiza-
tional outcomes. Previous research has established that marketing ca-
pabilities are important drivers of firm performance (Krasnikov &
Jayachandran, 2008; Vorhies & Morgan, 2005) and that dynamic mar-
keting capabilities enable firms to adapt to changing environments
(Barrales-Molina et al., 2014; Shanka, 2026). Our findings extend this
literature by explaining how these capabilities emerge over time. Spe-
cifically, data-enabled marketing practices strengthen operational
marketing capabilities that improve marketing efficiency, analytics-
driven marketing practices develop dynamic marketing capabilities
that enhance strategic marketing effectiveness, and integrated customer
intelligence practices build transformational marketing capabilities that
enable coordinated enterprise-wide marketing decisions. Rather than
viewing marketing capabilities as direct outcomes of digital technolo-
gies or analytics investments alone (Morgan, 2012), we demonstrate
that they emerge through the cumulative innovation, integration, and
institutionalization of marketing practices. This process perspective
suggests that sustainable competitive advantage depends less on
adopting advanced technologies than on continuously renewing the
organizational practices through which customer and market data are
transformed into superior marketing capabilities.
5.2. Managerial implications
This study provides several implications for managers. The findings
show that competitive advantage arises not from adopting digital
technologies alone but from continuously innovating marketing prac-
tices. They progressively build marketing capabilities, improve mar-
keting decision-making, and ultimately transform the entire marketing
function.
Managers should view data-centric marketing as a cumulative
capability-building process comprising three phases: data-enabled
marketing practices, analytics-driven marketing practices, and inte-
grated customer intelligence practices. Each phase requires a different
innovation process and change-agent configuration.
Managers should begin by developing data-enabled marketing
practices that strengthen operational marketing capabilities and
improve marketing efficiency. Practices such as customer dashboards,
lead prioritization, customer segmentation, and demand forecasting
enable firms to embed customer and market data into routine opera-
tional marketing decisions. At this stage, internal managers should lead
implementation, while external experts provide established analytical
solutions and support implementation.
Building on this foundation, firms should develop analytics-driven
marketing practices that strengthen dynamic marketing capabilities,
improve strategic marketing decisions, and enhance marketing effec-
tiveness. Managers should explore emerging analytical approaches,
experiment with AI-supported customer insights, and scale practices
that drive customer growth, enhance competitive positioning, and
improve market forecasting. Success depends on combining analytical
insights with managerial judgment rather than relying solely on algo-
rithms. External partners broaden the search for new analytical oppor-
tunities, while internal managers translate them into strategically
relevant marketing practices.
Finally, firms should establish integrated customer intelligence
practices that build transformational marketing capabilities, enable
enterprise-wide marketing decisions, and achieve marketing trans-
formation. Customer intelligence should become a shared organiza-
tional resource that connects marketing, sales, service, product
development, and business development through common governance,
shared data, and coordinated decision-making. Achieving this trans-
formation requires close collaboration between internal and external
change agents to co-create and institutionalize new customer intelli-
gence practices.
For manufacturing SMEs, these findings demonstrate that becoming
data-centric does not require large upfront investments in advanced AI
technologies. Instead, firms can progressively strengthen marketing
capabilities by continuously innovating marketing practices and
embedding customer intelligence throughout the organization. Sus-
tainable competitive advantage, therefore, depends less on technology
itself than on the organizational capability to transform customer and
market data into better operational, strategic, and enterprise-wide
marketing decisions.
5.3. Limitations and future research
As with all embedded longitudinal single-case studies, this study has
limitations that also provide opportunities for future research. Although
embedded case studies are particularly well-suited for developing theory

97

from rich contextual data (Eisenhardt, 1989) and longitudinal process
research enables researchers to uncover how organizational phenomena
evolve (Langley et al., 2013), our findings are based on 16 data-centric
marketing practices within a single European manufacturing SME.
Consequently, the three cumulative phases—data-enabled marketing
practices, analytics-driven marketing practices, and integrated customer
intelligence practices—should be regarded as analytically derived rather
than universally applicable. Future research should examine whether
these phases emerge similarly across firms of different sizes, industries,
and institutional contexts. Comparative longitudinal case studies could
investigate whether organizations follow similar innovation processes
and capability-building trajectories, or whether differences in digital
maturity, environmental uncertainty, or organizational complexity lead
to alternative pathways toward data-centric marketing.
A second limitation concerns the scope of the proposed process
theory. While this study identifies three distinct innovation processes
and three corresponding change-agent configurations, it examines them
within a single organizational context. Future research should therefore
investigate the boundary conditions of the proposed model by exam-
ining when and under what conditions firms follow the
adopt–implement–refine, explore–experiment–scale, and reframe-
–integrate–institutionalize innovation processes. Similarly, future
studies could examine whether the three change-agent
configurations—internal leadership with external support, external
exploration with internal translation, and internal–external co-creation
and institutionalization—remain consistent across different organiza-
tional settings or vary according to firm size, digital maturity, industry
characteristics, or ecosystem complexity. Such studies would further
refine the boundary conditions and broader applicability of the process
theory developed in this research.
