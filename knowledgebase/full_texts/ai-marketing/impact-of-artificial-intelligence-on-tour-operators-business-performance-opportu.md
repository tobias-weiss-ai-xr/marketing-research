ABSTRACT
Artificial Intelligence (AI) is changing the tourism industry from the gr ound up, altering how travellers search for,
plan and book their trips, and reshaping the traditional role that tour operators play along the way. Interest in AI -driven
tourism technology has grown steadily, yet most existing studies still look at this shi ft either through the traveller's
eyes or through a single AI application in isolation, leaving a gap in our understanding of what AI's combined
opportunities and challenges actually mean for a tour operator's business performance. This review sets out to close
that gap by working systematically through secondary literature published between 2019 and 2025, drawing on peer -
reviewed journals, a book chapter and a conference paper. Through thematic and comparative analysis, it traces the
patterns that recur across these works in terms of AI's opportunities, challenges, applications and research focus. What
emerges is that AI meaningfully improves operational efficiency, personalised marketing, customer communication
and decision -making, but it also brings real concerns around data privacy, the cost of implementation, workforce
displacement and the loss of the human touch that many travellers still care about. On that basis, the study
recommends that tour operators pair AI with human expertise, invest in their em ployees' digital skills, and adopt AI
selectively rather than across the board, so as to stay competitive without losing customer trust.
Keywords: Artificial Intelligence, Tour Operators, Business Performance, Tourism Technology, Customer Experience,
Digital Transformation.
1. INTRODUCTION
Over the past decade, the global tourism industry has gone through a rapid digital transformation, and Artificial
Intelligence (AI) has become one of the most influential technologies driving that change. AI, broadly speaking , refers
to computer systems that can carry out tasks normally requiring human intelligence — learning, reasoning, processing
language and making predictions. Within tourism, this takes the form of chatbots, virtual assistants, recommendation
engines, dyna mic pricing systems and predictive analytics, all of which have altered how travellers search for
destinations, weigh up their options and decide what to book. AI matters to the industry not just because it automates
repetitive tasks, but because it allows  a level of personalisation and speed that human staff alone would struggle to
deliver at scale.
Tour operators sit in a particular spot within this shifting landscape. They have traditionally put together travel
packages, offered on -the-ground expertise, arranged transport and accommodation, and supported customers
throughout their journey. Their value has always come from trust, local knowledge and the ability to handle situations
that don't go to plan. But as AI -powered platforms have made it easier for travellers to plan and book independently,
reliance on intermediaries like tour operators has started to fall. That creates a real business problem: tour operators
now have to work out how far to adopt AI without losing the personal service that sets them apart from platforms
driven purely by algorithms.
Looking at the current literature, most studies on AI in tourism focus on how travellers perceive it, on hotel operations,
or on broad trends across the industry. Comparatively few pull together, in one pla ce, the specific opportunities and
challenges AI creates for tour operators' business performance. That leaves a research gap — the tourism literature
offers pieces of insight, but not a consolidated, comparative picture built around tour operator decision -making. This
study aims to address that gap by synthesising secondary literature published between 2019 and 2025, with four
objectives in mind: (i) identifying the main opportunities AI creates for tour operators; (ii) identifying the main
challenges tour operators face in adopting AI; (iii) examining the AI applications most often discussed in the tourism
literature; and (iv) drawing out practical recommendations for tour operators trying to balance AI adoption with
personalised customer service.

Figure 1. Major Opportunities of AI Identified in the Reviewed Literature (Horizontal Bar Chart)
Figure 1 shows that enhanced customer experience and operational efficiency are the two most consistently reported
opportunities, each turning up in four of the five reviewed works . That points to a broader consensus: AI's main
business value lies in doing existing tasks faster and in a more personal way, rather than in inventing entirely new
service categories. Chatbots, virtual assistants and data -driven decision-making follow close behind, each appearing in
three works, which suggests conversational automation and analytics are now seen as mainstream rather than
experimental. Competitive advantage and business growth show up less often as a theme in their own right, which
suggests most studies treat it as something that follows from efficiency and personalisation rather than a separate
driver in itself. For tour operators, this ordering suggests that AI investment is likely to pay off fastest when it's aimed
at customer -facing comm unication and back -office efficiency, with competitive gains following on as a secondary
effect rather than an immediate result.
Figure 2 shows that data privacy and ethical concerns, alongside high implementation cost and infrastructure
requirements, are the most frequently cited barriers, each raised in three of the five works. This suggests the
constraints on AI adoption aren't purely technical — they're financial and reputational too, since mishandled customer
data can damage trust just as badly as a po orly performing algorithm. Concerns about the loss of human interaction
and workforce displacement appear in two studies each, pointing to an ongoing tension between automation and the
relationship-based service many tour operators depend on for repeat bus iness. The digital divide and unequal access,
raised in only one work, still matters for tour operators serving developing regions or older customers, where gaps in
infrastructure or digital literacy can limit how far AI tools can actually reach. Taken tog ether, these challenges suggest
tour operators can't treat AI adoption as a simple cost -cutting exercise — it needs to go hand in hand with investment
in governance, staff retraining and inclusive service design.

Figure 3 shows how often different AI applications come up across the reviewed literature. Chatbots and virtual
assistants dominate at 29 percent, which f its with their role as the most visible and widely used customer -facing AI

remains more of a longer-term consideration than an immediate one.

Figure 6. Conceptual Summary of How AI Influences Tour Operators' Business Performance
Figure 6 sets out a conceptual chain linking the themes discussed above. AI first reshapes the customer experience
through personalisation, chatbots and recommendation systems; that improved experience then f eeds into operational
efficiency, as routine enquiries, bookings and itinerary changes need less manual effort; efficiency gains and richer
customer data go on to translate into measurable business performance — cost savings, higher conversion, better
demand forecasting; and sustained improvements in performance ultimately support competitive advantage, provided
the challenges flagged in Figure 2 — particularly data privacy, cost and the risk of losing the human touch — are
actively managed rather than left  unaddressed. What this chain implies is that competitive advantage doesn't follow
automatically from adopting AI tools; it depends on tour operators carrying gains successfully from one stage to the
next while protecting the trust-based relationships that set them apart from self-service travel platforms.
Taken together, the five reviewed works point to one shared conclusion: AI gives tour operators well -evidenced gains
in efficiency, personalisation and decision -making, but those gains are conditional on managing privacy, cost and
human-touch concerns. This echoes earlier waves of tourism digitalisation — online booking, mobile apps, social
media marketing — which produced similar trade -offs between efficiency and disintermediation. What sets the AI -
specific literature apart is its stronger focus on ethical and workforce questions, which are becoming a growing
managerial concern for tour operators.
3. PRACTICAL IMPLICATIONS
These findings carry direct implications for tour operators, travel agencies and touris m managers looking to stay
competitive in a market increasingly shaped by AI.
AI adoption: Tour operators should bring AI in gradually, starting with proven, lower -risk uses such as chatbots for
routine enquiries and recommendation engines for itinerary planning, before moving on to predictive analytics or
automating more complex processes.
Customer retention: As travellers increasingly weigh AI-driven services against human -led ones, tour operators should
use AI to support, rather than replace, the personal relationship they have with repeat customers.

purely a cost -cutting move, are more likely to hold on to customer loyalty while still capturing the efficiency gains

4. CONCLUSION
This study set out to review secondary literature on how AI affects tour operators' business performance, with the aim
of identifying its main opportunities, challenges, common applications and research focus areas. Using a review -
based, qualitative design b uilt on thematic and comparative analysis of five works published between 2019 and 2025,
the study found that AI meaningfully strengthens operational efficiency, personalisation, customer communication and
data-driven decision -making for tour operators, wh ile the literature consistently flags concerns around data privacy
and ethics, implementation cost, workforce displacement, and the possible loss of the human interaction that underpins
traditional tour operator services. The contribution of this study lie s in bringing these themes together into a
consolidated, comparative view from the tour operator's perspective — an angle that has received relatively little
dedicated attention in the broader AI -in-tourism literature. Its central recommendation is that to ur operators pursue
selective, well -governed AI adoption that combines technological efficiency with sustained human expertise, rather
than treating AI as either a threat to resist or a wholesale replacement for personalised service.
5. FUTURE RESEARCH
This review has several limitations: it relies solely on secondary literature, without primary data such as surveys or
interviews with tour operators; it covers a five -year window (2019 -2025) and five purposively selected works, which
cannot capture the full breadth of a fast -moving field; and it focuses specifically on tour operators and travel agencies
rather than the tourism sector as a whole. Future research could build on this through case studies of individual tour
operators implementing AI,  financial performance analysis using company -level data, cross -country comparisons of
AI adoption across developed and developing tourism markets, closer examination of generative AI in itinerary design
and customer communication, and dedicated study of h ow small and medium -sized tour operators are adopting AI
given their more limited resources.
