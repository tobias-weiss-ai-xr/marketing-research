Abstract
Artificial intelligence (AI) is becoming increasingly visible across the hotel guest journey, yet
evidence remains limited on how guests evaluate AI -enabled service quality in luxury
hospitality and whether satisfaction explains its relationship with loyalty intentions. This study
examines the relationships among AI -enabled service quality, guest satisfaction and guest
loyalty intentions in Malaysian luxury hotels. Drawing on SERVQUAL, Expectation –
Confirmation Theory and Relationship Marketing Theory, three  direct hypotheses and one
exploratory mediation relationship were examined using cross-sectional survey data from 115
adults who had stayed at a luxury hotel in Malaysia within the previous 12 months and used
at least one AI -enabled hotel service. Data we re analysed using descriptive statistics,
Spearman’s rank-order correlations and regression-based analyses. AI-enabled service quality
positively predicted guest satisfaction (β = 0.561, p < 0.001) and loyalty intentions (β = 0.751,
p < 0.001), while satisfaction pos itively predicted loyalty when examined independently (β =
0.405, p < 0.001). However, satisfaction was not significant after AI -enabled service quality
was included in the loyalty model (β = −0.024, p = 0.755), and the estimated indirect effect
was negligible (ab = −0.015). The findings suggest that, within this exploratory sample, loyalty
intentions were associated more strongly with guests’ direct evaluations of AI-enabled service
quality than with an indirect satisfaction pathway. The study extends service-quality research
into an under-researched Southeast Asian luxury-hotel context and highlights the importance
of reliable, responsive, secure and appropriately personalised AI services. Conclusions are
tempered by the cross -sectional, purposive design and the marginal reliability of the short
satisfaction and loyalty scales.

Artificial intelligence is reshaping hospitality service delivery. Hotels increasingly employ
chatbots, mobile check -in, smart -room controls, digital concierges, biometric verification,
service robots and recommendation systems to make the guest journey fa ster and more
personalised. This transformation extends beyond operational automation. AI now influences

how guests search for information, communicate with hotels, access services and evaluate
the overall experience. A bibliometric review of 2,527 hospitality studies identified digital -

hospitality’s digital transformation (Peng et al., 2024). More recent work similarly describes
AI as a strategic service capability with implications for both customer experience and
organisational performance (Gursoy, 2025).

The implications are especially important in luxury hospitality. Luxury hotels compete through
exclusivity, personal recognition and memorable service rather than price alone. Technology
may strengthen this proposition by helping hotels remember preferences, anticipate requests
and deliver consistent support. It can also weaken the experience if guests perceive
interactions as impersonal, intrusive or difficult to navigate. AI is therefore most valuable
when it complements employee judgement and emotional e ngagement rather than simply
replacing human contact (Prentice et al., 2020; Gursoy, 2025).

This tension raises an important service -marketing question. Guests may appreciate AI
because it is innovative, but their longer -term responses should depend on whether it
improves the quality of service they actually receive. Traditional service -quality r esearch
emphasises reliability, responsiveness, assurance, empathy and tangibles (Parasuraman et al.,
1985, 1988). These dimensions remain relevant in AI -mediated encounters, although their
expression changes. Reliability concerns accurate and uninterrupted system performan ce;
responsiveness includes immediate digital assistance; assurance encompasses privacy,
security and confidence; empathy may be reflected in appropriate personalisation; and
tangibles extend to interfaces, devices and smart-room environments.

Recent empirical studies generally report positive associations between AI -supported hotel
services and customer outcomes. AI -enabled attributes have been linked to satisfaction and
loyalty in chain hotels (Chotisarn & Phuthong, 2025), while tailor-made AI services have been
associated with guest satisfaction in European hotel settings (Makivić  et al., 2024). A meta -
analysis also showed that functional, social and sensory AI characteristics shape cognitive,
emotional and relational outcomes, although their effects differ across contexts (Li et al.,
2025). These variations suggest that AI does not produce favourable outcomes automatically;
its value depends on how guests experience the service.

Two gaps motivate the present study. First, much AI research in hospitality has focused on
adoption, perceived usefulness or willingness to interact with robots and automated systems.
Less attention has been paid to how an integrated perception of AI -enabled service quality
relates to post -experience outcomes. Second, available evidence is concentrated in general
hospitality settings and technologically advanced markets. Luxury hotels present a distinctive
high-contact context in which efficiency must coexi st with personalised attention. Malaysia
also provides a relevant Southeast Asian setting in which hospitality organisations are
pursuing digitalisation while serving culturally diverse domestic and international guests.
Malaysian research has highlighted both org anisational alignment in hotels and practical
barriers to inclusive AI adoption, including affordability, skills, infrastructure and language
(Said et al., 2023; Kamrozzaman et al., 2025).

Accordingly, this study examines whether AI-enabled service quality is associated with guest
satisfaction and loyalty intentions in Malaysian luxury hotels and whether satisfaction
operates as an intervening mechanism. It contributes by applying established service-quality
logic to AI -mediated luxury service encounters, testing the model in an under -researched
national context and providing evidence on whether the familiar service quality–satisfaction–
loyalty sequence is observed in this setting. Given the sample and design, the study is
positioned as exploratory rather than as a definitive causal test.

Theoretical Background and Hypotheses
AI-enabled service quality and guest satisfaction
SERVQUAL conceptualises perceived service quality through reliability, responsiveness,
assurance, empathy and tangibles (Parasuraman et al., 1985, 1988). Although originally
developed for predominantly human service encounters, its customer -centred logic remains
useful in digitally mediated hospitality. Guests do not normally assess the sophistication of an
algorithm directly. They judge whether the service works, responds quickly, protects their
information, recognises relevant needs and fits the expected service environment.

AI changes how these judgements are formed. Automated systems can provide continuous
availability and standardised  responses, but they can also generate frustration through
inaccurate recommendations, poor recovery or an inability to understand unusual requests.
Assurance is similarly double -sided: biometric systems and predictive tools may improve
convenience while s imultaneously raising concerns about privacy and transparency.
Personalisation may resemble empathy when it is accurate and welcome, but feel intrusive
when it exceeds guests’ expectations. AI -enabled service quality should therefore be
understood as guests’ overall evaluation of how effectively intelligent technologies deliver or
support the service dimensions they value.

Expectation–Confirmation Theory proposes that satisfaction reflects a post -experience
judgement in which perceived performance is evaluated against prior expectations (Oliver,
1980). In a hotel context, satisfaction is shaped across multiple touchpoints ra ther than by a
single interaction. An AI chatbot may assist before arrival, a mobile application may support
check-in and smart -room features may influence the stay itself. When these services are
dependable, convenient and appropriately personalised , they should improve the overall
evaluation of the experience. Empirical evidence supports this expectation. AI -supported
personalisation and service attributes have been associated with greater hotel guest
satisfaction (Al -Hyari et al., 2023; Makivić et al., 2024; Chotisarn & Phuthong, 2025).
Accordingly:
H1: AI-enabled service quality is positively associated with guest satisfaction.

AI-enabled service quality and loyalty intentions
Relationship Marketing Theory explains that durable customer relationships develop through
value, trust and commitment (Morgan & Hunt, 1994). In hospitality, loyalty is expressed
through revisit intentions, recommendation and preference for the same hotel or brand. AI-
enabled service quality may contribute to these intentions by reducing inconvenience and
signalling that the hotel can deliver dependable future experiences.

This relationship may be particularly salient in luxury hotels. Guests expect a high degree of
consistency across the customer journey. Intelligent systems that remember preferences,
provide timely assistance and operate securely can reinforce perceptions of competence and
attentiveness. Conversely, a malfunctioning or insensitive AI service may be interpreted as a
failure of the hotel rather than merely a technical defect. Recent evidence indicates that AI
service quality can influence loyalty both directly and through broader customer evaluations
(Prentice et al., 2020; Sardesai et al., 2024; Chotisarn & Phuthong, 2025).

Technology alone, however, is unlikely to sustain a luxury relationship. The strongest
proposition is not that AI replaces human hospitality, but that well -integrated AI supports a
more reliable and personalised service system. When guests view this system favourably, they
should be more willing to return and recommend the hotel. Thus:
H2: AI-enabled service quality is positively associated with guest loyalty intentions.

Guest satisfaction, loyalty intentions and mediation
Satisfaction is one of the most established antecedents of customer loyalty. Positive post -
consumption evaluations increase willingness to repurchase, recommend and maintain a
relationship with the provider (Oliver, 1997, 1999). In hotels, satisfaction rep resents the
guest’s cumulative assessment of accommodation, employee interactions, facilities and
technology-supported services. Guests who believe that the overall experience met or
exceeded expectations should therefore express stronger loyalty intentions.
H3: Guest satisfaction is positively associated with guest loyalty intentions.

Service research commonly proposes a sequence in which service quality shapes satisfaction,
which subsequently influences loyalty. In this account, service quality is a relatively specific
performance evaluation, whereas satisfaction is the broader psychol ogical judgement that
translates performance into future behavioural  intention. Studies in technology -supported
hospitality have reported evidence consistent with this sequence (Prentice et al., 2020;
Sardesai et al., 2024).

Nevertheless, AI may also provide a direct signal of a hotel’s future capability. A guest could
develop confidence in the hotel because its digital services are consistently reliable and
responsive, even when overall satisfaction is influenced by many non -technological features
of the stay. This makes partial, absent or context-dependent mediation plausible. Testing the
indirect relationship is therefore important rather than assuming that satisfaction necessarily
carries the effect.
H4: Guest satisfaction mediates the relationship between AI -enabled service quality and
guest loyalty intentions.

Methodology
Research design and participants
The study adopted a quantitative, cross -sectional design to examine the proposed
relationships at one point in time. This design was consistent with the deductive purpose of
testing theoretically derived hypotheses using numerical data (Saunders et al., 20 23). The
target population comprised adults who had stayed at a luxury hotel in Malaysia and used at
least one AI -enabled hotel service, including a chatbot, mobile check -in application, self -

service kiosk, digital concierge, smart -room control, biometric system, service robot or
personalised recommendation service.

A complete sampling frame of eligible luxury -hotel guests was unavailable; therefore,
purposive non-probability sampling was used. Participants had to be at least 18 years old,
have stayed at a Malaysian luxury hotel within the preceding 12 months and have  direct
experience of at least one AI -enabled service. The questionnaire was administered through
Google Forms, with the survey link circulated through hotel -guest networks, travel forums
and social-media channels. Of 170 distributed questionnaires, 115 usable responses remained
after eligibility and data-quality screening. Luxury-hotel eligibility was based on respondents’
identification of the property as a luxury hotel; the property’s formal star classification was
not independently verified. Because par ticipation was purposive and voluntary, the sample
should not be treated as statistically representative of all luxury-hotel guests in Malaysia.

The study followed the institutional research -ethics procedures reported in the original
thesis. Participants were informed of the study’s purpose, the voluntary nature of
participation, confidentiality and their right to discontinue before providing conse nt. No
directly identifying information was collected, survey records were password-protected, and
results were reported only in aggregate.

Measures
The questionnaire contained demographic questions followed by measures of AI -enabled
service quality, guest satisfaction and guest loyalty intentions. Items were adapted from
established hospitality and service -marketing measures and reworded for AI -supported
luxury-hotel encounters (Parasuraman et al., 1988; Prentice et al., 2020; Sardesai et al., 2024;
Chotisarn & Phuthong, 2025). All construct items used a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1
(strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

AI-enabled service quality was measured with 14 items representing tangibles, reliability,
responsiveness, assurance and empathy. Guest satisfaction and loyalty intentions were each
measured with three items. Expert review was used to assess clarity and co ntextual
relevance, followed by a pilot study involving 50 eligible respondents. The pilot participants
were used to identify ambiguous wording and assess preliminary consistency, and their
responses were excluded from the final analysis. The results reported below are based on the
separate main sample of 115 responses.

In the main sample, the overall AI -enabled service -quality scale showed strong internal
consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.891). Reliability was marginal for guest satisfaction (α = 0.681)
and comparatively weak for loyalty intentions (α = 0.602). These latter values are reported
transparently because they increase measurement error and require cautious interpretation
of the estimated relationships. To reduce common -method bias procedurally, participation
was anonymous, construct measures were presented in separate questionnaire sections, and
previously validated items were adapted using clear and neutral wording.

Sampling adequacy for the exploratory principal component analysis was acceptable (KMO =
0.831), and Bartlett’s test was significant, χ²(231) = 1600.587, p < 0.001. Five components

Data analysis
IBM SPSS Statistics Version 29 was used for data screening and analysis. Frequencies, means
and standard deviations described the sample and construct scores. Spearman’s rank -order
correlations assessed bivariate monotonic relationships. Simple regressions  tested H1 –H3.
The proposed mediation was assessed using the available regression paths: path a, the total
effect c, the direct effect c′ and path b after both the predictor and mediator were included
in the loyalty model. The indirect effect was calculated as ab using the regression-based logic
described by Hayes (2022); its normal-theory significance was approximated using the Sobel
(1982) procedure. Because respondent -level data and resampling output were not retained
with the thesis, a bootstrapped conf idence interval could not be reconstructed; H4 is
therefore interpreted conservatively from the available regression evidence rather than
presented as a definitive modern mediation test.

The study evaluated statistical significance at p < 0.05. Given the cross -sectional design,
regression coefficients are interpreted as predictive associations and not as proof of temporal
causation.

Exploratory mediation assessment
AI-enabled service quality significantly predicted satisfaction (path a: B = 0.682, p < 0.001) and
loyalty before satisfaction was included (total effect c: B = 0.850, p < 0.001). In the combined
model, however, satisfaction was not associated with loyalty after AI-enabled service quality
was controlled (path b: B = −0.022, p = 0.755). AI-enabled service quality remained significant
and its coefficient did not decrease (direct effect c′: B = 0.865, p < 0.001). Model explanatory
power remained 56.5%.

The estimated indirect effect was ab = 0.682 × −0.022 = −0.015. Using the available path
standard errors, the corresponding normal -theory standard error is approximately 0.048,
producing z ≈ −0.31. This pattern provides no evidence of a positive indirect r elationship
through satisfaction. H4 was not supported by the available regression -based assessment.
Because a bootstrapped confidence interval could not be recovered without respondent-level
data, this conclusion should be regarded as exploratory.

Discussion
Principal findings
The study examined how guests’ evaluations of AI -enabled service quality related to
satisfaction and loyalty intentions in Malaysian luxury hotels. Three findings stand out. First,
AI-enabled service quality was positively associated with satisfaction. Thi s supports the
argument that established service -quality principles remain useful when service encounters
include intelligent technologies. Guests appear to evaluate AI not merely for its novelty but
for whether it supports dependable, prompt, secure and personalised service. This
interpretation is consistent with evidence that AI-enabled attributes and tailor-made services
contribute to favourable hotel evaluations (Makivić et al., 2024; Chotisarn & Phuthong, 2025).
Second, AI -enabled service quality showed a particularly strong relationship with loyalty
intentions. This finding is compatible with Relationship Marketing Theory because reliable
and responsive service can strengthen confidence in a provider’s future per formance
(Morgan & Hunt, 1994). In a luxury setting, guests may interpret well-designed technology as
part of the hotel’s overall service capability. A digital concierge or smart -room system that
works smoothly can therefore signal competence in much the s ame way as a well -managed

employee service quality to satisfaction and loyalty outcomes (Prentice et al., 2020; Sardesai
et al., 2024).

Third, satisfaction predicted loyalty when examined by itself but became non-significant once
AI-enabled service quality was included. The available regression evidence therefore did not
support the proposed indirect pathway. One interpretation is that the two evaluations share
substantial variance and that perceived AI service performance is more proximal to the
technology-related loyalty items used in this study. Indeed, the loyalty scale asked
respondents about recommending, reusing and choosing hotels w ith AI -enabled service
features. This measurement alignment may partly explain the strong direct relationship and
should prevent an overly broad conclusion that satisfaction has become unimportant.

The unsupported mediation hypothesis should therefore be interpreted carefully. It does not
demonstrate that AI has universally replaced satisfaction as a mechanism of loyalty
formation. Rather, it suggests that within this sample —and with the measures emp loyed—
loyalty intentions toward AI -enabled hotels were associated primarily with direct service -
quality evaluations. Differences from studies reporting mediation may reflect hotel segment,
cultural context, the specific AI applications encountered or measurement design (Al-Hyari et
al., 2023; Chotisarn & Phuthong, 2025).

Theoretical implications
The study offers two modest theoretical implications. First, it supports extending SERVQUAL’s
customer-evaluation logic to AI -mediated hospitality. Reliability, responsiveness, assurance,
empathy and tangibles remain meaningful, but their technological expressions require careful
interpretation. For example, personalisation may represent empathy only when it is relevant
and welcome; system security may contribute to both reliability and assurance. The
exploratory factor results, in which several dimensions combined, reinforce the possibility
that guests do not distinguish te chnology-mediated service dimensions as neatly as the
original SERVQUAL framework suggests.

Second, the findings qualify the conventional service quality –satisfaction–loyalty sequence.
The direct relationship between AI -enabled service quality and loyalty intentions remained
strong after satisfaction was considered. This does not invalidate Expec tation–Confirmation
Theory or Relationship Marketing Theory. Instead, it suggests that technology-specific loyalty
intentions may be formed partly through perceptions of service capability that are not fully
captured by a short overall -satisfaction scale. Future studies should compare alternative
mechanisms, including trust, perceived value, technology readiness, privacy concern and
perceived human–AI fit.

Managerial implications
For luxury hotels, the findings suggest that AI investments should be assessed through guests’
service experience rather than through technical functionality alone. Four priorities follow.

First, reliability should precede novelty. AI systems must provide accurate information,
operate consistently and offer a clear route to human assistance when they fail. A
sophisticated feature that produces errors can damage the hotel’s wider service promise.
Second, responsiveness should be designed across channels. Chatbots, mobile applications
and smart-room tools should share accurate information so that guests do not need to repeat
requests. Integration with frontline employees is especially important for complex or
emotionally sensitive situations.

Third, assurance requires transparent data practices. Luxury hotels should explain what
information is collected, how it supports personalisation  and how guests can control its use.
Biometric and predictive services may improve convenience, but only when confidence and
consent are maintained.

Fourth, personalisation  should complement human recognition. AI can remember room
preferences or suggest relevant services, while employees provide judgement, warmth and
recovery when circumstances depart from routine patterns. The managerial objective is
therefore not maximum a utomation, but a coherent service system in which technology
allows employees to deliver more attentive hospitality.

Limitations and Future Research
Several limitations qualify the findings. The purposive sample of 115 respondents limits
statistical generalisation, while the cross-sectional design cannot establish temporal causality
or actual repeat behaviour . All variables were self -reported in the same questionnaire,
creating a risk of common -method variance. Recruitment information retained in the thesis
is insufficient to establish the distribution of participants across specific hotel brands and
Malaysian destinations. Luxury -hotel status was also self -reported and could not be
independently verified against a consistent star-rating classification.

Measurement is an additional limitation. Although AI -enabled service quality showed strong
internal consistency, satisfaction and loyalty intentions recorded marginal alpha coefficients.
The exploratory component solution also showed cross -loadings and did  not cleanly
reproduce every theoretical dimension. These issues may have affected the regression
coefficients and are particularly relevant to the unexpectedly weak conditional relationship
between satisfaction and loyalty. The absence of respondent -level data further prevented a
bootstrapped indirect-effect confidence interval from being calculated.

Future research should employ larger probability-based or carefully stratified samples, retain
respondent-level data, use confirmatory factor analysis and test indirect effects through
bootstrap resampling. Longitudinal designs could distinguish stated loy alty intentions from
actual revisits. Cross-country comparisons would help determine whether expectations of AI-
enabled luxury service differ culturally. Models incorporating trust, perceived value, privacy
concern, technology readiness and human–AI service fit may also explain when AI strengthens
or weakens guest relationships.

Conclusion
This exploratory research paper assessed the AI -enabled service quality, guest satisfaction,
and loyalty intentions amongst guests with recent experience of AI -supported services in
Malaysia luxury hotel. The quality of service that is powered by artificia l intelligence was
positively linked with satisfaction and loyalty intentions. In addition, when considered in
isolation so was satisfaction with loyalty. Nonetheless, following the inclusion of AI -driven
service quality in the loyalty model, satisfaction became non -significant whereas the
estimated indirect effect was trivial. Based on the existing evidence, it can be inferred that
satisfaction did not mediate the relationship between AI -enabled service quality and loyalty
intentions.

The results of this research not only address the theoretical gap outlined in the Introduction
but also extend the established service -quality logic to AI-mediated luxury-hotel encounters
in an under -researched Malaysian context. Furthermore, it tests, rat her than assumes, the
conventional service -quality–satisfaction–loyalty sequence. The findings indicate that
SERVQUAL is still valid, as guests reacted positively when AI -enabled experiences were seen
as reliable, responsive, secure, and appropriately personalised. The result of the mediation
not being supported qualifies the sequence proposed in Expectation –Confirmation Theory:
within this sample, overall satisfaction did not mediate the relationship between AI -enabled
service quality and technology -specific loyalty intentions. Guests’ perceptions of AI service
performance seem to have operated as a more immediate indicator of the hotel’s
forthcoming service ability. This is in line with Relational Marketing Theory, which emphasizes
confidence in a supplier’s ability to provide dependable value.

Do not mistake the finding as satisfying is not important for hotel loyalty. It implies that the
relationship between service quality, satisfaction, and loyalty may not hold as these are
interdependent in AI -enabled luxury hospitality especially when loyal ty intentions refer to
recommending, reusing or choosing hotels with AI -enabled features. When technology
harmony is in place, human judgement and human warmth can be combined in the service
system. Considering that the study is exploratory and the satisfa ction and loyalty measures
exhibited limited reliability, larger studies using stronger measures, longitudinal designs and
bootstrapped mediation analysis will be necessary before the theoretical interpretation is
generalised beyond the present context.
