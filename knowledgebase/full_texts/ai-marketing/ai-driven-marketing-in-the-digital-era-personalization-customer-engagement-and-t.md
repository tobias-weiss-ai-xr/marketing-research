Abstract
Artificial Intelligence (AI) has moved from an
emerging analytical technology to a strategic
component of contemporary marketing. Machine
learning, predictive analytics, recommendation
systems, natural language processing and
generative AI allow firms to interpret large
volumes of customer information, automate
marketing activities and deliver more context-
sensitive interactions. This conceptual paper
examines how AI-driven marketing influences
personalization, customer experience, customer
engagement and consumer behavioural outcomes,
while also considering the boundary conditions
created by trust, perceived value, privacy
concerns, transparency and human oversight. To
address the conceptual nature of the study, a
structured integrative literature review was
undertaken using recent peer-reviewed and
scholarly sources, with particular attention to
studies published from 2022 to 2026. The review
indicates that AI can improve relevance,
responsiveness and analytical capability, but the
effects are not uniformly positive. Recent research
highlights the importance of trust, perceived
usefulness, transparency, authenticity and privacy
in determining whether consumers accept AI-
mediated marketing interactions. Building on
these findings, the paper proposes an integrated
conceptual framework in which AI marketing
capabilities influence consumer behavioural
outcomes directly and indirectly through AI-
enabled personalization, customer experience and
customer engagement. Consumer trust and
perceived value are positioned as positive
boundary conditions, whereas privacy concerns

are positioned as a negative boundary condition;
transparency and human oversight are identified
as governance mechanisms that can strengthen
responsible implementation. The paper
contributes by integrating the technology,
consumer and governance dimensions of AI-
driven marketing into one conceptual model and
by identifying testable propositions for future
empirical research. The study also provides
practical recommendations concerning data
governance, explainability, personalization
controls, human-AI collaboration, employee
capability development and continuous
performance monitoring.

1. Introduction
The marketing environment has undergone
substantial transformation as digital platforms,
data analytics and artificial intelligence have
become embedded in customer-facing activities.
Traditional marketing relied heavily on broad
segmentation, standardized communication and
periodic campaigns. Digital marketing introduced
continuous data collection and multi-channel
interaction, while AI has extended these
capabilities by enabling pattern recognition,
prediction, automated decision support and
content generation. Kumar, Ashraf, and Nadeem
(2024) describe AI-powered marketing as a
developing field affecting multiple marketing
functions, including customer analytics,

personalization and strategic decision-making.
Recent work also shows that generative AI is
expanding marketing applications from prediction
and automation toward content creation,
conversational interaction and decision support
(Cillo & Rubera, 2025; Grewal et al., 2025;
Kshetri et al., 2024).
Consumers now encounter brands through
websites, mobile applications, social media,
search engines, marketplaces, chatbots and
physical channels. This creates an expectation of
relevance and continuity across touchpoints. AI
can process behavioural and contextual
information at a scale that is difficult to achieve
through manual marketing processes, thereby
supporting personalized recommendations,
targeted communications, predictive service and
dynamic content. However, personalization can
also create a tension between relevance and
privacy. Recent empirical evidence indicates that
AI-driven personalization can enhance trust and
usefulness while privacy concerns can weaken
engagement (Teepapal, 2025). Similarly, recent
research on AI-based personalized advertising
indicates that trust and ethical perceptions are
important to consumer acceptance (Markou et al.,
2025).
The growing use of generative AI introduces
additional questions concerning authenticity,
transparency and human oversight. Wang, Sauka,
and Situmeang (2025), for example, examine how
anthropomorphism and transparency in
generative-AI marketing communication affect
trust and behavioural intentions. Kirk and Givi
(2024) further demonstrate that consumers can
respond differently when marketing
communications are perceived as AI-authored,
particularly when the communication is emotional
rather than purely factual. These findings suggest
that the effectiveness of AI-driven marketing
depends not only on technological capability but
also on how consumers interpret the source,
relevance, fairness and governance of AI-
mediated interactions.
The present article therefore examines AI-driven
marketing through an integrated consumer-centric
perspective. It retains the central themes of the
original manuscript—AI capabilities,
personalization, customer experience, customer
engagement, consumer behaviour and trust—
while strengthening the literature base, clarifying
the conceptual research gap, specifying the
methodology of conceptual analysis and
developing explicit relationships among the major
constructs.
2. Problem Statement
Organizations increasingly invest in AI to
improve targeting, personalization, service and
marketing productivity. Yet adoption of AI does
not automatically produce stronger customer
relationships or favourable consumer outcomes.
AI-generated recommendations may be
inaccurate, personalization may be perceived as
intrusive, algorithms may reproduce bias,
consumers may not understand how
recommendations are generated, and excessive
automation may reduce perceptions of
authenticity or human connection. Generative AI
additionally creates questions concerning
authorship, disclosure, originality and
accountability. The research problem is therefore
not simply whether AI can perform marketing
tasks, but under what conditions AI-enabled
marketing capabilities translate into positive
customer experience, engagement and behavioural
outcomes.

5. Literature Review
5.1 AI in Contemporary Marketing
AI marketing capabilities encompass the
organizational ability to collect and interpret
customer information, identify patterns, predict
outcomes, automate decisions and generate or
adapt marketing content. Earlier research
established the strategic importance of AI for
marketing, while more recent literature broadens
the discussion toward implementation,
organizational capability and consumer-facing
applications. Mishra, Ewing, and Cooper (2022)
examined firms' AI focus and its relationship with
operating efficiency, demonstrating that AI has
implications beyond isolated marketing tools.
Kumar et al. (2024) provide an updated overview
of where and how AI can be applied across
marketing functions and emphasize that strategic
implementation and organizational readiness
remain important.
The literature has also moved toward a distinction
between predictive AI and generative AI.
Predictive applications support segmentation,
forecasting, recommendation and propensity
modelling, whereas generative AI can create text,
images, dialogue and other marketing content.
Kshetri et al. (2024) identify personalization,
customer engagement, customer experience and
lead generation as important areas affected by
generative AI. Cillo and Rubera (2025) argue that
GenAI is reshaping both innovation and
marketing processes and identify substantial
opportunities for research on how consumers and
firms interact with these systems.
5.2 AI-Enabled Personalization
AI-enabled personalization refers to the use of
algorithmic systems to tailor content,
recommendations, offers, service interactions or
messages using customer-level or segment-level
information. Personalization can reduce
information-search effort and improve perceived
relevance when recommendations correspond
with consumer needs. Recent empirical work
strengthens this argument. Teepapal (2025) finds
that AI-enabled personalization can increase trust
and perceived usefulness in social-media
marketing, while privacy concerns can constrain
its effectiveness. Markou et al. (2025), using data
from 650 consumers, similarly show that trust and
ethical perceptions are associated with acceptance
of AI-based personalized advertising. These
studies indicate that personalization should not be
treated as an unconditional benefit; its

5.3 Customer Experience and Customer
Engagement
Customer experience reflects consumers'
cognitive, emotional, behavioural, social and other
responses across interactions with a firm, while
customer engagement captures consumers'
involvement with the brand through cognitive,
emotional and behavioural activity. AI can
support these outcomes through real-time service,
recommendation systems, conversational agents,
personalized content and social listening.
However, the quality of engagement depends on
more than response speed. AI interactions need to
be useful, accurate, context-sensitive and
appropriately transparent. Recent research on AI-
driven personalization reports that trust and
perceived usefulness can function as mechanisms
connecting personalization with consumer
engagement (Teepapal, 2025).
5.4 Consumer Behaviour and AI-Mediated
Decision Making
AI can influence how consumers search for
information, construct consideration sets, evaluate
alternatives and make purchase decisions.
Generative AI further changes this process
because consumers can use conversational
systems to obtain summaries, comparisons and
recommendations. Recent literature suggests that
the consumer-AI relationship is becoming more
interactive and potentially more delegated.
Sarstedt, Adler, and Imschloss (2026) argue that
AI assistants and AI agents can increasingly
participate in consumer decision processes,
creating a new context in which AI systems act as
intermediaries between consumers and firms. This
development extends the original article's focus on
consumer behaviour by showing that AI may
affect not only the content of marketing exposure
but also the process through which consumers
discover and evaluate alternatives.
5.5 Trust, Transparency and Privacy
Trust is central to AI-mediated marketing because
consumers must decide whether to rely on
recommendations, disclosures, automated service
and AI-generated information. Trust may be
influenced by perceived accuracy, fairness,
privacy protection, transparency, reliability and
human oversight. Recent evidence reinforces this
relationship: Markou et al. (2025) identify trust
and ethical perceptions as important factors in
acceptance of AI-based personalized advertising,
while Wang et al. (2025) examine transparency as
a factor shaping consumer responses to
generative-AI marketing communication.
The personalization–privacy tension is equally
important. Consumers may value relevant
recommendations while remaining concerned
about how much information is collected and how
it is used. Therefore, privacy concerns are treated
in the present framework as a boundary condition
that can weaken the positive effects of
personalization and customer experience.
Responsible data governance, meaningful
disclosure and consumer control over
personalization are consequently positioned as
managerial mechanisms rather than peripheral
compliance activities.
5.6 Generative AI, Authenticity and Human
Oversight
Generative AI expands marketing automation
from analytical tasks to content creation and
conversational interaction. Grewal et al. (2025)
describe GenAI as affecting communication,
content creation, service and sales, while
emphasizing the need to understand trade-offs
between AI inputs and human augmentation. Kirk
and Givi (2024) report that perceived AI
authorship can reduce positive word-of-mouth and
loyalty in some emotional communication
contexts, with authenticity helping explain the
effect. These findings support a balanced
approach in which AI augments rather than
indiscriminately replaces human judgement,

6. Research Gap
The existing literature has developed rapidly, but
four gaps remain relevant to the present
conceptual study. First, many studies examine
individual AI applications—such as
recommendation systems, chatbots, generative
content or personalized advertising—rather than
integrating multiple AI marketing capabilities into
one consumer-outcome framework. Second,
technology-oriented discussions often emphasize
efficiency and personalization, whereas recent
consumer research demonstrates that trust,
privacy, transparency and authenticity condition
whether these benefits are realized. Third, the
literature contains substantial discussion of AI
adoption but comparatively less conceptual
integration of the pathway from AI capability to
personalization, customer experience, engagement
and behavioural outcomes. Fourth, the emergence
of generative AI and AI agents after 2023 creates
a need to update earlier digital-marketing
frameworks so that they account for
conversational, content-generative and
increasingly delegated decision processes.
The present paper addresses these gaps by
integrating technology capability, consumer
experience and responsible-AI dimensions within
a single framework. Its contribution is conceptual
rather than causal: it synthesizes recent evidence
and converts the identified relationships into a set
of propositions that can be empirically tested
across sectors and consumer groups.
7. Methodology
7.1 Research Design
The study adopts a conceptual research design
based on a structured integrative literature review.
The purpose is to synthesize established and
recent knowledge on AI-driven marketing and
develop an explanatory framework rather than to
estimate causal effects using primary survey data.
The original manuscript was conceptual in
orientation; this revised methodology section
makes that basis explicit.
7.2 Literature Identification and Selection
The review gives priority to peer-reviewed journal
articles, scholarly research notes and established
academic publications addressing AI in
marketing, personalization, customer experience,
engagement, trust, privacy, generative AI and
consumer behaviour. Particular emphasis was
placed on literature published between 2022 and
2026 so that the framework reflects the rapid
development of generative AI and AI-mediated
consumer decision-making. Earlier foundational
works were retained where they provide
established concepts for customer experience,
marketing analytics and AI in marketing.
7.3 Analytical Procedure
The literature was analysed thematically in five
stages: (1) identification of AI marketing
capabilities; (2) mapping of AI capabilities to
personalization and customer experience; (3)
examination of engagement and behavioural
outcomes; (4) identification of boundary
conditions such as trust, perceived value, privacy
and transparency; and (5) synthesis of the
relationships into an integrated conceptual
framework. The resulting propositions are
therefore theory- and literature-derived, not
statistically tested in the present paper.
7.4 Scope and Limitation of the Method
Because the study is conceptual, the propositions
should not be interpreted as empirically
established causal relationships. The framework
requires validation through survey research,
experiments, longitudinal studies or mixed-
method designs. Future studies can operationalize
the constructs and test direct, mediating and
moderating relationships across industries,
countries and consumer segments.

associated with AI-enabled personalization
because predictive and generative systems
increase firms' ability to tailor content,
recommendations and interactions.
P2: AI-enabled personalization is positively
associated with customer experience when
consumers perceive the personalized interaction
as relevant, useful and convenient.
P3: Customer experience is positively associated
with customer engagement because relevant and
satisfactory interactions encourage cognitive,
emotional and behavioural involvement.

10. Major Applications of AI in Marketing
Customer Segmentation
AI can identify behavioural and transactional
patterns to support more granular segmentation
than conventional demographic approaches.
Predictive Analytics
Models can estimate purchase propensity, churn
risk, demand, customer lifetime value and
campaign response.
Recommendation Systems
AI can generate product or service
recommendations using browsing, purchase,
contextual and interaction data.
Chatbots and Virtual Assistants
Conversational systems can provide immediate
responses and support routine service activities.
Social Media Analytics
AI can analyse conversations, sentiment and
engagement patterns to identify emerging
preferences and issues.
Dynamic Advertising
AI can support audience selection, creative
variation and optimization based on observed
response patterns.
Generative AI Content
GenAI can assist with copy, product descriptions,
social posts, email content and conversational
responses, subject to human review.
AI-Assisted Customer Decision Support
Emerging AI assistants and agents can help
consumers compare alternatives and may
increasingly influence the customer journey.

11. Discussion
The conceptual synthesis indicates that AI-driven
marketing should be understood as a system of
interrelated capabilities rather than as a single
technology. Predictive analytics, recommendation
engines, conversational AI and generative AI can
each contribute to personalization and
engagement, but their value depends on how the
consumer experiences the resulting interaction.
This is consistent with recent literature that
emphasizes both the transformative potential of
AI and the importance of strategic implementation
(Kumar et al., 2024).
The literature also supports a shift from a purely
technology-centred view toward a consumer-
governance perspective. Teepapal (2025) shows
that trust and usefulness can help explain
engagement with AI-driven personalization, while
privacy concerns can constrain the effectiveness
of personalization. Markou et al. (2025) similarly
demonstrate that trust and ethical perceptions
matter for acceptance of AI-based personalized

also how consumers use AI to filter, compare and

12. Managerial Implications
Build an AI capability roadmap
Map AI applications to specific marketing
objectives such as segmentation, service response,
personalization, retention or content production.
Avoid adopting AI merely because it is
technologically available.

Adopt value-based personalization
Use personalization where it provides a clear
consumer benefit. Measure relevance, usefulness
and customer satisfaction rather than relying only
on click-through or conversion metrics.

Strengthen data governance
Establish clear rules for data collection, consent,
retention, access and use. Minimize unnecessary
data collection and communicate data practices in
accessible language.
Make personalization controllable
Provide consumers with practical controls over
recommendation settings, communication
preferences and personalization where feasible.
This can help reduce perceived loss of autonomy.
Use transparency strategically
Explain when AI is used, what information is
relevant to the interaction and how customers can
obtain human assistance. Transparency should be
understandable rather than merely a technical
disclosure.
Maintain human oversight
Use human review for emotionally sensitive,
high-value, high-risk or ambiguous interactions.
Establish escalation procedures when AI
confidence is low or customer complaints involve
fairness or harm.
Audit recommendations and content
Regularly test AI outputs for accuracy, bias,
relevance, brand consistency and inappropriate
personalization. Create documented review and
correction procedures.
Develop employee capabilities
Train marketing staff in AI literacy, prompt
design, data interpretation, privacy, ethics,
verification and human-AI collaboration. AI
implementation requires cross-functional
cooperation between marketing, technology,
analytics and compliance teams.
Measure trust alongside performance
Track trust, perceived usefulness, privacy
concerns, complaint rates and opt-out behaviour

13. Theoretical Contribution
The paper contributes conceptually by integrating
four perspectives that are often examined
separately: AI capability, consumer experience,
consumer response and responsible governance.
First, it conceptualizes AI marketing capability as
an antecedent rather than equating AI adoption
with marketing effectiveness. Second, it places
personalization and customer experience as
mechanisms through which AI capability may
affect engagement. Third, it connects engagement
with downstream behavioural outcomes. Fourth, it
explicitly incorporates trust, perceived value,
privacy concerns, transparency and human
oversight as conditions that may strengthen or
weaken the pathway.
The framework also updates conventional digital-
marketing thinking by acknowledging generative
AI and AI-mediated decision support. In doing so,
it provides a basis for future research that can
compare predictive AI, generative AI and agentic
AI across different stages of the customer journey.
14. Limitations and Future Research
The principal limitation is the conceptual nature
of the study. No primary data were collected and
therefore the propositions have not been
statistically validated. Future studies can develop
multi-item scales for AI marketing capability,
perceived personalization, customer experience,
engagement, trust, privacy concerns and
behavioural outcomes and test the model using
structural equation modelling. Experimental
studies can manipulate AI disclosure,
personalization intensity, recommendation
accuracy and human involvement to examine
causal effects. Longitudinal studies can
investigate whether repeated exposure increases
trust or, alternatively, creates fatigue and privacy
concerns.
Future research should also examine differences
across product categories, generations, cultures
and levels of AI literacy. The emergence of AI
agents creates an additional research opportunity:
scholars can investigate how consumers delegate
decisions to AI, how firms adapt to AI-mediated
consideration sets, and how machine-readable
product information influences brand visibility.
Research should also examine whether human-AI
collaboration produces different outcomes from
fully automated marketing communication.
15. Conclusion
Artificial Intelligence is reshaping marketing by
expanding firms' capacity to analyse customer
information, predict behaviour, personalize
interactions, automate service and generate
content. Recent research published between 2022
and 2026 shows that these capabilities can create
opportunities for personalization and engagement
but also introduce important concerns involving
trust, privacy, transparency, authenticity and
human oversight.
This revised conceptual paper contributes an
integrated framework linking AI marketing
capabilities with AI-enabled personalization,
customer experience, customer engagement and
consumer behavioural outcomes, while explicitly
recognizing trust, perceived value and privacy
concerns as boundary conditions and transparency
and human oversight as governance mechanisms.
The framework is intended as a foundation for
empirical testing rather than as a claim of causal
proof.
For managers, the central implication is that AI
adoption should be evaluated in terms of
consumer value, responsible data use, trust and
quality of experience as well as operational
efficiency. For researchers, the framework
provides testable propositions and a basis for
