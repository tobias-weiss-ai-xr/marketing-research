# Practice of Public Relations Marketing and Market Risk Identification Empowered by Artificial Intelligence

Practice of Public Relations Marketing and Market Risk

Fan Huang
Graziadio Business School, Pepperdine University, Malibu, United States

Abstract: Research was conducted to examine how generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) can be leveraged for public
relations marketing and market risk analysis. The study addressed three research questions. In response to RQ1, GenAI -enabled
tools, such as large language models, natural language processing and predictive sentiment analysis, can enhance content
personalization, audience responsiveness and the speed of crisis communication; however, these technologies also introduce ne w
rhetorical and authenticity r isks. AI-based risk monitoring is better than the former way in all the above operational indicators
for RQ2: time range, scope of channels, sensitivity to signals and analysis consistency. Based on the above analysis for RQ3,  a
governance system for the appropriate use of GenAI in public relations and marketing has been put forward. The four components
of this system are algorithm transparency, privacy and data protection, human supervision of high -risk communication
circumstances, and institutional respon sibility for AI-assisted decisions. One of the first results of this study is that the forms of
AI presented in traditional public relations and marketing theories, which are mainly rule -based automation and statistical pattern
recognition, are conceptuall y different from the new generative AI systems. Therefore, the current theoretical system needs to
be modified rather than directly applied. This paper presents a governance -oriented system for the ethical use of generative AI
in public relations and marke ting practices and puts forward directions for future research under the era of generative artificial
intelligence.
Keywords: Generative AI; public relations; marketing; crisis communication; market risk identification; sentiment analysis;
governance; large language models .

1. Introduction
1.1. Scope and Disciplinary Boundaries
Marketing and Public Relations (PR) are relatively close
but have always been considered separate areas. Marketing
focuses on the creation, communication, and delivery of value
to consumers throughout the customer journey, while serving
the interests of both consumers and organi zations.  PR, on the
other hand, refers to the communication processes through
which an organi zation engages with its various publics —
including the media, government agencies, investors,
employees, and communities —to build reputation, trust, and
legitimacy.  In fact, these two functions have become
increasingly intertwined in the digital age because both rely
on based on the same data infrastructure, social media
platforms and audience intelligence systems, and are exposed
to the same reputational risks in a real-time communication
environment.  The two will be considered a single, integrated
communication management function in this study: PR
marketing. The theoretical traditions of the two domains are
distinct and, as such, have been treated separately in the
theoretical system.
The scope of AI technology in PR marketing  practice
includes systems  such as natural language processing (NLP),
large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT and its
successors, machine learning -based predictive analysis, and
automated content creation platforms. A crucial distinction
must be drawn at the outset. The AI discussed in canonical PR
and marketing scholarship (such as the taxonomies of
Davenport and Ronanki and the digital marketing frameworks
of Chaffey and Ellis -Chadwick) generally falls into the
category of rule -based automation, statistical
recommendation systems,  and early machine learning models.
The foundation for generating AI models that can create new
text, images and other contents from large volumes of data
was established long before their development. This paper
studies the results of the above deficiency in applying theory.
1.2. Research Questions and Contributions
The three research questions of this study are as follows:
RQ1: What are the effects of AI technology and generative AI
on the production of PR marketing materials, audience
comprehension and crisis management? RQ2: What are the
differences in terms of spe ed, scope, depth of analysis and
signal sensitivity of AI -based market risk identification
methods compared to those of traditional market risk
identification methods? RQ3: What are the ethical risks and
governance mechanisms for the responsible deployment  of AI
in public relations and marketing? Review and summarize
existing studies, systematically compare AI -based and
traditional risk monitoring methods, present a case study of
an organi zational crisis, and put forward a governance
framework for the responsible use of generative AI.
2. Theoretical Framework
2.1. AI Capability Classification: Davenport
and Ronanki
Davenport and Ronanki identify  three types of AI
applications for businesses that are suitable for the roles of
public relations (PR) and marketing: process automation,
cognitive insight, and cognitive engagement [1].  Process
automation refers to the automatic operation of some regular
or rule -based processes, such as the advertising delivery and
personali zed notification modules, as well as performance
report generation. Although the PR marketing tool can
increase operating efficiency, it does not offer strategic

20
interpretation independently.  Cognitive insights are the
patterns discovered in a large amount of data. These
applications can detect emotions in text, forecast market
trends, and identify emerging issues.  Therefore, this function
will be used to enhance audience intelligence and early
warning systems in crisis communication.  Cognitive
engagement is the interaction among cognitive systems and
people in daily life, such as AI chatbots, virtual assistants and
recommendation engines [1]. Although the above
applications can improve the quality of life for all parties, they
may also  pose challenges such as inauthenticity and human
accountability in practice.
Taxonomies serve as useful classification systems for
analysis and are often based on examining applications of pre -
generative AI. Generative AI systems, on the other hand, fall
under all three categories: one can generate content, analy ze
the demands of the audience, and engage in conversation with
all parties. The cross -category potential exceeds the original
objective of the Davenport and Ronanki taxonomy, and this
paper will investigate how to apply it in PR marketing [1 5].
Rust and Huang's theory of the "feeling economy" shows
that as artificial intelligence assumes more of the analytic and
routine cognitive labor in marketing, its strategic value will
increasingly be in the uniquely human attributes of emotional
intelligenc e, empathetic communication, and relational
judgment [13]. PR is particularly sensitive to this, so to build
the trust of the public, we need to foster a sincere concern for
people in the organi zation's voice rather than algorithmic
optimi zation and achieve "genuine" communication. Kaplan
and Haenlein have put forward that "AI refers to a system's
capability for accurate processing of external data, learning
from that data, and applying the acquired knowledge to make
independent decisions ac cording to a specific purpose or task
flexibly" [2]. Although generated by artificial intelligence,
this does not make it a generative AI; it remains a rule -based
automated system.
2.2. Crisis Communication: Coombs' SCCT
Coombs' Situational Crisis Communication Theory (SCCT)
is a typical way in which AI-augmented crisis communication
is implemented [5]. SCCT is a high -efficiency crisis-response
mechanism that can accurately identify the type of crisis
(victim, accident, and preventable crises ), evaluate the current
level of reputation capital, and adjust communication
methods based on patterns of stakeholder attribution. AI
strengthens all links in the SCCT process: an early warning
system detects potential conflicts in tim e to prevent them
from spreading; NLP -based sentiment analysis observes
changes in stakeholder attribution in real time; and LLM -
based generation creates reply messages in line with the
SCCT approach.[5]. However, a typical constraint of SCCT
is that it wa s originally proposed for crisis communication
under conditions of a few hours' notice and mass media. In
the age of generative AI, crises are emerging at a rapid pace,
demanding real -time multi -platform solutions, and they may
even be initiated or exacerb ated by AI -generated
disinformation; thus, SCCT is now being applied to fields that
were not included in its original design [1 6, 17].
3. AI-Empowered Public Relations
Marketing Practices
3.1. Intelligent Content Creation and
Personalization
Generative AI systems can generate PR materials at a much
higher speed and a significantly lower cost now. LLMs
trained in accordance with the brand's standards, industry
language and regulatory requirements can generate press
releases, social media posts and executive statements
independently in a short period [2, 3].  Personali zation
represents another important application of AI.  Divide the
audience into many small groups and present personali zed
content based on their interests and communication history
via AI systems.[3]. Research shows that, with the help of AI -
driven personali zation, people are more likely to pay attention
to, remember and buy products based on personali zed
messages compared with older segmented methods [3]. This,
in turn, operationali zes the co-creation paradigm of Prahalad
and Ramaswamy to promote competitive advantage through
personali zed stakeholder experiences at an industrial scale
[14]. This development also reflects the Marketing 4.0
paradigm proposed by Kotler, Kartajaya, and Setiawan , who
argue that digital connection will enable communication with
all people as whole individuals rather than dividing them by
demographics. This aim is now being achieved on a large
scale for people through generative AI -powered
personali zation [9].
AI will boost efficiency, but it also has some rhetorical
problems. As Pillar's case study of Vanderbilt University's use
of ChatGPT to write a crisis response for a campus shooting
shows, when AI authorship is disclosed or discovered in
emotionally charged communication, the idea of "rhetorical
humanity" disintegrates – that is to say, the communication is
regarded as inauthentic regardless of its substantial content
[16]. This result indicates that in a few kinds of
communication situations, such as bereavement, crisis
empathy, and high -stakes stakeholder engagement, AI content
creation is not only less effective but also actively
counterproductive; thus, human authorshi p will need to be
preserved out of a strategic communication approach rather
than just for regulatory compliance.
3.2. Social Listening and Predictive Sentiment
Analysis
NLP-based social listening services collect information
about a company's situation from all sorts of places at the
same time and in large quantities, such as social media, news,
and online reviews. Stieglitz and Dang -Xuan [7] have found
that the degree of  negative emotions in one's own experience
will affect whether they share their personal lives on social
media; Therefore, to facilitate early warning of crises and
proactive narrative management, sentiment analysis can be
employed. More advanced algorithm s can identify the topic
cluster, narrative structure, influencer network and early
spread pattern of a story to predict whether it will be widely
shared [4-5]. Arceneaux and Johnson [6] have pointed out that
the audience of the media is increasingly divid ed politically
and by topic. Therefore, a system of all -encompassing
listening across multiple platforms is now required for the
operation of the system. If only the large channels of
distribution are used by a company, it will be out of touch with
the smaller areas that are more likely to generate a story for

21
building a reputation. A development of predictive sentiment
analysis. Train an AI model on historical crisis data to predict
public responses before they are publicly expressed by
stakeholders and adjust the communication plan proactively
[5, 18].
Guzman and Lewis do not think that generative artificial
intelligence will be a new way to carry out the company's
present public relations work; rather, they believe it will be
used in different parts of the organi zation to communicate
with all parties. AI literacy, which refers to a person's ability
to critically evaluate the accuracy, reliability and other
connotations of AI -generated content, is therefore considered
a required quality for public relations professi onals rather
than an extra form of training [1 5]. Bibliometric analysis of
AI-PR research from 2018 to 2023 has also shown that the
number of studies in this field has been rising rapidly.
Recently, rather than discussing the advantages of AI, some
scholars have focused on the technical and ethical pro blems
in specific applications [ 19].
3.3. Crisis Communication Support: The
OpenAI Case
AI's functions are aligned  with the operat ional
requirements  of SCCT. However, according to the actual
cases, both AI tools and the existing theory have their own
deficiencies. The November 2023 OpenAI crisis saw the
board suddenly remove CEO Sam Altman and issued a vague
statement that he had not shown "consistent  candor in his
communications"; as a result, some 700 of the company's
employees were at risk of leaving, major shareholders
intervened, and Altman was reappointed to his post within
five days [1 7]. OpenAI is now an all -around AI company.
Leadership in technology for AI does not necessarily result in
good crisis handling, internal management, or
communication with stakeholders.  The reasons for this are
that the board lacks a decision -making mechanism, and there
is a large information asymmetry among the board, staff,
investors and partners.
The first communication of the board did not meet the basic
SCCT requirements; that is, it failed to correctly identify the
nature of the crisis and did not provide the stakeholders with
a practical basis for attribution. No reasons were given;
therefore, a vacuum in the information was filled with
conjecture. The internal stakeholders (workers and key
partners, such as Microsoft) were not well prepared for the
public announcement [1 7], and thus the order and credibility
of the communication process were affected. Liu and Burnap
further pointed out the problems of new language and
contextual ambiguity in their study on using machine learning
for crisis classification, and the opaque st atement from the
OpenAI board provided an example of this classification
challenge for stakeholders [8].
But this mobili zation of employees should not be regarded
as an unusual phenomenon in the history of the AI era; there
were also collective pressures on management decisions in
the previous days before the current AI era. A new way of
reading this is that it is a case tha t shows a pattern of
communication that is less obvious in classical PR theory:
internal publics can use the visibility provided by social
media, mobility in the labor market, and investor attention to
prompt a rapid revision of management decisions. As a result,
the senior staff did not directly oppose the board, but they
exerted pressure that led to the change in plans.
Veil, Buehner and Palenchar studied literature on social
media in crisis communication and found that it has changed
the speed and form of crisis information dissemination; as a
result, new requirements and opportunities for early warning
have emerged [12]. According to the OpenAI case, the board
did not anticip ate the scale and speed of the response from
stakeholders at that time. In short, a high -tech AI company
does not need many AI tools; rather, its monitoring system,
alarm-response mechanism and human -review process all
need to work together smoothly. AI systems can enhance
organi zational intelligence and responsiveness, but they
cannot compensate for failures in governance,
communication processes, or stakeholder management.
Answer to RQ1: AI tools such as large language models
(LLMs) and natural language processing (NLP) sentiment
analysis systems can be used to customi ze the distribution of
public relations (PR) marketing materials and understand the
public's emotions more deeply. However, generative AI raises
problems of authenticity in the context of empathetic
communication, and the current framework of SCCT needs to
be revised to address the dynamics of crises in the age of AI.

4.2. Emerging and Novel Risk Categories
AI monitoring has broadened the range of risk
identification and corrected some long -standing issues. The
new problem of regulatory compliance risk in marketing
communications. An AI system will automatically determine
whether the advertised goods comply w ith laws on consumer
protection and privacy regulations for data in advertising.
Algorithmic bias audits are systematic analyses of biased
tendencies in AI marketing systems for targeting or
personali zation, and they should serve as both compliance
measures and proactive reputation risk management tools [1 -
2].
Answer to RQ2: AI -based market risk identification
performs better than the traditional method across all main
links of operation (see Table 1). The three kinds of
enhancements are continuous observation, weak -signal
identification and prediction. At the same time, AI has
introduced new problems for regulatory compliance,
algorithmic bias and governance supervision, and therefore
needs to be monitored and managed separately from
traditional ris k frameworks.
5. Discussion
5.1. Theoretical Reinterpretation for the
GenAI Era
Evidence synthesis in sections 3 and 4 shows that there is a
general trend: although classic communication theories, such
as Coombs' SCCT, Grunig and Hunt's two -way symmetrical
model, and Davenport and Ronanki's capacity taxonomy,
offer the required analytical support, they need  to be actively
reinterpreted in the context of generative artificial intelligence
(AI). Grunig and Hunt's two -way symmetrical model
proposes that, to the extent possible, an organi zation and its
publics should engage in mutual dialogue to perform public
relations [4]. The most practically implementable
operationali zations of this idea in history that allow
corporations to monitor and respond to the public mood
consistently and at a population level are AI -enhanced social
listening and real -time sentiment analysis. Veil and Palenchar
have investigated possible applica tions of GenAI in PR work,
and although practitioners are starting to use it, the main
obstacles preventing wider use are a lack of openness,
oversight and authenticity [2 0]. However, the same
technologies that allow for symmetrical communication also
enable one -way algorithmic manipulation of the public's
perception of mood; thus, a tension has arisen that the original
two-way model did not predict, and governance framework s
are required that extend beyond what any communication
theory has yet provided [1 5, 18].
5.2. The Human-AI Boundary in PR Practice
A general trend of the studies on content production and
crisis communication is that human rhetorical judgment
cannot be removed in high -risk situations. The Vanderbilt
case [16] and the OpenAI board debacle [1 7] both show that,
although AI technologies can enhance expressive capacity,
they still lack context -aware judgement, empathy, credibility,
and ethical responsibility expected of organi zational
communication.  This is consistent with Rust and Huang’s
“feeling economy” theory  [13]: with the rise of generative AI,
people's need for human connection due to emotions and
social relationships will increase, and AI will be used for
analytical and routine thinking. And it has been verified
empirically already. Practically speaking, the compe tence of
PR marketing in the AI era will not be about using AIGC tools,
but rather about the practitioner's ability to identify what kind
of communication should be created by human writers and
then deciding on that basis.
6. Conclusion
With the application of new methods, generative artificial
intelligence has begun to be used in some areas of public
relations marketing and market -risk assessment and is now
reshaping them. Generative AI promotes the development of
communication and intel ligence at a higher level. OpenAI's
crisis scenario suggests that AI -enhanced crisis monitoring
will be used to help reduce losses, and people will still be
involved in the response. The vague and ill -defined
statements of the board were not due to a failu re of AI
technology but rather problems in governance, information
flow and stakeholder perception that AI technology cannot
resolve on its own. The four principles of governance at the
end of the research are: transparency in AI -powered
communication, hum an oversight of high -risk situations,
continuous assessment of algorithmic bias, and ongoing
monitoring to ensure compliance with regulations. The above
are practical rules for organi zations that wish to use AI
responsibly in PR marketing.
In the future, some empirical studies will be carried out to
investigate the actual circumstances and results of different
AI governance models in various organi zations and
institutional cultures.  Future research will explore the long -
term impact of AI -mediated communication on stakeholder
trust, whether AI-enhanced public relations leads to improved
relation al outcomes or to the emergence of new forms of
perceived inauthenticity, and propose a revised crisis
communication theory that accounts for the specific nature of
crises in the era of AI, including crises caused by AI systems.
