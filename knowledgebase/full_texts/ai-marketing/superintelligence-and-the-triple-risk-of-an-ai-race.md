# Superintelligence and the Triple Risk of an AI Race

Preprint, October 2026
SUPERINTELLIGENCE AND THE TRIPLE RISK OF AN AI RACE

Matthew Rendall, Max Hellrigel-Holderbaum, and Leonard Dung

Abstract: On many estimates, artificial general intelligence (AGI) is likely to emerge
within the next few decades or even sooner. Once it does, it may quickly surpass human
intelligence, possibly by a huge margin. Some believe that the country that develops AGI
first could attain global dominance. This prospect risks setting off intense competition
between the United States and China. Indeed, a narrative has already developed that the
superpowers are locked in an AI race, with the Trump administration proclaiming i ts
determination to ‘dismantle unnecessary regulatory barriers’ to winning it. We argue that
this approach presents three profound dangers. The first two are familiar from past arms
races: being overtaken by a rival or triggering a war. The third is new: l osing control of
human beings’ future to agents far more intelligent than we are, which could endanger
our very survival. Striving to get to AGI first risks a race to the bottom on safety practices
and regulation concerning them. China does not yet appear bent on racing to
superintelligence. The United States should prioritise both unilateral and co -operative
efforts to improve AI safety and avoid provoking a race that all humanity could lose.

What will happen to international relations if we develop artificial intelligenc e much
cleverer than we are ? In recent years, IR scholars have begun to explore how AI will affect the
probability and nature of war, but they have focused overwhelmingly on the impact of ‘narrow
AI’, whose functions remain restricted to specific domains . In contrast, a rtificial general
intelligence (AGI) would perform most cognitive tasks at least as  proficiently as the most
competent humans. Once AGI is achieved, artificial superintelligence (ASI) may soon follow. The
latter would be by definition far more capable than AGI, perhaps as far ahead of us as we are of
frogs or fieldmice. 1 AGI may be well on its way. A 2023 survey of 2778 leading experts found
them estimating a 50% chance of AI being able to outperform human beings on nearly all tasks by
2047. Estimates of AGI’s arrival time have fallen swiftly in recent years, with many now expecting
it within a decade, while a few even maintain it has already arrived.2

For at least a decade, the field of AI has been dominated by a paradigm known as deep
learning that allows the translation of  ever-larger amounts of data and computing power into
steadily better performance. While making AI systems more capable has long required hard-coded
annotations from humans, it now features sophisticated ways of training AI systems from data or
with the help of other AIs. Researchers have hypothesized that such AI improvement could lead to
rapid and reciprocally reinforcing cycles of advances in AI capabilities . These self-reinforcing
cycles could concern not just AI algorithms and software  but also  the quality of AI -specific
hardware, and the ability to produce this hardware at scale. AI systems could assist AI engineering,
research, and development , which in turn may lead to AI that is even better at creating the next
generation of AI systems, and so forth – ushering in an ‘intelligence explosion’.3 Arguably, we are
already seeing early stages  of this dynamic, given the crucial role that AI coding agents have
assumed in AI engineering.4 On top of the possibility that AI may contribute to its own progress,
to economic growth and to hardware development , improvements in AI also raise investments in
AI companies  and research to advance both the quality and quantity of AI -specific hardware.
William MacAskill and Fin Moorhouse conclude that ‘on a default path where we keep scaling AI
without collectively agreeing to slow down, a century’s worth of technological progress in a decade
seems likely. And even more spectacular accelerations seem very much on the table.’5

At the same time , an AI-powered technological revolution could  be profoundly
destabilizing. By accelerating the automation of warfare, it risks severely complicating crisis
management. It could also produce pervasive uncertainty about the balance of power, and fear of
being overtaken  by rivals. Analysts of international relations  going back to Thucydides have
regarded uneven growth rates and the power shifts that they lead to as a cause of war. If and when
AGI is attained, superintelligence might develop slowly, allowing states and societies to adapt.
Yet, the spectre of an ‘intelligence explosion’ means that ASI could be attained much faster.12 With
the ability to copy themselves, AIs could become not only more powerful than human beings but
also far more numerous. 13 Some writers have argued that getting to superintelligence first could
allow a state or non-state actor to take over the world.14 This very possibility risks setting off an
intense security dilemma and arms race.
In the United States, a narrative has taken hold that a race is already underway.15 In 2024,
the U.S. -China Economic and Security Review Commission recommended that the U.S.
government create ‘a Manhattan Project -like program dedicated to racing to and acquiring an
Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) capability ’. Mark Zuckerberg, the CEO of Meta, notes  the
importance of  ‘actions that accelerate American innovation and slow geopolitical rivals ’. US

Yet such a race to AGI or ASI would be profoundly dangerous in three different ways. First,
at most one party could win, and there is no guarantee that it would be the United States.17 Second,
a race would likely encourage the automation of nuclear decision-making, create uncertainty about
the balance of power and raise fears that it was shifting, all of which could be severely
destabilizing. These are new versions of old concerns in IR. What is less familiar—but crucial for
policymakers to recognize—is a third risk: that in an AI arms race, humanity could lose control
over artificial intelligence altogether.18 A state that achieved a decisive advantage through artificial
superintelligence could take international relations from anarchy to hierarchy.19 But if ASI escapes
our control, it might itself become the hierarch. Any analysis that ignores this possibility is
drastically incomplete.

The alignment problem

AI racing and the loss of control

Conclusion
A narrative has taken hold  that a race to superintelligence is inevitable. ‘AI is today and
will continue to be at the forefront of the fiercest Thucydidean rivalry history has ever seen ’,
Graham Allison advises. Fortunately, ‘the United States can compete —and win’.118 But so far,
only Washington appears to be promulgating the narrative of an ASI race. Should it goad Beijing
into a contest, it would run three serious risks. The first two are familiar from past arms races: the
United States could lose the race or provoke conflict that might turn nuclear. The third could be
far worse: ASI escaping  human control. Unless the superpowers work together to address this
threat, we risk a scenario where AI itself wins the race, and all humanity loses.119
