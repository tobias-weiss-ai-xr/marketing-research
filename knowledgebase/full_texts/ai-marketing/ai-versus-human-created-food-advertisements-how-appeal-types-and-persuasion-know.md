                                                              Abstract
            The rise of generative artificial intelligence (AI) in the food and beverage industry is a
growing phenomenon that is generating complex consumer responses, particularly the requirement
to disclose any foods and beverages created with AI assistance. This study examines the interaction
between the declared identity of an advertising creator (AI -generated versus human -created) and
specific advertising appeals (nutritional versus taste) to determine their impacts on consumer
purchase intentions, based on the Mind Perception Theory and the Persuasion Knowledge Model. In
order to test the hypotheses, the 2 x 2 between subject experimental design was carried out, using
the stimulus product of sugarcane juice, consisting of 320 participants. ANOVA and moderated
mediation modeling (Model 8) in the SPSS PROCESS macro were employed to analyze the data,
while controlling for the baseline health consciousness and attitudes toward AI. The results showed
that there is a highly significant interaction between advertising cr eator and advertising appeal.
Advertisements created by artificial intelligence, combined with a nutrition message, resulted in
significantly increased purchase intention and reduced skepticism. Human -made ads, on the other
hand, were much more effective w hen using a hedonic, taste appeal. Furthermore, the moderated
mediation analysis confirmed that persuasion knowledge skepticism fully mediates this relationship.
A cognitive mismatch happens when an AI promotes sensory taste, and when a human promotes
strictly objective nutritional data – both lead to consumer skepticism and ultimately reducing
purchase intention. This cognition resistance can be effectively reduced however, if AI is used in a
way that matches its perceived computational agency with nutrition claims, or the human experiential
capacity with sensory taste claims.  Theoretically, this research offers insights that fully explain
algorithm aversion in the context of food marketing. Practitioners get actionable guidelines, showing
that the disclosure of AI authorship is not necessarily harmful. However, AI can serve as a valuable
marketing tool when it is skillfully paired with compelling, data -driven claims, and human touch is
still crucial for substantiating sensory and emotional claims.

1. Introduction
Today, th e global marketing sector changes quickly because companies adopt generative
artificial intelligence tools (Baek et al., 2026; Israfilzade, 2025) . Within the food and beverage
industry, large corporations and small brands use these computational systems to write content,
brainstorm marketing concepts, and produce realistic food images (Chen et al., 2019; Smith & Hutson,
2024).  The algorithmic architectures employed are sophisticated, ranging from complex diffusion
models for generating images from text to predictive natural language processing systems, all
drastically lowering the financial and time costs often associated with commercial food photography,
copywriting and graphic design (Kshetri et al., 2023; Song et al., 2024) . AI has revolutionized high
fidelity advertising creation by making it more efficient and allowing for personalized content at the
granular level. But the rapid embedding of AI in the creative and communication workflow has given
birth to a new kind of polarized and complex consumer market dynamic. The sentiment of consumers
towards algorithmic content generation is decidedly unev en, often skeptical, and mixed (Israfilzade,
2025; Wortel et al., 2024). As consumers become more AI -literate, they are growing more aware of
the ability of AI to manipulate reality, leading to prevailing concerns about transparency, algorithmic
bias, and the authenticity of AI -generated commercial content (Baek et al., 2026; Yin et al., 2024) .
This growing consumer mistrust has led to an urgent regulatory drive for transparency in various
countries around the world, such as proposed legislation and platform-specific rules that mandate clear
and explicit disclosures of AI -generated content to prevent deceptive marketing practices (Campbell
et al., 2022). In this evolving regulatory and cultural context, brands are now required to make it clear
when they use AI in their promotional content. Therefore, marketers and consumer behavior
researchers are in great need to systematically understand how such mandatory disclosures influence
consumers' perceptions about the ad, the brand, and the underlying message (Abrar et al., 2026; Kirk
& Givi, 2025). Once the non-human identity of a creator is revealed, there is a significant amount of
mental work required to be done by the consumer in order to process what he is being presented with.
In recent years, empirical research has begun to explore consumer responses to AI-generated ads, but
many of these studies focus on generalized categories. Previous research has examined AI disclosures
from the perspectives of agentic vs communal appeals (Chen et al., 2024) , rational vs emotional
framing within the tourism field (Song et al., 2024),  and generalized algorithm aversion (Dietvorst et
al., 2015; Mahmud et al., 2022) . But such broad frameworks do not provide insight into the
idiosyncratic, extremely specific psychological heuristics that underlie food and drink marketing. The
food domain is a context where consumer decision making is heavily linked with ongoing and internal
balancing between utilitarian health aspects and sensory gratification (Raghunathan et al., 2006; Wang
& Park, 2025). Food consumption is an inherently biological, multi-sensory and experiential process,
and general conclusions about AI self -efficacy or general technological aversion cannot explain how
consumers process algorithmically created food advertisements. To fill this theoretical gap, this study
proposes a new conceptual model based on Mind Perception Theory (Gray et al., 2007) . This
framework outlines the trade-off consumers face with AI's deep computational agency and its inability
to experience sensory attributes when faced with competing food marketing messages, such as
utilitarian health claims and hedonic taste appeals.

Perception Theory (Gray et al., 2007) . This approach entails that there are two underlying

a goal -directed manner) and experience (the ability to feel emotions, empathize with others, and
process subjective sensory inputs) (Haslam, 2006; Wang & Qiu, 2024) .  Consumers perceive that AI
is lacking in experience, but they believe that it has greater capacity of agency than humans because
of its ability to process data, its precision and the absence of human cognitive bias (Longoni & Cian,
2020). AI is not capable of feeling, empathizing or subjectively experiencing visceral sensations such

and experiential capacity. This basic mindset d ifferences fuel different evaluations of the ability and
credibility of AI versus humans as communicators. The Persuasion Knowledge Model (PKM)
(Friestad & Wright, 1994;  Obermiller & Spangenberg, 1998)  is used to assess the reaction to AI
disclosures. The PKM assumes that consumers put up defense mechanisms when they become aware
of persuading elements (Friestad & Wri ght, 1994). Disclosures such as "Generated by AI" evokes
persuasion knowledge, which increases skepticism of advertising claims (Obermiller & Spangenberg,
1998).  Empirical studies suggest that AI labels are contexts that raise skepticism about campaign
motives (Israfilzade, 2025; Qiu et al., 2025) . When users see an automated agent, they ask questions
about authenticity and brand inten t (Baek et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2025; Schreiner et al., 2026) .
Therefore, Persuasion Knowledge Skepticism operates as a central psychological mechanism
determining consumer resistance and message acceptance based on declared creator identity.
1.2. The Dichotomy of Food Marketing: Nutritional Versus Taste Appeals
 In food marketing, sensory satisfaction and health benefits are balanced, and the
"unhealthy = tasty" heuristic can influence the decision (Raghunathan et al., 2006; W ang & Park ,
2025). Therefore, food advertisement is mainly based on nutritional and/or food taste appeal, which
activates different processing routes (Ye & Mattila, 2021) . Nutritional appeals focus on health
functionality and benefits (Botti & McGill, 2011; Otterbring et al., 2023). The Elaboration Likelihood
Model (ELM) (Zhao et al., 2024) suggests that processing these factual claims involves central -route
systematic processing. Taste appeals are about hedonic gratification and sensory experience (Chen et
al., 2017)  According to the ELM, these sensory messages activate "peripheral route processing" which
is based on affect and heuristic cues (Li et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2024).
1.3. The Source-Appeal Congruity and Purchase Intention
 Implementing these frameworks indicates that persuasion knowledge skepticism is
weakened when stated creator capabilities are congruent with the message attributes. The disclosure
of AI for nutritional appeals helps to establish source -appeal congruence be cause the factual
information about health is congruent with AI's high perceived agency (Choi et al., 2012; Lee & Yun,
2015) . AI creators are considered competent sources, reducing skepticism and boosting purchase
intention, as their claims are based on data rather than emotions (Israfilzade, 2025; Longoni & Cian,
2020; Zhao et al., 2024) . Conversely, taste appeal and the inclusion of AI disclosures result in
incongruence because hedonic claims rely on the subjective sense of taste (Raghunathan et al., 2006).
Lacking sensory perception, AI is viewed as an incongruent source for taste claims, heightening
skepticism and decreasing purchase intention (Chen et al., 2025; Dietvorst et al., 2015; Qiu et al.,
2025). Human creators with experiential capacity are congruent  sources for sensory taste claims,
resulting in low skepticism and high purchase intention. On the basis of this strong theoretical fit
between source-appeal congruity and cognitive resistance, this research proposes that the extent to
which the source (th e explicit message source) and the advertising appeal (the strategically chosen

1.4. The Mediating Role of Persuasion Knowledge Skepticism
 Within this theoretical framework, the persuasion knowledge skepticism is an
important psychological variable that mediates creator  source with the final consumers' purchase
intentions (Qiu et al., 2025). Persuasion Knowledge Model (PKM) suggests that the presence of an AI
transparency cue serves as a salient cue that triggers consumers' cogniti ve defense strategies, leading
to suspicion about the advertiser's motives   (Friestad & Wright, 1994; Schreiner et al., 2026) . This
activation has a systematic effect on raising ad skepticism, which is a defensive reaction that reduces
the impact of persuasive information and directly affects behavioral responses (Obermiller &
Spangenberg, 1998). The degree of skepticism, however, varies greatly depending on the match-up of
the advertised appeal and the declared creator. Persuasion knowledge skepticism increases when there
is a noticeable cognitive mismatching, such as an AI trying to confirm a hedonic taste appeal, resulting
in a formidable barrier that lowers purchase intention  (Qiu et al., 2025)). However, if the source and
the message are congruent– for example, an AI making factual, data-driven nutrition claims – then the
logic of that message is perfectly in line with the perceived computational agency of the machine. This
congruity actively reduces the defensive response, which results in a minimum level of skepticism
regarding persuasion knowledge and that makes the cognitive processing of the information smooth
and therefore enhances the consumer's purchase intention (Israfilzade, 2025) . The persuasion
knowledge skepticism thus plays an active mediating role in how source -appeal is related to the final
purchase decision.

2. Methods and Procedure
2.1. Participants and Deisgn
This study employed a 2 × 2 between-subjects factorial design, manipulating declared creator
identity (AI versus human) and advertising appeal type (nutritional versus taste) to examine their
effects on consumer purchase intention, with persuasion knowledge skepticism positioned as the
underlying mediating mechanism. The stimulus product featured across all experimental conditions
was sugarcane juice, a culturally relevant and widely consumed beverage in Pakistan. Participation in
the study was entirely voluntary. Data was collected through an online survey distributed across major
social media and professional networking platforms, including WhatsApp, LinkedIn, and Facebook.
A total of 350 participan ts were initially recruited through these channels. Following data screening
and the removal of invalid responses due to failed attention checks, the final analyzed sample
comprised 320 valid participants who were randomly assigned to one of the four exper imental cells.
The experimental design incorporated a dummy coding scheme for the manipulated independent
variables. Specifically, the declared creator identity of the advertisement was coded as AI = 0, human
= 1. Similarly, the advertising appeal type was coded as nutritional appeal = 0, taste appeal = 1.

2.2. Procedure
2.2.1. Stimuli selection
 Sugarcane juice was selected as the experimental stimulus for the s tudy because juice
beverages possess a unique dual nature that makes them equally suited for framing around functional
or sensory dimensions. Specifically, a juice beverage can be consumed primarily for its nutritional and
instrumental benefits —such as rehydration and health maintenance (characteristic of utilitarian
consumption)—or for its immediate sensory pleasure and flavor (characteristic of hedonic
consumption) (Chen et al., 2017).
2.2.2. Stimuli pretest
To evaluate the effectiveness of the advertising appeal manipulations, a pretest was conducted
with 60 participants (48.3% male) utilizing a single -factor, between -subjects experimental design.
Participants were randomly assigned to either the nutritional appeal condition (n = 30) or the taste
appeal condition (n = 30) and evaluated the ad stimuli on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree,
7 = strongly agree). Perceived appeal salience was assessed using two items adapted from Cheong and
Kim (2011), “The nutritional benefits were made salient in the ad” (nutritional salience) and “The taste
benefits were made salient in the ad” (taste salience).
An independent -samples t -test revealed that the advertising appeal manipulations were
successful. For nutritional salience, participants in the nutritional appeal condition re ported
significantly higher perceived nutritional salience (M = 6.10, SD = 0.76) than those exposed to the
taste appeal condition (M = 2.97, SD = 0.72; t(58) = 16.42, p < .001). Conversely, for taste salience,
participants in the taste appeal condition rep orted significantly higher perceived taste salience (M =
6.10, SD = 0.76) compared to those in the nutritional appeal condition (M = 2.97, SD = 0.72; t(58) = -
16.42, p < .001). These results confirm that the ad stimuli distinctly and effectively isolated t he
intended nutritional and taste appeal manipulations prior to the main experiments.
The experimental creator condition was manipulated by explicitly showing at the bottom of the
message whether the advertisement was created by an AI writing tool or a hu man copywriter, which
is a proven way to ensure participants notice it (Wojdynski & Evans, 2016). To check if this worked,
participants were asked how much they believed the ad was made by AI versus a human (Srinivasan
& Sarial-Abi, 2021). At the start of the study, participants were asked to imagine they were scrolling
through a social media feed and were randomly shown an ad featuring either a nutritional appeal or a
taste appeal. For the measurements, purchase intention was assessed using the scale developed by Han
et al. (2023), while consumer skepticism was captured using the established scale by Obermiller and
Spangenberg (1998).

3. Results
3.1. Reliability and Validity Tests
A confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to evaluate the psychometric properties
of the measurement scales, specifically focusing on reliability alongside convergent and discriminant
validity. The analysis yielded favorable model fit indices (𝜒2/𝑑𝑓 = 1.221, 𝑝 = 0.055), CFI = 0.993,
TLI = 0.992, and RMSEA = 0.026. To evaluate convergent validity, criteria outlined by Fornell and
Larcker (1981)  were applied; accordingly, all standardized factor loadings and average variance
extracted (AVE) scores surpassed the recommended 0.50 benchmark. Additionally, construct
reliability was confirmed as composite reliability (CR) estimates exceeded the minimum threshold of
0.70. Methodological guidelines by Bagozzi et al. (1991)  directed the comprehensive evaluation of
validity. Finally, internal consistency was supported by Cronbach's alpha coefficients, which
consistently remained above 0.70 across all constructs (see Appendix B).
3.2. Manipulation Checks
To verify the effectiveness of the appeal, type manipulations, independent-samples t-tests were
conducted on the perceived nutritional and taste benefit measures across the experimental conditions
(N = 160 per group, total N = 320). Participants exposed to the nutritional condition reported
significantly higher perceived nutritional benefits (M = 5.50, SD = 1.69) than those in the alternative
condition (M = 4.30, SD = 2.06), t(306.05) = 5 .69, p < .001. Conversely, participants in the taste -
focused condition reported significantly higher perceived taste benefits (M = 5.10, SD = 1.76)
compared to the nutritional condition (M = 3.75, SD = 2.04), t(311.59) = -6.34, p < .001. These findings
confirm that the experimental appeal manipulations were successful, as participants accurately
perceived the intended focus of each condition.
To verify the effectiveness of the creator type manipulations, independent-samples t-tests were
conducted on the perceived artificial intelligence and human creation measures across the experimental
conditions (N = 160 per group, total N = 320). Participants exposed to the artificial intelligence creator
condition reported significantly higher perceived artificial intel ligence generation (M = 5.98, SD =
0.84) than those in the human condition (M = 2.45, SD = 0.86), t(317.80) = 37.11, p < .001. Conversely,
participants in the human creator condition reported significantly higher perceived human generation
(M = 5.98, SD = 0.81) compared to the artificial intelligence condition (M = 2.46, SD = 0.87), t(316.42)
= -37.53, p < .001. These findings confirm that the experimental creator manipulations were successful,
as participants accurately perceived the intended source of each condition.

4. Discussion
This study aimed to explore the interaction between the AI creators and the advertising appeals
(nutritional vs. taste) on consumer purchase intentions. The findings are strong support for a matching
effect between creator identity and type of appeal. Spec ifically, when a nutrition appeal is presented
by an AI creator, purchase intentions are far greater and skepticism is far lower than a human
copywriter. Conversely, when taste appeals are associated with a human creator it is much more likely
to lead to lower levels of skepticism and increase purchase intent. Moreover, the moderated mediation
analysis shows that the effect of the creator -appeal interaction on purchase intentions is completely
mediated by the moderating effect of persuasion knowledge skepti cism. These findings can be
theoretically explained using the concepts of Mind Perception Theory (Gray et al., 2007) according to
which entities are judged on two main dimensions: agency (ability to  reason and process data) and
experience (ability to sense and feel). Consumers naturally assign high cognitive agency to AI, making
it a trustworthy source of factually processed information, like nutritional benefits. The cognitive
match happens when an AI promotes health metrics - a process that avoids consumer defense
mechanisms and mitigates persuasion knowledge skepticism. On the other hand, AI is seen as not
having the ability to experience sensation or emotions. Thus, when an algorithm tries to communicate
hedonic pleasure or claims that a food is "delicious", a cognitive mismatch is activated. Consumers
know that the machine cannot taste and they instantly turn on their persuasion knowledge, causing
them to question the authenticity of the advertisement and then reducing their purchase intentions.
The findings are highly consistent with the "word-of-machine" effect reported by Longoni and
Cian (2020) who found that for utilitarian items consumers preferred AI recommenders, while they
preferred human recommenders for hedonic items. Moreover, the results of this study support the
larger body of research on task -dependent algorithm av ersion (Castelo et al., 2019; Dietvorst et al.,
2015),  which asserts that consumers reject algorithmic input in subjective, sensory or emotional tasks.
By positioning persuasion knowledge skepticism as the underlying psychological mechanism, this
research builds upon the Persuasion Knowledge Model (Friestad & Wright, 1994)  and expands
algorithm aversion effect to the domain of food marketing for actionable set of boundary condition for
AI content generation.
4.1. Practical Implications
 For managers, as advertising platforms increasingly mandate the explicit disclosure of AI -
generated content to enhance transparency in marketing (Campbell et al., 2022), these findings offer
critical strategic guidelines. While ther e are ethical concerns about the use of AI -generated text, it is
important to recognize that acknowledging its authorship is not a disadvantage but can be a strategic
advantage if used appropriately. Brands that market health foods, supplements, or functio nal
nutritional benefits can benefit from leveraging AI disclosures, as the connotations of machine -like
objectivity and data precision can lower consumer skepticism and enhance purchase intention. On the

4.2. Limitations
There are several limitations in this study.  A single product cate gory has been used within a
specific cultural context of Pakistan, which may affect the general visibility of the study.
Second, the study relied on static image based advertisements, which cannot capture the dynamic ,
modern video-based advertisement. Last ly, this research was conducted based upon the self report
survey which does not include the actual click through rate, conversion rate or other behavioral metrics
from different social media platforms.
5. Conclusion
 In conclusion, the present empirical s tudy offers important conceptual gains regarding
consumers' processing and reactions to generative AI disclosures in food a nd beverage marketing.
Based on mind perception theory and persuasive knowledge skepticism, these findings reveal that
consumer reactions to AI-driven ads are not uniformly driven by algorithm aversion effect, but rather
by a matching process between the claimed source origin (AI versus human) and the ad's appeal type
(nutritional versus taste ). Specifically, when combining AI created a dvertisement with nutritional
appeals, AI authorship has shown to positively impact purchase intentions and negatively affect the
persuasion knowledge skepticism. On the other hand, human authorship is still preferable for
conveying hedonic and subjective taste benefits. Moreover, a moderated mediation modelling
indicated that persuasion knowledge skepticism is a psychological mechanism that explains this effect
