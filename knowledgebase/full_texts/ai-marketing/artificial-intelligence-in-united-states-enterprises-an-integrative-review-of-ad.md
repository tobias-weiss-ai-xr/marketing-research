Abstract: Artificial intelligence has crossed
a threshold in American business. In just a
few years it moved from a research curiosity
to something firms now treat as ordinary
infrastructure for producing goods, serving
customers, and competing. This article re-
views how AI is actually used in United
States enterprises, drawing together peer -
-reviewed research, nationally representa -
tive government statistics, and large indus-
try surveys published through early 2026.
Three questions drive the analysis. How wi-
dely, and how evenly, has AI spread across
American firms? Where is it producing me-
asurable value? And what conditions decide
whether that value materializes? The eviden-
ce splits sharply. Surveys put self-reported
use above three-quarters of organizations,
while nationally representative firm-level
data place production-grade adoption in
the high single digits, closer to a fifth once
weighted by employment. Real deploy -
ments concentrate in a handful of places:
customer service, marketing, software engi-
neering, financial risk management, clinical
and administrative healthcare, and factory
quality control. Field experiments keep fin-
ding double-digit productivity gains, and
those gains fall mostly to less-experienced
workers. Yet most firms report no effect on
profits. The review reads this gap through
general-purpose-technology theory and
the complementary-intangibles hypothesis,
and locates it within an unsettled United
States governance landscape. Implications
for managers, policymakers, and future re-
search follow.
Keywords: artificial intelligence; generative
AI; enterprise adoption; firm productivity;
technology diffusion; AI governance; Uni-
ted States
1. Introduction
Few technologies have reached the
operational core of business as fast as arti -
ficial intelligence. For most of the last de -
cade, adoption sat near half of survey res -
pondents and clustered in a narrow band
of analytical uses. Late 2022 changed the
trajectory. Once capable generative systems
became public, the numbers jumped. By
2024, roughly seventy-eight percent of or-
ganizations reported using AI in at least one
business function, up from fifty-five percent
a year earlier, and regular use of generative
AI more than doubled, from about a third
to seventy-one percent (Stanford HAI,
2025; Singla et al., 2024). The shorthand
people reach for is that AI stopped being an
interesting technology and became infras-
tructure. This article asks what that shift ac-
tually means for United States firms: where
AI is used, what it demonstrably does, and
what separates the firms that capture value
from the ones that do not.
The United States is a revealing place
to ask the question. Most frontier-model
developers sit here, and the country absor-
bs the largest share of global private capital
flowing into the technology. United States
private AI investment reached an estimated
109.1 billion dollars in 2024 and climbed
to roughly 285.9 billion in 2025, figures no
other economy approaches (Stanford HAI,
2025, 2026). The firm population is also
strikingly varied in size, sector, and digital
maturity. That variety is useful. It lets us see
both the leading edge of deployment and
the long tail of firms for which AI remains
aspirational. Studying adoption here expo-
ses the promise of the technology and the
organizational friction that decides whether
the promise is kept.

One puzzle motivates the review. The
headline statistics that dominate public dis-
cussion come mostly from surveys of mana-
gers and professionals, and they suggest ne-
ar-ubiquity. Government data tell a quieter
story. In nationally representative, firm-level
samples, production-grade AI use remains
a minority practice, heavily concentrated
among the largest employers (McElheran et
al., 2024; Bonney et al., 2024). A second
tension sits on top of the first. Even among
firms that have deployed generative AI,
most report no effect on profitability, despi-
te solid experimental evidence of task-level
gains (Singla et al., 2025; Brynjolfsson et al.,
2025). A credible account has to reconcile
these signals rather than pick the most flat-
tering one.
The article pursues three goals. It first
maps the adoption landscape, triangulating
survey, census, and experimental eviden -
ce so the contradictions become legible. It
then traces practical applications across the
main sectors of the economy, separating ho-
rizontal uses that cut across functions from
vertical ones embedded in sector workflows.
Finally, it synthesizes the evidence on out-
comes, namely productivity, value capture,
and labor, against established theory and
the governance frameworks now shaping
deployment. The contribution is integrative
rather than empirical: it assembles uneven
evidence into one coherent picture and fo-
regrounds the conditions under which AI
pays off. Section 2 builds the framework,
Section 3 describes the method, and Sec -
tions 4 through 7 cover adoption, applica-
tions, outcomes, and governance. Sections 8
through 10 discuss implications, limits, and
conclusions.
2. Conceptual Background
and Theoretical Framework
2.1 Defining artificial intelligence in
the enterprise
In a business setting, artificial intelli -
gence is not one technology. It is a family
of capabilities for doing work that used to
require human cognition. One useful split
sets analytical AI, the machine learning that
turns structured and unstructured data into
predictions, classifications, and recommen-
dations, against generative AI, which uses
large foundation models to produce new
content such as text, images, audio, and
code. A third category is now emerging. In
agentic AI, models plan and carry out multi-
-step tasks with little human intervention. A
more managerial taxonomy sorts enterprise
uses into process automation, cognitive in-
sight, and cognitive engagement: software
executing routine tasks, algorithms spotting
patterns and forecasting outcomes, and sys-
tems talking directly to customers and staff
in natural language (Davenport & Ronanki,
2018). The categories overlap. The most
consequential systems combine them, pai -
ring a predictive model with a generative in-
terface inside a single customer-service flow.
What ties these together economically
is simple. Modern AI drives down the cost
of prediction and, with foundation models,
the cost of generating a competent first draft
of cognitive work. Reframing AI as cheaper
prediction explains why it spreads so far. Pre-
diction feeds a vast range of decisions, and
when the price of an input falls, demand
for it rises and its complements gain value
(Agrawal et al., 2018). Those complements,
namely judgment about goals, the data to
train and ground models, and the capacity

2.2 Theoretical lenses
Four perspectives organize the analysis.
The first treats AI as a general-purpose tech-
nology: pervasive across sectors, improving
over time, and able to spawn complemen -
tary innovation. Current assessments place
AI, and large language models especially,
in that class, expecting economy-wide ra -
ther than industry-specific effects (Furman
& Seamans, 2019; Eloundou et al., 2024).
This framing carries a warning about expec-
tations. The productivity benefits of such te-
chnologies usually arrive late, because firms
must first build complementary intangibles,
including new processes, skills, and organi-
zational designs, before potential becomes
measured output. Productivity can stall, or
even dip, during that investment phase and
accelerate only afterward. Brynjolfsson and
colleagues call the pattern a productivity
J-curve (Brynjolfsson et al., 2021).
The second perspective concerns adop-
tion itself. Classic technology-acceptance
models hold that use turns on perceived
usefulness and perceived ease of use, filtered
through organizational and individual fac-
tors (Davis, 1989), while diffusion theory
explains why adoption spreads first among
actors with the resources and absorptive ca-
pacity to experiment (Rogers, 2003). Both
predict the heterogeneity documented later
here, where large, young, digitally capable
firms move first and hardest. The third pers-
pective is the task-and-exposure approach
to labor. It breaks occupations into tasks
and rates each task’s exposure to AI, yiel -
ding sharper predictions than older job-le-
vel automation studies and separating what
AI can do from whole jobs it might replace
(Frey & Osborne, 2017; Felten et al., 2021;
Eloundou et al., 2024).
The fourth perspective is organizatio-
nal. The resource-based and dynamic-ca -
pabilities traditions argue that advantage
comes not from a widely available techno -
logy but from firm-specific capabilities for
deploying it, including data, talent, gover -
nance, and the ability to reconfigure work.
Early management research on AI made the
point bluntly: returns accrue to firms that
treat AI as an occasion for organizational le-
arning, not as a purchase (Ransbotham et
al., 2017). T ogether the four lenses set up
the review’s organizing expectation. AI is a
pervasive, improving, general-purpose capa-
bility whose value is gated by complemen-
tary investment, so adoption will be broad
but value capture concentrated and lagged.
3. Method
This is an integrative, structured nar -
rative review, not a systematic review run
under a formal screening protocol. The in-
tegrative form fits because the evidence is
methodologically mixed and comes from
communities, including academic econo -
mists, management scholars, government
statisticians, and industry research groups,
that rarely appear together in a single data-
base. The aim is synthesis across them, not
exhaustive enumeration within any one.
Three kinds of source were combined
on purpose. The first is peer-reviewed scho-
larship from leading journals in economics,
management, medicine, and interdiscipli -
nary social science. The second is nationally
representative statistics from United States

4. The Landscape of
AI Adoption in United
States Enterprises
4.1 Two narratives of adoption
Evidence on how widely AI has spre -
ad supports two narratives, and reconciling
them is the whole task. The first comes from
surveys of managers and knowledge workers,
and it points toward ubiquity. In successive
global surveys, overall organizational adop-
tion climbed from about half of respondents
across the late 2010s to seventy-two percent
in 2024, while regular use of generative AI
in at least one function rose from roughly a
third to seventy-one percent in a single year
(Singla et al., 2024, 2025). An independent
assessment put organizational adoption near
seventy-eight percent in 2024 and higher
still, approaching the high eighties, the year
after (Stanford HAI, 2025, 2026). On these
measures, AI use looks like the default.
The second narrative, from nationally
representative firm-level data, is far more
sober. An analysis of the 2018 Annual Bu-
siness Survey, covering more than eight
hundred fifty thousand firms, found fewer
than six percent had adopted any measured
AI technology. Because adoption clustered
among the largest employers, the employ -
ment-weighted rate was about eighteen per-
cent, and more than half of firms with at
least five thousand workers reported some
use (McElheran et al., 2024). Higher-fre -
quency data from the Census Bureau’s Bu-
siness T rends and Outlook Survey showed
production use rising from roughly 3.7
percent in late 2023 to about 5.4 percent
in early 2024, and climbing after (Bonney
et al., 2024; U.S. Census Bureau, 2025).
When the Census later widened the ques -
tion from production to any business func-
tion, firm-level use moved toward a fifth on
an employment-weighted basis, a jump that
reflects both genuine diffusion and how sen-
sitive these numbers are to wording (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025).
The gap is mostly an artifact of me -
asurement, not a contradiction. Surveys
over-represent large firms and professio -
nal respondents, count individual as well
as organizational use, and often ask about
use in at least one function, a threshold
one curious team can clear. Census instru-
ments sample the whole firm population,

4.2 Heterogeneity by firm size and
sector
Adoption is uneven along two axes.
The first is firm size. In both survey and
census evidence, bigger firms adopt earlier,
wider, and deeper, a pattern that follows
from their capacity to absorb fixed costs,
assemble data, and hire scarce talent (McE-
lheran et al., 2024; Singla et al., 2025). One
consequence is that firm-counted statistics
understate how much economic activity
AI touches, because adopters employ a dis-
proportionate share of workers and produ-
ce a disproportionate share of output. The
second axis is sector. Use runs highest in
information, finance, professional services,
and parts of manufacturing, and lowest in
places like accommodation, food service,
and construction. In recent firm-level data,
roughly one in four information-sector fir-
ms reported using AI, on the order of ten
times the rate in accommodation and food
service (U.S. Census Bureau, 2025; McE -
lheran et al., 2024).
The traits of adopting firms tell their
own story. AI use travels with other advan-
ced technologies, cloud computing and
robotics among them, and shows up more
often in younger, more productive firms run
by more educated and experienced owners
(McElheran et al., 2024). Adoption, on this
reading, is less a standalone decision than
one piece of a broader digital posture. Whe-
ther AI’s benefits spread across the economy
will depend on whether those capabilities
move beyond the existing frontier of firms
or stay locked inside it.
4.3 Investment dynamics
The investment backdrop is unusual in
both scale and shape. Global corporate in-
vestment in AI reached an estimated 252.3
billion dollars in 2024, with private invest-
ment climbing sharply. United States pri -
vate investment alone accounted for 109.1
billion and rose to roughly 285.9 billion in
2025 (Stanford HAI, 2025, 2026). Gene -
rative AI drew 33.9 billion of that in 2024,
more than a fifth of all AI-related private in-
vestment. At the same time, the unit cost of
using capable models collapsed. Querying
a model at a fixed performance level fell
by more than two orders of magnitude in
about eighteen months (Stanford HAI,
2025). That combination matters. Surging
capital and crashing unit costs turn use cases
once dismissed as uneconomic into viable
ones, widening the application surface even
when a firm’s budget has not changed. By
early 2026, the consumer value from widely
available generative tools, much of it flowing
through free or cheap services, reached into
the hundreds of billions of dollars a year, a
reminder that a large share of the technolo-
gy’s value lands outside conventional enter-
prise accounting (Stanford HAI, 2026).

5. Practical Applications
Across Sectors
Where AI creates value can be drawn
on two axes. The functional axis names the
activities where deployment concentrates,
most often marketing and sales, product
development, service operations, software
engineering, and information technology
(Singla et al., 2025). The sectoral axis na -
mes the industries where AI sits deepest in
core workflows. The subsections below walk
through the main sectors, with concrete
applications and, where it exists, evidence of
effect.
5.1 Financial services
Financial services adopted AI early and
use it hard. The sector has run machine lear-
ning for decades across credit and insurance
underwriting, trading, investment advice,
customer service, compliance, forecasting,
and process automation (U.S. Department
of the T reasury, 2024). Its most matu -
re applications are fraud detection, where
models flag anomalous transactions in real
time at a scale no manual review can match,
and credit scoring, where algorithms judge
default risk from large and varied data. A
scientometric review tracing the field from
1989 through 2024 documents how ma -
chine learning, natural language processing,
and related methods consolidated across
credit scoring, fraud detection, digital in -
surance, robo-advisory, and financial inclu-
sion (Vuković et al., 2025). Generative AI
has pushed these uses further, into financial
analysis and research, client-facing conver-
sational interfaces, and automated com -
pliance documentation.
Finance also shows the governance
problem more sharply than most sectors.
Credit and underwriting decisions bear di-
rectly on who gets access to capital, so wor-
ries about bias, discrimination, and opaque
model reasoning are acute. The same review
that charts the technical advance stresses
the matching need for explainable AI and
governance that can deliver transparency,
fairness, and accountability (Vuković et al.,
2025; U.S. Department of the T reasury,
2024). The sectors that adopt AI most dee-
ply, in other words, are often the ones where
the demand for trustworthy systems is most
pressing.
5.2 Healthcare
Healthcare has become a prominent
site for AI, across clinical, administrative,
and discovery work. The clearest sign of
clinical diffusion is regulatory. The num -
ber of AI-enabled medical devices cleared
by the United States Food and Drug Ad -
ministration went from a single device in
the mid-1990s, and only a handful by the
mid-2010s, to more than two hundred by
2023, most of them in diagnostic imaging
(Stanford HAI, 2025). Some of the highes-
t-value uses, though, are administrative.
Models support clinical documentation,
medical coding, prior authorization, and
revenue-cycle work, functions that swallow
enormous amounts of professional time. In
drug discovery, machine learning narrows
the search for candidate molecules and pre-
dicts compound properties.
Adoption has outrun careful local
evaluation. A study of AI and predictive
models in United States hospitals found that
most hospitals reported using such models,
yet far fewer independently checked them
for accuracy or for bias in their own patient

5.3 Retail and consumer goods
In retail and consumer goods, AI
points at demand and the customer rela -
tionship. Recommendation systems perso-
nalize discovery. Demand-forecasting and
inventory models cut stockouts and oversto-
cks. Dynamic pricing adjusts to conditions
in near real time. Further back, predictive
models support procurement, logistics, and
fulfillment. Generative AI added a horizon-
tal layer on top, drafting marketing copy,
product descriptions, and promotional ima-
gery, and powering conversational commer-
ce. The functional evidence fits: marketing
and sales is where generative AI adoption
is widest across organizations, and reported
use there more than doubled in a year (Sin-
gla et al., 2024, 2025). For consumer-facing
firms, the draw is personalization and con-
tent production at a scale and marginal cost
that were out of reach before.
5.4 Manufacturing and advanced
industries
Manufacturing shows some of the
deepest integration of AI into physical pro-
duction. Firm-level data make the point:
production use, present in every sector, con-
centrates in manufacturing and information
industries (McElheran et al., 2024). Typi -
cal applications include predictive mainte-
nance, where models anticipate equipment
failure from sensor data and cut unplan -
ned downtime; machine-vision inspec -
tion, which catches defects faster and more
consistently than people; and robotics and
automated material handling. Generative
methods are starting to feed engineering de-
sign. Survey evidence suggests firms in ad -
vanced industries, among them automotive,
aerospace, and semiconductors, are likelier
than others to use generative AI for images
and audio, consistent with design, simula -
tion, and technical documentation (Singla
et al., 2025). And the motivations adopters
report, chiefly automating tasks once done
by labor, show up in the numbers: techno-
logy adopters post higher labor productivi-
ty and lower labor shares than comparable
non-adopters (McElheran et al., 2024).
5.5 Information technology and
software
The software sector both builds AI and
consumes it eagerly. Its most visible appli -
cation is automated code generation, whe-
re assistants suggest, complete, and refactor
software from natural-language and code
context. Controlled evidence shows deve -
lopers using these assistants finish program-
ming tasks meaningfully faster than those
who do not (Peng et al., 2023). Software en-
gineering is among the functions where ge-
nerative AI use concentrates, and more than
a quarter of organizations using generative
AI report using it to write code, a share that
climbs sharply inside technology firms (Sin-
gla et al., 2025). Code generation is only
part of it. AI also supports IT operations, in-
cluding incident detection, log analysis, and
automated support, which helps explain
why information technology stays among
the leading functions for adoption.

5.6 Cross-functional generative AI
and knowledge work
Separate from these sector-specific uses
is a horizontal layer of generative AI applied
to knowledge work everywhere. T ext do -
minates. Among organizations using gene-
rative AI, roughly sixty-three percent use it
to create text, more than a third generate
images, and more than a quarter generate
code (Singla et al., 2025). The most com -
mon individual uses involve capturing and
delivering information through conversa -
tional interfaces, drafting and ideation for
marketing, and automation in contact-cen-

the top: a majority of surveyed executives
report regular use at work, ahead of mid -
dle managers. This layer matters because it
spreads faster than vertical integration. It
needs little specialized infrastructure, which
speeds adoption and helps explain why bro-
ad use coexists with thin measured impact,
the tension the next section takes up.
6. Organizational Outcomes:
Productivity, Value
Capture, and Labor
6.1 Field evidence on productivity
The most rigorous evidence on AI’s ef-
fects comes from experiments that randomize
access and measure performance directly. In a
study of more than five thousand customer-
-support agents at a large firm, access to a ge-
nerative-AI assistant raised productivity, me-
asured as issues resolved per hour, by about
fifteen percent on average (Brynjolfsson et
al., 2025). In a preregistered experiment with
several hundred college-educated professio-
nals doing mid-level writing, exposure to a
generative chatbot cut the time taken by rou-
ghly forty percent and lifted independently
rated quality by about eighteen percent (Noy
& Zhang, 2023). A field experiment with
management consultants found that AI hel-
ped on tasks inside the technology’s current
capability and hurt on tasks outside it, the
jagged technological frontier in the authors’
phrase (Dell’Acqua et al., 2023). Experimen-
tal evidence on software developers points
the same way, with materially faster task
completion under AI assistance (Peng et al.,
2023). Across very different kinds of knowle-
dge work, the task-level gains are consistent
and economically meaningful.
6.2 The skill-leveling effect
A robust and theoretically important
regularity runs through these studies: the
gains are unevenly distributed across worke-
rs, and in a specific direction. Less-expe -
rienced, lower-skilled workers benefit most.
The most experienced improve little, and on
some tasks not at all. Among the support
agents, novices and low-skilled workers
improved by roughly thirty-four percent
while the most experienced were largely
unaffected, and the assistant compressed
the learning curve, letting newer agents per-
form like tenured ones (Brynjolfsson et al.,
2025). The writing experiment found the
same compression of the productivity distri-
bution, with lower-ability workers gaining
more and inequality among them falling
(Noy & Zhang, 2023). The reading offered
is that AI assistants encode and redistribute
the tacit knowledge of high performers, clo-
sing the gap between novices and experts.
That refines the familiar story of skill-bia -
sed technological change. Within the tasks
studied, at least, generative AI can be skill-
-equalizing rather than skill-concentrating.

6.3 The value-capture gap
A sharp discontinuity separates this
task-level evidence from firm-level outcomes.
Despite consistent experimental gains and
wide deployment, most organizations, more
than eighty percent, report no tangible effect
of generative AI on earnings, and fewer than
one in five track well-defined performance
indicators for their deployments (Singla et
al., 2025). Macroeconomic assessments are
correspondingly cautious. One influential
analysis concludes that the aggregate total-
-factor-productivity effect of AI over a ten-
-year horizon is likely modest rather than
transformative (Acemoglu, 2024). None of
this contradicts general-purpose-technology
theory. It confirms it. The productivity J-cur-
ve holds that returns to such technologies
show up only after firms accumulate com -
plementary intangibles, including redesigned
processes, new skills, and reorganized work,
and that measured productivity can stall du-
ring the investment phase before accelerating
(Brynjolfsson et al., 2021). The gap, then,
reads better as an incomplete transition than
as proof the technology lacks value. The task-
-level gains are real. T urning them into enter-
prise results takes organizational change most
firms have not finished.
6.4 Labor-market implications
The labor-market story is best told
in tasks, not whole jobs. Exposure analy -
sis estimates that around eighty percent of
the United States workforce could have at
least ten percent of their tasks affected by
large language models, and roughly ninete-
en percent could see at least half affected,
with exposure spanning every wage level
and reaching well into higher-income occu-
pations (Eloundou et al., 2024). Exposure
means susceptibility to change, not certain
displacement. Firm-level evidence on reali-
zed employment is mixed and, on balance,
ambiguous: adopters report higher skill re-
quirements and stronger demand for skilled
labor, but limited or uncertain effects on
overall headcount, alongside higher produc-
tivity and lower labor shares (McElheran et
al., 2024). The most plausible near-term re-
ading is task reallocation and occupational
change rather than wholesale substitution.
Early signals still deserve attention, among
them reported declines in employment for
the youngest software developers as some
entry-level tasks become automatable (Stan-
ford HAI, 2026). How the distribution
shakes out will depend on whether the skill-
-leveling seen in controlled settings survives
at scale, and on what firms and institutions
invest in training and transition.
7. Challenges, Risks,
and Governance
7.1 Data, talent, and integration
barriers
The obstacles to capturing value are
mostly organizational. The most common
complaint is about data: roughly seventy
percent of organizations cite data-related
challenges, including building governance,
integrating data into models, and securing
enough training data (Singla et al., 2024).
Scarce specialized talent, the difficulty of
wiring AI into existing systems, and the stu-
bborn gap between a successful pilot and a
production deployment pile on top. These
barriers match the theoretical expectation
that complementary intangibles, not mo -
del access, constrain value, and they explain
why larger, better-resourced firms more of-
ten stand up dedicated teams and structured
practices for scaling (Singla et al., 2025).

7.2 Bias, privacy, and trust
A second cluster concerns trust. Algo-
rithmic bias is a live risk anywhere models
inform consequential decisions, including
credit, hiring, and clinical care, and the
under-evaluation of deployed models in
healthcare shows how unexamined systems
can do harm (Nong et al., 2025; Vuković et
al., 2025). Privacy worries follow the large,
often sensitive datasets these models need.
For generative systems, the most commonly
reported risk is plain inaccuracy: fluent ou-
tput that happens to be wrong. The stakes
are rising. Documented AI-related incidents
jumped by more than half in a single year to
a record level, even as public trust in AI pro-
viders to protect personal data fell (Stanford
HAI, 2025). Mitigation lags. Only about
twenty-seven percent of organizations using
generative AI review all of its outputs before
use, which leaves a large share of machine-
-generated content unverified at the point of
application (Singla et al., 2025). Rising inci-
dence plus partial oversight defines much of
the real risk surface.
7.3 The United States governance
landscape
Governance is now inseparable from
deployment, and the United States appro -
ach is distinctive: it leans on voluntary fra-
meworks and sectoral and state-level action
rather than one comprehensive statute. The
central reference point is the Artificial In -
telligence Risk Management Framework
from the National Institute of Standards
and T echnology, a voluntary, rights-preser-
ving, sector-agnostic framework organized
around four functions, namely to govern,
map, measure, and manage AI risk, and
built on a set of trustworthiness characteris-
tics including validity and reliability, safety,
security and resilience, accountability and
transparency, explainability, privacy, and
management of harmful bias (NIST , 2023).
A companion profile extends it to the speci-
fic risks of generative AI. Federal direction
has been volatile. A 2023 executive order
on safe, secure, and trustworthy AI was
rescinded in early 2025, a vivid illustration
of the policy uncertainty firms must plan
around (NIST , 2023). With comprehensive
federal legislation absent, individual states
have taken the lead in writing AI law, and
international standards, including the first
management-system standard for AI, incre-
asingly shape internal governance at United
States firms. The practical lesson for enter-
prises is to treat governance not as an after-
-the-fact compliance task but as a capability
built on purpose, which lines up with the
review’s larger argument about complemen-
tary investment.
8. Discussion
8.1 Reconciling the adoption
paradox
The evidence here dissolves the appa-
rent paradox between near-ubiquitous re -
ported adoption and modest measured
impact. Part of the answer is measurement:
headline survey figures count low-threshold,
individual use among large, advanced firms,
while representative data show deep produc-
tion use is still a minority practice across the
broader economy. Part is structural: gene -
rative AI spread fastest as a horizontal layer
over knowledge work, which needs little
specialized infrastructure and therefore mo-
ves quickly, whereas vertical integration into
core production demands the complemen-
tary investments most firms have not made.

These are two faces of one phenomenon,
an incomplete general-purpose-technology
transition in which capability has outrun
organizational absorption.
8.2 Theoretical implications
The findings bear out the framework
from Section 2. The pervasive, improving
character of AI, paired with the lag between
deployment and measured returns, fits its
classification as a general-purpose technolo-
gy subject to a J-curve (Furman & Seamans,
2019; Brynjolfsson et al., 2021). The con -
centration of the binding constraint in orga-
nizational complements, not model access,
supports the prediction-cost framing, in
which judgment, data, and the capacity to
act become scarce as prediction gets cheap
(Agrawal et al., 2018). The pronounced he-
terogeneity by size and sector matches dif -
fusion theory (Rogers, 2003). And the skill-
-leveling pattern enriches the literature on
technological change by showing that, wi -
thin affected tasks, AI can compress perfor-
mance differences rather than widen them
(Brynjolfsson et al., 2025; Noy & Zhang,
2023). The contribution to theory is inte -
grative: the evidence does not overturn exis-
ting frameworks so much as demonstrate
their joint explanatory power for the enter-
prise AI phenomenon.
8.3 Managerial implications
For managers, several conclusions
follow. First, value capture depends less on
acquiring models than on investing in their
organizational complements, including
workflow redesign, role-based training, data
governance, and outcome measurement
through defined indicators, a discipline only
a minority of firms practice today (Singla et
al., 2025). Second, aim deployment at the
functions and tasks where the evidence of
value is strongest, prominently customer
service, marketing and content, software
engineering, and well-bounded analytical
work, while respecting the jagged fron -
tier and keeping human oversight where
tasks exceed the technology’s reliable reach
(Dell’Acqua et al., 2023). Third, the skill -
-leveling effect opens a distinctive opportu-
nity: raise the productivity of less-experien-
ced staff and speed up onboarding. Fourth,
build governance as a capability rather than
a compliance afterthought, using established
frameworks to manage bias, inaccuracy, and
privacy risk before it bites (NIST , 2023).
The throughline is that AI rewards organi-
zational change, and firms that treat it as an
occasion for learning will capture outsized
returns (Ransbotham et al., 2017).
9. Limitations and
Future Research
As an integrative rather than systema-
tic synthesis, it follows no formal screening
protocol and may reflect selection among
available sources, though the deliberate
triangulation across peer-reviewed, govern-
mental, and industry evidence is meant to
blunt that risk. Because definitions differ
across instruments, headline figures are not
strictly comparable, and the review has tried
to interpret them rather than equate them.
And the rapid churn of the technology gua-
rantees that point estimates will age.
Adoption and outcomes among small
and medium-sized firms, which the data
show lagging, deserve dedicated work, as
do the distributional and labor effects of de-
ployment at scale.

10. Conclusion
Artificial intelligence is moving from
experimentation to infrastructure in Uni -
ted States enterprises, but the move is more
uneven and more contingent than headli -
ne adoption figures suggest. Reported use
is broad and rising fast. Deep integration
into core production is not. It stays concen-
trated among large, digitally mature firms,
and most organizations have yet to see a
measurable effect on their bottom line. The
applications are concrete and expanding all
the same: fraud detection and underwriting
in finance, diagnostic and administrative
support in healthcare, demand and custo -
mer work in retail, predictive maintenance
and quality inspection in manufacturing,
code generation in software, and a hori -
zontal layer of generative assistance across
all knowledge work. Controlled evidence
shows these uses produce real and often
substantial task-level gains, and those gains
fall disproportionately to less-experienced
workers. What decides whether the gains
harden into durable advantage is organiza-
tional: the complementary investments in
process, skills, data, measurement, and go-
vernance that turn capability into results.
For firms in the United States, the question
is no longer whether to adopt artificial in -
telligence. It is how to integrate it responsi-
bly, and how to build the complements that
let its value show up.
