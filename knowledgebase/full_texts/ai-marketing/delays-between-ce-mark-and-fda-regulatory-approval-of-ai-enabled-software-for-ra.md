Artiﬁcial intelligence (AI)-enabled radiology software requires regulatory authorisation before
marketing in Europe (EU) and the United States, but cross-jurisdictional approval sequencing remains
poorly characterised. We analysed 239 AI-enabled radiology software devices with CE marking and/or
FDA clearance from a publicly available European database (Health AI Register) and FDA sources
using Kaplan–Meier methods and Cox models. Overall, 128 devices were CE-marked only, 95 were
CE-marked before FDA clearance, and 16 were FDA-cleared before CE marking. Among dual-
authorised devices, CE- ﬁrst devices had longer median lags to second authorisation than FDA- ﬁrst
devices (17.5 months [IQR, 8.0–35.5] versus 3.5 [IQR, 1.0–11.3]; p < 0.001). Radiograph interpretation
software was associated with longer time to second authorisation (hazard ratio, 0.42; 95% CI,
0.21–0.84; p = 0.01), while EU Class IIa classi ﬁcation (4.99; 1.77 –14.04; p < 0.01) and peer-reviewed
evidence (1.44; 0.83 –2.49; p = 0.19) showed shorter intervals. These ﬁndings highlight persistent
transatlantic asymmetry and underscore the need for greater regulatory coordination and
transparency.
Recent reviews have documented a substantial increase in the number of
artiﬁcial intelligence (AI)-enabled medical devices1 in radiology, approved
by the United States (US) Food and Drug Administration (FDA) 2–7,
reﬂecting the accelerating integration of AI systems into clinical workﬂows8
while also highlighting considerable heterogeneity in their supporting
clinical evidence9. The translation of these technologies from innovation to
routine clinical practice is critically shaped by the regulatory environments
in which they are evaluated. Differences between regional regulatory fra-
meworks, particularly those governing Conformité Européenne (CE) mark
approval in Europe and FDA clearance in the US, may inﬂuence not only the
timing of market entry but also the broader adoption and dissemination of
AI-based solutions. A comparative analysis of AI-enabled medical devices
approved between 2015 and 2020 reported that most obtained CE marking
prior to FDA clearance10, which may be due to less rigorous evaluation of
medical devices in Europe under the former European Medical Device
Directive11.
These variations reﬂect structural differences between regulatory sys-
tems. In the US, medical devices including AI-enabled devices are reviewed
by the FDA through established premarket or 510(k) pathways, while in the
European Union (EU), devices undergo conformity assessment according
to risk class, by designated noti ﬁed bodies12,13.T h i sm a yi nﬂuence evi-
dentiary requirements, review processes, and manufacturers ’ regulatory
strategies, including the sequencing of submissions across jurisdictions.
The EU Medical Device Regulation (MDR)14, which entered into force
in May 2017 and became applicable on 26 May 2021 after a transition
period, introduced more stringent requirements for conformity assessment
and post-market surveillance. These reforms were intended to enhance
patient safety and regulatory consistency, but they may also have altered
authorisation timelines and strategic submission patterns. A key question
concerns whether the temporal gaps between EU and US authorisations

and whether differences in timing vary according to the sequence of reg-
ulatory approvals. Prior work suggested that AI-based medical devices
approved in both the EU and US were more often ﬁrst authorised in the
EU10, while broader device-regulation studies have raised concerns about
evidentiary limitations at approval and delayed recognition of safety
signals15,16. However, the magnitude and determinants of the authorisation
gap have not been systematically quantiﬁed.

In this study, we speciﬁcally examined the temporal gap between CE
marking and FDA clearance for AI-enabled radiology devices under the
contemporary MDR framework. We quantiﬁed time to second regulatory
authorisation, examined differences by MDR implementation period and
regulatory approval sequence, and evaluated device-level factors associated
with approval intervals. By quantifying these time lags and analysing trends
across device categories, we aimed to elucidate how regulatory pathways
shape the availability of AI technologies in the rapidly evolving ﬁeld of
medical imaging analysis and to discuss potential implications for devel-
opers, clinicians, and healthcare systems.
Results
A total of 289 AI-enabled radiology devices were extracted from the
Health AI Register website 17. After excluding entries with missing EU
availability dates or with dates reported only by year, 246 devices were
eligible for analysis. Seven devices with extreme cross-jurisdictional time
lags of 5–11 years were excluded according to the prespeciﬁed outlier rule,
yielding a ﬁnal dataset of 239 AI-enabled radiology devices: 128 (53.6%)
obtained CE marking only, 95 (39.7%) received CE marking followed by
FDA clearance, and 16 (6.7%) received FDA clearance followed by CE
marking.
Table 1 summarises descriptive statistics for all variables included in
the analysis. With respect to clinical subspecialty, neurology represented the
largest share (29%). Computed tomography was the most common imaging
modality (40%), and cancer the most frequent disease target (21%).
Regarding processing time, one-third of devices reported a duration
between one and ten minutes. Clinical evidence supported by peer-reviewed
publications was available only for approximately one-third of devices.
Concerning EU regulatory pathways, two-thirds of devices had been
approved under the MDR and one-third under the Medical Device Direc-
tive (MDD). The distribution of risk classes differed between frameworks:
under the MDD, devices were distributed mainly between Class I and Class
IIa, with none in Class IIb; under the MDR, most devices were Class IIa or
Class IIb, with only one Class I device.

Figure4 presents adjusted hazard ratios from the Cox proportional hazards
model, with computed tomography, EU Class I, absence of peer-reviewed
evidence and absence of other evidence as reference categories. Compared

Sensitivity analysis of FDA date selection
Among devices matched to FDA records and included in the analysis, 21
had multiple candidate FDA clearance dates because the same device
matched more than one FDA record. The primary analysis used the
earliest validated FDA clearance date. When the latest validated FDA
clearance date was used instead, the Kaplan –Meier median time-to-
second-approval increased from 54 to 59 months. Median absolute
approval lags changed from 17.5 (IQR, 8.0–35.5) to 25.0 (IQR, 10.0–38.75)
months for CE- ﬁrst devices and from 3.5 (IQR, 1.0 –11.3) to 4.0 (IQR,
1.0–11.0) months for FDA-ﬁrst devices. The Cox model showed poorerﬁt
under the latest-date assumption, with partial AIC increasing by 30, while
hazard ratio patterns remained broadly consistent (Supplemen-
tary Fig. S2).
Sensitivity analysis of extreme approval lags
Including the seven devices with extreme approval lags addedﬁve CE-ﬁrst
devices with FDA clearance more than 12 months later and two FDA-ﬁrst
devices. The Kaplan –Meier median time-to-second-approval increased
from 54 to 57 months. Among dual-authorised devices, median absolute
approval lags changed from 17.5 months (IQR, 8.0–35.5) to 19.0 months
(IQR, 8.0–38.0) for CE-ﬁrst devices and from 3.5 months (IQR, 1.0–11.3) to
6.0 months (IQR, 1.3 –13.5) for FDA- ﬁrst devices. The Cox regression
estimates remained directionally consistent and similar in magnitude after
inclusion of these observations, although modelﬁt was poorer, with partial
AIC increasing by 35 (Supplementary Fig. S3).
Discussion
In this study of AI-enabled radiology software devices with CE marking
and/or FDA clearance, we found substantial differences in the timing of
cross-jurisdictional authorisations. Most devices had not obtained a second
authorisation within theﬁrst year, and time-to-second-approval appeared
to differ more clearly by regulatory sequence than by timing relative to MDR
implementation. CE-ﬁrst devices experienced longer approval lags before
FDA clearance than FDA-ﬁrst devices did before subsequent CE marking,
although FDA-ﬁrst estimates were limited by the small subgroup size.

Device characteristics and publicly available peer-reviewed evidence also
showed associations with time-to-second-approval. These patterns suggest
that approval lags are unlikely to reﬂect a single factor alone, but instead may
arise from a combination of pathway-speciﬁc documentation expectations,
manufacturer strategies, and device-level characteristics.
Earlier comparative analyses reported that certain medical devices
entered the EU market before obtaining US approval but that earlier EU
access was associated with increased safety alerts and recalls11. More recent
analyses, however, indicate that this pattern may be shifting, with an
increasing tendency for manufacturers to prioritise FDA clearance18.W h i l e
these observations point to an evolving regulatory landscape, they remain
primarily concerned with the locus ofﬁrst approval and do not capture the
temporal sequencing of approvals across jurisdictions.
To address this, our analysis examined the timing and ordering of
cross-jurisdictional approvals. The observed asymmetry should be inter-
preted as a cross-jurisdictional approval lag rather than a direct measure of
regulatory review time, as submission dates were not available. This suggests
that differences between the two regulatory systems are not limited to initial
market entry, but also manifest in the temporal structure of subsequent
approval pathways, or in manufacturers’ decisions about when, or whether,
to apply for a second approval.
Several mechanisms may explain this asymmetry, including dif-
ferences in evidentiary expectations, regulatory procedures, manu-
facturer submission strategies, reimbursement conditions, market-entry
priorities, and documentation requirements. Both have undergone sig-
niﬁcant reform: the continued re ﬁnement of structured FDA pathways
such as 510(k) and De Novo for moderate-risk and novel devices 19,a n d
the introduction of the MDR 18 in the EU. However, these developments
have not eliminated divergence in evidentiary expectations, regulatory
structure, and classi ﬁcation logic. Under the EU MDR, Class IIa and IIb
represent medium- to higher-risk devices and generally require noti ﬁed
body conformity assessment, including review of technical doc-
umentation, clinical evaluation, and post-market surveillance
obligations20. In the US, Class II devices are moderate-risk devices
commonly reviewed through 510(k) based on substantial equivalence, or
De Novo for novel low- to moderate-risk devices without an appropriate
predicate21,22. As EU and FDA classi ﬁcations are not directly inter-
changeable, and clinical data requirements are device-speci ﬁc in both
systems, our ﬁndings should not be interpreted as indicating that one
system uniformly requires more clinical evidence than the other. Rather,
these pathway differences may affect the type, format, and timing of
evidence and documentation needed for authorisation in a second jur-
isdiction. For AI-enabled devices, transitioning from EU authorisation
to US approval may necessitate additional adjustments in dataset con-
struction, performance evaluation, and documentation, thereby
extending approval timelines 23. At the same time, the observed approval
lag may also re ﬂect manufacturer-level decisions, including delayed US
submission, prioritisation of the EU market, regulatory costs, and
available resources for preparing a second submission. Conversely,
FDA-ﬁrst devices may already possess the documentation and valida-
tion structure that facilitates subsequent CE marking. However, because
few devices followed an FDA- ﬁrst sequence in our analysis, this inter-
pretation should be considered exploratory rather than evidence that
FDA-ﬁrst entry generally facilitates CE marking.
Taken together, theseﬁndings suggest that the transatlantic diffusion of
AI-enabled radiology devices is shaped by both regulatory and
manufacturer-level factors, underscoring persistent challenges in achieving
greater regulatory coordination and transparency within an evolving reg-
ulatory landscape.
Variation in approval timelines appears partly related to device char-
acteristics, supporting evidence, and manufacturer location. Certain clinical
domains may face greater regulatory complexity due to higher perceived risk
or rapid innovation, indicating that delays re ﬂect structural differences
across ﬁelds rather than purely administrative factors16,24,25. Peer-reviewed
evidence available before second approval or censoring showed a positive
but uncertain association with second approval, whereas other non-peer-
reviewed evidence showed little association. Since peer-reviewed evidence
was deﬁned as publicly available publications related to the device, rather
than evidence known to have been submitted to regulators, this association
should be interpreted as re ﬂecting publicly visible evidentiary support,
scientiﬁc credibility, or device visibility, rather than direct regulatory evi-
dence. The uncertainty around the estimate further suggests that this rela-
tionship should be interpreted cautiously.
To minimise reverse-causation bi as, peer-reviewed evidence was
considered only when available bef ore second approval or censoring,
because devices that obtain second approval may otherwise have more time
and incentive to generate publications by the censoring date. Speciﬁcally,
peer-reviewed evidence was modelled as a time-varying covariate using a
counting-process structure, so that evidence contributed to the risk set only
after it became available. Nevertheless,residual reverse causation cannot be
excluded, because manufacturers pursuing authorisation in multiple jur-
isdictions may also be more likely to generate and publish supporting evi-
dence. More broadly, the evidence base for AI-enabled radiology devices
remains heterogeneous, with many d eep learning imaging studies being
retrospective, prone to bias, and limited in transparency, and relatively few
prospective or randomised evaluations 26. Thus, publication may signal

fusion, and post-market development of AI-enabled radiology devices. Even
after regulatory authorisation, implementation depends on patient trust,
perceived clinical beneﬁt, concerns about algorithmic error or bias, and
conﬁdence that imaging data are used securely. Recent studies suggest that
patients generally prefer to be informed when AI is used in imaging and

authorised AI-enabled devices are implemented and re ﬁned in routine
radiology practice.

On the other hand, the European Database on Medical Devices
(EUDAMED)32, is intended to provide a lifecycle picture of medical devices
in the EU through six interconnected modules covering actor registration,
device registration, notiﬁed bodies and certi ﬁcates, market surveillance,
clinical investigations and performance studies, vigilance and post-market
surveillance. As of 28 May 2026, theﬁrst four modules are mandatory, while
the remaining modules remain under development 32. However,
EUDAMED was not used as the primary data source because, during the
study period, its data were incomplete and not consistently available for the
aims of this analysis. Speciﬁcally, it did not provide a reliable way to identify
AI-enabled radiology devices, lacked detailed device characteristics such as
modality and subspecialty, and did not indicate when a device became
available on the EU market, which was central for estimating cross-
jurisdictional approval lags.
Future EU–US comparisons would be strengthened if EUDAMED
provided standardised, publicly acc essible device-level information,
including device identi ﬁers, submission dates, CE-marking dates, and
detailed device characteristics. Such information would make regulatory
timeline studies more complete and reproducible, while reducing reliance
on voluntary or curated sources.
Building on this need for more complete regulatory data infrastructure,
the EU AI Act33 may further shape lifecycle oversight for AI-enabled medical
devices. The Act entered into force in August 2024 and established a risk-
based framework for AI systems; high-risk AI systems are subject to speciﬁc
obligations related to risk manageme nt, data governance, transparency,
human oversight, accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity34. For AI-enabled
radiology devices, these requirements may strengthen accountability and
lifecycle surveillance, but may also increase documentation and compliance
burdens for manufacturers, particularly where AI Act obligations overlap
with MDR requirements. Their practical effects on authorisation timing,
clinical deployment, and post-market development remain to be evaluated.
This study has several limitations. Firstly, publicly available regulatory
data in Europe remain incomplete and fragmented. The Health AI Register
used in this analysis is a voluntary and curated source rather than an ofﬁcial
EU regulatory database. As a result, some devices may not have been cap-
tured, and the online list of AI-enabled medical devices with CE marking
used in this analysis may therefore not be comprehensive. Secondly, man-
ufacturers may not always disclose precise approval dates, and our reliance
on secondary sources may introduce minor inaccuracies. Thirdly, submis-
sion dates for regulatory approval were not available from the data sources
used. Therefore, it was not possible to determine whether differences in
approval lags reﬂected regulatory review processes, manufacturer decisions
about when to submit in each process, or both. In particular, longer intervals
between approvals among CE- ﬁrst devices may re ﬂect delayed US sub-
mission, strategic waiting or prioritisation of the EU market, rather than
longer FDA review alone. Fourthly, reverse causation cannot be fully
excluded for evidence variables. A lthough peer-reviewed evidence was
modelled as time-varying and counted only before second approval or
censoring, manufacturers pursuing additional approvals may be more likely
to publish supporting evidence. Fifthly, the analysis was restricted to devices
that had obtained CE marking, with or without subsequent FDA clearance.
We did not evaluate the reverse scen ario, that is, FDA-cleared devices
without CE marking, because absence from the Health AI Register website
cannot be interpreted as absence of CE marking, and FDA-only records
lacked the device-level characteristics required for the primary analysis.
Sixthly, the small number of FDA- ﬁrst devices limited the precision and
generalisability of subgroup-speci ﬁc estimates. Therefore, the shorter
approval lag observed among FDA- ﬁrst devices should be interpreted
cautiously, as additional FDA-ﬁrst cases could meaningfully affect subgroup
estimates and comparisons. Seventhly, excluding seven devices with
extreme approval lags may have affected approval-lag estimates and sub-
group comparisons. However, sensitivity analyses including these devices
showed broadly consistent results. Finally, the analysis is limited to AI-
enabled devices for radiology and may not generalise to AI tools in other
clinical domains.
In conclusion, AI-enabled radiology devicesﬁrst approved in the EU
experienced longer delays to US approval, than vice versa, although esti-
mates for FDA-ﬁrst devices were based on a small subgroup and should
therefore be interpreted cautious ly. Whether these differences re ﬂect
deferred regulatory submissions, prolonged review deliberations, or other
market access factors remains unknown. These ﬁndings highlight dis-
parities in timely availability of AI-enabled technologies across regions, and
underscore the value of harmonizing regulatory frameworks to promote
more equitable global access.

Statistical analysis
The unit of analysis was each AI-enabl ed radiology device. Descriptive
statistics were used to summarise device characteristics. Continuous vari-
ables are reported as medians with IQR, and categorical variables as fre-
quencies and percentages.
Time-to-second-approval (CE marking or FDA clearance) was
examined using Kaplan–Meier methods, with time zero de ﬁned as ﬁrst
approval and the event as second approval. Devices without a second
approval by the date of data extraction (30/11/2025) were right-censored.
The pooled median time-to-second -approval was estimated from the
Kaplan–Meier curve. Analyses were strati ﬁed according to: (1) MDR
implementation (before versus after 26/05/2021), and (2) regulatory
approval sequence (CE markingﬁrst versusFDA clearanceﬁrst), with group
differences assessed using the log-rank test.
Associations between regulatory sequencing and device characteristics
were examined using Fisher ’s exact tests (Monte Carlo approximation).
Among dual-authorised devices, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test assessed
whether time lags differed from zero, and the Mann –Whitney U test
compared delay magnitude between pathways.
The association between device char acteristics and time-to-second-
approval was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression, strati-
ﬁed by regulatory sequencing (CE- ﬁrst versus FDA- ﬁrst). Covariates
included selected from clinically relevant variables and association testing,
while avoiding collinear predictors: modality, EU class, peer-reviewed evi-
dence,a n dother evidence. Peer-reviewed evidencew a su s e da sam a r k e ro f
publicly visible evidentiary support and was not assumed to represent evi-
dence submitted to regulatory authorities. It was modelled as a time-varying
variable using a counting-process approach. Devices were coded as having
no peer-reviewed evidence until the ﬁrst peer-reviewed evidence became
available, and as having such evidence thereafter; devices without peer-
reviewed evidence before second approval or censoring remained coded as
having no such evidence. HR and 95% CI were reported. All analyses were
performed using Python 3.13.5.
