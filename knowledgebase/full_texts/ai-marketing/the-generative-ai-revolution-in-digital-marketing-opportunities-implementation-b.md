Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAI) has changed the way brands interact
with consumers and caused a paradigm shift in the digital marketing arena
with its explosive growth. In this paper, we analyze the multi-faceted impact
of GAI beyond automation to explore how it may be leveraged to create hyper-
personalized content in real-time, to enhance brand loyalty and raise Return
on Investment (ROI). Unlike prior reviews that examine artificial intelligence
in marketing at a broad, conceptual level, this study’s contribution to academic
knowledge is a function-based synthesis that maps GAI capabilities onto
specific marketing activities and integrates recent large-scale industry survey
evidence with peer-reviewed academic scholarship, an integration earlier
reviews have not undertaken. Based on a thematic synthesis of contemporary
literature and empirical findings, the study identifies a major transition in GAI
adoption within marketing. A large-scale survey of more than 1,000 marketing
professionals by the American Marketing Association and Lightricks found
that 71% use GAI tools such as ChatGPT in their weekly work. However,
usage remains largely productivity-focused rather than analytically oriented,
highlighting a gap in the deeper integration of GAI into data-driven marketing
decision-making. The results reveal that GAI achieves significant improvement
in content quality (52%) and operational efficiency (50%). But these advantages
come with significant implementation hurdles. Our work indicates that
there are substantial concerns of algorithmic bias, as models trained on the
internet tend to reproduce the biases associated with gender and race, and
produce biased results for sensitive issues such as finance. The report also
takes on growing issues about “hallucinations”, breaches of data privacy and
the substitution of routine-based labor. To deal with these complications, the
article argues for a change towards a more mature governance system. Future
research should focus on conceptual models that connect GAI-driven content
with long-term customer views, while addressing the legal and ethical vacuum
in the AI space. This study therefore concludes that GAI offers unique chances
for creativity but that its effective use requires a balance between technology
efficiency, ethical openness, and strategic human oversight.

1. INTRODUCTION
The last decade has seen tremendous progress in artificial
intelligence, resulting in a paradigm shift in the digital marketing
industry (Verma et al., 2021). The advent of Generative
Artificial Intelligence (GAI), including sophisticated chatbots,
image generators, and complex language models, has allowed
for the production of content that closely resembles human
creativity. The capabilities allow for very personalized audience
interaction and personalization (Gupta et al., 2024). The use
of AI in marketing has evolved from performing repetitive
administrative tasks to generating creative and high-quality
content that caters to specific customer needs (Waja et al., 2023).
This technology advancement offers ample opportunity for
marketers to drive user engagement, improve message efficacy,
and build brand loyalty (Bhattari, 2023). GAI allows advertisers
to develop data-driven content marketing strategies that are
customized to specific user behaviors and preferences, thereby
enhancing the brand’s level of engagement with the audience
(Kshetri et al., 2024). GAI also streamlines content creation
and optimization, giving marketing specialists more time for
higher-level strategic functions. This means that companies
may engage in more accurate marketing, which will result
in more sales and a better return on investment (Kumar
& Sharma, 2023). GAI allows for the creation of dynamic,
real-time content in a fast-evolving market to ensure that
organizations remain in tune with new trends and consumer
expectations (Golab-Andrzejak, 2023).
Notwithstanding the apparent strategic importance of these
instruments, the academic literature on the comprehensive
application of GAI technologies in digital marketing does not
address a sizable void (Kshetri et al., 2024). With the industry
undergoing seismic change, it’s time to ask the questions
about how GAI will impact current ways over the long term.
This paper aims to fill this gap by analyzing three key aspects:
the application of GAI in different marketing fields, the main
advantages it brings to tactical operations, and the ethical, social
and professional challenges arising from its massive deployment.
To address this gap, the study is guided by three specific
research objectives. First, it examines how Generative
Artificial Intelligence (GAI) is currently applied across distinct
digital marketing functions, including content creation,
personalization, campaign optimization, and audience
analytics. Second, it identifies the principal advantages of
GAI for tactical marketing operations, particularly in terms
of content quality, productivity, and customer engagement.
Third, it critically evaluates the ethical, social, and professional
challenges associated with the widespread deployment of GAI
in marketing and examines the governance responses required
to address these emerging challenges.
2. LITERATURE REVIEW
The last decade has seen a rapid growth of research on
Artificial Intelligence (AI) in digital marketing, driven by fast
technological progress and the increasing use of data-driven
marketing practices. The preliminary evaluation studies have
laid the framework for a study into the impact of AI on marketing
strategy, customer engagement, and analytics. Verma et al.
(2021) explored the paradigm shifts that AI brings to marketing,
and how the decision-making process is increasingly reliant on
automation, customer data analysis, and message optimization.
Similarly, Van Esch and Black (2021) highlighted the strategic
revolution that took place with the integration of AI, notably in
areas such as customization and predictive analytics. But those
early studies were done when AI was mainly classic machine
learning or basic automation systems, not the strong generative
models that we have now. So the studies discussed AI as a broad
strategic tool and not the creative and generative powers that
are at the heart of digital marketing today.
As research progressed, scholars began to explore the role of AI
in more specialized areas. In this respect, Mariani et al. (2022)
performed a comprehensive systematic review of mapping
the application of AI in marketing, consumer psychology, and
analytics. Their work demonstrated how machine learning
could be used to support tasks such as market segmentation,
behavioral prediction, and user profiling. This review extended
the scope beyond strategy, but did not include generative AI,
as tools for text generation, image synthesis, and creative
automation were not widely used or available at the time. The
literature then changed dramatically with the advent of more
sophisticated generative systems. One of the earliest attempts to
conceptualize the use of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAI)
in marketing is found in the work of Kshetri et al. (2024). In the
assessment, they recognised growing prospects in sectors such
as content creation, process automation and customisation but
recognised there were major difficulties with ethics, accuracy
and governance. This study was seminal, but it provided a broad
picture and did not offer a deep, function-based analysis of how
GAI performs across different segments of digital marketing.
To this topic Gupta et al. (2024) contributed by studying the
theoretical foundations of GAI adoption. Their work gave
insights into organizational readiness, perceived benefits, and
managerial concerns. But they focused mainly on conceptual
issues and did not map the practical implications of GAI for
particular marketing activities.
Read together, these five studies show a clear temporal pattern
rather than five independent snapshots: each successive study
narrows its focus from AI as a general strategic capability
(Verma et al., 2021; Van Esch & Black, 2021), to AI as a set of
discrete, learnable analytical techniques (Mariani et al., 2022),
and finally to generative, content-producing systems (Kshetri
et al., 2024; Gupta et al., 2024a). This progression tracks the
underlying technology itself, but it also means the strategic-level
conclusions of the earliest reviews cannot simply be extended
to GAI without re-examination, since generative systems raise
creative-authorship and content-originality questions that
classical predictive AI does not. The literature also contains an
unresolved tension: Gupta et al. (2024a) frame organizational
readiness and perceived benefit in largely optimistic terms,
whereas Kshetri et al. (2024) treat ethical, accuracy, and
governance risk as comparably significant, and neither study
reconciles how firms are expected to pursue the productivity
gains one review emphasizes while managing the governance
burden the other emphasizes. Three gaps cut across all five
studies. First, none provide a function-by-function account of
where in the marketing workflow- content, personalization,
search engine optimization, analytics, competitor intelligence,

The remainder of this review, and the theoretical grounding
introduced below, is designed to close these three gaps.
Situating these findings within established technology-
adoption theory helps explain the uneven adoption pattern
documented above. The Technology Acceptance Model (Davis,
1989) holds that a user’s intention to adopt a new technology is
driven primarily by its perceived usefulness and perceived ease
of use; applied here, it suggests GAI tools have been adopted
fastest in tasks, such as writing and content drafting, where
marketers can directly and immediately perceive a usefulness
and ease-of-use benefit, while adoption lags in tasks such as
data analysis or audience targeting where the usefulness of
a generative, rather than predictive, tool is less immediately
obvious. Rogers’ (2003) Diffusion of Innovations theory further
frames current GAI adoption in marketing as still concentrated
among innovators and early adopters, consistent with the
high but uneven usage levels reported later in this review,
and suggests that broader, more analytically sophisticated use
will depend on the technology’s relative advantage becoming
clearer to the late-majority of marketing practitioners. Finally,
the Task-Technology Fit model (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995)
offers a complementary explanation for why GAI’s marketing
impact varies so strongly by function: performance gains
materialize only where the specific capabilities of a generative
tool, such as producing fluent draft text, match the specific
demands of a task, which fits well-defined creative production
tasks but fits less well the more judgment-intensive tasks of
strategic targeting or bias-sensitive decision-making.
The earlier contributions reveal that there is no comprehensive
review that links GAI tools to individual marketing functions
such as content marketing, search engine optimization,
predictive analytics, user experience design, marketing
automation and competitor analysis in a structured manner.
These gaps matter, not least in a world where tools like
ChatGPT, Jasper, DALL·E and Midjourney are already changing
the creative and operational parts of digital marketing. This
review fills this gap by providing a comprehensive, function-
based review of the integration of generative systems into
marketing workflows. Unlike previous works that focused on
AI in general or at a conceptual level, this review integrates
recent empirical findings, academic publications, and industry
reports that demonstrate how GAI drives creativity, efficiency,
and engagement. It also points to the heightened concerns
about GAI use. Current debates have increasingly been over
issues such as data privacy, algorithmic bias, misinformation,
deepfake production, intellectual property disputes, and fear
of job displacement. Although previous reviews acknowledged
these issues briefly, they often did not explore them with the
depth they require given the transformative nature of GAI.

This analysis presents a contemporary and comprehensive
perspective of the evolving nature of the link between AI and
digital marketing, addressing both the benefits and risks of
generative systems. It outlines the current state of knowledge
and points out the significant gaps that need to be investigated
further. These include the requirement for regulatory
frameworks, longitudinal effect evaluations, organizational
preparation studies, and other more sophisticated examinations
of how GAI influences trust, authenticity, and customer
behavior. This integration gives significant insights to
researchers, practitioners, and policymakers concerned with
the rapid expansion of generative AI in the marketing area.
Because GAI adoption in marketing is evolving faster than
the peer-review publication cycle, this review also draws
deliberately on industry research alongside academic
scholarship, a combination the studies discussed above do
not attempt. Large-sample practitioner surveys, including
the American Marketing Association and Lightricks
survey of more than 1,000 marketers (Cashion & O’Brien,
2024), HubSpot’s survey-based research on generative AI
tool adoption (Butler, 2023), and the Wharton Human-AI
Research and GBK Collective survey of over 800 senior
business leaders (Korst et al., 2024), provide real-time, large-
sample evidence of adoption rates and tool usage that peer-
reviewed studies, with their longer publication lag, cannot
yet offer. These sources are treated as a complement to, not
a replacement for, peer-reviewed evidence: they are not
subject to academic peer review and are typically published
by organizations with a commercial interest in GAI adoption,
so the quantitative findings drawn from them in Section 4 are
reported descriptively and cross-checked against one another
and against the peer-reviewed literature rather than treated as
validated causal evidence.

3. METHODOLOGY
3.1. Research design
This study employed a qualitative integrative review design to
investigate the role of data analytics and artificial intelligence in
digital marketing, detailing both the organizational advantages
and implementation challenges. This paper uses an integrative
review approach, as it enables the synthesis of conceptual,
empirical, and practice-oriented scholarship across several
domains, including marketing analytics, customer relationship
management, personalization, automation, privacy, and
algorithmic bias (Davenport et al., 2020; Huang & Rust, 2021;
Wedel & Kannan, 2016). The methodological aim was not to
test a single causal hypothesis, but to systematically identify,
categorize, and interpret recurring themes in the literature
on the use of analytics-driven digital marketing systems. This
approach is appropriate for issues with rapid technological
development and intertwined managerial, ethical and technical
issues (George et al., 2014; Kannan & Li, 2017).
3.2. Data sources and literature selection
Before the selection criteria described below were applied, a
systematic search was conducted between February and April
2026 across four databases selected for their strong coverage
of marketing, information systems and business scholarship:
Scopus, Web of Science, EBSCOhost Business Source Complete,
and Google Scholar. Search strings combined generative-
technology and marketing-practice terms using Boolean
operators and truncation, for example (“generative artificial
intelligence” OR “generative AI” OR “GAI” OR “ChatGPT”
OR “large language model*”) AND (“digital marketing”
OR “marketing analytics” OR “consumer engagement” OR
“content personalization” OR “marketing automation”). The
search was restricted to English-language, peer-reviewed
journal articles, edited academic volumes, and named industry
reports from recognized research organizations, published
between 2012 and 2026; this window was chosen to capture
both the foundational big-data and predictive-analytics
literature that preceded GAI (Chaffey & Patron, 2012) and the
more recent generative-AI-specific scholarship that followed
the release of widely used generative tools.
The analysis was developed through a review and integration
of 10 academic references from peer-reviewed journals and
foundational scholarly texts in the areas of digital marketing,
big data, artificial intelligence, and analytics. For academic
rigor, the literature was chosen according to four specific
criteria: the source needs to deal with digital marketing
analytics and customer targeting; it must talk about
organizational benefits or implementation risks; it must be
published in a scholarly journal or edited academic volume;
and it must offer conceptual or empirical insights pertinent
to marketing strategy. Based on these criteria, this study
successfully investigates the digital marketing analysis in
terms of performance improvement and critical governance.
The initial search returned a substantially larger pool of
candidate records than could be closely analyzed within a single
integrative review. To manage this pool and limit selection
bias, records were screened in two stages: an initial title-
and-abstract screen against the four criteria above, followed
by a full-text screen of the remaining records for thematic
relevance, methodological credibility, and non-redundancy
with sources already selected. From this process, ten core
academic references were retained as the primary analytical
basis for the review because, taken together, they span the
field’s evolution from early AI-enabled marketing (Verma et al.,
2021; Van Esch & Black, 2021) through systematic reviews of AI
in marketing (Mariani et al., 2022) to GAI-specific scholarship
(Kshetri et al., 2024; Gupta et al., 2024a), giving the broadest
thematic coverage achievable for a review of this scope. This
purposive, criterion-based selection follows integrative-review
guidance that favors analytical relevance and conceptual
saturation over exhaustive enumeration (Davenport et al.,
2020; Kannan & Li, 2017). We nonetheless acknowledge that a
purposively selected sample of ten core references cannot be
fully representative of the entire, rapidly expanding body of
work on GAI in marketing, and that screening conducted by
a single reviewer, rather than the dual-reviewer or PRISMA-
style protocol used in some systematic reviews, introduces a
degree of subjectivity that could have shaped which sources
were ultimately retained.
This balanced approach, grounded in basic research (Chaffey &
Patron, 2012; Erevelles et al., 2016), assures that the results are
not too narrowly technological or managerial, but provide a
comprehensive interpretation of the field.
3.3. Analytical procedure
In order to ensure a rigorous analysis of the subject, a four-
stage process of thematic synthesis was followed. The first step,
literature identification, involved screening relevant scholarly
works for their alignment with the role of analytics in digital
marketing. Stage two, concept extraction: key themes were
extracted from the selected sources, including automation,
targeting accuracy, personalization, generating strategic insight,
and the various types of risks such as privacy, organizational
resistance, and algorithmic bias. In the third stage, thematic
grouping, these concepts were grouped into two major analytical
dimensions: the benefits of analytics in digital marketing and
the related limitations and challenges of implementation.
Finally, the fourth stage involved comparative synthesis, in
which themes across studies were compared, and an integrated
framework was developed. This framework demonstrates how
firms derive a lot of value from analytics while at the same time
facing ethical, technical, and organizational constraints (Akter
et al., 2016; Wedel & Kannan, 2016).

3.6. Synthesis of benefits and challenges
Literature suggests that analytics contributes significantly
to operational efficiency through automation of repetitive
marketing functions and increasing speed of decision making
(Davenport et al., 2020). Moreover, it increases the accuracy of
targeting as it allows firms to identify consumers, which would
not be possible without large-scale behavioral, transactional,
and contextual data (Wedel & Kannan, 2016). Moreover, analytics
supports personalization by allowing marketers to tailor
messages, offers, and digital experiences to increase engagement
and customer satisfaction (Bleier et al., 2020; Huang & Rust,
2021). However, the same literature also indicates that high
adoption costs, including technological infrastructure, software
acquisition, and talent development, constrain implementation
(Akter et al., 2016). Furthermore, organizational resistance is
typical for firms, especially when adopting analytics, as this
may necessitate new workflows, cross-functional integration,
and changes in managerial decision authority (George et al.,
2014). The ethical concerns are also important, especially
with regard to data privacy, consumer consent, and black-box
automated decision systems (Martin & Murphy, 2017). Finally,
scholars warn that algorithmic systems can reproduce bias,
misinformation, or skewed targeting outcomes if the basis of
data or models is flawed (Huang & Rust, 2021).

4. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
4.1. Overview of empirical insights
The results of the five figures presented, together with additional
analytical modeling and accumulated academic evidence,
suggest a rapid but uneven adoption of generative AI (GAI)
in digital marketing practices. The results collectively reveal
three dominant patterns that define the current landscape.
First, the combination of high adoption frequency and
moderate daily integration. Seventy-one percent of marketers
use GAI weekly, indicating broad institutional acceptance,
but not yet full operational embedding. Second, the value
realisation is driven by productivity, with the biggest gains in
quality of content (52%), time efficiency (50%) and total output
volume (49%). Finally, use is significantly weighted toward
creative production, with writing (38%) and content creation
(33%) dominating, and other complex analytical capabilities
neglected. This aligns with recent studies that suggest that at
this stage, the adoption of GAI is mostly driven by content and
efficiency concerns, indicating the ongoing evolution of the
field in terms of strategic and analytical applications (Rahman
et al., 2026; Borimnejad & Borimnejad, 2025).
4.2. AI Use survey
Fig. 3 illustrates the use of generative AI among a surveyed
workforce of more than 1,000 marketing professionals. It shows
that 71% of respondents use generative AI weekly or more,
within which approximately 19% use it daily; a further 20% use
it occasionally but less than weekly, while 9% of respondents
have never used the technology  (Figure 3).
A survey of over 1,000 experienced marketers found that 71%
of respondents use generative AI at least once a week, with
over 20% utilizing it everyday (Cashion & O’Brien, 2024). This
increase is consistent with a recent Wharton study that found
that the weekly usage of generative AI by corporate leaders
jumped from 37% in 2023 to 72% in 2024 (Korst et al., 2024). This
is a case of generative AI being rapidly accepted as a critical
marketing and business tool.

4.5. Critical reflection on the survey evidence
The empirical patterns described in Sections 4.1–4.4 rest on
industry survey data that merit closer scrutiny before being
treated as generalizable evidence of GAI’s impact on marketing
practice. The principal dataset (Cashion & O’Brien, 2024) draws
on a voluntary, self-selected sample of marketers who chose
to respond to an industry-run survey; such samples typically
over-represent professionals who are already engaged with,
and favorably disposed toward, the technology being studied,
which likely inflates both the reported adoption rate and
the reported satisfaction with GAI’s benefits relative to the
marketing workforce as a whole. The Wharton Human-AI
Research and GBK Collective figures (Korst et al., 2024) cited
alongside it describe a related but distinct population, senior
business leaders in large organizations with 1,000 or more
employees, rather than front-line marketing practitioners, so
the similarity between the two studies’ headline percentages is
best read as corroborating evidence of a broader trend rather
than as two measurements of the same underlying population.
The benefit measures reported in Section 4.3, improved
content quality (52%), time savings (50%) and increased output
(49%), are respondents’ self-reported perceptions rather than
independently measured outcomes; marketers evaluating a
tool they have personally chosen to adopt are susceptible to
social-desirability and novelty effects that can inflate perceived
benefit relative to objectively verifiable gains in campaign
performance, conversion rates, or return on investment.
Neither survey publishes a response rate, industry distribution,
organizational size profile, or full geographic breakdown, which
limits the extent to which the headline 71% weekly-usage figure
can be generalized beyond the professional networks each
organization sampled. Taken together, these considerations do
not undermine the value of the survey evidence, which remains
the best currently available large-sample indicator of GAI
adoption in marketing, but they do mean the figures are best
interpreted as directional evidence of a fast-moving trend rather
than as precise population estimates. Future research combining
self-reported adoption measures with objective performance
indicators, such as engagement metrics, conversion data, or
independently audited content-quality assessments, would help
validate the benefit estimates synthesized in this review.
4.6. Challenges of GAI in digital marketing
The challenges discussed below are not free-standing concerns;
they map onto the adoption patterns documented in Section
4. GAI use is concentrated in creative and productivity-
oriented tasks, writing (38%), content creation (33%), and
visual storytelling (28%) (Cashion & O’Brien, 2024), while
adoption for higher-stakes analytical tasks such as audience
targeting (25%) and data analysis (22%) remains comparatively
limited, a pattern consistent with the Task-Technology Fit view
introduced in Section 2 that GAI’s fit is strongest for fluent-text-
generation tasks and weakest for judgment-intensive analytical
tasks. This distribution is also consistent with, and may partly
reflect, the algorithmic-bias risk discussed below: marketers
may be implicitly limiting GAI’s role in decisions with greater
potential for discriminatory or reputationally damaging
outcomes. Similarly, the comparatively low reported use of GAI
for personalization and translation (5%) (Cashion & O’Brien,
2024), despite the technology’s demonstrated personalization
capability (Kshetri et al., 2024; Gupta et al., 2024a), may reflect
practitioner caution around the data-privacy and consent issues
discussed later in this section. At the same time, the sheer scale
of adoption identified earlier, 71% of surveyed marketers using
GAI weekly or more (Cashion & O’Brien, 2024), means that even
a modest per-interaction risk of biased, hallucinated, or privacy-
compromising output accumulates into substantial aggregate
exposure for organizations, which reinforces the urgency of the
governance concerns raised throughout this study..
Generative AI (GAI) is proliferating rapidly in digital marketing,
and with it, deep ethical and operational issues. Algorithmic bias
is chief among these: because models like ChatGPT are trained
on large internet datasets, they tend to reflect and exacerbate
societal biases. A recent study by Walch et al. (2023) shows that
GAI has the potential to produce biased outcomes in finance or
management (e.g., gender, race), which may reinforce systemic
inequities. Technical restrictions also hamper GAI, such as the
spread of misinformation and “hallucinations, ” in which the
model confidently gives absurd answers. AI data of poor quality
leads to operational inefficiencies and bad decision-making in
the business context (Rana et al., 2022). Labor troubles are also
very much present. Automation jeopardizes jobs in sectors
that depend a lot on repetitive work, including manufacturing
and retail, whereas positions that need creativity and social
interaction are more secure (Soueidan et al., 2024). Finally, GAI
presents major dangers to privacy and security. The technique
can be used to create synthetic media (deepfakes) and whole
social profiles that can be misused for identity theft, spying,
and non-consensual data exploitation (Wach et al., 2023).
There’s a real possibility for GAI to manipulate consumer
behavior through hyper-targeted, invasive marketing, due to
the commodification of personal data, which means there is an
urgent need for strong AI regulation.

5. CONCLUSION
The present analysis of generative technologies in digital
marketing opens up various areas for further research.
Researchers should initially focus on building conceptual
frameworks that clarify the influence of generative material
on customer perceptions, brand interaction, and purchase
intentions. Longitudinal and experimental studies, in particular,
that empirically examine these frameworks would enrich the
theoretical underpinnings of the field. More research is needed
into the moral, legal, and regulatory implications of generative
material in marketing. These include concerns of algorithmic
transparency, data privacy, authorship and the perils of
consumer manipulation. Comparative research between nations
could bring vital new information on how these technologies
are regulated in different legal and cultural contexts. Third,
future study should investigate how generative technologies
will impact the roles, processes, and skills that marketing
professionals will need. This can include surveys measuring
the changing nature of marketing labour or qualitative studies
examining practitioners’ experiences. Moreover, there is a need
for cross-industry and cross-cultural study to increase our
understanding of how generative technologies are adopted,
welcomed, and adapted in different organizational and customer
contexts. Such studies would provide enlightening information
on factors impacting efficacy or difficulty of these instruments.
