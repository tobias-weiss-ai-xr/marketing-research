I. Artificial Intelligence
Artificial Intelligence (AI) is one of the most recent,
most important, and most fascinating and exciting
branches of science and engineering. Serious
interest in it began shortly after the end of the
Second World War, and the term “artificial
intelligence” was adopted during the 1950s,
coinciding with the emergence of other sciences
such as molecular biology. However, the
“Dartmouth Summer Research Project” of 1956 is
often considered the event that launched the field
of artificial intelligence as an independent field of
research. Credit for coining this term and defining
the general direction of this scientific field is
attributed to the scientist John McCarthy. It is
interesting to wonder whether the field would
have taken a different course had it been named,
for instance, “computational intelligence” or one of
the other names that were proposed at the time.1
I.1 Definition of Artificial Intelligence (AI):
There exist in the academic literature various
descriptions and definitions of what constitutes
artificial intelligence, but to date there is no
universally agreed-upon or accepted definition. For
this reason, several definitions encompassing
diverse concepts of artificial intelligence have
emerged over time, owing to the multifaceted
nature of this field, which can be viewed from
different angles including philosophy, computer
science, psychology, and engineering. In what
follows, the definitions most important and
relevant to this study will be presented, ordered
from the oldest to the most recent, taking historical
sequence into account, with the aim of achieving a
better understanding for the use of artificial
intelligence in marketing.
As noted above, artificial intelligence is a
fascinating and exciting field, and also a multi-
purpose technology, yet its nature has not been
defined. Table (01) presents eight definitions of

whereas the definitions on the right measure
success according to the criterion of ideal
performance, referred to as “rationality”; a system
is rational if it does “the right thing” based on what
it knows.2

The author Nils J. Nilsson4 in his book "The Quest
for Artificial Intelligence: A History of Ideas and
Achievements," offers an important definition of
artificial intelligence: “Artificial intelligence is that
activity devoted to making machines intelligent,
and intelligence is that quality that enables an
entity to function appropriately and with foresight
in its environment.”5
This definition includes the concept of “functioning
appropriately,” meaning acting or interacting in a
manner consistent with the context or
requirements in a fitting way, and “foresight,”
which refers to the ability to anticipate or make
informed decisions in response to potential
changes in the environment. From this perspective,
the classification of intelligence depends on the
degree of credit given to software and hardware
for their ability to achieve these two elements.
The (Stanford University, 2016, p.12) report
reinforces this definition with an identical
formulation: “Artificial intelligence is the activity
devoted to making machines intelligent, and
intelligence is that quality that enables an entity to
function appropriately and with foresight in its
environment.”6 It emphasizes the same aspects, as
both focus on the duality of “appropriate action”
and “foresight” as fundamental pillars for
understanding intelligence.
In a recent report on the current state of artificial
intelligence, a committee of prominent researchers
defined this field as “a branch of computer science
that studies the properties of intelligence by
synthesizing intelligence.” Indeed, this definition
may seem somewhat circular, but the committee
itself also acknowledged the difficulty of defining
the field — and this may perhaps be a positive
thing, as it states: “The absence of a precise and
universally standardized definition of artificial
intelligence has probably helped this field grow,
flourish, and advance at an ever-accelerating pace.”
Furthermore, the committee notes that
“practitioners, researchers, and developers in the
field of artificial intelligence are instead guided by
a general sense of direction and an urgent drive to
move forward.”7
From the preceding definition, artificial intelligence
can be considered a branch of computer science
that links the study of intelligence and its
generation as an integrated process, though it is
considered circular because it relies on “generating
intelligence” without clarifying its fundamental
properties. The committee acknowledges the
difficulty of formulating a unified definition, noting
that the absence of a precise global definition has
contributed positively to the field's growth,
flourishing, and rapid advancement, as it provides
flexibility that allows researchers to explore new
directions without rigid constraints.
(Poole, D., & Mackworth, A., 2023) define artificial
intelligence, or AI, as “the field that studies the
synthesis and analysis of computational agents that
act intelligently,” and consider intelligence to be a
relative matter (i.e., measured by degrees). Among
the aspects that make an agent act intelligently are
the following8 :
 Its actions are appropriate to its
circumstances, goals, and perceptual and
computational capabilities.
 It takes into account the short- and long-
term consequences of its actions, including
effects on society and the environment.
 It learns from experience.
 It is flexible in the face of changing
environments and changing goals.

intelligence, as its authors (Russell, S. J., & Norvig,

decision-theoretic agents.”9

with its environment and makes decisions based
on specific inputs, such as robots and predictive
systems. The following are the main points it
emphasizes10:

practical application. As a result, methods of

and intersect depending on the context.

adopted to understand this branching field by
analyzing three main pillars: components

production and distribution system as a whole.”13
This definition provides a flexible analytical
framework that allows for its application at
different levels (organization, market, economy),
and links marketing to social and economic
systems. However, the emotional, human
dimension of the consumer–brand relationship is
not a central focus within it.
Sharp (2013), relying on an evidence-based
approach, defines marketing as “a set of activities
aimed at creating value for customers and building
strong relationships with them by understanding
their needs, developing products or services that
meet these needs, promoting them effectively, and
delivering them in a way that achieves
organizational objectives.”14 This definition
focuses on the practical and strategic aspects of
marketing, emphasizing value creation and
relationship-building with customers, consistent
with Sharp's evidence-based approach, which
highlights the importance of understanding
consumer behavior and using data to improve
marketing performance.
Kotler and Keller (2016), meanwhile, hold that
marketing is “the art and science of choosing target
markets and acquiring, retaining, and growing
customers through creating, delivering, and
communicating superior value to them.”15 This
definition expands strategically to present
marketing as both an art and a science, focusing on
specific stages: selecting the audience, attracting
customers, then retaining them and growing their
relationship with the organization — that is, it
focuses on the full cycle of the customer
relationship, reflecting the shift of marketing from
“product marketing” to “relationship marketing.” It
also links marketing innovation to loyalty and
highlights the importance of superior value, i.e.,
delivering more than what is expected, which
enhances loyalty and sustainability in the customer
relationship.
Philip Kotler and Armstrong (Kotler & Armstrong,
2020) define marketing as “a social and managerial
process by which individuals and organizations
obtain what they need and want through creating
and exchanging value with others.”16 This
definition integrates the social and managerial
dimensions of marketing, presenting it as an
interactive process that contributes to achieving an
exchange of value between organizations and
customers. Marketing here is presented as a
mechanism for satisfying needs and wants, not
merely selling products; what distinguishes this
formulation is the comprehensiveness of the vision,
which takes marketing out of the narrow profit-
oriented framework and into a human and societal
one.
The definitions of marketing by McCarthy (2002),
Sharp (2013), Kotler and Keller (2016), and Kotler
and Armstrong (2020) demonstrate a diversity of
perspectives in understanding marketing as a
managerial and social activity aimed at creating
value and meeting customer needs. While
McCarthy provides an analytical framework, Sharp
focuses on scientific evidence, Kotler and Keller
highlight the artistic and scientific aspects, and
Kotler and Armstrong broaden the vision to
include the social dimension. These definitions
reflect the evolution of the field in response to
economic and technological transformations,
enhancing our understanding of marketing as a
dynamic process.

III. Big Data
Big Data is one of the most prominent features of
the digital age, representing a natural consequence
of the information explosion resulting from the
rapid development in communication and
information technology. The widespread diffusion
of the internet, the growing use of smart devices,
and the expansion of activity across digital
platforms have led to the production of vast
quantities of data at an unprecedented pace. In this
context, Big Data has become a critically important
strategic resource, which organizations and
governments seek to leverage to enhance their
ability to understand the environments in which
they operate, monitor trends, and predict future
changes. This phenomenon has gained particular
importance as it represents the foundation upon
which advanced applications such as artificial
intelligence and business analytics are built,
making it a central pivot for digital transformation
and innovation across various fields.
III.1 Definition of Big Data:
Definitions and concepts of this field vary and
differ among experts, companies, and specialized
organizations. The McKinsey Global Institute
defines Big Data as “a collection of data whose size
exceeds the ability of typical database tools to
capture, share, transfer, store, manage, and analyze
within an acceptable period of time.”19.
Herman (E. L. Herman) defines it as “large and
complex data sets that exceed the processing
capabilities offered by traditional database systems.
Big Data is characterized by large volume, high
velocity, and great variety, which requires the use
of specialized techniques and analytical methods
such as (Hadoop) and cloud-computing-based
analytics to efficiently extract meaningful
information.”20.
Gartner, a company specializing in information-
technology research and consulting, defines Big
Data as “high-volume, high-velocity, and/or high-
variety information assets that demand cost-
effective, innovative forms of information
processing that enable enhanced insight and
decision-making.”21.
IBM Global Services defines Big Data as “a
collection of information assets characterized by
high Volume, Variety, and Velocity, which require
innovative methods and technologies to process
them in order to enhance understanding, decision-
making, and the achievement of competitive
advantage.”22.
According to the International Telecommunication
Union (ITU), the term Big Data “is defined through
its main characteristics; it is noted that definitions
of Big Data are somewhat vague, but there are
common characteristics that help describe it —
four main dimensions known as the ‘Four Vs’ are
used to describe different aspects of Big Data.”23.
The term “Big Data” refers to an emerging concept
in the field of information technology and data
science, and has seen considerable diversity in its
definitions among experts, companies, and
international organizations. This diversity reflects
the evolution of the concept since its early
beginnings in the early part of the second decade of
the twenty-first century, where the earliest
definitions focused on technical challenges (such as
volume and processing), before expanding to
include economic and strategic benefits.
Accordingly, it can be said that the majority of
definitions agree that Big Data is not merely an
increase in data volume, but rather an integrated
system characterized by enormous volume, variety,
high velocity, and sometimes veracity — factors
that make it exceed the capabilities of traditional
tools and require the deployment of innovative
analytical techniques and methods. These
definitions also reveal two complementary
dimensions: the first is descriptive-technical,
highlighting the characteristics of the data, and the
second is practical-applied, linking it to the process
of generating insights, supporting decisions, and
achieving competitive advantage. This interplay
between the theoretical and applied aspects
reflects the dynamic nature of Big Data and its
strategic role in the digital economy.

integrating data from multiple, disparate
sources.

analyzing enormous quantities of it
enables the extraction of high value that
contributes to supporting decision-making
and achieving competitive advantage.

applies to all dimensions; there are no fixed
universal standards for the volume, variety, or
velocity that define Big Data, as the thresholds vary
according to the size, sector, and location of the
organization, and these thresholds evolve over
time. It is also important to recognize that these
dimensions are not independent of one another —
as one dimension changes, the likelihood of
another dimension changing accordingly increases.
IV. The Relationship between Artificial
Intelligence (AI), Big Data, and Marketing
The contemporary world is witnessing an
unprecedented surge in the volume of data
generated by human interaction and digital
systems, which has given rise to the concept of Big
Data as one of the strategic resources for
companies and organizations. At the same time,
artificial intelligence has developed into an
integrated system of algorithms and technologies
capable of processing this complex data and
extracting applicable patterns and insights.
The integration between Big Data and artificial
intelligence enables companies to improve
marketing decisions by predicting customer
behavior, personalizing offers, and improving the
consumer experience. This integration has become
a fundamental pillar in formulating modern
marketing strategies, as organizations rely on real-
time, intelligent analytics to deal with highly
competitive, dynamic markets. Studying this
relationship therefore represents an important
step toward understanding how advanced
technologies can be deployed to build competitive
advantages and achieve added value for both
customers and companies. The following axes
briefly illustrate this:
1- Integration between Big Data and Artificial
Intelligence: Big Data provides a rich and diverse
environment for training artificial-intelligence
algorithms, which enhances the accuracy and
generalizability of models, while artificial
intelligence enables the analysis of this enormous
data and the extraction of hidden patterns and
knowledge. For example, “the intersection between
‘deep learning’ and ‘Big Data’ points to the common
use of deep-learning methodologies in managing
and analyzing large commercial datasets…”26.
Likewise, “deep learning's ability to process vast
amounts of unstructured data enhances business
intelligence by revealing insights that go beyond
traditional analytical methodologies.”27.
Big Data and artificial intelligence are thus two
complementary technologies: Big Data provides a
rich and diverse environment for training AI
algorithms, enhancing the accuracy and
generalizability of the models, while artificial
intelligence enables the processing of this
enormous, complex data and the extraction of
hidden patterns and knowledge from it. This
interaction produces a continuous feedback loop
that improves model performance and supports
practical applications in multiple fields such as
healthcare, smart cities, and smart transportation.
A number of researchers hold that this interaction
contributes to the development of innovative
applications in multiple fields, such as healthcare,
smart cities, and e-commerce, while emphasizing
the need to take into account the ethical and social
considerations associated with the use of Big
Data28
2- Personalization and Improving the Customer
Experience: Artificial intelligence contributes to
personalization by analyzing diverse data (digital
and non-digital) to provide customized
recommendations, which improves the customer
experience in online shopping, as in the Stitch Fix
business model. According to Davenport et al.
(2020): “Stitch Fix's proprietary machine-learning
algorithms scan numbers, words, and content

V. Ethical and Privacy Challenges
The rapid development of digital technologies and
the spread of artificial intelligence and Big Data
analytics have brought issues of ethics and privacy
to the fore as central challenges facing individuals
and organizations. This development has facilitated
the collection and processing of personal
information, raising fundamental questions about
the legitimate use of data and individuals' rights to
protect their private lives. These issues can be
addressed through the following axes:
1- Privacy Violation and Informed Consent: The
collection and processing of personal data without
obtaining clear, informed consent from consumers
represents one of the most prominent ethical
problems associated with the use of artificial
intelligence and Big Data in marketing. This
practice raises questions about the availability of
genuine informed consent, particularly given the
reliance on complex, opaque algorithms. In this
regard, Mittelstadt (2021) notes that obtaining
genuine informed consent when personal data is
processed by opaque algorithms is extremely
difficult, as users often lack sufficient
understanding of how their data is used.32

Conclusion
The integration between artificial intelligence and
Big Data represents a fundamental turning point in
the trajectory of contemporary marketing.
Marketing success is no longer based on intuition
or traditional trial-and-error, but has come to
depend on in-depth analysis and precise
predictions grounded in vast quantities of data
processed through intelligent methods. This
convergence has enabled marketing to shift from a
general approach targeting broad segments toward
a personalized strategy based on a precise
understanding of customers' needs, behaviors, and
expectations.
Given the intense competitiveness of markets,
companies that invest in this duality have become
more capable of innovation, achieving added value,
and building sustainable relationships with their
customers. Going forward, the future of marketing
will witness further integration between artificial-
intelligence technologies and Big Data analytics,
opening new horizons for formulating smarter and
more effective decisions, while taking into account
ethical dimensions and the protection of consumer
privacy.
