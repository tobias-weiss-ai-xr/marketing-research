Abstract
Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) has moved within a few years from
experimental tool to routine instrument in marketing departments, and the
academic literature is still catching up with that speed. This chapter offers a
structured review of work published between 2020 and 2026 on how GenAI is
used in marketing, how consumers respond to it, and which governance questions
follow. Drawing on journal articles from marketing, information systems,
economics and law, together with regulatory documents from the European
Union and Türkiye, the chapter first separates predictive from generative
systems and maps their uses in content production, personalization, customer
interaction and marketing research. It then organizes the consumer evidence
around three recurring themes: task-dependent preference for machine advice,
authenticity penalties attached to perceived AI authorship, and the ambiguous
effects of humanlike design in service encounters. Field and experimental studies
of workplace use point to productivity gains that are uneven across workers,
while creativity research shows a tension between individual output quality and
collective diversity . The chapter also discusses hallucination, representational
bias, privacy and dark patterns, the energy demand of large models, and the
transparency duties introduced by the EU Artificial Intelligence Act alongside
T urkish data protection guidance. Much of the evidence rests on short online
experiments and on firms in North America and Europe, so conclusions about
long-term behavior and about Türkiye remain provisional. The chapter closes
with a research agenda on longitudinal field designs, disclosure formats, cross-
cultural replication and the measurement of environmental cost in marketing
operations. For managers, the evidence suggests that the source, tone and
disclosure of generated content matter as much as its quality .

1. Introduction
Until recently , discussions of artificial intelligence (AI) in marketing
concerned systems that classified customers, predicted churn or ranked
recommendations. The public release of ChatGPT in late 2022 shifted the
conversation toward systems that write, draw and converse, and marketing
departments were among the first to experiment with them. Kshetri et al.
(2024) observe that, among the functional areas of organizations, marketing
has been particularly affected by generative AI. One plausible reason is that
the technology produces the artefacts on which marketing runs: copy , images,
scripts and personalized messages. The academic response has been quick but
uneven. Cillo and Rubera (2025) describe the marketing literature on GenAI
as still in its infancy , and their technical overview and research roadmap read
as an attempt to give the field a shared vocabulary .
Work on AI in marketing did not begin with generative models. Davenport
et al. (2020) proposed a framework built around levels of intelligence, task
types and robotic embodiment, and argued that AI is likely to be more effective
when it augments human managers than when it replaces them. Huang and
Rust (2021) distinguished mechanical, thinking and feeling AI and applied the
distinction to marketing research, to segmentation, targeting and positioning,
and to marketing action. Puntoni et al. (2021) turned to the consumer side
and described four kinds of experience that AI produces, namely data capture,
classification, delegation and social interaction, each carrying tensions of
its own. Systematic reviews followed; Chintalapati and Pandey (2022), for
instance, reviewed the literature on AI in marketing in a structured way .
T urkish-language scholarship has mostly taken stock of the field through
bibliometric and review designs. Ekinci and Bilginer Özsaatçi (2023) and Açan
(2025) mapped publications on AI and marketing, Akyılmaz (2022) did the
same for AI and consumer behavior, and Durmuş Şenyapar (2024) assessed
generative AI in marketing strategy through a SWOT lens. The most recent of
these studies analyzed 1,465 Web of Science records covering 2015 to 2025.
It reports a marked rise in AI-related marketing publications after 2020 and
names ChatGPT and generative AI as emerging themes (Akbulut, 2026).
What remains less developed is a view that connects bodies of evidence that
are usually read apart: consumer experiments on reactions to AI, studies of
how organizations and workers use generative tools, and the policy documents
that increasingly define acceptable practice. A marketer deciding whether to
publish an AI-drafted message has to weigh audience reaction against the cost
and quality effects of the tool, and to do so within the limits that disclosure
rules set.

This chapter therefore has two aims. The first is to give readers a compact
map of recent evidence, drawing on peer-reviewed work from 2020 to 2026
and on regulatory documents from the European Union and Türkiye. The
second is to say plainly where that evidence is thin. The review is narrative
and not systematic. Sources were chosen for their relevance to marketing
decisions and for the standing of the outlet, and no formal search protocol

the field and not as an exhaustive inventory . Section 2 clarifies concepts and
applications. Section 3 turns to consumer responses, Section 4 to organizational
implications, and Section 5 to ethical, environmental and regulatory questions.
Section 6 sets out a research agenda, and Section 7 concludes.
2. Conceptual Foundations
2.1. From Predictive to Generative Systems
Hermann and Puntoni (2024) organize the past fifteen years of AI in
consumer settings into two phases. In the first, algorithms predicted and
decided. In the second, systems began to produce new text, images and
other artefacts on request. The authors further separate convergent-thinking
GenAI, which is domain-specific and geared toward pre-defined tasks, from
divergent-thinking GenAI, which is domain-general and oriented toward
new tasks. The distinction matters for marketing because a tool that fills a
product-description template and a tool that proposes a campaign concept
place very different demands on judgment and oversight.
From a technical standpoint, Feuerriegel et al. (2024) present generative
AI as a family of models that learn patterns from large bodies of data and
produce new content when prompted, and they discuss what this means for
business and information systems. Peres et al. (2023) wrote from within
marketing, reading the same technology through its possible effects on research,
teaching and practice. Both contributions appeared early in the diffusion of
the technology , and capabilities and costs have moved since.
Huang and Rust’s (2021) typology was published before generative
systems reached a mass audience, yet it remains a usable lens. Mechanical
AI automates repetitive tasks, thinking AI processes data to reach decisions,
and feeling AI analyzes interactions and emotions. Generative systems blur
these boundaries. A chat assistant that drafts a reply is mechanical when it
fills a template, thinking when it chooses among candidate answers, and
feeling when it adjusts tone to a customer’s apparent mood. The typology is
therefore more useful for asking which capability a given deployment draws
on than for sorting tools into fixed boxes. The augmentation argument in

Davenport et al. (2020) carries over as well. Much of the generative evidence
reviewed below describes people working with a tool, and not a tool working
in place of people.
T wo families of models account for most of the applications discussed in this
chapter. Large language models generate text by producing likely continuations
of a prompt, having been trained on very large text collections. Diffusion-based
image models create pictures from a text description by gradually refining a
noisy starting image. Both can be placed behind conversational interfaces,
and both can be constrained or supplemented with a firm’s own material.
Newer systems increasingly accept and return more than one kind of input, so
that a single prompt can combine text, image and sound. For marketing this
blurs the line between copywriting and visual production, and it means that
the consumer evidence reviewed below, most of which concerns text or still
images, may not transfer unchanged to such combined formats. The practical
consequence is that output varies with the prompt, the model version and
the surrounding instructions. Claims about what GenAI does in marketing
are therefore claims about particular systems used in particular ways, a point
that matters whenever evidence from different studies is compared.
2.2. Application Areas in Marketing Practice
Applications cluster in a few areas, and the strength of the evidence differs
from one to the next.
2.2.1. Content Creation and Creative Production
Visual content offers the clearest quantitative comparison. Hartmann et
al. (2025) prompted seven text-to-image models to create 10,320 synthetic
marketing images from 2,400 human-made images and then compared the
two sets on dimensions that matter for marketing. The design combined large-
scale human ratings with a field test in online advertising, and the authors
released the image collection for other researchers to use. They report that AI-
generated images matched or surpassed human-made ones on several criteria,
including click-through in the field test, at lower cost. Such results come from
particular models, image types and platforms at a particular moment. They
describe what was possible in the authors’ setting and do not establish that
synthetic imagery will outperform human work in general.
Text raises a different problem. As Section 3 shows, audiences judge a written
message by more than its quality; they also judge who they believe wrote it
(Kirk & Givi, 2025). Cillo and Rubera (2025) follow generative AI through
the innovation process, from development and testing to communication

Suat Okay | 149
and consumer engagement, and they caution that consumers may accept
machine-generated proposals without adding contributions of their own.
Content production is thus the application where efficiency gains are easiest
to demonstrate and where the consequences for brand meaning are hardest
to predict.
2.2.2. Personalization and Customer Interaction
Personalization was already a central promise of predictive AI. Kshetri et al.
(2024) argue that marketing content produced by GenAI is likely to be more
personally relevant than content produced by earlier digital technologies, and
that insights drawn from it could support sales lead generation. The claim is
plausible but largely prospective, because the article is an editorial contribution
that sets out a research agenda and does not test effects.
Customer service offers firmer empirical ground. Brynjolfsson et al.
(2025) studied the staggered introduction of a generative conversational
assistant among 5,172 customer-support agents. Access to the assistant was
accompanied by an average gain of 15 percent in issues resolved per hour,
with substantial differences across workers. The study concerns agents who
remained responsible for the conversation, so it speaks to AI as an aid to
employees. How consumers respond when a generative system speaks to
them directly is a separate question, taken up in Section 3.
2.2.3. Marketing Research and Insight Generation
In their framework, Huang and Rust (2021) assign mechanical AI to
data collection, thinking AI to market analysis and feeling AI to customer
understanding at the research stage. Generative systems add the possibility of
summarizing open-ended responses, drafting survey instruments and producing
synthetic material for testing. Cillo and Rubera (2025) pose research questions
about using GenAI to analyze the relationship between market-based assets
and firm value and to examine consumers’ skills, preferences and roles in
marketing processes. Hermann and Puntoni (2025a) place researchers among
the stakeholders whom GenAI may empower. Whether synthetic respondents
or machine-coded responses are valid substitutes for human data remains an
open empirical question that these sources raise without settling.
3. Consumer Responses to Generative AI in Marketing
Whether generative tools pay off depends on how audiences receive them.
Hermann and Puntoni (2024) note that research on algorithmic prediction
and decision-making already documents both positive and negative consumer

reactions, and the newer generative work is beginning to add to that record.
Three themes recur.
3.1. Preference, Resistance, and Task Context
Longoni and Cian (2022) identify what they call the word-of-machine effect.
Across a series of experiments, the weight that consumers give to utilitarian
or hedonic attributes shaped whether they preferred an AI recommender
or a human one. When utilitarian attributes were salient, more participants
favored AI-recommended options; when hedonic attributes were salient, they
resisted. The authors trace this pattern to a lay belief that AI recommenders
are more competent in the utilitarian realm and less competent in the hedonic
one. The pattern reversed for utilitarian goals when a recommendation had to
match a person’s unique preferences, and it disappeared when AI and human
input were combined in a hybrid arrangement.
T wo features of this work deserve attention. The recommenders studied
were framed as AI in general and not as generative systems, so extending the
findings to GenAI remains an inference. And the effect rests on a belief about
competence, which the authors present as a belief and not as a fact about
relative performance. Preferences for or against machine input may therefore
shift as experience with the tools accumulates. For practice, the hybrid result
is the most useful one: presenting machine output as support for a human
advisor appears to remove the resistance that appeared when consumers were
asked to choose between the two.
Hedonic value is not the only task feature that may matter. A systematic
review of 80 empirical studies groups the drivers of algorithm aversion into
algorithm, individual, task and higher-level factors and reports that task
characteristics have received comparatively little attention (Mahmud et al.,
2022). Whether the stakes of a consumer decision, for instance its financial,
health or privacy consequences, raise resistance to generative advice beyond
what the utilitarian–hedonic distinction predicts is therefore still an open
question for marketing research.
3.2. Authenticity, Disclosure, and Moral Reactions
Kirk and Givi (2025) report seven preregistered experiments in which
consumers who believed an emotional marketing message had been written
by an AI, and not by a human, gave less positive word of mouth and reported
lower loyalty . The authors label this the AI-authorship effect. It was serially
mediated by perceived authenticity and moral disgust, a mechanism they
develop from authenticity theory . The effects appeared with both personalized

Suat Okay | 151
and mass communications, across several emotions, and with hypothetical as
well as behavioral measures.
Several boundary conditions bear directly on practice. The effect was
weaker for factual messages than for emotional ones, weaker when the AI
only edited a message, and weaker when the message was signed directly by
an AI. It weakened as well when consumers believed that most marketing
communications are now written by AI. It reversed when consumers believed
the message had been reused and not originally composed by the sender. Read
together, these results suggest that the penalty attaches less to the use of AI
as such than to a perceived breach of expectations about who is speaking and
whether the sentiment is the sender’s own.
The contrast between factual and emotional messages has a practical
reading. Much routine marketing text, such as specifications, delivery notices
and answers to frequently asked questions, is factual, and the authorship penalty
reported for such text was weaker. Emotional content, including thank-you
notes, apologies and founder stories, is where a firm’s voice is most exposed.
The experiments do not say where a promotional slogan or a product story
falls on this continuum, and that classification is a judgment each team has
to make for itself.
Disclosure choices interact with consumers’ limited ability to detect AI
authorship. Jakesch et al. (2023) report that people rely on intuitive cues
when judging whether a text was written by an AI, and that these cues can
mislead them. If audiences cannot reliably tell, the decision to disclose falls
to the firm. That is where the ethical and regulatory questions of Section 5
become commercial ones.
3.3. Anthropomorphism and Service Encounters
Crolic et al. (2022) examined chatbot-led service interactions in five
studies, one of which analyzed a large real-world dataset from an international
telecommunications company , with the other four being experiments. When
customers entered the interaction angry , chatbot anthropomorphism, meaning
the use of humanlike qualities such as a name or an avatar, was associated with
lower satisfaction, weaker evaluations of the firm and lower purchase intentions.
This did not hold for customers who were not angry . The authors point to
expectancy violations: a humanlike bot raises pre-encounter expectations of its
efficacy , and angry customers react badly when those expectations are not met.
The result cautions against a common design reflex, which is to make every
conversational agent more humanlike. It also shows why aggregate satisfaction
scores can mislead, since a design that works for the typical contact may

perform poorly in the complaint queue. Generative models make humanlike
conversation cheaper and more fluent, so the expectancy mechanism identified
by Crolic et al. may become more relevant and not less. That remains a
hypothesis and not a finding.

Prompts and uploaded material are a form of data capture. Personalization

4. Organizational and Managerial Implications
4.1. Productivity and the Distribution of Gains
In an online experiment, Noy and Zhang (2023) asked participants to
complete mid-level professional writing tasks with or without access to
ChatGPT . Access reduced completion time and raised rated output quality ,
and it narrowed differences between participants. Brynjolfsson et al. (2025)
report a related pattern in a workplace: gains varied considerably across support
agents, and earlier versions of the work reported the largest improvements
among less experienced agents. Taken together, the two studies suggest that
generative tools may compress performance differences in structured text
work, although neither study follows workers long enough to show whether
the compression lasts.
Neither setting is marketing in the strict sense. Support chat and mid-
level writing resemble parts of marketing work, but strategy , brand judgment
and analytics involve different tasks, and gains observed in structured text
production may not transfer. Hermann et al. (2025) add a psychological
dimension by discussing how generative tools may alter the experience of
work itself, which matters for motivation and retention even where measured
output rises.
4.2. Creativity, Homogenization and Brand Distinctiveness
Doshi and Hauser (2024) asked writers to produce short stories with or
without AI-generated ideas. Stories written with AI ideas were rated as more
creative at the individual level, yet they resembled one another more than
stories written without such help. The finding separates two questions that
are easily merged: whether a given piece of work improves, and whether the
pool of work becomes less varied.
For marketing the implication is collective. If many brands draw on similar
models and similar prompts, their output may converge, and distinctiveness,
which brand strategy has long treated as valuable, could erode. The risk is

154 | Generative Artificial Intelligence in Marketing: Consumer Responses, Authenticity...
sharpest in categories where competitors already share suppliers, agencies
and visual conventions, because the tools would add one more common
input. Firms that rely on generative content may therefore need to invest
deliberately in what the models cannot supply , such as proprietary brand assets,
a recognisable voice and human creative direction. The study concerns short
fiction, and the sources reviewed here contain no direct test of convergence in
advertising or branding. Cillo and Rubera’s (2025) concern that consumers
may accept machine-generated proposals without contributing ideas of their
own points in a similar direction.
4.3. Human Oversight and Capability Building
Kshetri et al. (2024) discuss both facilitators and barriers to GenAI
use in marketing, and Grewal et al. (2025) offer a practical framework for
implementation. The T urkish data protection authority’s 2025 guide stresses
that model outputs should be checked by people, a point that applies to
campaign copy as much as to personal data questions (Kişisel V erileri Koruma
Kurumu [KVKK], 2025). Bibliometric evidence points the same way . Akbulut
(2026) identifies governance, including regulation and responsible AI, as one
of three thematic clusters in research on AI in marketing.
Hermann and Puntoni (2025a) describe four forms of empowerment that
GenAI may offer marketers, consumers and researchers: operational, creative,
agentic and normative. For managers these offer a way to ask whose capabilities
a particular deployment actually extends. A tool that raises operational efficiency
for the firm while narrowing the agency of customers, for example, extends
capability unevenly , and the framework makes that visible.

5. Ethical, Environmental, and Regulatory Issues
Hermann and Puntoni (2025b) build a conceptual framework along two
dimensions, the degree to which GenAI enhances or replaces humans and the
stage of the marketing cycle, and derive from the AI ethics literature a set of
principles under the acronym ASSURANCE: autonomy , security , sustainability ,
representativeness, accountability , nonbiasedness and nondiscrimination,
crediting, and empowerment. They also set out public policy implications for
each principle. The subsections below take up issues that map onto several
of these principles.
5.1. Accuracy, Hallucination, and Brand Risk
Ji et al. (2023) survey hallucination in natural language generation, meaning
text that reads fluently but is unfaithful to a source or wrong as a matter of
fact, and review how it is measured and mitigated. Bender et al. (2021) raised

156 | Generative Artificial Intelligence in Marketing: Consumer Responses, Authenticity...
an earlier and broader concern: language models can produce coherent text
without any grounding in meaning, and the scale at which they are built
brings costs and risks that are often poorly documented.
In marketing the stakes are concrete. A generated product description
can state a feature the product lacks, and a generated sustainability claim can
assert a certification that does not exist. Errors of this kind create exposure
under consumer protection rules and damage reputation, and it is the firm,
not the model vendor, that publishes the message. The T urkish data protection
authority lists hallucination among the risks of generative systems and
recommends human verification of outputs (KVKK, 2025).
5.2. Bias and Representation
Bianchi et al. (2023) found that ordinary prompts to widely used text-to-
image models produced stereotyped images, including prompts that mentioned
only traits, occupations or objects. The stereotypes persisted despite mitigation
attempts, whether users asked for counter-stereotypical images or providers
added guardrails. Advertising depends on images of people and on decisions
about whom to show, so a campaign that uses synthetic imagery at scale
can reproduce such patterns at scale. T wo of the ASSURANCE principles,
representativeness and nonbiasedness, speak to this risk (Hermann & Puntoni,
2025b). Hartmann et al. (2025) show that such images can be competitive
on performance measures, and performance on those measures does not, by
itself, say anything about representation.
5.3. Privacy, Manipulation, and Dark Patterns
Acquisti et al. (2020) review evidence that consumers care about privacy
yet find it hard to act on that concern, in part because their preferences
depend on context and can be influenced by how choices are framed. Luguri
and Strahilevitz (2021) tested dark patterns, meaning interface designs that
steer users toward choices they might not otherwise make, in an online
experiment. Acceptance of a dubious service was roughly one in nine in the
control condition, about one in four under mild dark patterns and above two
in five under aggressive ones.
Generative interfaces add a new question. A system that converses can
adjust its wording to a particular user in real time, and whether such adaptivity
changes susceptibility to manipulative design is an empirical matter that studies
using fixed interface variants did not address. Puntoni et al. (2021) place
data capture among the four consumer experiences of AI, and conversational

Suat Okay | 157
systems that invite users to disclose more, more casually , make that experience
more prominent.
5.4. Environmental Footprint and Sustainability Claims
De Vries (2023) examines the growing energy footprint of AI and relies on
scenarios whose outcomes depend heavily on hardware supply and adoption,
which leaves considerable uncertainty . Bender et al. (2021) had already flagged
the environmental cost of ever larger language models. For marketers the issue
has a reputational side. A brand that promotes its environmental credentials
while producing large volumes of synthetic content with energy-intensive
systems may find the two activities set against each other.
Such a comparison would not necessarily be fair, because energy use
per asset depends on the model, the hardware and the usage pattern, and
reliable figures are hard to obtain. Even so, the sustainability principle in the
ASSURANCE framework (Hermann & Puntoni, 2025b) suggests that firms
ought to be able to account for the environmental cost of their own GenAI
use before they claim leadership elsewhere. For green marketing research
this is a practical opening, because consistency between a firm’s claims and
its operations is the kind of question on which greenwashing assessments
already turn.
5.5. Regulatory Developments in the European Union and Türkiye
Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the Artificial Intelligence Act, was published
in the Official Journal on 12 July 2024 and entered into force on 1 August
2024 (European Union, 2024). Its obligations apply in phases. For marketing,
the transparency duties in Article 50 are the most directly relevant: people
must be told when they interact with an AI system, synthetic outputs must be
marked in a machine-readable way , and deepfakes must be disclosed. EUR-Lex
lists a consolidated version of the Regulation dated July 2026, so application
dates and details should be checked against the current text. Disclosure duties
and the authenticity penalty documented by Kirk and Givi (2025) may pull
in different directions, since the law can require a label that consumers may
penalize. Research on label wording and placement is therefore practically
urgent.
In Türkiye, the Personal Data Protection Authority issued recommendations
on protecting personal data in the field of AI in 2021 for developers, producers,
service providers and decision makers, framed within Law No. 6698 (KVKK,
2021). Its 2025 guide on generative AI and personal data, organized around
fifteen questions, addresses data processing at the training, use and output

158 | Generative Artificial Intelligence in Marketing: Consumer Responses, Authenticity...
stages, the roles of controller and processor, and risks such as hallucination,
bias, security breaches and intellectual property (KVKK, 2025). These are
supervisory guidance documents issued within the data protection framework,
and they bear on marketing indirectly , through personalization, customer
service and content generation. Legal analysis lies beyond the scope of a
marketing chapter, and readers making compliance decisions should consult

6. Research Agenda
The first priority is durability . The AI-authorship effect (Kirk & Givi, 2025)
was established in experiments, and it is unknown whether it persists, grows
or fades as consumers become accustomed to AI-written communication.
Longitudinal designs and field data from real brand communications would
show whether the penalty is a transitional reaction or a stable feature of how
authenticity is judged.
The second concerns disclosure design. If the AI Act requires labels
(European Union, 2024) and labels may carry a cost, then wording, placement
and timing become empirical variables. Experiments could compare formats,
and they should test for differences among consumers, since the mediators
identified by Kirk and Givi, perceived authenticity and moral disgust, are
unlikely to be evenly distributed.
Third, evidence from Türkiye and from comparable markets is thin. The
T urkish studies reviewed here are mainly bibliometric or review-based (Açan,
2025; Akbulut, 2026; Akyılmaz, 2022; Ekinci & Bilginer Özsaatçi, 2023) or
conceptual (Durmuş Şenyapar, 2024). Replicating the experiments on word-of-
machine preferences, authorship penalties and chatbot anthropomorphism with
T urkish consumer samples would show which findings travel, and measurement
equivalence across languages should be checked before comparisons are drawn.
Fourth, homogenization needs measurement at the level where marketers
operate. Doshi and Hauser (2024) studied short stories. Similarity metrics
applied to the advertising copy or imagery of competing brands in categories
with heavy GenAI use could show whether distinctiveness declines, and
whether consumers notice.
Fifth, the environmental side of GenAI in marketing has barely been studied.
Estimates of energy use per generated asset, combined with experiments on
how consumers react to a firm’s disclosure of its digital footprint, would
connect the AI literature with work on green marketing and greenwashing
(de Vries, 2023; Hermann & Puntoni, 2025b).

Finally , the validity of GenAI as a research instrument deserves its own
program. Cillo and Rubera (2025) raise research questions on using GenAI
to analyze consumers and markets. Studies that compare synthetic and
human responses on the same instruments, across topics and populations,
would indicate where substitution is defensible. Work on the psychology
of employees who use these tools (Hermann et al., 2025) and on the four
forms of empowerment proposed by Hermann and Puntoni (2025a) could
be brought into the same program.
7. Conclusion
Generative AI has entered marketing practice faster than the evidence base
has grown. The studies reviewed in this chapter allow some careful statements.
Consumers do not respond to machine involvement in a uniform way: their
reactions depend on the task, on what they believe about authorship, and
on the emotional state in which they meet the system. Gains in productivity
appear to be uneven across workers, and creative output may improve
for individuals while becoming more similar across firms. Risks such as
hallucination, stereotyped imagery , manipulative interface design and energy
use are documented in general terms, although few studies test them in
marketing settings.
Regulation is moving toward transparency . The EU Artificial Intelligence
Act introduces disclosure and marking duties, and T urkish supervisory guidance
emphasizes verification and lawful data processing. These texts raise a practical
tension, because the act of disclosure may itself alter how consumers judge a
message. Marketers cannot resolve that tension by intuition, and researchers
can help by testing formats rather than assuming effects.
For scholars, the chapter points to a mismatch between what is easy
to study and what matters. Online experiments on authorship labels are
quick to run, whereas questions about homogenization, long-run trust and
environmental cost need data that firms hold and rarely release. Collaboration
with practitioners who can share campaign-level data under suitable safeguards
would help. For practitioners, the main message is modest: test before scaling,
verify before publishing, and keep a person visibly responsible for what the
brand says.
The chapter has clear limits. It is a narrative review, it relies on a selected
set of sources, and many of the underlying studies use online experiments and
Western samples. Some of the reasoning offered here, such as the mapping
of generative tools onto the four consumer experiences or the discussion of
expectancy effects with more fluent chatbots, is interpretation that needs

most valuable next step is evidence from field settings, from longer time
horizons and from markets such as Türkiye, where the literature so far has
mostly counted and classified studies and has tested behavior less often.
