Abstract. Artificial intelligence has become a major force in digital marketing because it
allows firms to automate content creation, personalize recommendations, and communicate
with consumers through chatbots. However, the same technology also raises concerns about
authenticity, privacy, transparency and the loss of human connection. This paper examines
how generative artificial intelligence influences consumer trust and purchase decisions in
digital marketing. Using a qualitative literature review and conceptual analysis, the paper
compares academic studies on AI marketing, personalization, privacy, chatbot disclosure,
and algorithm aversion. The central research focus is to examine how generative AI affects
consumer trust and purchasing behavior, and how firms can use it responsibly. This paper
argues that generative AI can improve purchase decisions when it provides relevant
information, faster interaction, and personalized recommendations. However, its positive
effect depends strongly on transparency, privacy protection, and human oversight. The
conclusion suggests that AI should be used as a supportive marketing tool, not as a complete
replacement for human judgment.
Keywords: Generative AI, Digital Marketing, Consumer Trust, Purchase Decision,
Personalization
1. Introduction
Artificial intelligence has become an important part of digital marketing. Brands now use AI to write
advertising content, recommend products, answer customer questions, analyze consumer data, and
predict buying behavior. This shift matters because AI not only changes how companies market
products; it also changes how consumers experience brands and make purchase decisions.
Davenport et al. argue that AI is likely to transform marketing strategy, customer engagement, and
the relationship between firms and consumers [1].
AI is especially important in digital marketing because online platforms produce large amounts of
consumer data. Firms can use this data to understand what consumers search for, what they click on,
what they buy, and what they ignore. Huang and Rust explain that AI can support marketing
research, segmentation, targeting, positioning, and customer-facing actions [2]. More recently,
generative AI has made this change more visible because it can produce text, images, product
descriptions, chatbot replies, and social media content at high speed [3]. Instead of only analyzing
data, AI can now create marketing content directly.

However, AI marketing also creates new tensions. Consumers may enjoy faster service and more
relevant recommendations, but they may also feel uncomfortable when brands collect too much
personal data or hide the use of automated systems. Puntoni et al. show that consumer experiences
with AI involve data capture, classification, delegation, and social interaction, meaning that AI
affects both practical convenience and emotional response [4]. In service contexts, AI can automate
routine interactions, but this also raises questions about when human judgment is still needed [5].
Privacy is especially important because personalized marketing often depends on collecting and
analyzing consumer information [6].
This paper examines the impact of generative AI on consumer trust and purchase decisions in
digital marketing. The research focus is to examine how generative AI influences consumer trust and
purchasing behavior, and how firms can use AI responsibly. Using a literature review method, this
paper argues that generative AI can increase marketing efficiency and support consumer decision-
making, but only when firms manage transparency, privacy, authenticity, and human oversight
carefully.
2. Conceptual background: AI and generative AI in digital marketing
Artificial intelligence in marketing refers to the use of machine-based systems to analyze
information, predict consumer behavior, automate communication, and support marketing decisions.
In traditional marketing, many decisions relied heavily on human judgment, market research, and
past experience. AI changes this process because it can process large amounts of consumer data
much faster than humans. For example, AI can identify customer groups, predict product interest,
rank advertising audiences, and recommend content based on previous behavior [1].
Generative AI is a more recent and visible form of AI. Unlike older AI tools that mainly classified
information or predicted outcomes, generative AI can create new content. In digital marketing, this
includes product descriptions, email campaigns, chatbot responses, image drafts, video scripts,
search advertisements, and social media captions. This gives firms a new way to produce marketing
material at speed and at lower cost. Peres et al. argue that generative AI can affect research,
teaching, and business practice because it changes how people create and use information [3]. In
marketing, this is especially important because communication is central to brand building.
AI is now used in several major areas of digital marketing. The first is personalized
recommendations. Online platforms can suggest products based on browsing history, purchase
history, and similar users' behavior. The second is customer service automation. AI chatbots can
answer routine questions about delivery, returns, stock availability, and product details. The third is
content generation. Brands can use generative AI to create early drafts of advertisements, product
pages, or social media posts. These applications show that AI is not limited to one part of marketing.
It can influence the entire consumer journey, from awareness to purchase and post-purchase support
[2].
However, the meaning of AI in marketing should not be reduced to efficiency. AI also changes
the way consumers judge brands. If AI makes the experience easier, consumers may see the brand as
helpful and modern. If AI feels intrusive, fake, or confusing, consumers may become less trusting.
Therefore, the value of AI depends not only on what the technology can do, but also on how
consumers interpret its use.

3. How generative AI improves digital marketing
One major advantage of generative AI is that it can improve personalization. In online shopping,
consumers often face too much information. They may need to compare many products, prices,
reviews, and brands before making a decision. AI recommendation systems can reduce this search
cost by showing consumers products that are more likely to match their needs. Bleier and Eisenbeiss
show that personalized online advertising can be effective, but its success depends on what
information is shown, when it is shown, and where it appears [7]. This means that personalization is
not simply about showing more advertisements. It is about showing more relevant information at the
right moment.
Generative AI can also improve marketing efficiency. Digital marketing requires constant content
production. Brands need website copy, product descriptions, emails, advertisements, social media
posts, and customer replies. Creating this content manually can be slow and expensive. Generative
AI can help firms create first drafts quickly, test different versions of marketing messages, and
adjust tone for different platforms. For example, the same product can be described in a formal tone
for a website, a casual tone for social media, and a short promotional tone for an email campaign.
This does not mean AI should replace human marketers, but it can reduce repetitive writing work
and allow human staff to focus on strategy and creativity [3].
Another important benefit is real-time customer interaction. Consumers increasingly expect quick
answers when they shop online. If a customer wants to know whether a product is available, how
long delivery takes, or how to return an item, a slow response may reduce the chance of purchase.
AI chatbots can answer simple questions instantly and operate outside normal business hours.
Huang and Rust argue that AI has an important role in service because it can perform routine service
tasks and support customer interaction [5]. In this sense, AI can improve convenience and reduce
waiting time.
These benefits can influence consumer purchase decisions. If AI helps consumers find the right
product faster, gives useful information, and removes friction from the buying process, consumers
may be more willing to buy. However, these benefits are not automatic. If the recommendation feels
too personal, if the chatbot gives poor answers, or if AI-generated content feels artificial, the same
technology may reduce trust. Therefore, the positive impact of AI depends on how well firms
manage the consumer experience.
4. The effect of AI on consumer trust
Trust is central to digital marketing because consumers often make decisions without physical
contact with the product or the seller. In online environments, consumers rely on brand reputation,
website design, reviews, product descriptions, and customer service. AI can support trust when it
improves usefulness and convenience. If consumers receive accurate recommendations, fast
answers, and clear information, they may view the brand as competent and reliable. Huang and
Rust's framework suggests that AI can support customer-facing marketing actions, which means AI
can help firms respond more effectively to consumer needs [2].
However, AI can also damage trust when consumers feel that their privacy is at risk. Personalized
marketing often depends on collecting consumer data. This can include search history, purchase
history, location, browsing time, and interaction patterns. Martin and Murphy explain that data
privacy plays an important role in marketing because consumers care about how their personal
information is collected and used [6]. The problem is that consumers often want personalized
services, but they do not want to feel monitored. Aguirre et al. describe this as the personalization

Transparency is another important factor. Consumers may react differently when they know
whether they are interacting with a human or a machine. If a company hides the use of a chatbot,
consumers may feel deceived when they later discover it. Luo et al. find that chatbot disclosure can
affect customer purchases, especially when consumers hold negative assumptions about machine
interaction [9]. This does not mean firms should always avoid disclosure. Instead, it suggests that
firms should design AI communication carefully. Consumers may accept AI more easily when the
chatbot is clearly introduced, helpful, and able to transfer complex issues to a human staff member.
A further challenge is algorithm aversion. Some consumers may distrust AI because they believe
machines lack emotion, creativity, or moral judgment. Castelo et al. show that algorithm aversion is
task-dependent, meaning that people may accept algorithms for objective tasks but reject them for
subjective or personal tasks [10]. This is highly relevant to marketing. For simple tasks, such as
tracking an order or recommending similar products, consumers may accept AI. For more emotional
or high-involvement decisions, such as luxury purchases, complaint handling, or sensitive product
categories, consumers may prefer human interaction.
Therefore, AI affects trust in two opposite ways. It can build trust through speed, usefulness, and
relevance. But it can also weaken trust through privacy concerns, lack of transparency, and the
feeling that the brand is becoming less human.
5. The effect of AI on purchase decisions
Generative AI can influence purchase decisions by changing what consumers see, how they evaluate
options, and how confident they feel before buying. Personalized recommendations are one of the
clearest examples. When consumers receive product suggestions that match their interests, they may
spend less time searching and more time considering the recommended items. This can increase
purchase intention because the consumer feels that the brand understands their needs. However, the
recommendation must feel useful rather than invasive. If consumers think the brand knows too much
about them, the same recommendation may create discomfort [7].
AI-generated content can also shape brand perception. Product descriptions, advertisements, and
social media posts are not neutral. They affect how consumers understand the brand's personality
and value. Generative AI can help firms create content quickly, but there is a risk that the content
becomes generic or emotionally weak. If consumers feel that a brand's communication is fully
machine-generated, they may question its authenticity. Puntoni et al. suggest that consumer
experiences with AI involve not only function but also emotion and social meaning [4]. This means
firms need to consider how AI-generated messages feel to consumers, not only whether they are
grammatically correct.
Chatbots can influence purchases during the decision process. In many online shopping
situations, consumers ask questions before buying. A quick and useful chatbot response may help
consumers move forward. For example, if a chatbot confirms stock availability or explains a return
policy clearly, the customer may feel more comfortable purchasing. However, if consumers know
they are speaking to a chatbot and believe it cannot understand their concern, their willingness to
purchase may decrease [9]. This is especially true when the issue is complex or emotional. A chatbot
that cannot solve the problem may create frustration and push consumers away from the brand.
Privacy risk can also reduce willingness to buy. Even if AI recommendations are accurate,
consumers may avoid buying if they feel that the brand is using their data in an unclear or invasive
way. This is important because trust and purchase decisions are closely connected. A consumer may

6. Discussion: How firms should use generative AI responsibly
The discussion above suggests that firms should not use generative AI only as a tool for speed and
cost reduction. Responsible AI marketing requires a balance between efficiency and trust. The first
responsibility is to balance personalization and privacy. Firms should avoid collecting more data
than necessary and should explain how consumer information is used. They should also provide
consumers with control, such as the ability to manage preferences, reject certain tracking methods,
or understand why they are receiving a recommendation. This can reduce the feeling of hidden
surveillance and make personalization feel more acceptable [6, 8].
The second responsibility is to use AI as support, not the replacement. AI is strong at processing
data, creating drafts, answering routine questions, and identifying patterns. However, marketing also
requires human judgement, emotional understanding, and ethical awareness. A brand message may
be technically correct but still feel cold or inappropriate. Human marketers are needed to check tone,
context, and brand meaning. This is especially important when consumers are making subjective or
emotional decisions, because algorithm aversion is stronger in those situations [10].
The third responsibility is transparency. Firms should clearly disclose when consumers are
interacting with AI chatbots or AI-generated communication. However, transparency should be
combined with good design. Simply telling consumers that this is AI is not enough if the AI
experience is poor. The chatbot should be useful, easy to understand, and able to pass the customer
to a human employee when needed. This approach can reduce the feeling of deception and help
consumers understand the role of AI in the service process [9].
The fourth responsibility is to protect brand authenticity. AI-generated content can make
marketing faster, but overuse can make the brand feel generic. Consumers may not reject AI content
simply because it is made by AI, but they may reject content that feels empty, repetitive, or
disconnected from the brand's identity. Therefore, firms should use AI to support communication
while still keeping a clear human voice. AI can help draft the message, but human marketers should
decide whether the message fits the brand and the audience [4].
In short, responsible AI marketing is not about avoiding AI. It is about using AI in a way that
consumers can understand, accept, and trust. The most successful firms will likely be those that
combine AI efficiency with human judgment.
7. Conclusion
This paper has examined how generative AI influences consumer trust and purchase decisions in
digital marketing. The analysis shows that generative AI has a strong but mixed impact. On one
hand, AI can improve the consumer journey by making marketing more personalized, efficient, and
convenient. Recommendation systems can reduce search effort, chatbots can provide instant
responses, and generative AI can help brands create marketing content quickly. These benefits may
increase purchase intention when consumers see AI as useful, accurate, and relevant.

On the other hand, AI does not automatically create trust. The same personalization that improves
convenience can also create privacy concerns if consumers feel that their data is collected without
enough control. AI-generated content may reduce perceived authenticity if consumers believe that a
brand is relying too heavily on machines. Chatbot disclosure can also create resistance when
consumers prefer human interaction, especially in situations involving emotion, judgment, or
complex decisions. Therefore, the effect of AI on purchase decisions depends not only on technical
performance but also on consumer perception.
The answer to the research question is that generative AI can positively influence consumer trust
and purchase decisions when it is transparent, useful, privacy-conscious, and supported by human
oversight. However, if firms use AI in a hidden, invasive, or overly automated way, it may damage
trust and reduce consumers' willingness to buy. This means that companies should not treat AI as a
simple replacement for human marketing. Instead, they should use it as a support tool that improves
speed and relevance while preserving human judgment, emotional connection, and ethical
responsibility.
The main limitation of this paper is that it is based on secondary literature rather than primary
consumer survey data. Future research could compare different age groups, industries, or countries
to examine whether consumers respond differently to AI marketing. For example, younger
consumers may be more comfortable with AI chatbots, while older consumers may place greater
value on human service. Future research could also examine whether consumers respond differently
to AI in low-risk purchases compared with high-risk purchases. Overall, generative AI will continue
to shape digital marketing, but its long-term success will depend on whether firms can use it in a
way that strengthens rather than weakens consumer trust.
