Abstract. In recent years, Artificial Intelligence (AI) has rapidly changed digital marketing,
and the rise of Artificial Intelligence Generated Content (AIGC) has made the industry feel
like a "content factory that never closes." From advertising copy to social media posts, AI
can quickly create huge amounts of content and provide personalised experiences for
consumers. However, as AI-generated content becomes more and more "human-like," an
interesting question appears: are consumers trusting the brand or the algorithm? In digital
marketing, trust is like an "invisible admission ticket" — without it, even the best content
may fail to impress consumers. This study explores what makes consumers trust AI-
generated content, what causes that trust to weaken, and how trust affects marketing results.
By reviewing existing studies on AI, marketing and consumer behaviour, the research finds
that authenticity, credibility, transparency and personalization can strongly influence trust.
At the same time, privacy concerns and AI disclosure may make consumers feel
uncomfortable. Sometimes AI feels like a "too enthusiastic friend" who suddenly knows
everything about you after you casually look at one product online. Although AI makes
marketing faster and smarter, some AI-generated content still feels like "every sentence is
correct, but something is missing." It may sound logical, but it does not always create real
emotional connection. Overall, this study argues that building trust in AI marketing is not
only about making technology more advanced, but also about finding a balance between
"machine efficiency" and "human warmth."
Keywords: Artificial Intelligence Generated Content (AIGC), Consumer Trust, Digital
Marketing
1. Introduction
In recent years, artificial intelligence has become an important force driving changes in digital
marketing and is transforming the way businesses communicate with consumers at an
unprecedented speed. The emergence of Artificial Intelligence Generated Content (AIGC), including
text, images, videos and advertising materials, etc., has made enterprises seem to have a "content
factory that never goes out". Whether it's advertising copy, social media tweets, or product
recommendations, AI can quickly generate a large amount of content, not only improving efficiency
but also providing more personalized content based on different consumers. With the continuous
development of generative artificial intelligence technology, it is increasingly being applied to

digital platforms such as social media and e-commerce. However, behind the rapid development of
these technologies, issues regarding authenticity, credibility and transparency keep emerging like

Consumer trust is of great significance in the digital environment because the online world itself
is full of uncertainties, and a lot of information is hard to distinguish as true or false. With the
increasing amount of AI-generated content, trust has become even more crucial. Many consumers
will start to wonder: Is the content they see written by real people or was it generated by algorithms

decline significantly. But if they are willing to believe, they will be more likely to interact, purchase
products, and even pay long-term attention to this brand. Nowadays, more and more people are
beginning to pay attention to: What exactly is influencing consumers' trust in AIGC? Research has
found that whether consumers are willing to believe in AI marketing is not only determined by the
technology's capabilities, but also related to their understanding of AI and whether they feel at ease
in their hearts [1]. To put it bluntly, what consumers care about is not only whether AI is "smart", but
also whether it "looks like a real person" and whether it has warmth when communicating.
Against this backdrop, this study primarily examines whether consumers trust AI-generated
content in a marketing context. It focuses on analysing the factors that influence consumer trust, the
issues that lead consumers to distrust AI content, and the consequences of such trust. Particular
attention is paid to authenticity, credibility, transparency and personalisation, as these factors all
influence how consumers perceive AI marketing. Additionally, the study examines privacy concerns
and the potential negative implications of 'AI identity disclosure'. Sometimes, AI is like an "overly
considerate" friend: it not only knows what you like, but can even precisely push advertisements to
you before you even realize you want to shop.
To achieve the above research goals, this paper adopts the method of qualitative literature review
to systematically analyze and integrate the existing academic research in the fields of artificial
intelligence, marketing communication and consumer behavior. The research identified the key
themes in the literature and classified the factors influencing trust into different dimensions such as
technology, psychology and relationship. By integrating multiple research results, this paper
provides a relatively systematic understanding of the formation mechanism of consumer trust and
how it affects marketing effectiveness.

2. Artificial intelligence generated content in marketing
If digital marketing in the past was like a "handicraft workshop dominated by human creativity",
then today's marketing industry is more like the sudden arrival of a "super employee" - artificial
intelligence - who doesn't get off work, doesn't complain, and doesn't even ask for a raise. Artificial
Intelligence Generated Content (AIGC) refers to various types of content generated through artificial
intelligence technology rather than direct human creation, including text, images, videos, and
promotional materials, etc. With the rapid development of generative artificial intelligence tools, an
increasing number of enterprises are beginning to adopt these technologies to enhance efficiency,

Nowadays, many studies are suggesting that the role of artificial intelligence in marketing is
becoming increasingly significant. Huang and Rust believe that AI has begun to take over many
tasks that previously required human intervention, gradually transforming the entire service and
marketing approach [2]. Davenport et al. also mentioned that AI can help enterprises automatically
complete many marketing tasks and adjust different interactive contents based on consumers' usual

the rules of the marketing industry. In the past, for a single advertising copy, the team might have to
stay up late many times to revise it. Nowadays, AI can directly generate over a dozen versions in
just a few seconds, and its efficiency is so fast that one can't help but wonder: "Is it people using AI
now, or is AI pushing people to work?"
Artificial intelligence can also help enterprises generate a large amount of content quickly, so it is
particularly useful in social media and e-commerce platforms. Verma et al. pointed out that AI not
only enhances work efficiency, but is also gradually transforming the entire way of marketing
communication [4]. In the past, marketing was more like "casting a wide net", while today's AI
marketing is more like "precise navigation" - it always tries to guess what consumers want to see
before they even speak. Sometimes, consumers just chat with friends about wanting to buy sports
shoes, and the next second, social media has already started to push related advertisements
frantically. This experience of "AI understanding you better than yourself" is both astonishing and
inevitably gives people the illusion that "the phone might be eavesdropping on me".
However, despite the numerous advantages brought by artificial intelligence, the increasing
reliance of enterprises on AI-generated content has also raised concerns about its authenticity and
credibility. As trust is a crucial determining factor influencing consumer behavior, especially in the
online environment, it is becoming increasingly important to study consumers' trust in AIGC. After
all, consumers may be willing to accept AI-recommended products, but they may not be willing to
fully trust an algorithm that "understands them better than themselves". In a sense, AI marketing is
like an overly enthusiastic friend: it can always precisely recommend things you like, but when it
knows too much, people can't help but start to wonder - "How on earth does it know?"
3. Factors influencing consumer trust in AIGC
Existing research has found that multiple factors can affect consumers' trust in AI-generated content,
including authenticity, credibility, transparency and personalization. These factors jointly influence
how consumers view AI marketing content. To some extent, when consumers are confronted with AI
content, they are like "Sherlock Holmes on the Internet", constantly judging whether this
information is true and reliable or just a "perfect coat" put on by the algorithm.
As shown in Table 1, these factors will affect consumer trust in different ways. They interweave
with each other, like an invisible net, jointly influencing consumers' overall perception of AI
marketing content.
Among them, Perceived authenticity is regarded as one of the most important factors. Kirk and
Givi proposed the "AI-Authorship effect", pointing out that when consumers know that the content
is generated by AI rather than humans, they tend to reduce their positive responses [5]. This
indicates that whether consumers trust content depends not only on its quality but also on "who
wrote it". Even if the content generated by AI is logically clear and grammatically perfect,
consumers may still wonder: Are these words merely "expressing emotions" or just "executing

Furthermore, Credibility is also very important in the process of trust formation. When AI-

4. Factors reducing consumer trust in AIGC
Although artificial intelligence has demonstrated extremely strong capabilities in the marketing
field, consumers' trust in AI-generated content is not as stable as algorithmic results. Sometimes, this
kind of trust is more like a mobile phone battery — it may seem to have 80% remaining, but in
reality, it can suddenly drop to 20%. Therefore, understanding the true impact of AI marketing
depends not only on how advanced the technology is, but also on the moment when consumers
begin to feel that "it really kills the mood."
Often, the problem lies in the disclosure of AI identities. Nakano et al. pointed out that when
consumers know that the content is written by AI rather than humans, they often feel that the content
lacks a bit of sincerity and is not as trustworthy [9]. Transparency is supposed to be a good thing, but
in reality, it sometimes acts like a "blunder" in a movie - the audience is already immersed in the
plot, but as soon as they notice the behind-the-scenes special effects, their emotions are immediately
pulled back to reality. When consumers realize that those seemingly natural interactions are actually
just code and data running, the original emotional warmth of the content will gradually fade away.
What many people start to care about is not just "whether the content is well-written or not", but
"whether it really looks real or not".
Related to this is also consumers' perception of the lack of authenticity. People usually associate
marketing content with genuine emotions, creative expressions and personal experiences. However,
although AI-generated content is highly efficient, it often gives people the impression that the
"standard answers are too standard". It's like a "buyer's show retouched photo" in online shopping -
it's stunning at first glance, but after looking at it for a while, you always feel that something is not
quite real. Consumers may recognize the logic and fluency of AI content, but it is difficult for them
to truly resonate emotionally.
Privacy issues are another factor that makes consumers easily "alert to going online". AI
marketing requires a large amount of data support. The system will continuously analyze user
behavior and interest preferences to achieve precise recommendations. However, this kind of
precision is sometimes so precise as to be "too coincidental". Many people have experienced this:
just casually mentioning a certain product, and the next second, related advertisements start to "pop
up in groups". Martin and Murphy pointed out that consumers' concerns about data usage would
significantly reduce their sense of trust [10]. When people feel that their actions are constantly being
tracked, the original sense of convenience may gradually turn into a sense of oppression.
Furthermore, many consumers have no understanding of AI technology itself. To them, AI is like
a "silent but all-knowing colleague" - it can always provide quick results but never explain its
thought process. This sense of mystery, on the one hand, enhances the AI's "high depth perception",
and on the other hand, it also creates a sense of distance.
Overall, consumers' trust in AI-generated content is not simply determined by the level of
technology. AI may be able to achieve the ultimate in efficiency, but trust is more like an emotional
experience that requires a sense of reality, understanding, and the gradual accumulation of warmth
between people. If these issues are not handled properly, the convenience that AI originally intended
to offer may eventually become a source of unease in consumers' minds.

5. Outcomes of consumer trust in AIGC
Consumers' trust in AI-generated content will directly affect the effectiveness of marketing. It not
only affects how consumers "think", but also how they "act". Often, trust is like a "ticket" to enter
the digital world - with it, consumers are more likely to accept information and make decisions.
Without it, even the most complex AI system might just be "one person working frantically in the
background". In a certain sense, the success of AI marketing does not merely depend on how
advanced the technology is, but on whether consumers are willing to believe in it.
As illustrated in Table 2, trust influences key outcomes such as purchase intention, consumer
attitudes and online behaviour.
One of the most obvious influences is the willingness to purchase. When consumers find AI-
generated content trustworthy, they are more likely to shift from "thinking about it" to "buying it
right away". In the constantly updated online environment of information, trust is like a "power-
saving mode", helping consumers reduce thinking costs and make decisions more quickly.
Therefore, reliable AI content can increase the possibility of consumers making purchases. However,
if consumers lack trust, even if the recommendations are highly accurate, there may still be a
situation where the advertisement is pushed over and over again, but consumers remain as stable as
"viewers of anti-fraud promotional videos". Sometimes, AI has worked so hard that it's like a
"salesperson working late into the night", but consumers still quietly click "not interested".
In addition to influencing purchasing behavior, trust also affects consumers' overall impression of
a brand. When consumers find AI marketing content reliable, they are usually more willing to
recognize the brand and develop a more positive view of it. Trust is like an "emotional buffer",
which makes consumers more willing to interact and pay more attention to the brand. Once
consumers start to doubt AI content, even if the content is highly professional, it is easy to make
people feel a sense of distance. In this Internet era, building trust is as slow as nurturing an account.
Sometimes, losing trust is like sending a message to the wrong group and vanishing in an instant.
Trust also affects whether consumers will stay on the platform for a long time. Consumers who
trust AI-generated content are more likely to engage actively, interact with platforms and remain
loyal over time [11]. People who are willing to believe in AI content are usually more willing to
continue using the platform and enjoy participating in interactions. Over time, they may gradually
form a long-term habit. To some extent, trust is like an "invisible attraction" that gradually keeps
consumers on the platform, turning what was originally a casual look into a daily habit.
However, AI marketing is not without problems. If enterprises overly rely on AI-generated
content, consumers may gradually feel that the interaction lacks a sense of reality. Over time, people
may even feel that they are not communicating with a brand but rather with a system that "always
replies instantly but never has any emotion". After all, most people hope to be understood rather
than just "precisely identified".
Overall, trust is not a natural outcome that comes with marketing success; rather, it is an
important force driving consumer action in itself. AI can make marketing faster and more precise,
but whether consumers will ultimately make a purchase still depends on whether they are willing to
believe in the content they receive. For enterprises, what truly matters is not only making AI
increasingly intelligent, but also ensuring that consumers do not feel as though they are interacting
with "emotionless push machines." This is consistent with Chaffey and Ellis-Chadwick, who
emphasise that long-term customer relationships depend on both technological innovation and trust-
based engagement strategies [12].

6. Conclusion
In conclusion, this study discusses the issue of consumers' trust in AI-generated content (AIGC) in
the digital marketing environment. Research has found that consumers' trust is influenced by
multiple factors such as authenticity, credibility, transparency and personalization, which jointly
determine whether consumers are willing to accept marketing information generated by AI. At the
same time, issues such as AI identity disclosure, lack of realism, and privacy concerns may also
undermine consumers' trust in the content. This indicates that what determines whether consumers
are willing to believe in AI content is not only the technology itself, but also whether the content can
bring a sense of security and emotional identification. To some extent, when consumers encounter
AI content, it's like ordering takeout online - no matter how exquisite the packaging is, if they
always feel "something is not quite right", they will still hesitate whether to place an order or not.
From a research perspective, what this study aims to illustrate is: why exactly do consumers trust
content generated by AI? Research has found that when people see AI marketing content, what they
care about is not only whether the AI is "powerful", but also whether the content looks real, natural
and emotional. Many consumers actually do not want to be merely "analyzed" and "calculated" by
the system. They hope that brands can communicate with them seriously rather than treating them as
a set of data.
For enterprises, this research indicates a very realistic issue: Relying solely on AI to enhance
efficiency is actually not enough. Enterprises also need to make the content look genuine and
seriously handle the issues of AI transparency and user privacy. If marketing content increasingly
resembles something mass-produced on an assembly line, it will be very difficult for consumers to
develop an emotional identification with it. To put it bluntly, no matter how precise the
advertisement recommendation is, as long as it gives people the feeling of "automatic mass sending
by robots", many people still won't truly believe it. What enterprises should truly do is not merely to
make AI run faster, but to make consumers feel that "this brand seems to be really talking to me."
In the future, it may become increasingly difficult to distinguish between content written by
humans and that generated by AI. As AI becomes increasingly intelligent, consumers may not even
be able to tell which parts are written by humans and which are generated by AI. In this way, there
will be more and more questions about authenticity and transparency. A more effective marketing
approach in the future may not rely entirely on AI, but rather combine the advantages of AI and
