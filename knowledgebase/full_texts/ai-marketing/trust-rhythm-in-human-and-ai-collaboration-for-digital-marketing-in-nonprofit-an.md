This paper builds a conceptual model that explains how artificial intelligence changes the tem -
poral structure of  trust in a brand and defines the threshold beyond which automation erodes
trust instead of  supporting it. The study applies framework synthesis to peer-reviewed litera -
ture published from 2018 onward, supplemented by four earlier works whose definitions and
instruments later studies reuse. Antecedents of  trust are coded into four operational domains,
namely transparency, authenticity, timeliness and human accountability, and converted into six
propositions with explicit falsification conditions. A simulation on synthetic parameters tests the
internal consistency of  the model. Trust under machine mediation depends on three parameters
of  communication rhythm: frequency, amplitude and predictability. Artificial intelligence raises
frequency and predictability because it removes the dependence of  regular communication on
staff  availability. It lowers amplitude because content produced in volume without organiza -
tional substance carries no verifiable information. The simulated trust index reaches an interior
maximum, after which further automation pushes it below its unautomated level. Disclosure
of  machine use is not the decisive variable. The decisive variable is whether the audience can
read human authorship behind the message. The paper contributes a matrix for allocating mar-
keting tasks between people and machines, indicators for monitoring communication rhythm,
and a disclosure rule that reports the configuration of  human control rather than the bare fact
of  technology use. The model is theoretical, its evidence base is concentrated in commercial
settings, and the nonprofit case is therefore a boundary condition awaiting longitudinal and
experimental testing.

INTRODUCTION
Artificial intelligence has moved from an experimental
tool to a standard layer of  marketing infrastructure, as
chatbots, predictive analytics, sentiment analysis and
programmatic advertising now shape how organizations
target and personalize messages (Ozikor et al. , 2026).
Systems write advertising copy, segment audiences, select
the moment of  contact and answer the first questions of
a customer. The mechanisms that produce trust did not
change at the same speed. Trust still forms through signals
that an audience reads as coming from a responsible
party, and its definition as acceptance of  vulnerability on
the basis of  positive expectations about future conduct
holds regardless of  who produced the message (Mayer
et al. , 1995). Review-based work in digital marketing
positions such systems as amplifiers of  human effort
with identifiable constraints rather than as substitutes
for it (Demidova, 2025a). A review of  empirical research
reports that trust in artificial intelligence depends on
the form in which a system appears to a person and on
the transparency and reliability it displays rather than
on capability alone (Glikson & Woolley, 2020). This
paper begins with the gap between adoption speed and
trust formation. A second mismatch compounds it.
Most available studies measure the reaction of  a person
to a single message, while marketing practice builds
relationships out of  long sequences of  messages. A result
obtained on one advertisement says little about trust
accumulated over a year.
Two groups of  findings pull organizations in opposite
directions. Disclosure of  machine authorship worsens
the evaluation of  an advertisement through perceived
credibility, and the evidence comes from prosocial
advertising, the genre closest to nonprofit communication
(Baek et al., 2024). Disclosure of  chatbot status before a
purchase reduces conversion, although an undisclosed
chatbot performs at the level of  a competent employee
(Luo et al., 2019). The loss attaches to the label and not to
the quality of  the output. Silence is not a safe alternative.
The present model treats undisclosed use as a separate
failure once a third party reveals it, since the damage then
extends from the message to the channel through which
every other claim reaches the audience; this claim is stated
below as a testable proposition. Organizations therefore
lose trust in two ways at once, by automating more than
the audience accepts and by concealing what has already
been automated. The technology is not the source of
the problem. The source is the configuration in which it
is placed, that is, the distribution of  authorship and the
visibility of  that distribution.
The practical problem follows from this configuration.
Organizations raise the volume and speed of  marketing
communication with artificial intelligence and
simultaneously lose the part of  the signal that carries
trust, because the elements an audience reads as human
are automated first. The loss is invisible in operational

The purpose of  this paper is to construct a conceptual
model that explains how artificial intelligence can support
the building of  trust between an organization and its
audience instead of  destroying it, and to specify the
threshold beyond which support turns into destruction.
Six objectives serve that purpose: to separate trust
in a brand from trust in a machine and from adjacent
constructs; to describe the continuum of  autonomy and
locate on it the threshold of  readable human authorship;
to reduce the documented antecedents of  trust to four
domains with an admissible level of  autonomy attached
to each; to show how artificial intelligence moves the three
parameters of  the trust rhythm in different directions; to
formulate a rule for allocating marketing tasks between
people and machines; and to specify monitoring indicators
and testable propositions. Four research questions follow.
RQ1 asks how artificial intelligence should be integrated
so that it supports rather than destroys trust. RQ2 asks
which elements of  communication must remain under
human control. RQ3 asks how the process of  building
trust changes after such systems enter the marketing
function. RQ4 asks why nonprofit organizations require
particular caution, given that trust, authenticity and
human contact form their resource base rather than an
addition to it (Cheng & Wang, 2025).
The paper contributes on three levels. Theoretically, it
develops the trust rhythm construct, which treats trust
as the product of  periodic reproduction of  signals, for
human and machine collaboration and introduces signal
authorship as a parameter. Integratively, it links each
domain of  trust to an admissible level of  autonomy, which
converts a general recommendation about oversight into
a specific constraint. Practically, it offers a task allocation
matrix and a set of  monitoring indicators. The reference
base combines two groups of  sources that rarely appear
together. Experimental work in marketing and decision
psychology explains the reaction of  a person to machine
authorship (Dietvorst et al. , 2015; Puntoni et al. , 2021).
Applied methodological work on lead generation,
retention and attribution explains how regularity of
contact is produced in practice (Demydova, 2025a).
The first group is strong on mechanism and weak on
operations; the second is strong on operations and rarely
asks what happens to trust. The value of  combining them
lies in that asymmetry. The logic of  calibrated reliance
serves as a reference point throughout, since excessive
trust and insufficient trust both carry a cost (Lee &
See, 2004). The paper proceeds from a review of  the
literature to the method and the simulation specification,
then presents and discusses the model, and closes with
conclusions.
LITERATURE REVIEW
Trust in a Brand and Trust in a Machine
Trust is defined in the organizational literature as
willingness to accept vulnerability on the basis of  positive
expectations about future conduct (Mayer et al. , 1995).
Trust is directed at the future and is extended under
incomplete information, and neither property disappears
when a machine produces part of  the communication.
The transfer of  this definition to human and machine
interaction proceeded along two lines. Research on
automation treats reliance as a calibration problem, since
excessive and insufficient trust both produce losses (Lee
& See, 2004). A review of  empirical research organizes
the evidence by the form of  representation of  a system
and by its capabilities (Glikson & Woolley, 2020). Both
lines describe trust in a system.
Marketing poses a different question. An audience does
not evaluate the reliability of  a recommendation engine.
It evaluates the organization that placed the engine into
the communication. Research on consumer experience
with artificial intelligence separates that experience into
distinct types, none of  which reduces to a relationship
with a brand (Puntoni et al., 2021). The construct used
here is therefore trust in the organization under machine
mediation, while trust in the system stays outside the
dependent variable. The demarcation matters because a
large share of  publications titled as studies of  trust in
artificial intelligence in fact measures acceptance of  a tool
by its user. Acceptance by a marketer and confidence of
an audience are different variables.
Consumer Response to Machine Authorship
Decision research contains two results that appear to
conflict. People reject an algorithm after observing it err,
even when it outperforms a human forecaster (Dietvorst
et al., 2015). People also prefer algorithmic advice in tasks
of  estimation and prediction (Logg et al. , 2019). The
type of  task, not the quality of  the studies, resolves the
conflict, as the review of  task boundaries below shows.
Marketing evidence is more consistent, and the two key
disclosure studies were introduced above (Baek et al. ,
2024; Luo et al., 2019). The state of  this stream imposes a
constraint. Almost every available study is an experiment
with a single-exposure design and an immediate
measurement. Such a design captures the reaction of  a
person to a novel signal and says nothing about an audience
receiving disclosed machine-assisted communication for
a year. Survey evidence from online advertising points the
same way: advertising drove purchase intention directly
while trust did not moderate the relationship (Aktar &
Gacho Segumpan, 2025), which suggests that point
measures of  intention capture the reaction to a message
rather than the accumulated relationship. The absence
of  longitudinal designs is the principal weakness of  the

Human Control as a Moderator
The most useful correction to the disclosure literature
comes from work on organizational frontlines. Two
experiments show that collaboration between a person
and a machine reduces negative consumer responses,
but only when the configuration signals human control.
Machine authorship without such control produces
worse responses where perceived morality of  the use
is low, while messages produced under human control
are evaluated as messages of  human origin (Haupt et
al., 2025). Rejection of  an algorithm also weakens once
people may modify its output, even slightly, which shows
that control need not be extensive to work (Dietvorst et
al., 2018). Both results support the treatment of  human
authorship as a threshold rather than as a binary attribute.
Work on public relations and advertising practice
reaches a compatible conclusion, since over-reliance on
automated systems is reported to erode authenticity and
creativity (Ozikor et al., 2026).
Task Boundaries and the Limit of  Personalization
Aversion depends on the nature of  the task. Resistance
is stronger in subjective tasks than in tasks resting on
measurable facts (Castelo et al., 2019). Resistance grows
sharply when a task touches the identity of  a consumer,
since automation there is read as a denial of  the personal
character of  the choice (Leung et al., 2018). It also grows
where the recipient believes that individual circumstances
will be ignored (Longoni et al., 2019). Personalization has
a similar limit. Personalized advertising works only where
trust in the collector of  data is present and turns into
a perception of  surveillance where it is absent (Aguirre
et al., 2015). The same technology therefore strengthens
a relationship in one configuration and damages it
in another. These four studies form the basis of  the
allocation matrix presented in the Results and Discussion.
Combined, they stop being a list of  effects and start
behaving like a rule.
The Operational Layer and the Nonprofit Case
Trust is produced by regular communication, and regular
communication is produced by an operational system.
Sequential deployment of  chatbots, automated funnels
and application collection systems reports gains at the
entry stage of  a funnel and in mid-funnel progression
(Demidova, 2025b). The path from a content strategy
to a completed application is treated as a designed
sequence rather than as a set of  independent publications
(Demidova, 2026). Staged warming outperforms single-
exposure contact on advertising platforms, which supports
the claim that distributed regular communication beats
concentrated intensity (Demydova, 2025a). Regularity
usually fails for resource reasons. Predictive scoring
architectures for capacity-constrained allocation describe
this failure directly (Avagimyan, 2026d), and scoring
models for lead qualification manage it at the level of  a
single contact (Andreasyan, 2026a). Automation therefore
has a legitimate and narrow function, namely removal of
the dependence of  the communication calendar on staff
availability.
Measurement forms the second block. Integration
of  multi-touch attribution with incrementality testing
establishes when a budget decision can be called evidence-
based (Andreasyan, 2026d). An attribution protocol
for advisory services adapts the same logic to delayed
results (Avagimyan, 2026b), and the trace map method
converts attribution into client reporting standards
(Balaian, 2026a). Diagnostic frameworks linked to key
performance indicators supply the structure into which
such reporting fits (Andreasyan, 2026b). These sources
serve one purpose here: construction of  indicators for
monitoring the rhythm of  communication.
Retention research supplies the third block and a caution.
Lifecycle messaging and churn prevention describe the
vocabulary of  frequency (Andreasyan, 2026e), and a
key performance indicator system built around trust,
outcomes and renewal shows how retention is measured
in advisory relationships (Avagimyan, 2026e). Gamified
engagement raises client lifetime value through extrinsic
reward (Demydova, 2025b), yet such mechanics operate
on loyalty and satisfaction rather than on expectation
of  future conformity to a mission. Repeat behavior
can therefore grow while trust stands still. When repeat
purchase is the only proxy, the indicator profile misleads.
Channel architecture and positioning form the fourth
block. Omnichannel communication with evidence-
based segmentation describes the environment in which
a rhythm has to be maintained (Avagimyan, 2026c).
Trust signals and funnel design are treated as engineered
elements of  conversion (Balaian, 2026d). Chain models
linking a value proposition to a revenue model explain
why positioning acquires force only through consistent
reproduction (Andreasyan, 2026c; Balaian, 2026c).
Diagnostic toolkits for growth bottlenecks name the
most common managerial error, namely investment in
the easiest domain rather than in the constraining one
(Avagimyan, 2026a; Balaian, 2026b).
The nonprofit case sharpens every element above.
Research on chatbots in nonprofit organizations
examines the antecedents and outcomes of  chatbot trust
together with social media engagement (Cheng & Wang,
2025). The reading adopted in this paper goes one step
further, since the technology is treated here as entering
an environment in which trust is the resource base
rather than an addition to it. Three structural features
distinguish that environment. The resource base rests
on voluntary contribution, the result is poorly verifiable
by the contributor, and the payer and the beneficiary are
different parties.
The review thus locates the gap precisely. Experimental
research explains the direction of  the disclosure effect
and its moderator, and applied research explains how
automation sustains frequency. The literature lacks

MATERIALS AND METHODS
Research Design
The study is conceptual, and the choice follows from the
state of  the field. Controlled experiments have already
estimated the effect of  disclosure, so an additional
estimate would add a coefficient to a dense set. What
the field lacks is an architecture specifying how those
coefficients relate over time. The deficit is theoretical
and is addressed by theoretical means.
The model was built through framework synthesis in
four steps. An initial scaffold was derived from the trust
rhythm construct, which treats trust as the product of
periodic reproduction of  signals rather than as a sum
of  attributes. Evidence from the reviewed corpus was
coded against that scaffold. Codes that did not fit were
retained rather than discarded, and the scaffold was
revised to absorb them; this is how signal authorship
entered the model. The revised structure was converted
into propositions stated so that each can be contradicted
by observation. The stepwise logic follows the discipline
of  algorithmic frameworks for marketing audit and
prioritization, where every step must be reproducible
by another analyst (Balayan, 2026). Framework
synthesis was preferred to meta analysis because the
reviewed studies measure trust through incompatible
instruments, and review based work reports the same
heterogeneity across methods and settings (Demidova,
2025a). A fifth step followed the formulation of  the
propositions. The relationships stated in the model were
expressed as functions and evaluated numerically on
synthetic parameters to test whether the propositions
can hold simultaneously. The simulation plays the role
that a formal proof  plays in an analytical paper: it
demonstrates that the stated relationships are mutually
consistent and yield a prediction that observation can
contradict. The unit of  analysis is the organization as a
collective author of  communication.
Source Selection and Coding Protocol
Records were retrieved from journals indexed in Scopus
and Web of  Science through database queries and
citation tracking. The window opens in 2018, since the
experimental study of  consumer response to machine
authorship formed a distinct stream from that year
onward. Four earlier works were admitted because later
studies reuse their definitions or instruments without
revision: the integrative model of  organizational trust
(Mayer et al. , 1995), the calibration logic of  reliance
on automation (Lee & See, 2004), the personalization
paradox (Aguirre et al. , 2015) and the baseline
demonstration of  algorithm aversion (Dietvorst
et al. , 2015). Peer reviewed articles and academic
book chapters in English were retained. Conference
abstracts, unreviewed preprints, non indexed outlets
and descriptive case reports without a theoretical frame
were excluded.
Six search strands structured the retrieval: definitions of
trust and their transfer to machine mediated interaction;
consumer response to disclosure; human control and
shared authorship; task dependence of  aversion;
operational methods of  lead generation, retention
and attribution; and adoption of  artificial intelligence
in nonprofit communication. Two strands returned
thin results. Empirical work on artificial intelligence in
nonprofit marketing remains scarce, and longitudinal
measurement of  brand trust under automation is
effectively absent. Both gaps are carried into the
discussion rather than filled by inference.
Coding proceeded in two passes. The first extracted
every reported antecedent of  trust with the direction
of  the effect, the design and the sample. The second
reduced the antecedents to domains under a single rule:
two antecedents belong to the same domain when they
reach the audience through the same class of  observable
signal. The reduction produced four domains, and no
antecedent remained unassigned. Seven comparison
vectors were then applied to each item, following the
criteria that structure Table 3, and diagnostic frameworks
that link criteria to key performance indicators informed
their wording (Andreasyan, 2026b). One restriction
is deliberate. Industry reports on adoption were read
during preparation but were not admitted into the
coded corpus, because their sampling procedures
are rarely documented. The coded corpus contains
thirty five records. Fifteen belong to the theoretical
and experimental stream: ten experiments, one survey
based study, two reviews and two conceptual works
that supply definitions. Twenty belong to the applied
methodological stream and describe lead generation,
retention, attribution and diagnostic practice. Thirty
one records fall inside the publication window, and four
earlier works were admitted under the rule stated above.
The retrieval was purposive rather than exhaustive, so
the corpus supports a conceptual synthesis and does
not claim the coverage of  a systematic review.

published without a human review point, so that a
equals zero under full human authorship and one
under full autonomy. Three functions translate a into
the parameters of  the rhythm. Frequency F, measured
in communication acts per month, rises linearly from
four to twelve acts, because automation removes
the dependence of  the calendar on staff  availability
(Equation 1). Amplitude A, the informational weight
of  a single act, falls quadratically: slowly at first and
then rapidly, because the decline accelerates once
routine content displaces content with organizational

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Demarcation of  the Construct and the Continuum
of  Autonomy
Four objects of  trust operate simultaneously in machine
mediated marketing, and their separation is the first result
of  this study. Trust in the brand is the expectation that
the organization will act in future accordance with its
declared mission. Trust in the system is the assessment
of  accuracy by the person who uses a tool. Trust of
the marketer in the tool governs the internal decision to
delegate. Perceived acceptability of  machine use is the
judgment of  an audience about whether the technology
belongs in this communication, and it moderates every
other relationship. Table 1 sets out the holder of  each
assessment, its evidence base and its status in the model.
The separation matters because applied literature merges
these objects and then draws conclusions about one from
evidence about another. The second result concerns the
form of  the automation variable. Autonomy is not binary,
and four states can be distinguished. In the assisted state a
person writes and the system suggests. In the augmented
state the system drafts and a person revises. In the
delegated state the system produces and publishes within
rules set by a person. In the autonomous state no review
point remains. The response of  an audience does not fall
evenly across these states. It falls sharply where human
authorship stops being readable, which lies between the
delegated and the autonomous state, as the dependence
of  consumer response on signalled control indicates
(Haupt et al., 2025). A parallel result obtained on decision
makers rather than on audiences points in the same
direction, since acceptance of  an algorithm returns once
a person may modify its output (Dietvorst et al., 2018).
A consequence follows for the disclosure debate.
Whether to disclose is secondary. What the disclosure
reports is primary. A statement that a machine generated
the content gives the audience the label that damages
evaluation. A statement describing who set the rules and
who checked the result gives the audience the position of
the person in the process, which the evidence identifies
as decisive.

Rhythm Parameters under Automation
The central result concerns the direction in which
artificial intelligence moves each parameter. Frequency
rises, because automated funnels and application
collection systems remove the dependence of  the
calendar on staff  availability (Demidova, 2025b),
and staged sequences outperform single exposures
precisely because they are sustained (Demydova,

2025a). Predictability rises for the same reason, since a
scheduled process misses fewer intervals than a manual
one (Avagimyan, 2026d). Amplitude falls. Amplitude is
the informational weight of  a single act, and content
produced in volume without organizational substance
carries no verifiable information. Analyses of  artificial
intelligence in public relations and advertising warn in
similar terms that over-reliance on automated systems
erodes authenticity and creativity (Ozikor et al., 2026). A
message that reports nothing new occupies an interval
without filling it. The organization keeps the calendar
and loses the signal. At that point a maintained interval

Destruction of  trust follows an order that can be stated
explicitly. Undisclosed use revealed by a third party ranks
first. Personalization crossing into perceived surveillance
ranks second, and its mechanism is documented, since
personalization works only where trust in the collector
of  data already exists (Aguirre et al. , 2015). Targeting
errors that place content in a sensitive context rank
third, automated response to a crisis ranks fourth, and
loss of  amplitude under a preserved calendar ranks fifth
as the slowest and least visible. The first two triggers
damage the channel rather than the message, which is
why the harm is disproportionate to the scale of  the
incident. Calibration applies in full here, since excessive
delegation costs as much as insufficient use (Lee & See,
2004).
Recovery proceeds in five steps whose order is not
interchangeable. The organization first acknowledges
the incident before the facts are fully established,
because attribution consolidates faster than verification.
It then discloses the configuration of  use, since the
audience now assesses the process rather than the
output. Third, it narrows or suspends the automated
loop in the affected domain. Fourth, it places the
content that continues to be published under human
verification. Finally, it restores the ordinary cadence.
This last step is the one most often abandoned once
acute pressure subsides.
Task Allocation, Indicators and Propositions
Allocation follows from two properties of  a
communication act, the objectivity of  the underlying
task and its relational weight, both established in the
review of  task boundaries. Figure 2 presents them as
the axes of  a matrix. The horizontal axis runs from
subjective to objective tasks, and a strip beneath it maps
the four states of  autonomy onto that axis, from assisted
through augmented and delegated to autonomous. The
vertical axis runs from low to high relational weight. A
dashed line divides the matrix horizontally and marks
the threshold of  readable human authorship. The line
shows that the threshold can be crossed in two ways:
by raising the level of  autonomy, or by delegating an
act of  higher relational weight at an unchanged level
of  autonomy. The four quadrants define the modes of
allocation, namely full delegation, delegation with human
revision, human authorship with machine support, and
exclusively human authorship. Segmentation, scheduling
and routine reporting fall under full delegation, while
fundraising appeals, crisis statements and apologies after
a failure fall under exclusively human authorship. The
matrix is the most transferable element of  the paper,
because it converts a general principle into a decision
a team can make about a specific task on a specific day
(Fig. 2).

Theoretical Position and Practical Implications
Three accounts compete for the explanation of
consumer response to machine authorship. Intention
based models explain the reaction of  a consumer to
an advertising stimulus, and evidence from online
advertising shows that trust may even fail to moderate
that reaction (Aktar & Gacho Segumpan, 2025).
Research on aversion and appreciation explains the
reaction of  a person to a machine recommendation and
resolves its internal contradiction through the type of
task (Dietvorst et al., 2015; Logg et al., 2019). Research
on disclosure explains the reaction of  an audience to
a label (Haupt et al. , 2025). All three explain a single
act. None explains the trajectory that accumulates
across a year, and the trajectory is what a marketing
department actually manages. The contribution of  the
present model lies in that shift of  unit. The position is
compatible with the experiential account of  consumer
interaction with such systems (Puntoni et al., 2021) and
with chain models holding that positioning acquires
force only through consistent reproduction (Balaian,
2026c). One objection deserves an answer. Amplitude
may appear unmeasurable, yet it is observable indirectly
through the share of  communication acts carrying
verifiable external evidence. Difficulty of  measurement
is not an argument against the existence of  a variable.
Four practical implications follow. Organizations with
weak trust usually increase the volume of  what they
publish. The model instead recommends stabilizing the
interval first and placing automation where regularity
breaks down for resource reasons, a task for which
lifecycle messaging practice supplies the vocabulary
(Andreasyan, 2026e). Disclosure should report the
configuration of  human control rather than the
bare fact of  machine use, which is a design task of
the same class as the design of  trust signals inside a
funnel (Balaian, 2026d). Relational acts remain with
people even where this reduces throughput, and an
omnichannel architecture makes that feasible by letting
the routine layer run automatically (Avagimyan, 2026c).
Engagement metrics and trust indicators are reported
separately, and reporting standards that make attribution
logic explicit provide the format for that separation
(Avagimyan, 2026b; Balaian, 2026a).
Boundary Conditions in Nonprofit and Service
Organizations
The model applies to commercial and nonprofit
organizations alike, but the threshold within it
moves. The three structural features of  the nonprofit
environment identified in the literature review explain
the movement. A contributor who cannot verify the
result relies on the credibility of  the account rather
than on a product received. The separation of  payer
and beneficiary weakens every analogy borrowed from
commercial personalization, since the recipient of  the
message is not the source of  revenue. The practical
consequence is stated in P6. A configuration that is safe
in a commercial funnel becomes risky in a fundraising
appeal, and the same message that reads as convenient
automation from a retailer reads as indifference from
a charity. The evidence supporting this claim is thin
(Cheng & Wang, 2025), which is why it appears as a
boundary condition and a proposition rather than as a
result.
Professional service organizations occupy an
intermediate position between the two poles and
fall under the same logic. The result of  an advisory
engagement is delayed and partly unobservable, so a
client extends credit against future conduct in a way that
resembles the position of  a donor. Key performance
indicator systems built around trust, outcomes and
renewal describe exactly this dependence (Avagimyan,
2026e), and trust signals inside a consulting funnel
are engineered rather than incidental (Balaian, 2026d).
The threshold of  admissible automation in such
organizations sits below the commercial retail level and
above the nonprofit level. All three sectors therefore lie
on a single scale rather than in separate categories.
Limitations and Directions for Further Research
The model is untested. Every proposition in Table 4
states a relationship that the reviewed literature makes
plausible, and none has been estimated on data collected
for this purpose. The simulation illustrates internal
consistency and is not evidence, since the authors
chose its parameters rather than estimating them from
observation. The limitations of  the method apply to
the results in full, and the thin evidence on nonprofit
communication makes the boundary conditions for that
sector the least secure part of  the paper.
Four directions follow. Panel measurement of
publication timing matched to periodic assessment of
brand trust would test P1 and P4 and requires only
archival collection of  dates paired with an existing
survey series. An experiment varying the configuration
of  disclosure while holding the fact of  machine use
constant would test P2, and testing protocols used

CONCLUSION
This paper reduced the documented antecedents of
trust in machine mediated marketing to four operational
domains, namely transparency, authenticity, timeliness and
human accountability, and attached to their enactment
three parameters of  rhythm, namely frequency, amplitude
and predictability. Trust is sustained by the periodic
reproduction of  these domains rather than by their
presence, and artificial intelligence acts on the parameters
in opposite directions at the same time.
Three further results follow from that architecture.
Automation raises frequency and predictability because
it removes the dependence of  the communication
calendar on staff  availability, and it lowers amplitude
because volume without organizational substance carries
no verifiable information. The decisive variable in the
disclosure debate is not the label attached to a message
but the readability of  human authorship behind it, which
locates the threshold between delegated and autonomous
operation. Destruction of  trust proceeds hierarchically,
and the triggers that damage the evidentiary channel
cause harm disproportionate to the scale of  the incident.
The study contributes a matrix that allocates marketing
tasks by the objectivity of  the task and the relational
weight of  the act, a set of  indicators for continuous
monitoring of  rhythm, and six propositions stated with
explicit falsification routes. Its principal limitation is
that the framework is theoretical and rests on evidence
concentrated in commercial settings, so its value depends
on longitudinal and experimental testing, with comparative
work across sectors as the most urgent recommendation.
The practical claim it supports is narrower than the
usual prescription and more demanding. Trust is not
accumulated through the volume of  communication and
is not defended through speed. It is held only while a
person remains visible inside a rhythm that the audience
can predict.
