Abstract
The aim of this paper is to review related studies on impact of machine learning predictive
models towards crop yield in rural areas, the also looked at theories/algorithms on crop yield
prediction, conceptual reviews on crop yield prediction using machine learning algorithms and
also to investigate the disease that affects plants which hi nders plants from producing affective

some theories such as (Statistical Learning Theory, Ensemble Learning Theory, Kernel Methods
& Representer Theorem, Deep Learning Theories (Universal Approximation, Backpropagation,

region. Thirdly, the review also looked at the (50) related studies on crop yield prediction in
rural areas using machine learning algorithms and associated disease on plants that affects crop
yields whereby providing an enhanced knowledge on the study for better understanding and

government to adopt artificial intelligence in their agricultural activities so that there will be
improvement in food production as crop yield will be also improved if there is provision of

Introduction
Machine Learning is a sub -domain of artificial intelligence that gives a computer the possibility
to learn from data without having to be initially programmed. Machine Learning has found more
utility with the arrival of big data technology. Big data can be simply defined as a large volume
of data that comes from different sources with a high velocity. Fundamentally, machine learning
involves building mathematical models to help understand data  (VanderPlas, 2016) . Machine
learning (Mihoub et al., 2020) is used to solve three main types of problems, namely supervised
learning problems, unsupervised learning  problems, and reinforcement problems.
Mathematically, machine learning takes as input a tuple of where is a so -called independent
feature and represents the dependent variable or the target. We are in a supervised learning
problem when the variable is known before the training. Supervised learning is divided into two
common problems, which are regression and classification problems. The housing price
prediction problem (Truong et al., 2020)  is known as one of the regression problems. One talks
about unsupervised learning when the variable is unknown before the training. Their algorithms
are widely used in the case of clustering problems.
Machine learning is fundamentally set apart from artificial intelligence, as it has the capability to
evolve. Using various programming techniques, machine learning algorithms are able to process
large amounts of data and extract useful information. In this way, they can improve upon their
previous iterations by learning from the data they are provided. Any type of AI is usually
dependent on the quality of its dataset for good results, as the field makes use of statistical
methods heavily. Machine learning is no exception, and a good flow of organized, varied data is
required for a robust ML solution. In today’s online-first world, companies have access to a large
amount of data about their customers, usually in the millions. This data, which is both large in
the number of data points and the number of fields, is known as big data due to the sheer amount
of information it holds (Abu Al-Haija, M. Krichen and Abu Elhaija, 2022).
Crop yield prediction retains a lot of attention for researchers around the world. You et al. (2017)
presented a deep learning framework for crop yield prediction using remote sensing data. That
approach used a Convolutional Neural Network  (CNN) with the Gaussian process component
and dimensional reduction technique to forecast crop yield of mostly developing countries
throughout the year. The technique was applied to a dataset of soybeans obtained by combining a
sensing dataset, soil dataset, and climate dataset from the US. Their approach shows that the
gaussian approach was used to improve the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of the model from
6,27 to 5,83 on average with the Long Short-Term Memory(LSTM) model and from 5,77 to 5,57
with the CNN model.  Machine learning (ML) predictive models have revolutionized agriculture
by enabling accurate crop yield forecasts, which directly impact rural areas where farming is the
primary livelihood. These models analyze factors like weather, soil, and historical data to
optimize resource use, reduce risks from climate variability, and enhance food security in
underserved regions. By providing early insights, they empower rural farmers to make informed
decisions, potentially increasing productivity and economic stability. Many innovations have
been made in the agriculture industry to increase crop productivity. This will help farmers
increase their operational efficiency, lower costs, produce high -quality food, and guarantee food
safety and hygiene

Conceptual Framework of Ml in Crop Yield Prediction
Machine learning (ML) predictive models have emerged as powerful tools in modern agriculture,
particularly for enhancing crop yield prediction. In rural areas often characterized by smallholder
farming, limited resources, variable climate conditions, and reliance on rain -fed agriculture these
models offer conceptual advancements that go beyond traditional statistical or mechanistic
approaches.
At its core, ML -based crop yield prediction operates on a data -driven paradigm that learns
complex, non-linear relationships from multi -source inputs to forecast outputs (yield per hectare
or total production). Unlike traditional crop simulation models (e.g., DSSAT or APSIM), which
rely on biophysical equations, ML models treat yield as a function of features such as:
1. Climatic variables (temperature, precipitation, solar radiation, humidity)
2. Soil properties (pH, nutrient levels, organic matter, texture)
3. Agronomic practices (planting date, fertilizer application, irrigation)
4. Remote sensing indicators (NDVI, EVI from satellites; vegetation indices)
5. Socio-economic factors (in some extended models: access to inputs, market prices)

The conceptual shift introduced by Machine Learning (ML) includes:
1. Pattern recognition over mechanistic simulation:  Machine Learning (ML) excels at
capturing interactions (e.g., temperature × rainfall effects) that are hard to model explicitly.
2. Scalability from field to regional levels:  Machine Learning (ML) Models can generalize
across heterogeneous rural landscapes using ensemble or deep learning techniques.
3. Integration with emerging data sources:  Machine Learning (ML) Satellite imagery, IoT
sensors, and weather APIs enable near real -time predictions, bridging the gap between data
scarcity and actionable insights in remote rural settings.
4. Supervised regression models (Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost):  This areas
provides robustness to noisy data and interpretable via feature importance.
5. Deep learning architectures (CNN for image -based inputs, LSTM/RNN for time -series
weather data): These areas handle high-dimensional spatiotemporal data.
6. Hybrid approaches:  Combine Machine Learning (ML) with crop models or ensemble
methods for improved accuracy and explain-ability.

Positive Impacts on Rural Areas
Machine Learning (ML) predictive models conceptually empower rural communities in several
interconnected ways:
1. Risk Reduction and Climate Resilience Rural farmers, especially in rain-fed systems
prevalent in sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and parts of Latin America, face high uncertainty
from erratic weather. ML models forecast yields under various climate scenarios, enabling
proactive adaptation (e.g., crop switching, adjusted planting windows, or insurance claims).
This supports resilience against droughts, floods, or heatwaves exacerbated by climate change.
2. Resource Optimization and Precision Agriculture Predictions guide efficient use of scarce
inputs like fertilizers, water, and seeds critical in resource-poor rural contexts. Conceptually, this
shifts farming from uniform blanket applications to variable-rate management, reducing costs
and environmental footprint while boosting yields.
3. Informed Decision-Making and Empowerment Yield forecasts inform crop choice, timing,

Theoretical Reviews
Machine learning (ML) predictive models for crop yield prediction, particularly in rural areas,
rest on several foundational theories and principles from statistical learning, computer science,
and domain-specific agronomic adaptations. While applied ML in agriculture is largely empirical
and data-driven, underlying theories provide the justification for why these models work, their
generalization capabilities, and how they handle the complexities of rural contexts (e.g., data
scarcity, non -stationarity due to climate variability, heterogeneous smallholder farms, and
limited labeled data).

1. Statistical Learning Theory (VC Dimension, Generalization Bounds)
The core theoretical foundation for most supervised ML models used in crop yield prediction is
Statistical Learning Theory  (developed by Vapnik and Chervonenkis). It explains how models
learn from finite data and generalize to unseen examples without overfitting. This theorem
provides the following towards improved model development, accuracy and proper evaluation of
crop predictive models.
1. Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM): Models minimize training error (e.g., MSE for
regression tasks like yield prediction).
2. VC Dimension & Capacity Control: Measures model complexity; low-capacity models
(e.g., linear regression) generalize better with limited rural data, while high -capacity models
(deep networks) risk overfitting unless regularized.
3. Generalization Bounds: PAC (Probably Approximately Correct) learning guarantees that
with enough data, the model performs well on new rural fields/seasons.
4. Relevance to Rural Areas: Rural datasets are often small, noisy, and imbalanced (e.g., few
extreme drought years). Theories justify techniques like cross -validation, regularization (L1/L2),
and ensemble methods to achieve robust generalization despite data limitations.
Common models grounded here: Support Vector Regression (SVR), which explicitly uses
margin maximization (from SVM theory) to handle non -linear rural yield patterns with fewer
parameters.

2. Ensemble Learning Theory (Wisdom of Crowds, Bias-Variance Tradeoff)
Ensemble methods dominate crop yield literature (e.g., Random Forest, Gradient
Boosting/XGBoost) due to strong theoretical underpinnings.
1. Bias-Variance Decomposition: Single weak learners (e.g., shallow trees) have high bias or
variance; ensembles reduce variance (bagging in RF) or bias sequentially (boosting).
2. Margin Theory & Error Bounds: AdaBoost and related methods maximize margins,
improving robustness.
3. Rural Impact: Rural environments exhibit high spatial/temporal variability (soil
heterogeneity, microclimates). Ensembles handle this by averaging diverse "opinions" from base

3. Kernel Methods & Representer Theorem
Support Vector Machines/Regression rely on the kernel trick and Representer Theorem.
1. Theory allows implicit mapping to high-dimensional feature spaces (e.g., RBF kernel
captures non-linear weather-soil-yield relationships) while computing only in original space.
2. Enables effective modeling of complex rural interactions with moderate data.

4. Deep Learning Theories (Universal Approximation, Backpropagation, Generalization in
Overparameterized Regimes)
For CNNs (satellite imagery), LSTMs/RNNs (time -series weather), and Transformers
(multimodal data):
1. Universal Approximation Theorem: Neural networks can approximate any continuous
function given enough neurons/layers to justifies modeling highly non-linear crop responses.
2. Implicit Bias in Overparameterized Models: Modern theory (e.g., (NTK) Neural Tangent
Kernel) explains why large networks generalize well despite fitting noise.
3. Information Bottleneck & Representation Learning: Deep layers learn hierarchical
features (e.g., CNNs extract vegetation patterns from NDVI images relevant to rural yield).
4. Rural Challenges & Adaptations: Overparameterization helps with sparse rural data, but
requires techniques like transfer learning (pre -trained on large satellite datasets) or data
augmentation to avoid poor generalization in data-poor villages.
  Hybrid models (crop simulation + DL) leverage process -based theory while using DL for
residual correction.

5. Transfer Learning & Domain Adaptation Theories
Rural areas often lack local high -quality and data transfer learning applies models trained on
data-rich regions (e.g., US Corn Belt) to data-scarce rural Africa/Asia.
1. Theoretical basis: Distribution shift minimization, covariate shift adaptation.
2. Federated Learning (emerging) preserves privacy for decentralized rural data collection.

6. Causal Inference & Counterfactual Theories (Emerging)
Traditional ML is associational with recent work incorporating causal graphs or do -calculus to
answer "what if" questions (e.g., impact of fertilizer under climate change). Relevant for rural
policy: distinguishing correlation (rainfall-yield) from causation
