1. Introduction
The organic food sector has grown substantially in the
recent years. Willer et al. (2023) reported that global
organic agricultural land surpassed 76 million hectares across
187 countries, ranking India as a leading nation according
to certified organic production area. In India, organic
agriculture is predominantly led by smallholder producers
who operate under the Participatory Guarantee System
(PGS-India). This is an affordable, peer-verified community
certification model developed for resource-constrained farm
settings. India’sorganicexportsreachedaboutUSD1.1billion
in2022–23(AgriculturalandProcessedFoodProductsExport
Development Authority, APEDA, 2023). South Tamil Nadu,
particularly districts of Madurai, Thoothukudi, Tirunelveli,
Dindigul, and Virudhunagar, has a growing community
of PGS-certified organic farmers producing cotton, spices,
millets,andhorticulturalcrops.
Despite a growth on the production-side, organic supply
chainsinSouthTamilNaduremainorganizationallyinefficient.
Farmers continue to receive only a limited share of the price
premium due to intermediary-dominated market networks.
Also, insufficient cold-chain infrastructure and paper-based
PGS documentation restrict efficient market access and
integrationwithdigitaltradingplatforms.
Digitaltransformationreferstotheintegrationofadvanced
digital technologies into organization processes to enhance
value creation, delivery, and capture (Hanelt et al., 2021;
Vial, 2019). It has emerged as a promising pathway world
wide to address supply chain inefficiencies across agri-food
platforms. Verhoef et al. (2021) characterized digital
transformation as an organizational approach, a technological
method, and an institutional challenge. This characterization
resonatesstronglyinthePGS-Indiacontext,whereinstitutional
constraints are as important as the technological ones.
AI-powered applications including machine learning for crop
advisory, Internet of Things (IoT)-enabled environmental
monitoring, and blockchain traceability have demonstrated
significantbenefitsacrossvariousagriculturalsystems(Klerkx
et al., 2019; Wolfert et al., 2017). However, this digital
transformation to organic smallholder supply chains in South
Tamil Nadu has not been examined in any prior systematic
review.

This review addresses this gap through three objectives:
(1) synthesizing global evidence on digital transformation
and AI-based applications relevant to organic supply chain
management; (2) identifying challenges and opportunities

2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Researchdesign
A systematic literature review design was adopted, following
the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews
and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Moher et al.,
2009). This method was selected for its transparency,
reproducibility, and suitability in synthesizing evidence on
digital transformation, organic agriculture, AI, and supply
chainmanagementwithinaunifiedanalyticalframework.
2.2. Searchstrategyanddatabases
Systematic searches were conducted across Scopus, Web
of Science (WoS), Elsevier ScienceDirect, Springer Link,
Taylor & Francis Online, Wiley Online Library, IEEE
Xplore, MDPI, and Emerald Insight. Grey literature sources
includedAPEDA,FAO,NABARD,TamilNaduAgricultural
University(TNAU),andMinistryofAgricultureandFarmers
Welfare (Government of India). Publications from 2010 to
2024 were searched. Three keyword clusters were applied in
Booleancombinations(Table1):
1. ClusterA(Technology): digitaltransformation,artificial
intelligence, blockchain, precision farming, machine
learning,IoT,smartfarming.
2. Cluster B (Supply Chain): organic supply chain,
agri-food supply chain, PGS-India, farm-to-consumer,
traceability,coldchain,certification,NPOP.
3. Cluster C (Context): India, South Asia, Tamil Nadu,
developingeconomy,smallholder.
2.3. Inclusionandexclusioncriteria
The inclusion and exclusion criteria that were applied are
summarizedinTable2. Onlypublicationsthatwerepublished
in English between 2010 and 2024, addressed at least one
technology and one supply chain dimension, and originating
fromjournalsindexedinScopus,WoS,orAustralianBusiness
Deans Council, or from credible institutional or government
sources were included. Sources that lacked an identifiable
research methodology, from predatory journals, contained
fabricated or future-dated data, or were conference abstracts,
were excluded. Prior to inclusion, all references were verified
byDOIorinstitutionalURL.
2.4. Screeningandfinalsample
Theliteraturesearchidentified412recordsacrossalldatabases.
After removing duplicates, 341 records remained. Title and
abstract screening yielded 87 studies. Of these, 30 sources
met inclusion criteria, comprising 22 peer-reviewed journal
articles, 2 books or book chapters, 3 conference or technical
reports, and 3 institutional or government reports (Figure 1).
AllincludedsourceswereverifiedthroughDOIsorinstitutional

3. Review of Literature
Research on digital transformation in agriculture has
consistently shown that technology adoption depends as
much on organizational and institutional readiness as on
the availability of digital tools themselves. Hanelt et al.
(2021) and Vial (2019) frame digital transformation as a
multidimensional process spanning organizational strategy,
technological capability, and institutional structure, a view
laterextendedbyVerhoefetal. (2021)todescribehowfirms,
technology ecosystems, and markets evolve together. Within
agriculture specifically, Klerkx et al. (2019) and Wolfert et
al. (2017) document how artificial intelligence, sensor-based
monitoring, and blockchain systems are reshaping farm
advisoryservices,thoughtheirdiffusionremainsunevenacross
smallholdersettings.
A recurring theme in the literature is the barrier posed by
inadequate rural infrastructure and limited digital capability.
Kamble et al. (2019) and Lezoche et al. (2020) identify
connectivity gaps, data interoperability issues, and weak
institutional coordination as persistent constraints on Internet
ofThings(IoT)andblockchainadoptioninfoodsupplychains.
Lioutas and Charatsari (2020) add that digital tools designed
withoutinputfromfarmingcommunitiesoftenfailtointegrate
meaningfully into short, localized supply chains, reinforcing
theneedforparticipatorytechnologydesign.
Ontheproductionside,severalstudiespointtomeasurable
gains from AI-based tools. Kamilaris and Prenafeta-Boldú
(2018) report high accuracy for machine-learning-based
pest and disease detection, while Liakos et al. (2018)
find that supervised learning models consistently outperform
conventional decision-support tools in crop and livestock
management. Verdouw et al. (2021) extendthis discussion to
digitaltwintechnology,showinghowreal-timedatacaninform
adaptivefarmmanagementdecisions.
Logistics and cold-chain literature highlights the scale
of post-harvest loss in developing regions. FAO (2021)

4. Results
4.1. Digitaladoptionbarriers
The reviewed literature identified three categories of
barriers to digital adoption in smallholder organic supply
chains. Infrastructure barriers include poor rural broadband
connectivity, limited smartphone penetration, and unreliable
electricity supply (Klerkx et al., 2019; Wolfert et al.,
2017). NABARD (2022) reported that the five southern
districts of Tamil Nadu lag behind the state average in
digital connectivity, with cold storage capacity accounting
for less than 8% of the state’s installed capacity. Capability
barriers comprise of limited digital knowledge, inadequate
technical support, and the lack of AI-based advisory tools
designed to PGS-India production systems. Kamble et al.
(2019) identified capability deficits as a persistent obstacle
to Internet of Things (IoT) adoption in food retail supply
chains. Lioutas and Charatsari (2020) reported that smart
farming and short food supply chains are not inherently
compatible; digital technologies must be co-designed for
smallholder farmers to deliver value. Institutional barriers
arise from the structural incompatibility between PGS-India’s
community-basedcertificationdocumentationandmainstream
e-commerce data standards (Kamble et al., 2019; Lezoche et
al., 2020). These six barriers are summarized in Table 6
and illustrated in Figure 4. The overall thematic synthesis is
presentedinFigure5.
4.2. AI-enabledproductionplanning
Wolfert et al. (2017) documented the transformative
potentialofbigdataandsmartfarmingtechnologiesincluding,
AI-driven advisory for input optimization, pest and disease
detection, irrigation scheduling, and yield forecasting. The
convolutional neural networks achieved a 95% crop disease
identification accuracy. This is directly applicable to
organic pest management without any chemical intervention
(Kamilaris and Prenafeta-Boldú, 2018). Liakos et al.
(2018)confirmedthatsupervisedmachinelearningconstantly
outperforms traditional decision tools across crop, livestock,
and soil management contexts. Real-time feedback loops
between field conditions and management decisions can be
generated using AI and digital twins (Verdouw et al., 2021).
However, the effective implementation of these technologies
depends on essential foundational prerequisites, like reliable
mobile connectivity, sensor networks, and standardized data
infrastructure. Klerkxetal. (2019)emphasizedthatsuccessful
adoption in smallholder farming systems relies critically on
intermediary organizations (Farmer Producer Organization
(FPOs), cooperatives, extension services), which play an
importantroleintranslatingcomplexAIoutputsintopractical,
actionable recommendations for farmers with limited digital
literacy.
4.3. Logistics,coldchain,andIoT
FAO (2021) estimates that agri-food systems in developing
countries lose approximately 14% of production between
harvest and retail. In South Tamil Nadu’s organic
horticultural and spice sectors, ambient first-mile transport
and the absence of pre-cooling facilities at FPO collection
centres are the primary loss drivers. IoT-based cold-chain
monitoring — temperature and humidity sensors transmitting
real-time data to farm management platforms — offers
documented solutions, but Kamble et al. (2019) identify
cost, interoperability, data security, and lack of standardised
protocols as the primary barriers. Lezoche et al. (2020)
establish that Agri-Food 4.0 adoption asymmetries between
large agri-businesses and smallholders are widening without
deliberate policy intervention. Ivanov and Dolgui (2021)
demonstrate that digital supply chain twins enable proactive
disruption management — relevant for organic supply chains
subject to seasonal production volatility. The farm-gate to

Blockchain technology enhances accountability, traceability,

blockchain-enabled traceability is most effective when supply
chain participants share compatible data standards and
governance frameworks. Accordingly, these conditions must
precedeblockchaindeploymentinPGS-Indiagovernedchains.
Galvez et al. (2018) identified critical challenges namely,
standardization, interoperability across supply chain tiers, and
regulatory acceptance, that must be resolved in food chains
before blockchain traceability can reach its full potential.
Duan et al. (2020) found cost, technical complexity, and
lack of supply chain-wide governance frameworks as the
primary blocks in the adoption of blockchain in food supply
chains. This confirms that blockchain benefits are conditional
prior to governance arrangements. Tripoli and Schmidhuber
(2018)identifiedtraceabilityinsmallholderorganicproduction

blockchain technology, while emphasizing that its successful
implementation depends on key prerequisites, including
reliable mobile connectivity, standardized data entry, and
trust-basedinter-institutionalgovernance. Similarly,Wognum
et al. (2011) identified information asymmetry across
supply chain tiers as a fundamental barrier to transparency
challengeinfoodsystems,highlightingtheneedforintegrated
information-sharingmechanisms.

Wolfert et al., 2017). In South Tamil Nadu, TNAU,
NABARD, SFAC, and the state’s FPO network constitute a
potentially supportive institutional architecture. NABARD
(2022) documents active FPO formation support across the
southern districts. Drawing on the six thematic domains,

5. Discussion
Thesynthesisrevealsaconsistentpattern: technologyadoption
insmallholderorganicagricultureisnotprimarilyconstrained
by technology unavailability, but by sequential failure
of foundational preconditions — connectivity, capability,
platform compatibility, logistics infrastructure, and consumer
trust — that must be addressed in order before downstream
technologyinvestmentscandeliverintendedreturns.
This finding converges with Hanelt et al. (2021) and
Vial’s (2019) central observation that digital transformation
is fundamentally an organizational and institutional challenge
before it is technological. Verhoef et al. (2021) extended
this concept to the industry level. They established that
digital transformation develops co-evolutionary interactions
among organizations, technology ecosystems, and market
structures,whicharehighlyrelevanttothePGS-Indiaorganic
supply chain ecosystem. Correspondingly, the DARF-OSC
frameworkexpandsexistingsupplychaintechnologyadoption
models (Kamble et al., 2018, 2019; Ivanov & Dolgui,
2021) by adapting them to the unique institutional setting of
community-certifiedorganicsmallholderproductionsystems.
On the consumer end, studies by Nandi et al. (2017)
and Wang et al. (2019) indicate that consumers in
South Indian markets are willing to pay a premium for
certified organic produce, provided there is certification
credibility. Furthermore, Lioutas and Charatsari (2020)
proposed that digital technologies must be co-designed with
farming communities to ensure meaningful integration with
short supply chains. Importantly, digital traceability systems
that communicate PGS peer-review narratives at the point
of purchase offer a practical and cost-effective approach of
enhancing certification credibility. Such systems narrow the
pricepremiumgapbetweenPGSandNPOP-certifiedproducts
without requiring costly certification upgrades (Behnke &
Janssen,2020;Duanetal.,2020;Galvezetal.,2018).
5.1. Managerialimplications
For FPO managers and organic producer collectives,
the DARF-OSC framework recommends a phased
adoption approach to digital adoption. Initial efforts
should prioritize mobile-based PGS documentation and
WhatsApp-enabled direct-to-consumer marketing, followed
by gradual implementation of blockchain traceability
systems and integration with formal e-commerce platforms
as organizational capacity and digital readiness improve.
Pre-cooling units at FPO collection centres should be
prioritisedoverIoTmonitoringsystems,sinceIoTpresupposes
the absence of refrigerated infrastructure across most of the
region.
5.2. Policyimplications
Three sequenced priorities emerge: (1) rural broadband and
electricity infrastructure investment across the five southern
districts must precede digital transformation programs; (2)
PGS-India-compatible digital documentation standards must
bemandatedbeforeplatformonboarding,informedbyBehnke
and Janssen’s (2020) and Duan et al.’s (2020) finding that
blockchainbenefitsareconditionalondatagovernance;and(3)
targeted digital literacy for PGS group leaders must precede
platform rollout — Klerkx et al. (2019) find that adoption
reversal is common when capability-building follows rather
thanprecedestechnologydeployment.
6. Limitations
This study has five principal limitations. First, as a secondary
synthesis, itcannotsubstituteforprimaryempiricaldatafrom
South Tamil Nadu’s five southern districts. Second, grey
literature search may have missed unpublished institutional
reports. Third, the DARF-OSC framework is inductively
derived and requires empirical validation through primary
fieldwork before prescriptive application. Fourth, the rapid
pace of advancements in AI and blockchain technologies
meansthatspecificassessmentsmaybecomepartiallyoutdated
within 12–18 months. Fifth, the review is based on a fixed
sampleof30sources;alargerandmorediverseevidencebase,
including more region-specific studies and primary field data,
wouldstrengthenthegeneralisabilityofthefindings.
6. Conclusions
This systematic review examines the challenges and
opportunities associated with digital transformation and
artificial intelligence across the production, logistics, and
distribution levels of organic agri-food supply chains, with
particular focus on south Tamil Nadu. It reveals that digital
and AI-enabled technologies have significant potential to
improve production planning, minimize post-harvest losses,
strengthen certification traceability, and enhance farm-gate
prices. However, these benefits can be realized only through
coordinatedandsequentialprogressacrossfiveinterdependent
preconditions identified in the DARF-OSC framework
(Figure2).
Main conclusions of practical importance are: (1)
Strengthening cold-chain infrastructure throughout the
southern districts is an urgent physical requirement for
translatingdigitallogistictechnologiesintopracticalbenefitsat
the producer level; (2) Development of digital documentation
standards aligned with PGS-India requirements and making
them a critical prerequisite for implementation of platform
integration initiative; and (3) Consumer-accessible QR
code-linked PGS peer-review narratives constitute the most
feasible and affordable intervention for establishing the
certification credibility requirement to justify organic price
premiums.
For the academic community, five research gaps define
a primary field-research agenda: empirical study of digital
adoption among PGS-certified producers in South Tamil
Nadu; development and evaluation of organic-specific AI
advisorytools;evaluationofblockchaintraceabilitywithinthe
PGS-India governance framework; longitudinal tracking of
FPO digital adoption impact; and crop-system-specific digital
transformation research across the region’s diverse organic
production base. The DARF-OSC framework provides a
structured, region-specific conceptual foundation for all five
researchdirections.
