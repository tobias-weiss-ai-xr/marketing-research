Abstract
Land sales have been a significant source of revenue for Chinese local governments since the early 2000s.
Contrary to popular belief, local governments did not turn to land finance to make up for lost tax revenues
after the 1994 tax sharing reform. Rather, both before and after 1994, corrupt land sales were widespread,
as officials sold land far below market prices in return for bribes. Even as the fiscal incentives for local
governments to adopt land finance increased in the 1990s, state agents continued acting on their own per-
sonal interests rather than the institutional interests of local governments. Local governments only began
toselllandathigher,marketpriceswhenananti-corruptioncampaignlaunchedbythecentralgovernment
made doing so a political imperative in the early 2000s. Local governments were much more responsive to
political imperatives than to fiscal incentives.

Because the literature argues that fiscal deprivation drove local governments to land finance, this
article focuses narrowly on showing that most local governments did not even try to sell land at
market prices, a relatively simple task, until the central government forced them to do so. While
fierce competition to attract investors motivated local governments to keep land prices low,6 this
does not explain their refusal to sell land at market prices: market prices are, by definition, clearing
prices.
While selling land at market prices was clearly in the fiscal interests of local governments, it was
often not in the personal interests of local officials, who sold land below market price in return for
bribes.Thiscorruptionwasoftentheworkofbroadnetworks:sometimes,higher-levelofficialsasked
subordinates – who dared not refuse – to grant discounted land to particular developers; sometimes,
officials across multiple agencies collaborated to trade land for bribes. With so many officials partak-
ing of the rents from corrupt land sales, local officials seldom tried to impose market prices; when
they did attempt to do so, they rarely succeeded. When the central government pushed local gov-
ernments to sell land at market prices, the move encountered pockets of stubborn resistance; only
after a multi-year anti-corruption campaign did market pricing of residential and commercial land
become the national norm.

Sources of Evidence
This section introduces the sources of evidence for the paper’s four core arguments.
Argument 1. Land sales did not become a substantial source of government revenue until the early
2000s.
This argument is obvious fromFigure 1.
Argument 2. Using backroom negotiations, local governments routinely sold land well below
market prices until the central government intervened.
We know this because occasional auctions revealed market prices far above negotiated prices.
In the 1990s, local governments could allocate land in several ways. They could assign (huabo
划拨) land at or below cost to an end user, a protocol inherited from the planned economy. Such
land could not be traded on the secondary market. Local governments could also sell tradable land
use rights through negotiation (xieyi 协议). In this commonly used method, the price was agreed,
generally in secret, between the government and the end user. While these two mechanisms obscure
market prices, two others shed light on market prices: bid solicitation (zhaobiao 招标) and live auc-
tions (paimai 拍卖), collectively referred to as “auctions” hereafter. Before an auction, the local
government would publish planning restrictions for a land parcel. When soliciting bids, it would
invite developers to submit sealed bids. Officials then evaluated each developer’s price, track record
and project proposal. While bid solicitation allowed officials some leeway to choose underpriced
proposals, live auctions did not: developers gathered in person to bid, and land went to the highest
bidder.

negotiation. Thus, I use the share of land sold at auction as a measure of the extent to which local

Throughout the paper, I focus on residential and commercial markets, which are the most lucra-

Unfortunately, much of the data on land sales in the 1990s are not broken down by land use, so
some evidence necessarily draws on the broader land market.

Fiscal Opportunities
Local governments had many opportunities to charge market prices for land. The initiation of
land sales in 1987, and their institutionalization in 1990, provided the institutional structure and
political legitimacy for local governments to sell land. Local governments did sell land, but they
quickly turned away from seeking high prices. When a real estate bubble emerged in 1992, rising
values created opportunities to extract more land revenue but also generated huge incentives for
corruption, as many local officials chose to allocate land at steep discounts in return for bribes.

The legalization of land sales in the late 1980s opened up a significant new revenue source for
local governments. After enthusiastically selling land at price-maximizing auctions, local govern-
ments opted to hide their wealth from a central government that demanded a share, and so they
turned to backroom negotiations, which created enormous scope for rent-seeking opportunities.
Even after the centre stopped demanding a share of land revenue, and even when local governments
sought to return to auctions, there was resistance from officials who preferred to bargain directly
with developers.
The land bubble
Following Deng Xiaoping’s邓小平1992 Southern Tour, China experienced a land bubble.22 Despite
rapidly rising market prices, local governments continued to sell land cheaply and often failed to
collect even half of land payments.23 The windfall went instead to speculators and the officials who
helped them to access land at below-market prices. The speculators then flipped the land for huge
profits. While prices fell after 1993, the corrupt sale of discounted land, which had been normalized
during this period, continued through the 1994 tax sharing reforms.
To some extent, this underpricing of land was intentional. Beihai, for instance, adopted a “low
barrier to entry” (dimenkan 低门槛) policy, which allocated land cheaply to investors.24 The result
was a mad dash for land by those with good connections. Speculators frenetically traded the right to
purchase land from the government without actually paying the government. When they did pay,
prices were set through backroom negotiation. After the bubble burst, much of Beihai’s municipal
leadership was implicated in a sprawling bribery scheme through which developers circumvented
or received discounts on land payments: the leaders of the municipal disciplinary inspection com-
mission, the organization department, the land bureau and the planning bureau were all removed
from office.25 Although officials presented cheap land as an incentive to attract investors, the same
officials were taking bribes to keep land cheap.

New institutional foundations
By the late 1990s, not only was the 1994 tax sharing reform firmly in place, but the central govern-
ment had laid the foundations for land finance. New legislation made it easier for local governments

reforms made it harder for local governments to borrow without collateral, but banks were happy
to lend against land if municipalities would extract higher revenues from land sales. The central
government also accelerated housing reforms, banning subsidized housing for state employees and
promoting the private housing market, increasing demand for residential land. Together, these
reforms offered local governments higher profit margins when selling land at market prices, bank
financing for initial capital outlays and increased demand.29

Ultimately, the national government lost patience. Although some localities had tried to charge mar-
ket prices for land, the vast majority had not. In the early 2000s, the centre instructed local govern-

The rectification campaign was ambitious: the centre sought to force local governments not only
to sell land at market prices but also to monopolize the land market and engage directly in land
takings. These reforms promised much higher land revenue for local governments; however, they
also required a sharp increase in state capacity and changes in the relative power of local govern-
ments and “socialist land masters” such as state-owned enterprises.37 While each of these reforms
contributed to the advent of land finance, this paper’s focus remains on market pricing through
auctions, a comparatively straightforward task that nonetheless evinced significant opposition from
local governments.

Conclusion
Most local governments turned to land finance only when the central government forced them to do
so. This paper not only demonstrates that the 1994 tax sharing reforms did not motivate most local
governments to replace lost tax revenue with land sales, but it also challenges the underlying model
that local governments respond reliably to fiscal incentives. As this paper shows, local governments
had the fiscal incentives to sell land at market prices in the 1980s, and these incentives increased as
land sales were legalized, land values rose and the centre built the institutional foundations of land
finance. Yet local governments did not consistently act on these fiscal incentives. For local cadres,
opportunities for personal enrichment outweighed fiscal incentives for their institutions.
In the case of China’s land finance, state agents were brought into line with a stick, not a car-
rot. They were called to task by a top-down anti-corruption campaign. Although the Hu–Wen-era
campaign used work teams to pressure local governments and institutional changes to hem them in,
rather than the arrests that have characterized Xi Jinping’s习近平 anti-corruption campaign, the
intent was anti-corruption and the means were a campaign. The central government’s decisive role
in initiating land finance strongly supports claims that it was instrumental in engineering China’s
land-centric model of government finance and economic growth.108
The central government’s imposition of market and even monopoly prices for land revealed
how great the tension between self-interested state agents and their institutions had been. The ratio
between land revenue and local government on-budget revenues shot up from under 10 per cent in
2000 to over 50 per cent in 2003.
China’s economic growth model shifted with local governments’ revenue model: growth came
increasingly to revolve around the construction sector, which built the real estate developments
that purchased government landand the infrastructure projects paid for by land revenue. In turn,
infrastructureprojectselevatedrealestatevalues,furtherpromotinggrowth.ThatChina’srealestate-
centred growth model has its origins in an anti-corruption campaign is, of course, a great irony: real
estate has become a byword for corruption in contemporary China.109
