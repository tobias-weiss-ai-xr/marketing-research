Abstract:
In emerging markets, artificial intelligence (AI) has become a key enabler of economic diversification and
sustainable development, but its possible impact on the marketing policy of Sub -Saharan Africa has not been
theorised. This paper investigates the impact of AI on the economic landscape in Nigeria and its implications
for the practice and policy of marketing in the country. The research approach used is a secondary source
design, which involves document analysis, combini ng macroeconomic indicators from Nigeria's National
Artificial Intelligence Strategy and the National Bureau of Statistics, International Finance Corporation, and
PwC as well as the thematic review of ten empirical and conceptual studies on AI in Marketing  in Nigeria
published from 2023 to 2026. The study also reveals that Nigeria's ecosystem in AI is growing rapidly with an
estimated growth rate of 27 per cent annually and a projected US$15bn impact on GDP by 2030, all while the
country faces significant digital and welfare inequality. The thematic review reveals that in Nigeria, marketing
research around AI is focused primarily on adoption of digital-marketing tools and personalisation, while there
is comparatively less attention to issues of consumer behaviour and regulation that are sustainable. The paper
asserts that the marketing -policy framework in Nigeria, including data -protection and consumer -protection
tools, have not adequately followed the evolution of marketing practice using AI, resulting in governance gaps
in the area of algorithmic bias, data extraction and digital exclusion. The study brings together a synthesis
framework relating the macro levels AI development indicator s to the micro levels of marketing practice and
presents a policy roadmap to enable harmonization of AI -enabled marketing and Nigeria's sustainable
development goals.

1. INTRODUCTION
Artificial intelligence has moved from a specialised  computing concern to a general -
purpose technology with acknowledged consequences for productivity, service delivery
and economic structure worldwide. PwC (2018) estimated that AI could add US$15.7
trillion to global output by 2030, roughly evenly split be tween productivity gains and
consumption-side effects from new products and services (Federal Ministry of
Communications, Innovation and Digital Economy [FMCIDE], 2023). For Africa, the
International Finance Corporation projects that strategic AI adoption could add up to
US$234 billion to continental GDP by 2030 (FMCIDE, 2023). Within this continental
picture, Nigeria occupies a distinctive position: it possesses the largest population and
consumer market in Africa, a youthful and mobile -connected demograph ic, and a
technology start -up ecosystem that attracted roughly a quarter of the US$1.3 billion
raised by African tech start-ups in 2021 (FMCIDE, 2023).

These national conditions have translated into deliberate policy action. In 2023, Nigeria's
Federal Ministry of Communications, Innovation and Digital Economy began co-creating
a National Artificial Intelligence Strategy (NAIS), culminating in an April 202 4
stakeholder workshop and a policy blueprint that targets a US$15 billion contribution to
GDP, a 27 percent annual AI -market growth rate and the equipping of 70 percent of
Nigeria's youthful workforce with AI skills by 2030 (BusinessDay, 2026; Policy Vaul t
Africa, 2024). Government -led investment has been complemented by private capital:
Google's continuing digital -infrastructure commitment is reported to have generated
roughly US$16 billion in regional economic activity in 2023 alone ( Techpoint Africa,
2024). Alongside this policy momentum, Nigeria's underlying digital infrastructure has
expanded quickly, with the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS, 2024) recording 164
million active internet users and 219 million mobile -phone subscript ions by the first
quarter of 2024, figures that make Nigeria one of the largest mobile -first digital markets
on the continent (Ogunbodede & Atchrim, 2025).
Marketing is one of the functional domains most directly exposed to this transformation.
AI-enabled tools now support consumer segmentation, personalised advertising,
automated pricing, chatbot-based customer service and predictive demand analysis, and
a g rowing empirical literature documents their diffusion into Nigerian firms (Nyong,
Samson & Akarika, 2023; Kalu & Ogungbangbe, 2024). Global marketing scholarship has
already reframed AI as a structural force in the discipline: Davenport, Guha, Grewal and
Bressgott (2020) propose a multidimensional framework for understanding how AI
reshapes both marketing strategy and customer behaviour, while cautioning that its
diffusion raises unresolved policy questions of privacy, bias and ethics. These questions
acquire particular weight in a market such as Nigeria's, where 40 percent of the
population lives below the national poverty line of ₦137,430 (approximately US$381.75)
per year (NBS, 2024), and where the same AI systems that promise efficiency gains for

Despite the scale of this transformation, the relationship between Nigeria's AI -led
economic development trajectory and its marketing -policy architecture has not been
systematically examined. Existing Nigerian studies on AI and marketing are
predominantly firm-level and sector -specific: they document AI's role in digital -
marketing tool adoption (Nwachukwu, 2023), advertising strategy (Israel, Ibiyeye &
Adedeji, 2026), e-commerce personalisation (Kalu & Ogungbangbe, 2024) and banking-
sector efficiency (Eze, Chinedu & Ugochukwu, 2023), but they rarely connect these firm-
level findings to the macroeconomic AI -development indicators being pursued by
national policy, nor do they interrogate whether Nigeria's consumer-protection and data-
governance instruments are adequate to the marketing practices AI now enables. This is
a contextual and analytical gap: the literature on AI and the Sustainable Development
Goals (SDGs) shows that AI can enable progress on 134 of 169 SDG targets while
simultaneously inhibiting 59 of them, particularly where governance and oversight are
weak (Vinuesa et al., 2020), yet this enabling or inhibiting duality has not been examined
for Nigeria's marketing-policy environment specifically.
This paper addresses that gap by pursuing four objectives. First, it assesses the trajectory
and projected macroeconomic contribution of AI adoption to Nigeria's economic
development, using secondary indicators from national and international sources.
Second, it evaluates the extent and thematic pattern of AI adoption in Nigerian marketing
practice, through a synthesis of the empirical and conceptual marketing literature. Third,
it examines the implications of AI -driven economic transformation for sustainab ility-
oriented marketing policy in Nigeria, drawing on the SDG -enablement/inhibition
framework. Fourth, it proposes a policy pathway for aligning AI -enabled marketing
practice with Nigeria's sustainable-development priorities. The paper's contribution is a
synthesis framework that connects macro-level AI-development indicators to micro-level
marketing practice and to the specific policy instruments (data protection, consumer
protection, digital-skills development) through which Nigeria's marketing regulators can
respond.
       2. METHODOLOGY
The study adopts a qualitative, secondary-source document-analysis design, appropriate
for a policy -oriented synthesis that draws on both macroeconomic indicators and firm -
level empirical literature. Two categories of secondary data were assembled. The fir st
category comprises macro -level, quantitative indicators of Nigeria's AI -economic
trajectory, sourced from official and institutional documents: Nigeria's National Artificial
Intelligence Strategy documentation (FMCIDE, 2023; Policy Vault Africa, 2024), the
National Bureau of Statistics (NBS, 2024), the International Finance Corporation's
continental projections as reported in FMCIDE (2023), and PwC's (2018) global AI -

economic-impact estimate. The second category comprises Nigerian peer -reviewed and
institutionally published studies on AI in marketing and consumer behaviour, identified
through structured searches of Google Scholar and affiliated repositories using the terms
“artificial intelligence,” “marketing,” “digital marketing,” “consumer behaviour” and
“Nigeria,” restricted to items published between 2023 and 2026 to capture the period
coinciding with Nigeria's National AI Strategy process.

Ten studies meeting these criteria were retained for thematic synthesis (Table 2). Each
study was read in full or in extended abstract form and coded inductively against its stated
research focus, generating five recurring themes: digital -marketing and advertising tool
adoption; AI -driven personalisation and consumer behaviour; sustainable or green
consumer behaviour; e -commerce and pricing optimisation; and sector -specific
organisational efficiency. This thematic coding followed the logic of a critical, synthesis -
oriented literature review rather than a simple descriptive catalogue, in that studies were
grouped and compared by focus and finding rather than listed sequentially by author. The
macro-level indicators in Table 1 were similarly compiled and cross -checked across at
least two independent secondary sources where possible, to reduce the risk of relying on
a single institutional projection. Simple frequency counts and descriptive com parison,
rather than inferential statistics, were used to analyse the data, consistent with the
exploratory, policy-synthesis purpose of the study. The principal limitation of this design
is that it relies on projected and self -reported institutional figures rather than primary
firm-level survey data; these projections should therefore be read as directional evidence
of Nigeria's AI-development trajectory rather than as precise forecasts.

Table 1 and Figure 1 summarise Nigeria's AI-development trajectory against comparative
continental and global benchmarks. Three patterns are evident. First, there is a clear scale
gradient: the global AI -economic-impact estimate of US$15.7 trillion by 2030 (PwC,
2018) dwarfs the IFC's continental projection of US$234 billion for Africa, which in turn
dwarfs Nigeria's own national target of US$15 billion (FMCIDE, 2023; BusinessDay,
2026). Nigeria's targeted contribution therefore represents a modest but not negligi ble
share of the projected continental gain, and the government's own 27 percent projected
annual AI-market growth rate suggests policymakers expect this share to expand rapidly
across the decade (BusinessDay, 2026). Second, Nigeria's digital infrastructur e base
already supports this ambition at scale: 164 million active internet users and 219 million
mobile subscriptions (NBS, 2024) constitute one of the largest addressable digital -
consumer populations on the continent, a precondition for AI-enabled marketing to reach
mass-market scale rather than remaining confined to a small formal -sector elite
(Ogunbodede & Atchrim, 2025).
Third, and most significant for a sustainable -development reading of these figures,
Nigeria's AI ambitions sit alongside substantial unresolved welfare deficits. Forty percent
of the population lives below the national poverty line of approximately US$381. 75 per
year, and reported per -capita income for 2023 was as low as US$362.81 (NBS, 2024;
World Bank, 2023, as cited in Ogunbodede & Atchrim , 2025). The juxtaposition of a
US$15 billion AI -growth target with these poverty indicators illustrates the central
tension this paper investigates: AI-led growth, as currently projected, is a macroeconomic
aggregate that does not by itself specify how it s gains will be distributed across a
population where four in ten citizens live in poverty. The National AI Strategy's parallel
target of equipping 70 percent of Nigeria's youthful workforce with AI skills by 2030
(BusinessDay, 2026) is best read as an imp licit distributional mechanism, intended to
convert aggregate AI growth into broad -based employability rather than concentrated
returns to capital. Whether this skills target materialises at the pace and scale required is,
however, an implementation question that lies beyond what secondary macro-indicators
alone can answer, and it is precisely this gap between aggregate projection and distributed
outcome that motivates the marketing-policy focus of the remainder of this paper.

The thematic synthesis in Table 2 and Figure 2 shows that Nigerian marketing scholarship
on AI is heavily weighted toward digital -marketing and advertising tool adoption, which
accounts for four of the ten reviewed studies. Nyong, Samson and Akarika (2023)  and
Nwachukwu (2023) both report that AI tools are increasingly used by Nigerian digital
marketers for content personalisation, audience targeting and campaign automation, but
that exposure to and competence with these tools remains uneven across marketing
practitioners, constrained by cost, training and infrastructural access. Israel, Ibiyeye and
Adedeji (2026) extend this finding to advertising strategy specifically, documenting how
a major Nigerian telecommunications operator has adapted AI -enabled advertising to a
globalising competitive environment. Personalisation  and consumer -behaviour studies
form the second-largest theme: an emerging study using partial least squares structural
equation modelling with 303 Nigerian respondents finds that digital trust and consumer
attitudes toward AI significantly and positively predict online purchase intention, with
consumer awareness acting as a partial mediator (Academia.edu/IJFMR, 2026), a finding
consistent with Davenport et al.'s (2020) broader argument that AI is reshaping customer
decision journeys globally, not only marketing execution.
Sustainability-oriented consumer -behaviour research forms a smaller but analytically
important cluster. Oke, Ramachandran, Afolayan, Ihemereze  and Udeh (2024), in a
cross-sectional study of southwest Nigeria, report that AI tools are beginning to shape
sustainable consumption decisions, while a related AI -and-Internet-of-Things study
demonstrates how Nigerian retailers are using real-time consumer-data streams to align
marketing with environmentally conscious purchasing behaviour. E -commerce-specific
applications, exemplified by Kalu and Ogungbangbe (2024), show AI supporting
personalised consumer experiences, optimised  pricing models and targeted marketing
within Nigeria's expanding online -retail sector, while sector -specific organisational -
efficiency research, such as Eze, Chinedu and Ugochukwu's (2023) study of the banking

3.3 Objective Three: Implications for Sustainability -Oriented Marketing
Policy
Read together, the macro -level and firm -level findings support Vinuesa et al.'s (2020)
argument that AI functions simultaneously as an enabler and an inhibitor of sustainable-
development outcomes, and this duality is clearly visible in the Nigerian marketi ng
context. On the enabling side, AI -driven personalisation  and targeting can improve
marketing efficiency for Nigerian firms operating in a large but fragmented consumer
market, reduce customer-acquisition costs for small and medium enterprises, and, as the
sustainability-focused studies in Table 2 suggest, suppo rt the promotion of
environmentally conscious products and consumption patterns (Oke et al., 2024). AI -
enabled advertising and e -commerce personalisation  can also extend market access to
previously underserved consumers, consistent with Nigeria's mobile -first digital -
infrastructure base of over 160 million internet users (NBS, 2024).

On the inhibiting side, the same AI systems raise governance risks that Nigeria's current
marketing-policy architecture is not yet fully equipped to address. Davenport et al. (2020)
identify privacy, algorithmic bias and ethics as the central unresolved po licy questions
accompanying AI's diffusion into marketing globally, and each has a distinctly Nigerian
inflection. Privacy risk is heightened where AI-enabled personalisation depends on large-
scale behavioural-data collection from consumers who may have limited digital literacy
or bargaining power over how their data are used; regulatory enforcement action against
a major Nigerian financial-services provider for data-protection breaches illustrates that
this is not a hypothetical concern (Reuters, 2024, as cited in governance literature on
Nigerian AI regulation).
Algorithmic bias risk arises where AI -driven segmentation and pricing models, trained
predominantly on data from urban, higher -income, English -literate consumers,
systematically underserve or misrepresent rural, lower -income or non-English-speaking
segments of Nigeria's market, a risk consistent with the 40 percent poverty prevalence
identified in Objective One. Digital -exclusion risk arises because AI -enabled marketing,
by construction, privileges consumers who are connected, data -visible and digitally
literate, potentially widening the gap between Nigeria's digitally integrated urban -
middle-class consumers and its poorer, less-connected majority. None of the ten Nigerian
marketing studies reviewed in Table 2 directly examines these governance questions,

                4. IMPLICATIONS FOR MARKETING POLICY IN NIGERIA
These findings carry three specific implications for Nigeria's marketing -policy
environment. First, consumer -data governance instruments, anchored in the Nigeria
Data Protection Act and enforced by the Nigeria Data Protection Commission, need
explicit extension to cover AI -driven marketing personalisation  and profiling practices,
given documented enforcement gaps in the financial -services sector (Reuters, 2024).
Second, sector regulators and marketing professional bodies should develop AI -
marketing codes of conduct that address algorithmic bias in consumer  segmentation,
consistent with the ethics and bias concerns identified by Davenport et al. (2020) and
with the enabling/inhibiting SDG framework of Vinuesa  et al. (2020), so that efficiency
gains for firms do not translate into systematic exclusion for lower -income or rural
consumers. Third, the National AI Strategy's workforce-skilling target should be explicitly
linked to marketing and digital-commerce curricula, so that the 70 percent AI-skills target
(BusinessDay, 2026) builds Nigerian capacity not only to deploy AI marketing tools but
also to audit and regulate them, closing the demonstrated gap between Nigeria's rapidly
growing firm -level AI -marketing l iterature and its comparatively underdeveloped
governance scholarship.
5. CONCLUSION
This paper set out to examine how AI -driven economic transformation in Nigeria is
reshaping marketing practice and the policy architecture within which that practice
operates. Drawing on secondary macroeconomic indicators and a thematic synthesis of
ten Nigerian studies, it shows that Nigeria's AI -development trajectory is ambitious and
rapidly advancing, but that its distributional and governance implications for marketing
remain under -examined in the existing literature, which has concentrated on tool
adoption and commercial performance rather than on sustainability and consumer
protection. The paper's contribution lies in connecting these two, previously separate,
strands of evidence, macro -level AI -economic projection and micro -level marketing
practice, into a single synthesis framework, and in showing that Nigeria's marketing -
policy instruments have not kept pace with the governance questions this connection
raises.
5.1 Recommendations
Based on these findings, the study recommends, first, that Nigeria's National Information
Technology Development Agency and Data Protection Commission jointly issue sector -
specific guidance on AI -driven marketing personalisation, profiling and pricing, closing
the gap between general data -protection law and marketing -specific practice. Second,
marketing professional bodies, such as the National Institute of Marketing of Nigeria,
should incorporate AI -ethics and algorithmic -bias m odules into professional

this review (Oke et al., 2024), so that the empirical evidence base can better inform the
distributional questions raised by Nigeria's national AI -growth targets. Finally,
policymakers should consider disaggregating the National AI Strategy's GDP  and skills
targets by region and income group, enabling future monitoring of whether AI-led growth
is narrowing or widening Nigeria's existing digital and economic inequalities.
