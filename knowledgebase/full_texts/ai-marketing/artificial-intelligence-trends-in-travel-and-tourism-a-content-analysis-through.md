Abstract: This study aims to reveal the main directions of AI-based transformation in travel and tourism,
the application areas in which it has become institutionalized, and the risk themes around which it has
been discussed. AI trends in travel and tourism were exa mined through content analysis based on
international reports and peer -reviewed academic publications. The corpus consisted of three major
international reports and selected academic studies addressing AI use in tourism from different
perspectives. Findings show that international reports frame AI in terms of innovation, sustainability,
efficiency, data use, and governance, while academic publications focus on tourist experience, smart
destination marketing, customer service automation, personalization, and  ethical risks. The reviewed
texts indicate that AI in tourism is conceptualized not only as an automation tool but also as a strategic
infrastructure for destination management, demand forecasting, tourist flow regulation, content
production, marketing communication, and decision support systems. At the same time, data privacy,
algorithmic bias, high investment costs, technical infrastructure deficiencies, transparency, workforce
transformation, and the weakening of the human dimension of service emerge as  key risks. Overall,
these trends cluster around personalization, forecasting, operational efficiency, smart destination
management, generative AI-based content production, and responsible AI governance.
Keywords: Artificial Intelligence, Tourism and Travel Technologies, Smart Destinations, AI
Governance

1. Introduction
In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has transcended being merely a technical innovation
in the tourism sector and has transformed into a structural transformation area reshaping the
planning, marketing, experience design, and governance processes  of the industry. The
widespread adoption of AI applications, particularly in areas such as travel planning, booking
processes, customer service, demand forecasting, price optimization, tourism flow
management, and destination communication, has made the data-intensive nature of the tourism
industry more visible. The UN Tourism report emphasizes that AI is transforming the way
tourists plan, experience, and remember their travels; while the OECD report states that this
transformation should be considered in conjunction with tourism innovation and sustainability.
In this context, AI stands out as an infrastructure that affects both experiential processes for
tourists and coordination between businesses, destinations, and public institutions. (UN
Tourism, 2024, p. 2; OECD, 2024: 5).
The significance of this research question has increased because AI -based applications are no
longer limited to experimental tools in tourism; they are increasingly embedded in booking
interfaces, destination promotion, customer relationship management, pr icing systems, and
visitor mobility governance. This makes the issue current not only for technology adoption, but
also for the competitiveness of destinations, the sustainability of visitor flows, and the
protection of tourists' data rights. Therefore, id entifying the thematic direction of AI trends
through both sectoral reports and academic publications is necessary for understanding how
tourism actors are expected to respond to a rapidly changing technological environment.

The tourism sector internalizes artificial intelligence applications differently than other sectors
because service production is largely based on a simultaneous, experience-oriented, and multi-
stakeholder structure. The OECD states that tourism is a compl ex service economy largely
composed of small and medium -sized enterprises, and therefore, the adoption of artificial
intelligence depends not only on technical capacity but also on entrepreneurship, the level of
digitalization, public policies, and cross -border cooperation. This assessment shows that
artificial intelligence in tourism not only produces “in -house efficiency” but also connects to
issues such as destination -scale mobility, accessibility, sustainability, and competitiveness
(OECD, 2024:14).
Academic literature similarly highlights the multifaceted nature of artificial intelligence in
tourism. A systematic review by López -Naranjo et al. reveals that the most frequently used
artificial intelligence technologies in tourism are natural language processing, neural networks,
deep learning, chatbots, and generative systems like ChatGPT. The same study emphasizes that
these technologies have a wide range of applications, from booking and customer service to
strategic data analysis. This finding is important because it shows that artificial intelligence in
tourism is not a singular technology, but rather a cluster of interrelated tools, platforms, and
analytical processes (López-Naranjo et al., 2025: 1, 7, 9).
However, artificial intelligence is not discussed in the literature solely within an optimistic
narrative of innovation. Studies on tourists' perceptions of AI systems show that while users
positively evaluate advantages such as speed, convenience, persona lization, and access to
information, they also harbor concerns such as data privacy, security, loss of authenticity, and
excessive dependence on technology. The study by Sousa et al. reveals that a significant portion
of tourists attribute positive feeling s to AI systems, but data privacy and security stand out as
the most prominent concerns. This dual structure explains why discussions on AI in tourism are
simultaneously based on both innovation and ethical governance literature. (Sousa et al., 2024:
20).
The starting point of this study is precisely this dual structure. On the one hand, international
organizations' reports position artificial intelligence as a strategic tool supporting the efficiency,
sustainability, and competitiveness of tourism. On the other hand, academic publications
discuss how these technologies are experienced in practice, in which areas they are accepted,
and which risks they can deepen. Therefore, instead of evaluating artificial intelligence trends

More specifically, the research problem is defined as the need to explain how AI -related
opportunities and risks in travel and tourism are simultaneously framed by policy -oriented
international reports and by academic studies focusing on practice, user perception, destination
management, and organizational transformation. This problem is feasible because it is
examined through a limited and clearly identifiable document corpus; it is significant because
AI adoption in tourism increasingly affects service de sign, visitor mobility, data governance,
marketing communication, and sustainability-oriented destination policies.
The subject of the study is directly related to the thematic scope of contemporary tourism
transformation debates because it addresses digitalization, smart destinations, sustainability,
governance, service innovation, and risk management within the same a nalytical framework.
In this respect, the article does not treat AI as a general technological phenomenon, but limits
the discussion to its concrete implications for travel and tourism systems.
The travel and tourism field was selected as the application area because it is one of the sectors
in which AI has visible effects across multiple stages of the value chain, including pre -travel
search, reservation, mobility, destination experience, post -travel communication, and
managerial decision -making. The field is also characterized by high data intensity, strong
service interaction, and multi -stakeholder coordination; therefore, it offers a suitable context
for examining both the opportunities and the risks of AI adoption.

In order to keep the literature review within the research field, the study excluded sources that
discuss artificial intelligence only in a general technological, engineering, medical, financial,
or industrial context without a direct link to tourism, trav el, destination management,
hospitality, tourist experience, or tourism marketing. This delimitation was adopted to prevent
conceptual dispersion and to ensure that the analysis remains aligned with the article's research

The additional literature used in this revision was selected according to the same principle. Only
studies that directly examine AI within tourism, hospitality, travel, smart destinations,
sustainable tourism, or rural tourism contexts were included. Therefore, general AI studies from
computer science, engineering, health, finance, or unrelated management fields were not
incorporated into the argument. This choice responds to the need to avoid references outside
the research area and to keep the literature review analytically aligned with the article's central
focus.
The selected documents were not used to produce universal claims about all AI applications.
Rather, they were used to identify dominant tendencies, recurring concepts, and major
risk/opportunity framings within the travel and tourism literature. Thus, the findings should be
read as a bounded thematic interpretation of representative documents rather than as a
comprehensive bibliometric mapping or a sector-wide measurement.
In this context, the study focuses on the following question: When international reports and

intelligence trends in travel and tourism concentrated? To answer this main question, recurring
concepts, application areas, opportunity narratives, risk headings, and future -oriented
suggestions in the texts have been analyzed using the logic of content analysis. Thus, both the
current direction of the sector and the conceptual depth of the academic field have been brought
together on the same analytical ground. (UN Tourism, 2024: 2; López-Naranjo et al., 2025: 1).
Accordingly, the objectives of the study are clearly limited to four interrelated aims: (1) to
identify the dominant AI trends discussed in travel and tourism documents, (2) to compare the
emphasis of international reports and academic publications, (3) to  reveal the relationship

2. Method and Analytical Framework
This study is a document review and thematic content analysis positioned within a qualitative
research approach. The corpus of studies was determined by purposive sampling. The corpus
includes three reports that directly focus on artificial intelligence in  the field of travel and
tourism and have international reference value, as well as selected academic articles
representing different dimensions. The reports; The publications included are: UN Tourism's
"Artificial Intelligence Adoption in Tourism," OECD's  "Artificial Intelligence and Tourism,"
and ETC's "Artificial Intelligence in Tourism – Assessing & Supporting NTOs’ Research &
Marketing Operations." On the academic side, studies representing the axes of systematic
review, user perception, and smart destination marketing were selected. This choice was made
to be able to read both macro -level policy orientation and micro -level user and business
experience together. (UN Tourism, 2024: 2; OECD, 2024: 1, 5; ETC, 2025: 3, 8).
References were evaluated according to their direct relevance to travel, tourism, hospitality,
smart destinations, destination marketing, tourist experience, institutional readiness, and AI
governance in the tourism sector. Studies that discuss artificial intelligence only as a general
technological development, without a clear connection to tourism -related applications or
sectoral transformation, were deliberately kept outside the literature review. This delimitation
responds to the need to prevent the literature section from moving beyond the research area.
The recently added sources were used as complementary academic references rather than as a
new independent dataset that changes the design of the study. Their function is to strengthen
the theoretical location of the research problem, support the interpret ation of current AI trends
in tourism, and clarify the boundaries of the literature review. In this respect, the original
analytical structure of the article was preserved, while the discussion was enriched with
tourism-specific review, content analysis, a nd bibliometric evidence (Taşdağıtıcı et al., 2025,
pp. 379-380; Wang et al., 2025, pp. 1-3; Sánchez-Sánchez et al., 2026, pp. 676-678).
The content analysis process evaluated the texts in five stages. In the first stage, document
selection and scope limitation were made. In the second stage, recurring key concepts in the
documents were identified. In the third stage, these concepts were coded under broader themes.
In the fourth stage, the commonalities and differences between the reports and academic studies

Another methodological choice of the study is to consider the differences in discursive emphasis
in the texts. International reports mostly use normative and governance -centered expressions
such as "responsible adoption," "organizational readiness," "polic y alignment," and
"sustainable development." Academic publications, on the other hand, rely on more concrete
and empirically focused categories such as “user experience,” “perceived benefit,” “emotions,”
“adoption barriers,” “cost,” and “authenticity.” The refore, the same technological
transformation is interpreted differently in different document types. This study addresses this
difference in discourse specific to document type as part of its analysis. (OECD, 2024: 5; Sousa
et al., 2024: 20; Florido-Benítez & del Alcázar Martínez, 2024:. 2, 14).
This method also has some limitations. Firstly, the study is not a fully systematic review
covering the entire literature; it is based on highly representative texts selected through
purposeful sampling. Secondly, because the field of artificial intelligence is changing so rapidly,
the trends that emerge in 2024 –2025 may quickly expand with new topics. Thirdly, since
international reports and academic articles have different purposes for generating and writing
data, comparing them on the same level requires careful interpretation. Nevertheless, it can be
said that the selected texts strongly reflect the current trends in artificial intelligence discussions
in travel and tourism. (OECD, 2024: 1, 5; ETC, 2025: 3; López-Naranjo et al., 2025: 1).

3. Findings: Thematic Content Analysis
3.1. Framing Artificial Intelligence as a “Transformative Force” in Tourism
The strongest common theme in the documents examined is the definition of artificial
intelligence as a “transformative” force in tourism. UN Tourism states that artificial intelligence
is reshaping tourists’ planning, experiencing, and post-travel evaluation practices. The OECD,
more explicitly, positions artificial intelligence as a force with the potential to support
innovation and sustainable development in tourism. The ETC report also emphasizes the
capacity of artificial intelligence to transform research and marketing functions, particularly for
national tourism organizations. This discursive commonality shows that artificial intelligence
is accepted as a strategic, not a marginal, technology in tourism. (UN Tourism, 2024: 2; OECD,
2024 5; ETC, 2025: 3).
Academic publications also confirm this transformation narrative. López -Naranjo and
colleagues state that artificial intelligence (AI) is transforming the way companies operate by
optimizing operations, personalizing customer experience, and improving reso urce
management. Florido-Benítez and del Alcázar Martínez, on the other hand, argue that AI is
being used to develop new business models and marketing services in smart tourist destinations,
thereby increasing sales, revenue, productivity, and service qual ity. Thus, both reports and
academic studies address AI not at the level of technology transfer, but at the level of business
model transformation (López-Naranjo et al., 2025: 1; Florido-Benítez & del Alcázar Martínez,
2024:2).
This finding also explains why the discourse on AI in tourism is rapidly linked to concepts such
as "competitive advantage" and "future agenda." In these documents, AI is often considered not
just a software or tool, but a new ecosystem that needs to be ad apted to. In particular, public
policy documents address this transformation as a strategic turning point related to the digital
transition of tourism (OECD, 2024, p. 14; ETC, 2025: 8).
This interpretation is also consistent with the more recent systematic and bibliometric literature.
Wang, Wang, Cui, and Lan argue that AI is systematically reshaping the tourism ecosystem
across marketing, promotion, operational models, recommendation sys tems, and service
processes. Their systematic literature review of 177 core articles also emphasizes that the field
needs an integrated framework connecting AI opportunities and risks with sustainable tourism
goals. This supports the present study's claim that AI in tourism should be treated as a structural

3.2. Personalization and Customer Experience are the Main Trends
The second main trend shown by the content analysis is the positioning of artificial intelligence
in tourism most intensively in the areas of personalization and customer experience. The OECD
report clearly lists the potential of artificial intelligence to  make the visitor experience more
interactive, more personalized, and more seamless. The same report states that elements such
as 24/7 service, faster response capacity, content accessibility, and personalized
recommendations are the key components of an A I-powered tourism experience (OECD,
2024:5).
In the study by López -Naranjo et al., customer service also stands out as one of the most
frequently mentioned application areas. According to the study, chatbots provide instant
responses; facial recognition systems provide faster check -in; Virtual assist ants, on the other
hand, function to offer suggestions and personalized support throughout the trip. Such systems
transform the tourism experience from a standard service flow into a data-driven and adaptable
interactive space (López-Naranjo et al., 2025: 9).
The study by Sousa et al., focusing on the tourist perspective, shows that this trend is also
reflected on the user side. According to the research results, participants cite easy access to
information, simpler booking processes, shorter waiting times, and  more efficient services as
the main advantages of artificial intelligence systems. Moreover, a strong relationship was
found between positive emotions and perceived benefit; users who have a positive attitude
towards artificial intelligence use these systems more. Therefore, personalization is not only a
feature offered by institutions but also functions as a psychological variable affecting user
acceptance (Sousa et al., 2024: 20).
In the context of smart destinations, Florido -Benítez and del Alcázar Martínez emphasize that
ChatGPT, chatbots, and various travel planning platforms are transforming tourism marketing
through customized suggestions, digital promotions, and content creati on. Here, artificial
intelligence becomes a communication tool that not only improves service delivery but also
anticipates tourist interests and shapes demand. Therefore, personalization is central to both
experience design and marketing strategy (Florido-Benítez & del Alcázar Martínez, 2024: 2).

3.3. Operational Efficiency and Automation are a Strong Second Axis
The second major theme following personalization in the texts examined is operational
efficiency and automation. The OECD emphasizes the potential of artificial intelligence to
automate internal processes, improve core customer service, optimize pricing an d costs, make
resource utilization more efficient, and increase data utilization capacity. This approach
positions artificial intelligence in tourism not only as a technology with a "customer face" but
also as an internal decision-making and process improvement mechanism (OECD, 2024: 5).
López-Naranjo et al. similarly state that artificial intelligence technologies have found
widespread application in the areas of booking, customer service, and strategic decision -
making. It is stated that natural language processing and deep learning -based systems enable
the simultaneous automation of routine interactions and advanced data analysis. This is
considered particularly important in terms of operational flexibility and scalability in the
tourism industry, known for its labor-intensive nature (López-Naranjo et al., 2025: 1, 9).
Florido-Benítez and del Alcázar Martínez's assessments also highlight the functions of artificial
intelligence in managing sales information, revenue management, reducing error predictions,
streamlining operations, and lowering marketing costs. Here, unlike classic digitalization tools,
artificial intelligence offers the capacity to not only store data but also process it and transform
it into recommendations. Therefore, for tourism businesses, artificial intelligence can be
considered not merely as digital ization but as an indicator of a transition to the "intelligent
automation" phase. (Florido-Benítez & del Alcázar Martínez, 2024: 2, 14).

terms of reducing overcrowding pressure. AI -powered analytics is becoming a tool for
destination management that not only measures demand but also manages demand distribution
(OECD, 2024:. 5, 22).
The same axis is strongly visible in academic studies as well. López -Naranjo et al. state that
classification, optimization, neural networks, and machine learning -based models are
increasingly used in the field of tourism; these are prominent in customer s egmentation, data
classification, and decision support processes. In the study, models such as support vector

3.5. Smart Destination Marketing and Content Production are Becoming More Prominent
One of the most visible effects of artificial intelligence in the context of smart destinations is
the transformation of marketing communication into a data -driven and productive structure.
Florido-Benítez and del Alcázar Martínez demonstrate that artificial intelligence is being used
in smart tourist destinations to develop new marketing strategies, improve tourist experience,
and increase investment attractiveness. In particular, ChatGPT, chatbots, and travel planning
platforms offer a new communication i nfrastructure in terms of promotional texts, digital

3.6. The Discourse on Sustainability and Inclusion is Gaining Strength
In almost all of the international reports examined, artificial intelligence is presented not only
as a tool for generating efficiency or commercial success, but also in relation to sustainable
tourism goals. The OECD report argues that artificial intellig ence can support sustainable
tourism practices through energy use, waste reduction, skill -based workforce allocation, and
optimization of tourist flows. This assessment shows that technological innovation and
environmental and social goals are being integr ated into the same policy document (OECD,
2024: 5).
In the same report, topics such as a more balanced distribution of tourist flows, reducing
pressure on popular areas, and a fairer sharing of benefits for local communities are linked to
the sustainability capacity of artificial intelligence. However, the OECD also states that this
positive potential is conditional on continuous evaluation in line with environmental and social
sustainability goals. In other words, artificial intelligence is not automatically sustainable;
sustainability depends on how the technology is governed. (OECD, 2024: 5: 22).

At this point, the results of the study should be interpreted within a clearly defined boundary.

offering an analytical framework that can guid e future empirical studies, policy discussions,

4. Discussion
When the content analysis findings are considered together, it can be said that AI trends in travel
and tourism are concentrated in six main axes: personalization, operational efficiency,
forecasting and flow management, smart destination marketing, sustainability, and responsible
governance. However, there is not a simple linear progression between these axes, but strong
tensions. For example, personalization requires more data, while this increases privacy and
security risks. Automation reduces costs, but  it can put pressure on the human nature of the
service and the employment structure. Tourist flow management can provide environmental
benefits, while algorithmic routing can also produce an invisible power relationship. Therefore,
AI in tourism should be read not merely as a sum of opportunities, but as a transformative area
where multiple interests and risks must be managed together. (OECD, 2024: 5, 22; Sousa et al.,
2024: 20).

discussion to sources within the tourism research area. Content analysis, systematic review, and

5. Conclusion
This study concludes that AI trends in travel and tourism should be understood as a bounded
but strategically important transformation field. The main contribution of the article is not to
repeat the general claim that AI changes tourism, but to show how t his change is framed
differently in international reports and academic publications. International reports mostly
emphasize policy alignment, sustainability, competitiveness, and institutional readiness,
whereas academic studies provide more detailed insig ht into tourist experience, smart
destination marketing, automation, personalization, and user-related concerns.
The findings indicate that AI -based transformation in tourism is concentrated around
personalization, forecasting, operational efficiency, smart destination management, generative
content production, and responsible governance. However, these trends should  not be
interpreted as universally applicable or risk -free solutions. Their effectiveness depends on the
digital maturity of institutions, the quality of data governance, the protection of tourists' privacy,
the development of employee skills, and the capa city of destinations to integrate AI into
sustainable and inclusive tourism policies.
Accordingly, the article's results are limited to the thematic interpretation of selected
international reports and academic publications. This limitation also defines the direction of
future research. Further studies may test these themes through field re search with tourists,
tourism employees, destination management organizations, and small and medium -sized
tourism enterprises. Such empirical work would make it possible to evaluate whether the
opportunities emphasized in policy documents correspond to actual sectoral practices and user
experiences.
The added academic sources also show that future research should move beyond general
statements about AI and tourism and focus on more clearly delimited problems such as AI -
supported forecasting, tourist decision -making, service automation, sustainable des tination
governance, rural tourism, employment effects, and data ethics. This delimitation strengthens
the article's contribution by positioning its results as a thematic synthesis that can guide
subsequent empirical, bibliometric, or policy -oriented studies rather than as a comprehensive
measurement of the entire AI tourism ecosystem (Erdős et al., 2025, pp. 1254 -1257; Wang et
al., 2025, pp. 1-3; Sánchez-Sánchez et al., 2026, pp. 676-678).
