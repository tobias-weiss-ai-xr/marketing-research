Mass Communication and Public Relations Department, College of Communication and Media

Grounded in Uses and Gratifications Theory (UGT), the Stimulus–Organism–
Response (S-O-R) Model, and the Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM), this
study examines how social media use is associated with purchasing behavior
among Bahraini consumers across five decision-journey stages: Need
Recognition, Information Search, Evaluation of Alternatives, Purchase
Decision, and Post-Purchase Behavior. A cross-sectional survey was
administered to 384 active social media users; data were analyzed with
Pearson correlations, one-way ANOVA (Tukey HSD), and independent-samples
t-tests (IBM SPSS v. 27; Cronbach’s α = 0.87). Social media influence was
strongest at Evaluation of Alternatives (M = 3.54, SD = 0.98; r = .487, p < .001)
and weakest at Post-Purchase Behavior (M = 2.48; r = .241, p < .001), revealing
a “silent consumption” asymmetry; all five stages were significant (H1
supported). Instagram dominated platform usage (53.3%; M = 4.21) as the
primary cognitive-evaluative tool, while TikTok showed a disproportionate
influence-to-usage ratio (M = 3.85, 17.5%) via shoppertainment mechanisms.
One-Way ANOVA confirmed significant between-platform differences, F(5,
378) = 12.91, p < .001, η2 = .146 (H2 supported). No significant differences were
found across gender (p = 0.963), age ( p = 0.093), education (p = 0.708), or
income (p = 0.328)—all with negligible-to-small effect sizes—consistent with
a “demographic convergence” pattern that may plausibly be linked to
algorithmic personalization, though the mechanism itself was not directly
measured. Findings advance stage-differentiated consumer behavior theory
and offer actionable implications for social commerce strategy, influencer
policy, and GCC digital advertising regulation.

1 Introduction
The early twenty-first century has brought a fundamental shift in how people
communicate and how economies operate (Kotler et al., 2021). Whereas earlier social
media sites were mere connective tools between individuals, the current reality has
seen the development of significant commercial social media sites which greatly impact
conventional business dynamics (Abdalla et al., 2025; Wan et al., 2025). The
convergence of peer interaction, content creation, and commercial transactions within
these platforms has given rise to what is termed ’social commerce’ – an environment

where interaction, content, and commerce are in perfect harmony
(Hajli, 2015; Liang and Turban, 2011).
It follows that the process would begin with need recognition
through content recommendations and algorithms; information
search would occur in branded pages and threads; alternative
evaluation occurs in terms of social validation cues; purchase
decisions occur within the context of social commerce sites; and
finally, the post-purchase process involves review, comment,
feedback, and electronic word-of-mouth (Cheung and Thadani,
2012; Kothari et al., 2025; Sokolova and Kefi, 2020; Zhang and
Li, 2025).
Such challenges however take on greater importance in the case
of Bahrain. It should be noted that the GCC country displays
consistent high levels of social media penetration on a per-capita
basis. Specifically, Bahrain holds a social media penetration of
97.4% ranking it fourth among countries globally as far as social
media penetration is concerned (DataReportal, 2024), with social
media sites such as Instagram, Snapchat, and Tiktok forming part
of everyday life in the country. Nevertheless, little research has
been done to examine the social media influence on consumer
purchase decisions in Bahrain during various phases of the
process. This is particularly important for the implications it poses
to marketers in developing consumer content campaigns based on
consumer data, the government in regulating the process, and
academics in understanding the differences that exist between the
GCC economies and Western ones. This research helps fill this gap
by providing an empirical understanding of the influence of social
media on consumer purchase behavior in Bahrain.
This study makes three distinct contributions to knowledge.
Firstly, it provides an initial stage-differentiated analysis of social
media influence on all five phases of consumer decision journey
in Bahrain, helping to close the gap created by the absence of
similar GCC literature on this matter. Secondly, whereas
Mohammed (2025) studies age alone as the demographic
variable affecting consumer decision making in Bahrain, the
present study examines age, gender, income, and education
simultaneously for the first time in Bahrain, creating the first
multi-demographic convergence analysis of the GCC. Lastly, the
study seeks to create a conceptual framework for demographic
convergence, incorporating the algorithmic personalization and
adding to existing theory in terms of the S-O-R model and
ELM. The reason is simple; the exceptionally high social media
penetration (97.4%) of Bahrain makes it an excellent candidate
for such study (DataReportal, 2024).
There is an existing knowledge gap regarding the influence of
social media on consumer behavior when it comes to making
purchases and the particularities of the situation in the Bahraini
market. The high reach of the social media platforms in no way
guarantees that the reactions of the local consumers would be
predictable as they could vary depending on the values,
economic system, and digital competence of different
generations. In addition to that, the growing prominence of the
mechanisms of digital marketing creates uncertainty as to
whether social media platforms are used more for thoughtful
decision-making or impulsive spending.
• To determine the relationship between social media content
exposure and purchase intention and impulse buying
among consumers in Bahrain.
• To identify the social media platforms most frequently used
and most influential in shaping Bahraini consumer
purchase decisions.
• To examine differences in purchasing behavior attributable
to gender, age, educational level, and household income.
2 Background and theoretical
framework
2.1 Social media as a commercial
ecosystem
Social media is defined as applications that operate via the
internet where users create their own profiles, establish
relationships, and communicate with others through engaging
content (Kaplan and Haenlein, 2010). The transformation of
social media into businesses was accompanied by a transition
from old-school non-interactive broadcast advertisements
towards interactive, algorithm-driven business-to-consumer
communications (Kothari et al., 2025; Kotler et al., 2021).
According to Zhang and Li (2025), social media ads play a
direct role in the consumer’s decision-making process
concerning purchasing products by offering necessary
information and emotional engagement. Social commerce can
be described as a new type of business activities that emerge
from the combination of social media communication and
online shopping tools (Liang and Turban, 2011). In their paper,
they described social commerce as a unique form of e-
commerce that makes use of the power of social networking for
purchase decisions. In his groundbreaking study, Hajli (2015)
used the constructs of social commerce and revealed that all
three factors – social support, ratings and reviews, and referrals
had a significant effect on the intention to purchase, and social
support played the most influential role among them.
2.2 Theoretical frameworks
2.2.1 Uses and gratifications theory (UGT)
Uses and Gratifications Theory (UGT) originally suggested in
studies on mass communication implies that an audience consists
of goal-oriented individuals who actively seek specific media
forms for their needs in cognition, affect, and social interactions
(Katz et al., 1974). Extending UGT to digital consumerism,
prior descriptive accounts characterize Instagram as oriented
toward aesthetic and hedonic gratifications, TikTok toward
entertainment gratifications delivered through short-form video,
and Snapchat toward immediacy and parasocial intimacy with
influencers. These platform-gratification pairings are conceptual
characterizations drawn from the broader UGT literature rather
than classifications empirically tested within the present study,
and they are treated here as background assumptions that
motivate, but are not directly verified by, the analyses that follow.
In accordance with the findings reported by Wan et al. (2025),
involvement in social media, which refers to making friends, self-
exposure, and pleasure, has a positive impact on the intention of
consumers to purchase something through self-disclosure

2.2.2 The stimulus–organism–response (S-O-R)
model
The stimulus-oriented model presented by Mehrabian and
Russell (1974) suggests that consumer behavior consists of three
steps, including Stimulus (S), Organism (O) and Responses (R).
In the context of social media, consumers’ behavior is
influenced by means of digital stimuli such as advertising,
influencers, video trends, etc. and hence, their emotional
processes (Abdalla et al., 2025; Kothari et al., 2025). For
example, the analysis of 359 Saudi consumers, conducted by
Abdalla et al. (2025), showed that social media trends, as well as
the quality of information disseminated via social media,
influence positively consumer behavior, which can be seen as an
example of the influence of Stimulus (the use of trends in social
media) on Response (consumer buying behavior). At the same
time, according to Bui et al. (2025), the S-O-R model has been
empirically proven in the field of visual electronic word-of-
mouth communication and showed the positive impact of
informativeness and entertainment of visual social media
content (Stimulus) on online purchase intention (Response). As
a result of structural equation modeling, it was found that
Stimulus influences Responses more successfully if content is
perceived visually than textually, which can be a reason why
Instagram and TikTok have achieved great popularity within the
current Bahraini environment. Therefore, it can be concluded
that the extension of the S-O-R model described initially as an
approach to influencing consumer behavior in retail stores
(Mehrabian and Russell, 1974) should take into account not
only the Stimulus but also its nature in social commerce
environment. The major difference between Stimulus in retail
and social commerce environments is the algorithmic selection
and optimization of stimulus in the latter. In this regard, the
extension of the model proposed can be considered extremely
important in the case of Bahrain due to high platform
penetration. It should be noted, however, that the present study
operationalizes the S-O-R sequence only at its Stimulus
(platform-specific content exposure) and Response (stage-level
purchasing outcomes) endpoints; the Organism component –
the internal cognitive and affective processing that theoretically
mediates the two – is not directly measured and is instead
inferred from the pattern of Stimulus-Response associations.
This is acknowledged as a limitation of the present design in
Section 7.
2.2.3 The elaboration likelihood model (ELM)
The Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM), developed by Petty
and Cacioppo (1986), describes two routes of attitudinal change
and behavior influencing mechanisms: the central route is
characterized by elaborate processing of the arguments
contained within a particular message; while in the case of the
peripheral route, contextual cues like attractiveness of the
source, visual aesthetics, and elements of social proof are used
to trigger attitudes without requiring any effort at all. The
model can be easily applied to illustrate two main findings of
the current research. First, ELM provides a theoretical basis for
anticipating that platforms built around fast-paced, high-social-
proof video content could generate purchase influence
disproportionate to their usage share, since peripheral-route
processing does not require sustained deliberate engagement;
whether this pattern holds for TikTok in the present sample is
examined empirically in Section 5.3. This phenomenon is
explained by the activation of the peripheral route due to the
fast pace of video content, the use of background sounds, and
high social proof density, which leads to the generation of
intentions to buy goods or services without requiring further
analysis. Second, ELM offers grounds for predicting that
education level would not moderate susceptibility to platform-
driven persuasion: if social media usage is dominated by
peripheral-route persuasion through commercially driven
content, then individuals with higher education – whose
resistance to persuasion typically operates through central-route
counter-argument – would be expected to benefit comparatively
little from that resistance. This prediction is tested as H3c and
revisited in light of the empirical results in Section 6.
2.3 Integrated framework: UGT, S-O-R, and
ELM in combination
In this current paper, UGT, S-O-R and ELM are combined in
a tri-level model of explanation, which is as follows: (1) The use of
UGT provides an opportunity to comprehend the motivational
level, i.e., the motivations of consumers that explain why they
are motivated to opt for some platform in pursuit of their
cognitive, social and hedonic requirements; (2) The S-O-R
approach describes the level of mechanism, explaining how,
upon entrance into selected platform, commercial message leads
to evaluation and arousal on an organic level, which, in its turn,
triggers certain reactions on the part of consumers through
purchase phases, and (3) finally, ELM explains the level of
processing route, demonstrating the path by which a platform
influences users (analytic/central vs. affective/peripheral),
explaining why visual-intensive media platforms have
overwhelming commercial efficiency and education does not
moderate influence. Figure 1 depicts these three layers as they
map onto the study’s empirical design: the UGT layer
corresponds broadly to RQ2 (platform selection and usage,
tested via H2), the S-O-R layer corresponds to RQ1 (stimulus-
to-response associations across the five purchasing stages, tested
via H1) (Figure 2), and the ELM layer corresponds to RQ3
(processing-route differences reflected in the demographic
moderation tests, H3). It should be emphasized that the
empirical analyses reported in Section 5 test associations and
between-group differences consistent with these three layers
rather than directly measuring the internal mechanisms
(motivational states, organismic processing, or route of
elaboration) that the theories posit; the integrated framework is

2.4 Social media influence across the
consumer decision journey
A growing body of research demonstrates the influence of
social media platforms across all five phases of the consumer
decision-making process (Engel et al., 1968), although to varying
extents. Need recognition can now easily be triggered by
algorithm-generated recommendations of images and videos.
Information gathering is largely done online; according to
numerous scholars, well over 75 percent of buyers browse social
media websites prior to purchase (Zhang and Li, 2025). User
ratings and eWOM communications act as trust signals to
decrease the risk involved in the purchase (Kothari et al., 2025;
Sokolova and Kefi, 2020). Cheung and Thadani (2012) provide
an exhaustive review of existing studies on the effects of eWOM
and found that the level of eWOM informativeness, credibility,
and amount were three major drivers of behavioral change in
consumers, while evaluation of alternatives was identified as the
phase at which eWOM influence tends to peak – a pattern this
study’s stage-level analysis (Section 5.2) also examines. In
George et al.’s (2025) systematic review on the success of
influencer marketing published in Frontiers in Communication,
credibility, trustworthiness, and attractiveness of an influencer

2.5 Short-form video, interactivity, and
purchase behavior
Using the CAB-based chain mediation analysis technique, Li
and Sun (2025) in Frontiers in Communication have established
that perceived interactivity in videos results in intention to
purchase through a series of mediators as empathy and
immersion occur due to interactivity. This literature is
practically relevant to TikTok’s position in the Bahraini market,
where usage share and purchase-influence scores are compared
directly in the Results section (Section 5.3). In particular,
“shoppertainment”, the strategy through which purchasing is
triggered using video entertainment and interactivity in a video
stream, is what is used by TikTok as its main approach to
stimulate purchase behavior (Bernstein and Guo, 2025). As
indicated by Bernstein and Guo (2025) in their choice model,
social commerce platforms stimulate impulsive buying decisions
by optimizing product display and creating attention as well as
social proof.
2.6 Credibility, trust, and authenticity in
social media advertising
Credibility and authenticity have been proven to be important
precursors to effectiveness in social media advertisements. For
instance, according to Kothari et al. (2025), in their study they
found out that credibility and sustainability were significant
determinants of consumer behavior where trust acted as a
partial mediator in the process. Such findings further support
the importance of authentic communication between firms/
brands and consumers (George et al., 2025; Sokolova and Kefi,
2020). Additionally, any relatively high influence attributed to
alternatives’ evaluation could plausibly be explained by the trust
transferred from sources to recipients, which according to
George et al. (2025) is one of the most common theoretical
views used in social media influencer marketing studies; this
stage-level pattern is examined empirically in Section 5.2.
According to the systematic review carried out by Sardar et al.
(2025), there are three important channels through which
influencers’ credibility affects consumer intentions to buy,
namely (a) transfer of expertise in that the uncertainty level of
consumers would decrease due to domain expertise, (b)
trustworthiness in terms of building up a parasocial relationship,
and (c) attractiveness whereby physical attractiveness acts as an
important peripheral ELM cue affecting consumers irrespective
of message strength. In this regard, in Bahrain, influencers are
involved in promoting products through expertise,
trustworthiness, and attractiveness, which offers a theoretical
account for Instagram’s platform-level influence as reported in
Section 5.3.
2.7 Demographic moderators and the
homogenization hypothesis
Classical consumer behavior theory predicts significant
behavioral heterogeneity across demographic groups (Dukes and
Liu, 2023). However, emerging scholarship suggests algorithmic
personalization may be eroding these boundaries. Abdalla et al.
(2025) found no significant age-based differences among Saudi
social media users, and Kannan and Li (2017) theorized that
digital marketing disrupts income-based segmentation through
hyper-personalized delivery independent of socioeconomic
status. While these studies address personalization effects in
isolation, no prior GCC study has simultaneously tested all four
major demographic moderators—gender, age, education, and
income—within a single design. The present study provides the
first such four-variable test in the GCC. Unlike Kannan and Li’s
(2017) personalization thesis, which concerns content-delivery
mechanisms, the “homogenization hypothesis” advanced here is
a behavioral proposition: that algorithmic curation renders
demographic categories non-predictive of commercial
susceptibility, with direct implications for audience segmentation
strategy and consumer protection policy.
2.8 GCC and Bahraini consumer context
The GCC region constitutes an interesting case study for
researching digital consumer behavior due to its distinct
characteristics. With one of the highest global rates of social
media penetration (full statistics in Section 1; DataReportal,
2024), platforms are present in the lives of all generations and
income levels in Bahrain. In a market where Islamic
consumption ethics, collectivist social norms, and income
heterogeneity characteristic of hydrocarbon-based economies
intersect with high-penetration digital infrastructure, purchasing
behavior patterns may differ from those documented in Western
contexts in theoretically meaningful ways. The absence of
comprehensive research on social media’s purchasing influence
at different stages, especially in Bahrain where empirical
evidence is lacking, poses the main research question of this work.
The literature review reveals several consistent ideas: (1) social
media exerts stage-differentiated influence, with effects in the
evaluation stage being significantly more impactful; (2) visual
design and shoppertainment processes mediate marketing
influence irrespective of demographics; and (3) the post-

2.9 Social commerce architecture and the
shortened purchase funnel
The integration of social networking, content publishing, and
transactions in one platform interface that Hajli (2015) refers to
the existence of the so-called “social commerce funnel”, an
abbreviated form of the customer buying process during which
all three steps take place without having the consumer leave the
platform. According to Liang and Turban (2011), this
abbreviated nature is exactly what distinguishes social commerce
from traditional e-commerce: social activities (like sharing,
reviewing, and recommending) and commercial activities (such
as browsing, evaluating, and buying) complement each other.
The significance of this abbreviation lies in its effect on the
amount of cognitive work required by consumers. As stated
previously, on Instagram, for example, consumers can see a
product advertised via Reels, read users’ reviews in the
comments section, compare similar products in influencers’
posts, and make a purchase using in-app checkout—in the same
session. This structural feature leads to the theoretical
expectation that Evaluation of Alternatives would register
comparatively strong social media influence relative to the other
stages, a prediction examined empirically in Section 5.2. If
supported, this would suggest that social commerce platforms
should be designed to prioritize evaluation of alternatives by
providing rich comparison data in the context when it is
needed most.
2.10 Impulse buying and algorithmic
triggers in social commerce
Impulse shopping, where there is an absence of decision-
making prior to the purchase made and whose action happens
as a result of environmental cues, is becoming increasingly
prevalent via social media thanks to the architectural attributes
of these platforms that influence the finite attention span and
emotional aspects of their consumers (Bernstein and Guo,
2025). Scarcity cues, social proof cues, and ambient
entertainment are all used to make a trigger-based system for
making purchases by incorporating them into commercial user
interface design that differs from the traditional offline and
online environments. Thus, Tiktok’s shoppertainment approach
and the product tags available in Instagram’s Stories represent
the architecture of impulse trigger delivery in Bahrainian social
commerce. Yet, this reasoning suggests that Purchase Decision
triggers may exert comparatively less influence than earlier
deliberative stages, with a degree of residual rationality emerging
once the actual purchase is made – a pattern examined
empirically in Section 5.2 and interpreted, if supported, in light
of Abdalla et al.’s (2025) findings on the mediating role of social
media interactions in consumer decisions.
3 Research questions, objectives, and
hypotheses

4 Materials and methods
4.1 Research design
This study involved human participants: data were collected
directly from 384 adult Bahraini social media users via a self-
administered electronic survey. The study adopts a quantitative,
cross-sectional, descriptive-analytical design. A survey
methodology was employed for measuring attitudinal and
behavioral variables across a large, geographically distributed
sample of social media users. The study adheres to the ethical
principles for human subjects’ research, including informed
consent, data anonymization, and voluntary participation.
Ethical clearance was obtained from the University Research
Council at Gulf University, Kingdom of Bahrain. All
participants provided written informed consent prior to
participation. No personally identifiable data were collected or
retained. The study protocol complied with the Declaration of
Helsinki (2013 revision) and applicable Bahraini data
protection regulations.
4.2 Population and sampling
The target population was composed of all Bahraini residents
aged 18 and above who make use of at least one social media
platform for product/service discovery purposes as well as
making informed purchase decisions. Non-probability
convenience sampling was adopted, with an aim of achieving
approximate stratification on several factors including gender,
age range, level of education, and income group. The
questionnaire was distributed electronically through social media
platforms, university and professional networks, and community
groups, using a snowball approach in which initial respondents
were invited to share the survey link further; quota targets by
gender and age band were monitored during collection but not
strictly enforced, which is reflected in the demographic skew
discussed below. A sample size of 384 respondents was obtained
from Yamane’s (1967) formula (confidence interval = 95%;
margin of error = ± 5%) which is adequate for conducting
multivariate statistical analyses (Hair et al., 2019). Post hoc
power analysis using G*Power 3.1 software found sufficient
statistical power for the one-way ANOVA test with k = 6 groups
and N = 384 respondents with power = .99 for detecting a
medium effect size ( f = .25) with α = .05; and Pearson
correlation analysis had power = .96 for detecting a small effect
size (r = .15) with α = .05. Even though convenient sampling
may not guarantee probabilistic generalizability, the current
methodology is widely practiced in investigating consumer
behavior using social media in GCC countries (Abdalla et al.,
2025). Given the lack of a complete sampling frame for Bahraini
social media users, convenience sampling in such a scenario was
justified. However, there were three notable factors concerning
sample attributes which limit the generalizability of the study
findings. To begin with, the current sample favors female
participants (60.9% vs. 39.1%). The proportion of female
respondents is higher than the proportion of female Bahraini
internet users (approximately 50/50). The second limitation
concerns the over-representation of the young generation; 70.8%
of the respondents were below the age of 35. This would lead to
under-estimation of social media influence patterns for the
mature population. Lastly, the lack of a complete sampling
frame prevents any attempt to calculate the coverage error and
design effect for the survey. Overall, the sample represents
young and digitally savvy females in Bahrain who use
social media.
4.3 Instrument
Data were collected via a structured electronic questionnaire in
2025, comprising: (1) demographic characteristics; (2) social
media platform usage ranking; (3) a 9-item scale measuring
social media influence across the five purchasing stages; and (4)
supplementary platform-specific influence items. All attitudinal
items used a five-point Likert scale (1 = Never; 5 = Always).
Content validity was established through expert panel review by
three specialists in marketing communication and digital media.
Construct validity was confirmed via exploratory factor analysis
on pilot data (n = 40), yielding a five-factor structure
corresponding to the five purchasing stages. Internal consistency
reliability: Cronbach’s α = 0.87, exceeding the α ≥ 0.70 threshold
(Nunnally, 1978). Discriminant validity was assessed by
confirming that each factor correlated more strongly with its
intended construct than with other factors; the average inter-
factor correlation was .31, indicating acceptable construct
differentiation (Hair et al., 2019). Stage-level means reported in
Results were computed as unweighted averages of the item(s)
assigned to each subscale (e.g., IS mean = mean of IS1 + IS2; EA
mean = mean of EA1 + EA2). The Need Recognition subscale
comprises a single item (NR1); as a result, subscale-level
reliability (Cronbach’s α) cannot be estimated for this
dimension. For the remaining two-item subscales (Information
Search, Evaluation of Alternatives, Purchase Decision, Post-
Purchase Behavior), only the overall 9-item Cronbach’s α (.87)
is reported here; subscale-level reliability estimates were not
computed for the present analysis and their absence limits
confidence in the measurement structure at the individual-stage
level, given the small pilot sample (n = 40) underlying the
factor solution.

6 Discussion
6.1 Cognitive-evaluative primacy and the
hierarchy of stage effects
The existence of hierarchy of influence in social media
platforms, more evident during Evaluation of Alternatives
(r = .487) and Information Search (r = .453), than Purchase
Decision (r = .374), is arguably the most important result of this
study from a theoretical perspective. This phenomenon differs
from the transactional paradigm regarding social media
influence, as it empirically supports the findings of Wan et al.
(2025) about how social media participation mainly impacts on
purchase intentions in terms of self-disclosure and social
interaction, as opposed to commercial influence. The rationale
provided by the S-O-R approach for explaining this result is
quite simple: organism-based processes, which include
comparative perception, risk management, and credibility
evaluation, become most activated at evaluation time, thereby
making the electronic word-of-mouth stimulus the most
effective. From an applied standpoint, this result has obvious
managerial implications for practitioners who decide to invest
their social media efforts in comparison-based content formats,
since they can expect greater business outcomes than direct
purchase-related materials.
6.2 Silent consumption and the eWOM
structural Gap
The marked disparity between pre-purchase engagement levels
(r = .421–.487) and post-purchase contribution (r = .241)
constitutes empirical evidence of what this study terms “silent
consumption”: consumers are actively influenced by social
media during deliberative stages but rarely reciprocate by
generating post-purchase content. Possible explanations include
privacy concerns associated with Bahraini consumer culture, the
lack of review incentives at the platform level, and satisfaction
imbalance (Sokolova and Kefi, 2020). Indeed, Zhang and
Abdullah (2025) found out that digital marketing content affects
customer loyalty because of emotional commitment
mechanisms, which would drive customers to create post-
purchase content if they existed. Thus, the aforementioned

6.3 Platform differentiation: Instagram’s
dominance and TikTok’s disproportionate
influence
In the case of Instagram (53.3%; M = 4.21), the near monopoly
represents how well the commercial architectural aspect of
marketing blends into the Bahraini consumers’ sense of
aesthetics. As observed in the marked difference in the effect
and actual use of TikTok, the theory provided by Li and Sun
(2025) on the Commercial Architecture of Behavior (CAB)
clearly explains that the use of short video clips may produce
purchase intention among users regardless of the presence of
prior product interest based on the S-O-R stimulation theory.
Based on the ELM’s peripheral route to persuasion, the success
of TikTok in terms of marketing comes from emotions evoked
during consumption of content rather than any logical
reasoning involved in understanding its content.
6.4 Demographic convergence as evidence
of algorithmic dominance
The non-significant results from all four demographic
moderators – gender, age, education, and income – counter an
assumption inherent to demographic-based audience
segmentation. According to classical advertising theory, such
demographic factors should generate significantly different
response patterns ( Dukes and Liu, 2023). However, our results
fail to confirm this assumption for the Bahraini social media
environment. In conjunction with the Saudi findings reported
by Abdalla et al. (2025) regarding non-significant age-based
moderation, both sets of results support a regional trend that
we refer to as “demographic convergence”. One plausible
explanation for this convergence is algorithmic personalizati on:
recommendation engines may provide commercial messages to
users somewhat independently of demographic profile, which
could equalize exposure that would otherwise vary by
demographic group. This explanation is consistent with the
pattern of null results but was not directly tested, since the
study did not measure algorithmic delivery processes; it is
offered here as an interpretation rather than a demonstrated
mechanism. The algorithmic convergence hypothesis does not
necessarily rule out the statistical power hypothesis. Post hoc
power analysis (Section 4.2) shows power ≈ .99 for medium
effects, but ≈ .35 for very small effects ( f < .10). Hence, very
small differences between the groups might have been missed
in the experiment. However, given that null results were
obtained across four theoretically independent demographic
moderators each associated with its own classical prediction, it
seems more plausible to attribute them to the convergence
phenomenon rather than insufficient power on all fronts. Two
other hypotheses, however, merit mention here. One possibility
is sample demographics: the present sample is 60.9% female
and 70.8% under the age of 35, thus making it less diverse on
both counts, especially on the latter one (there are only n = 9
people aged 55 or above). There is good reason to suspect that
the failure of demographic factors to have any effect on the
results is at least partly attributable to limited variation
between groups as opposed to, or in addition to, true
homogenization of algorithms. The second possibility concerns
insufficient sensitivity at the composite scale level: the total
scale average was used as the dependent measure in the
demographic ANOVA analyses. Differences between individual
stages may exist even if the overall composite score stays the
same; thus, an additional ANOVA test of each stage in turn
against four demographic variables is advisable.
6.5 Practical implications for marketers,
regulators, and scholars
This study has implications for three different stakeholders.
First, marketing practitioners are advised to reassign
budget allocation from triggering content for purchase to
deliberative content such as product comparisons, aggregate user
reviews, and influencer demonstrations that communicate
instrumental value. The finding of the interaction between
informational value and entertaining value established by Kim
and Ko (2012) shows that a content strategy focused on
deliberation rather than promotion can boost customer equity
gains. Second, the demonstration by Hollebeek et al. (2014) of
cognitive, affective, and behavioral dimensions of brand
engagement shows that evaluation-stage campaigns that induce
all three outcomes are associated with more stable purchase
intention formation than campaigns limited to affective reactions.
Third, social media commercial influence poses a consumer
protection problem that current regulatory frameworks cannot
resolve effectively because of demographic convergence. Policies
that focus only on protecting the youth, low-income, and less-
educated consumers leave most consumers vulnerable when
social media commercial influence cuts across demographics
equally. Voorveld et al. (2018) found that type of platform
rather than demographic characteristics determines engagement
in social media advertising. While disclosure increases
advertisement recognition on Instagram, as shown by Evans
et al. (2017), we find that there was no effect of education level
on recall, meaning that educated individuals also can be
influenced via peripheral processing routes. Thus, our results
support the conclusion that platform-level policies should
govern social media commercial influence, as Shareef et al.
(2019) found platform architecture as a moderator of advertising
effectiveness. Policy tools based on mandatory labeling of
commercial content, transparency requirements, and platform-

For researchers in the field, the multi-model integrative

7 Conclusions
Among the surveyed sample – predominantly younger and
female relative to Bahrain’s general population (see Section
4.2) – findings show that social media platforms have embedded
themselves as a prominent part of the consumer journey,
shaping purchase-related behavior in differentiated ways across
the five decision-making phases. These conclusions should be
read as characterizing this sample rather than the Bahraini
consumer population as a whole. Social media platforms are
shown to be primarily used as a cognitive evaluation resource
among Bahraini consumers; these platforms help users in
reducing the uncertainties related to their purchasing behavior,
demonstrated by high Pearson correlations for Evaluation of
Alternatives (r = .487) and Information Search (r = .453). Using
the three layers of UGT + S-O-R + ELM theoretical framework,
Instagram was found to be the prominent commercial platform
driven by aesthetic gratification (UGT), visual stimulus response
process (S-O-R) and peripheral route persuasion (ELM). On the
other hand, the strong persuasion capabilities of TikTok were
identified, driven by shoppertainment through peripheral route
processing. Absence of any significant demographic moderating
effects in terms of gender, age, education and income levels – all
with negligible to small effect sizes – offer empirical proof of
demographic convergence.
Implications can be drawn for various stakeholders on
account of this study. Marketing professionals must take
cognizance of the evaluative primacy result, which
recommends investment in content in social media platforms
that allow comparisons such as side-by-side product
evaluations, amplified consumer reviews and influencer
demonstrations instead of direct purchases. In view of the
finding of the silent consumption, there should be an emphasis
on the design of incentive architecture that encourages post-
purchase review processes such as review gamefication and
reward systems to address the eWOM gap and its
consequences. Platform regulators will find the evidence of
need generation, shoppertainment-based impulses and
demographic convergence of consumer vulnerability as
grounds that necessitate considerations of disclosure
requirements and consumer protection measures not covered
by existing GCC frameworks. Finally, communication
researchers will benefit from the three-layer UGT + S-O-
R + ELM framework, stage-differ entiated correlation analysis,
and demographic convergence as advances made towards a
mechanism-based understanding of digital consumer behavior.
Among the limitations identified are the convenience
sampling technique employed that makes generalizations about
the population statistically tenuous, the cross-sectional survey
design that rules out cause-effect inference, self-report bias and
weak statistical power to identify very small demographic
effects. There are four directions for future research: (1)
experimental designs involving random assignment of Bahraini
consumers to condition (Instagram, TikTok or a text-based
control group) for establishing the causal effect of the
differential stimulus-response (S-O-R) prediction; (2)
equivalence testing designs utilizing TOST procedure with pre-
registered minimum threshold effect sizes (d = .20) for
establishing whether the four null demographic results reflect
the truth versus Type II error; (3) cross-national GCC study of
Qatar, Oman and Saudi Arabia comparing demographic
