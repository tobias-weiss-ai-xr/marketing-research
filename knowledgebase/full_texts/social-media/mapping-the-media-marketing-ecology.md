# Mapping the Media-Marketing Ecology

Mapping the Media- Marketing
Ecology
This text examines the ongoing convergence of marketing and media
across industries and formats and offers an authoritative analysis of
branded content and forms of media- marketing integration.
Drawing on the findings of the three-  year Branded Content
Governance Project, this book outlines the expansion of media-
marketing ecologies , from marketers, agencies, and media to include
platforms, creators, and advertising technology (adtech). It charts the
rapid take- up of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning tools,
the role of platforms, and the expansion of actor- types across the cre-
ator economy. The authors define and analyse key concepts including
polycentric governance, governance- in- practice, and ethics, and map
‘problems’ and ‘mitigations’ concerning branded content practices
and governance.
Providing an authoritative taxonomy for branded content adver -
tising forms and formats, this book provides a guide for students as
well as useful reading for researchers and advanced scholars of adver-
tising and marketing communications.

the Branded Content Governance Project 2022–  2025, funded by UK
Research and Innovation.

at the University of Stirling, UK.
Patricia Núñez Gómez is a Professor and Director of the Department
of Applied Communication Sciences at the Complutense University
of Madrid, Spain.

1  Introduction
1.1 About the book
The advertising industry, including the advertising-  supported media
industry, is a dynamic and complicated mix of interacting and
interlocking elements and logics, many of which are hidden from or
poorly understood by the wider public. It has been so for a long time.
Yet the digital media era has added even more multifaceted, complex,
and non- transparent aspects. The complexity includes political eco -
nomic structures, multiplying forms and formats for advertising, and
a fast- changing, internationally varied regulatory environment. Above
all, it includes the acceleration of convergences occurring across
marketing communications and media, across the promotional indus-
tries and the media and communication industries. Our term for this is
media- marketing. The aim of this book is to help readers understand
how the advertising, media, and adtech industries are organised, how
they shape advertising today and in doing so affect our lives.
Mapping the Media-  Marketing Ecology  describes the main
features, actor-  types and processes involved in contemporary
marketing communications. It examines the ongoing convergence
of marketing and media across industrial arrangements, forms, and
formats and seeks to offer an authoritative analysis of branded con -
tent and forms of media-  marketing integration. The book provides a
short, accessible overview of the main elements of communications
at the interface of marketing and media. This includes both mapping
and analysis of advertising technology (adtech), the rapid take-  up of
generative artificial intelligence (GAI) and machine learning tools,
the role of digital platforms and the expansion of actor-  types across
the creator economy. The book also set out key concepts from the

Branded Content Governance Project (BCGP) including an expanded
analysis of polycentric governance, governance-  in- practice, and
ethics which connect the mapping of practitioners and other actors
with rulemaking and rule- shaping processes affecting production, cir-
culation, and consumption. The book outlines the shift from a triad
(marketers, agencies, media) to a sextet (all the above in expanded
form, plus platforms, creators, and adtech). It sets out a mapping of
‘problems’ and remedies (‘mitigations’) concerning branded content
practices and governance, across the domains and production ‘stages’
of system, strategic, operational, dissemination, and societal.
There are various reports, research studies and other documents
that map the ‘ecosystem’ involving digital platforms, media, and
marketing. Our analysis draws on this work but makes a distinctive
offering by mapping the activities and industrial interconnections rele-
vant to the main forms of branded content produced today. In doing
so we aim to provide both a more detailed and granular map than
exists to date and an analytical overview that situates branded content
activities within the media-  marketing ecosystem. In scientific terms,
ecology refers to the study of how organisms interact within their
environments, while ecosystem refers to objects of study, interactions
of living (biotic) and non- living (abiotic) components within specific
environments. We use the nouns ecology and ecosystem interchange-
ably following their usage in sociology and related disciplines to
describe the interaction of people and other components in their envir-
onments. Our book title favours ecology because it better indicates
‘mapping’ and exploring what the interacting components are, rather
than claiming to explain fully how they interact within any specific
(eco)system. Moreover, these components come together in different
configurations in different contexts across countries and regions. We
use the singular ecology/ ecosystem for clarity but also as a preamble
to the much greater task of mapping the specific ecologies as they
change and develop worldwide. This book aims to meet the needs of
students and educators by bringing together what is often fragmentary
into a coherent, usable and insightful overview of key components
that make up contemporary media- marketing ecologies.

1.3 Executive summary
1994– present: from triad to sextet
The media- marketing ecosystem has been transformed over the last
30 years. The core triad of actors from the mass media era— marketers,
marketing agencies and media— remain, in much expanded form, but they
also now coexist in a sextet, alongside significant new actors: platforms,
creators, and adtech. Over the period from the birth of the commercial
internet in 1994 to the present, this more complex ecology has emerged
and continues to expand in dynamic and unpredictable ways.
Payment and control
Within the broader scope of promotional communications, and
communications that include brands, we identify two required
components for marketing communications to be ‘payment’ and
‘control’. Neither component is straightforward, but we argue their
presence delineates marketing communications, and within that
broader category, branded content.
Mapping branded content practices and governance
Branded content, in our broad definition, refers to content that is
funded or produced by brands. Such branded content occurs in an
ever- increasing variety of forms and formats across legacy and digital

Introduction 5
media, across screens, spaces, events and experiences in which a brand
presence can be achieved. We organise branded content according to
the following categories: publishing, audiovisual, audio, digital adver-
tising, social media marketing, outdoor, experiential, other.
We outline an analysis of media- marketing governance that extends
from formal laws and regulation, through self-  regulation to industry
bodies that promote standards (‘industry regulatory organisations’)
and ‘governance-  in- practice’, the ways practitioners engage with
regulations and ethics in their deliberations and creative practices.
Branded content problems and mitigations
We identify four main ‘problem areas’ for branded content: first, con-
sumer identification of advertising (advertising transparency and dis -
closure); second, the impact of integrated advertising on media quality
and integrity (editorial and aesthetic independence); third, marketers’
power to dominate communications, and fourth, the capacity of cre -
ative workers to act ethically.
We make recommendations for branded content governance that
include a comprehensive legal duty for advertising transparency. We
recommend that the key principle that all marketing communications
should be recognisable as such should be established clearly in law
at national and relevant supranational levels. We propose that adver -
tising transparency should be established as a principle in law and as
a duty on all those engaged in ‘paid for’ marketing communications.
We also propose a strengthening of links across the spectrum
from voluntary standards to legal measures that we call integrated
polycentric governance, to better serve training, support, and enforce-
ment in advertising regulation.
1.4 Marketing communications across the media- marketing
ecosystem
According to the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC 2024: 22):
“marketing communications” includes advertising as well as other
techniques, such as promotions, sponsorships, direct marketing,
data- driven marketing and digital marketing communications,
and should be interpreted broadly to mean any communications
produced directly by or on behalf of marketers intended primarily

6 Mapping the Media-Marketing Ecology
to promote products or to influence consumer behaviour. Likewise,
when influencers create content for their own brands or products,
or use affiliate links in their content, such content is marketing
communications.
This helpful definition highlights the range, and expansion, of pro -
motional ‘techniques’ and tools that make up modern marketing. It
can be used to consider the ‘marketing mix’ of forms, and platforms,
of media and other promotional ‘channels’ available to brands and
other marketers today. We share this definition but use the term
media- marketing ecology to include and encompass more profound
and multi-  dimensional changes. In particular, rather than thinking
in terms of new techniques being ‘added’ to the marketing mix, we
seek to highlight how change and convergence across marketing and
media also involves reconfiguration, blurring and uncertainty across
institutional and corporate arrangements, practices, professional/ prac-
titioner identities, and marketing communication forms and formats,
with impacts and implications across the economy, societies, culture,
and education and training at all levels. An expanded understanding
of marketing communications is not only the aggregation of adver -
tising and marketing public relations, as industry practices and edu -
cational disciplines, it is also their ongoing transformation within a
broadening media- marketing ecology. That ecology is currently being
formed in the context of massive shifts in spending and business activ-
ities towards digital and social media as key sites for promotion and
engagement. This makes it tempting to treat the media-  marketing
ecology as a digital one. But this should be resisted. For contemporary
marketers, the ‘marketing mix’ of channels and promotional oppor -
tunities may be dominated by digital but it extends far beyond. The
media channels referred to as ‘traditional’ or ‘legacy’ media, origin -
ating in ‘analogue’ formats, such as publishing, radio, television, and
cinema remain important for marketers wherever large and valuable
audiences assemble and, in global terms, continue to mix but also shift
from analogue to digital. The new marketing channels are overwhelm-
ingly digital, but by no means exclusively so. Amongst the growing
forms of branded content, are live events and ‘experiential’ marketing.
Distribution and shifts in advertising spending
This section provides an overview of the range of marketing
communications and shifts in advertising expenditure and highlights

the importance of considering differences across regions and within
countries in the attractions of media to advertisers and audiences.
Overall, there has been a significant shift in spending to digital
advertising, and to digital-  only platforms and publishers. In 2024,
nine in every ten net dollars entering the advertising market went
to online ‘pure play’ media, online-  only businesses without legacy
media interests ( McDonald 2025). However, ‘digital’ can be a con -
fusing term. The term ‘digital advertising’ is commonly used by
marketers, and in calculating advertising market share, to include
online and mobile promotional activities across ‘channels’ such as
search engines, social media, websites, and apps. Key forms include
Search Engine Marketing (SEM), Display Advertising, Social Media
Marketing, Video Advertising, Email Marketing, and Programmatic
Advertising. This is distinguished from advertising on legacy media
‘channels’ that originated in pre- digital eras, such as print publishing,
television, radio and cinema. However, most of the output from these
media is now ‘digital’, including the advertising they carry, even if
there is also an analogue component, such as a publisher producing a
printed newspaper as well as digital versions and content, or a radio
station available via analogue as well as digital audio.
Spending on legacy media, media institutions that originated in
analogue forms of mass communication such as print publishing
(newspapers, magazines), broadcasting (radio and television) and
cinema, has fallen and is generally in structural, downward decline,
rather than cyclical patterns of growth and decline. However, legacy
media seek to attract digital audiences and advertising revenue and so
the fortunes of each media type is more variable than a simple account
of old media decline versus new media ascendency would suggest.
Further, ‘legacy’ media forms such as linear television, remain sig -
nificant and highly valued channels for brand advertising and spon -
sorship. Linear TV advertising revenues on UK commercial public
service broadcasters’ (ITV , Channel 4, Channel 5), main and portfolio
channels have fallen by nearly £1bn (-  30%) in real terms since 2019
(down £1.5bn, or 39%, since 2014) (Ofcom 2025: 23). Yet the £2.3bn
spent in 2024 shows these remain significant channels. Linear TV was
the world’s third largest single advertising medium with 15% of the
market, or $163bn predicted for 2024 (WARC 2023a).

broadcaster video-  on- demand (BV oD), to compete with digital
streamers’ streaming video- on- demand (SV oD). However, while their

1.5  Branded content definitions
The following section discusses key terms and definitional issues
relevant to media-  marketing as a whole, but with a focus on terms
associated with branded content.
Branded content
A broad term that encompasses brand sponsored editorial content,
native advertising, influencer marketing, and other forms of marketer-
supported communications is branded content. We define branded
content as the practice of marketing by the creation and circulation
of content that is funded or produced by marketers. This refers to
all forms of brand involvement in the production and reception of
communications content and experience.
An obvious limitation of this definition is that it encompasses ‘trad-
itional’ advertising forms, such as display advertisements, television
commercials or internet banner ads, which are all also ‘content that
is funded or produced by marketers’. Other definitions seek to dis -
tinguish between branded content as a category of ‘non-  traditional’
forms of marketing communications. However, any ‘traditional vs.
non- traditional’ division is imprecise, and can be misleading too. For
instance, the term native advertising has come to supplant ‘display
advertising’ even in contexts where the ad formats being discussed,
and transacted, are ‘traditional’ (e.g., banner ads) and ‘interruptive’.
The term ‘content’ is also problematic, as it lacks defined scope and
precision, and has become a much hyped and overused term. ‘Content’
usually refers to informational or entertainment communications
using any form or combination of text, image, audio, and video on
media communications platforms.
We favour the term ‘branded content’ because it is encompassing,
covering the range of existing forms of marketer-  media- platform

Introduction 11
content creation and communication, and is suitable to cover emer -
gent and ever- increasing forms and formats of marketing- media inte-
gration. However, many other terms that we would subsume under our
definition of branded content, continue to have greater salience and
usage in particular contexts of practice arrangements and practitioner
identities and discourses, such as ‘influencer marketing’. Branded
content took institutional form in trade bodies such as the Branded
Content Marketing Association, established in London in 2003 as a
forum for brands to meet producers.
Other general terms for branded content include content marketing,
or consumer content marketing (CCM), the latter described as ‘[p]  aid
marketing messages developed to simulate a news story or enter -
tainment program that is cohesive with the media’s content struc -
ture, including assimilated design that is consistent with the media
platform. Also known as native advertising and custom publishing’
(PQ Media 2018 ). Content marketing involves the use of marketers’
‘owned’ media, including publications, blogs, newsletters, email, and
social media to provide information and entertainment and involves
product or service companies operating in ways associated with media
companies, including producing forms of branded content such as ori-
ginal film series and live shows (Rose 2025).
Some academic accounts suggest that definitions are clear and
that terms such as native advertising are ‘not contested’ ( Bachmann,
Hunziker and Rüedy 2019 : 96), others argue that overlapping terms
are used interchangeable. Neither is wholly accurate. Instead, ‘defin -
itional clarity exists but it is circumscribed, applicable only across spe-
cific contexts of usage by industry practitioners, regulators, academics
and others’ (Hardy 2021a: 868).
Branded content: three main types
The BCGP identifies three main categories of branded content: owned
media; ‘native’ paid content; publisher-  hosted. The first is brands’
‘owned media’, this is the ‘creation of entertaining or informational
media content controlled by brand owners’ ( Hardy 2022: 4, 6). This
is marketers’ own content, appearing on their websites, publications,
and apps, such as brands’ Facebook pages, brand sites on Instagram
and YouTube channels.
The second is the ‘native’ distribution of marketers’ paid con -
tent: ads integrated into web pages, apps, and news feeds in social

media. Much of this is programmatic, part of the increasing automa -
tion of advertising buying, selling, and placement.
The third kind of branded content is material that is hosted by, or
made by, publishers. This includes advertorials in news media and
magazines, advertising-  funded programming (AFP) on broadcast
channels or on streaming and other video- on- demand channels, influ-
encer marketing, and promoted or sponsored posts on social media,
such as Facebook, Twitter, TikTok, Snapchat, and Instagram.
Of these three main types, the first is brands’ own media
(‘owned’); the second and third are forms of paid-  for marketing
communications. The term ‘native advertising’ is used to cover both
the second and third types of branded content and has been defined as
‘paid advertising where the ad matches the form, feel, and function
of the content of the media on which it appears’ ( Native Advertising
Institute 2015 ). Native advertising is increasingly used to describe
digital advertising formats where the marketer exercises control over
the communications. However, we draw an important distinction
between types two and three. In type three, ‘marketers may pay for
content without exercising full editorial control. This is then closer to
practices, and regulatory definitions, of sponsorship than advertising’
(Hardy 2022 : 7). Across both type two and three, it is the blurring
of advertising with editorial, and the confusion about where con -
trol over content lies, that have generated much of the controversy
surrounding branded content.
Native advertising
Marketing company Sharethrough (n.d.) defines native advertising as
‘a form of paid media where the ad experience follows the natural
form and function of the user experience in which it is placed’. Native
Advertising is ‘content that has been designed so it doesn’t look out
of place in the habitat within which it’s being viewed’ ( CAP, 2023).
The term ‘native’ captures the increasing variety of ways in which
advertising is intermingled with content in online, mobile, and social
media. The aim is to get users to engage with advertiser-  sponsored
content as readily as they would non-  sponsored editorial content. In
the context of news and magazine publishing, native advertising refers
to ‘paid advertising content that is designed to look like, and published
alongside, nonpaid editorial content’ ( Wojdynski, 2019, 2026) and
as ‘any paid advertising that takes the specific form and appearance

Introduction 13
of editorial content from the publisher itself’ ( Wojdynski and Evans
2016: 157).
Reviewing the growth of brand sponsored content in US news
publishing in the mid-  2010s, Wojdynski (2016) identifies sponsored
content, partner content, advertorials, and branded journalism, as
among the key terms used, but argues native advertising became
the most widely adopted. He suggests this is because the two words
convey its inclusion criteria: messages that are ‘native’ to non-  paid
content, while at the same time being paid ‘advertising’. It is also
important to recognise different contexts in which native advertising
is used.
For instance, Ferrer- Conill (2016 : 905) defines native adver -
tising as ‘a form of paid content marketing, where the commercial
content is delivered adopting the form and function of editorial con -
tent with the attempt to recreate the user experience of reading news
instead of advertising content’. This captures features relevant to
news publishing, although for many newspapers/ news brands, brand-
sponsored content appears in sections on health, beauty, travel, and
leisure, in lifestyle journalism ( Hanusch, Banjac and Maares 2020 )
and service journalism, and so involves reader’s experience of ‘non-
news’ content.
The term native advertising emerged in the digital era but is used to
refer to brand sponsored content in publishing (distributed in digital
but also print formats) as well as digital (only) advertising on websites,
mobile apps, and social media. In social media, native advertising
includes sponsored posts that appear alongside other posts in your
feed, so- called ‘in feed’ advertising. However, the core claim of being
‘native’, of brand content that fits or blends in, is itself promotional,
and so definitions that represent it as a putatively neutral description
are problematic, at least as a foundation for research.
Sponsored editorial content
‘Sponsored editorial content is material with similar qualities and
format to content that is typically published on a platform or by a
content provider, but which is paid for by a third party’ ( Hardy
2021a: 865; Hardy 2023b). Sponsored editorial content encompasses
both editorial- like material that is paid for and controlled by the mar -
keter/ source and brand ‘sponsored’ but publisher-  controlled editorial
that falls outside those definitions.

Advertorial
An advertorial is a paid advertisement that is designed to appear like
an editorial article in a newspaper/ newsbrand, magazine, online publi-
cation or other publisher site. For marketing professionals, the drive to
establish positive associations with novel practices, disassociated from
the old, discredited ones, is evident in the rearticulation of advertorial.
Advertorial is a portmanteau term describing advertisements that
appear as publishers’ editorial content (Cameron and Ju- Pak 2000). In
common use since the 1950s, the term is associated with advertising-
as- article in magazines, and to a lesser extent, news publications.
While contexts vary, the term is still used by publishers to label con -
tent, including to meet legal requirements to identify advertising
material for users, but has been superseded by the term native adver -
tising within industry discourses, with the latter encompasses digital
production and evolving formats. Yet the term native advertising is
used to enact a set of claims for a transition from old to new, that
need to be interrogated, not merely endorsed. Sonderman and Tran
(2013) state:
Advertorials seek to present advertising as editorial content to
convey claims and messages the reader wouldn’t otherwise find
credible. By contrast, sponsored content (done well) is properly
labelled and clearly associates the brand with the content— the goal
is to have the reader know and appreciate the brand’s involvement,
not to hide it.
Such differences are rhetorically constructed, but inaccurate and
misleading as descriptions of either past or present practices. Fulgoni,
Pettit and Lipsman (2017 : 362) adopt a similar schema, describing
advertorials as ‘interruptive to the print experience and [which] can be
seen as misleading readers by offering funded content that masquerades
as editorial’. A more persuasive argument is that advertorials are
associated with marketer- supplied material (content that is produced,
paid for and ‘controlled’ by marketers) rather than content produced
by the publisher, or co-  produced between marketer and publisher
(Sirrah 2019; Apostol 2020). As we discuss further, the creation by
media publishers of in-  house units to co-  develop brand sponsored
content/ ’native advertising’, rather than prepare brand’s own content
as ‘advertorial’ features, was a ‘new’ and rapidly expanding practice

continued use of ‘advertorial’ by publishers to cover diverse kinds of
publisher- hosted sponsored content, from advertiser- supplied to pub-

Definitional issues
Definitions are integral to the exercising of regulatory authority,
making formal legal and regulatory documentation arguably the most
authoritative sources, followed by the codes and statements of self-
regulatory agencies, professional associations, and industry trade
bodies that are components of broader governance arrangements.
Legislation, such as European Union Directives or national laws,
often include definitions for the scope of activities subject to specific
laws. Definitions are also a fascinating site of discursive struggles to
persuade various stakeholder interests, and to shape and order the way
practices are understood, valued, and governed.
There are then a range of terms used, and definitional inexactitude.
It is vital to recognise, and analyse, the discursive efforts to detoxify
‘branded content’ across industry and some academic discourses,
including to serve processes of camouflage that make up the phe -
nomena of ‘native’ advertising itself ( Serazio 2021a , 2021b). The
problems of definition are compounded when we extend to consider
both the terms in use in different languages and contexts. In some
cases, English language terms are used but in localised contexts of
practice, and regulation, that differ. Such differences may then be
missed or misrecognised by some of those involved with practices,
or by researchers. These semantic and geocultural issues are among
the challenges for comparative research discussed in the BCGP’s 32-
country analysis of branded content regulation ( Hardy et al 2025a ).
We suggest that the best approach is to identify and differentiate the
components in industry practices, and to engage reflexively and critic-
ally with the terms used in relevant practice and governance discourses
in specific media system contexts. Key differential features include
advertising placement type, content type, content location, and pub -
lisher type. All these are subject to formal, informal, and increasingly

Payment and control
We define marketing communications as communications intended
to serve promotional purposes that involve payment and control by
marketers (Hardy 2023a). All such content involves payment, or other
economic consideration, by a party other than the publisher, except where
the marketer and ‘publisher’ are the same, as in brands’ ‘owned media’.
Control is not axiomatic, rather it is precisely at issue in assessing
the outcome of payment. Designated advertising –   paid media—
is ‘controlled communication’ by marketers: the marketer pays to
exercise certain kinds of control, such as over the presentation and
placement of its material, in exchange for payment. Earned media is
content supplied without payment, with control over usage lying with
the publisher, rather than the marketer. However, such demarcations
are increasingly blurred and hybridised by many factors beyond
branded content, including the transactional relationships and inter -
dependencies between sources of content (such as marketers) and
content publishers. This makes the examination of ‘control’ a matter
of some complexity and contingency, as the precise forms and extent
of ‘control’, can vary across creative executions and contexts; con -
trol is ‘less a description than an entry- point for investigation’ (Hardy
2023a: 82).
