ABSTRACT :
The Chinese online expression 'shang'an' literally means arriving at the shore. Now it is widely used to refer to passing examinations, gaining admission to
graduate school, obtaining certificates, or securing an ideal job. This article discusses how the metaphor turns ordinary changes in education and professional life
into a moral narrative based on the opposition between safety and failure. This article explores how this narrative forms anxiety-based persuasion in social media
education marketing, and what kind of responsibilities content creators, education businesses and platforms should bear. This study adopts a focused critical
literature review, and selects an anonymized Douyin public post as an analysis case. The post criticizes advertising rhetoric such as "ashore class" and all kinds of
marketing campaigns that promise to help users 'shang'an'. The research combines upward social comparison, fear appeal, persuasion knowledge, platform
structure, and social media marketing ethics, and uses the Potter Box to carry out ethical assessment. The study found that the expression 'shang'an' itself is not
unethical; ethical risks occur when commercial communicators shape a single result as the only safe and decent life path, conceal persuasion intentions,
exaggerate risks, or rely on algorithms to repeatedly push similar content and amplify the psychological vulnerability of the audience. This kind of
communication may narrow people's perceived choices and transfer social pressure to individuals. Responsible communication practice requires clear disclosure
of information, moderate publicity and advocacy, and recognition of the diversity of life paths. At the same time, the platform should introduce measures to curb
deceptive advertising and reduce the repeated amplification of anxiety.
Keywords: media ethics; shang'an; anxiety marketing; Douyin; social media manipulation; education marketing; platform responsibility
1 Introduction
In the context of Chinese social media, 'shang'an' has moved beyond the literal meaning of moving from water to land, and has been widely used to
describe situations such as examinations, postgraduate admission, civil-service interview, professional certification, successful employment, etc.
Successful people are said to have 'reached the shore', while those still preparing for an examination remain 'in the water'. This metaphor is condensed
and emotionally powerful. It sets a clear endpoint for the uncertain struggle process, but at the same time, it also depicts the unsuccessful stage of life as
a state of danger, struggle and regret.
When this expression appears in the content of commercial promotion, the issue of media ethics is highlighted. Educational and vocational training
institutions sell courses, tutoring services, application guidance, and exam preparation training with the promise of "going ashore". Social media
creators will also repeatedly use similar expressions in inspirational copywriting, success stories and promotional content. This kind of content is not
only about introducing products and services, but also defining what a safe, decent and successful life is, and implies that delay or failure in life is an
unacceptable result for individuals. In the environment of fierce competition in education and employment, this narrative framework will make it easier
for anxious audiences to accept commercial persuasive messages.
Existing research provides a theoretical foundation for this discussion. Research on Chinese youth groups shows that the high-frequency use of
social media and upward social comparison are related to employment anxiety and social anxiety (Jin et al., 2024; Xu & Li, 2024). Research on fear
appeal explains how threat-based information changes people's attitudes and behaviours; when the audience is in a vulnerable situation and the threat is
deliberately exaggerated, it will cause ethical controversy (Hastings et al., 2004; Tannenbaum et al., 2015). Research in the fields of social media
marketing ethics, persuasive knowledge, and attention economy points out that the platform structure will blur business intentions and prefer more
emotionally inflammatory content (Bhargava & Velasquez, 2021; Granstedt, 2024; Voorveld et al., 2024). However, the existing literature rarely
focuses on the Chinese online 'shang'an' narrative or brings the above theories together.
This article addresses this research gap by examining the topic as a form of possible social media manipulation. This article does not assume that
every use of 'shang'an' is manipulative, nor does it assume that all educational institutions engage in ethical misconduct; instead, it focuses on analyzing
under what conditions the metaphor will evolve into a tool that relies on anxiety to achieve persuasion. This study revolves around three core issues:
(1) How does the 'shang'an' narrative define success and failure?
(2) How does it amplify anxiety and serve educational marketing?
(3) What are the ethical responsibilities of content creators, educational institutions and social media platforms?
The core argument of this article is that the ethical problem does not lie in the metaphor itself, but in the consequences of the deterministic

2 Literature and Conceptual Background
2.1 'Shang'an' as a Social Metaphor
Metaphor is not merely a form of language decoration. It uses one kind of life experience to understand another reality and thereby shapes people's
understanding. Zhang (2026) traces the development of the meaning of 'shang'an' and analyses the conceptual logic and cultural foundation behind it. In
the current online usage, the image of water-shore constructs a set of simple narratives: uncertainty is equivalent to being in the water, personal efforts
are equivalent to crossing the river, and institutional or examination success represents reaching a safe shore. This set of metaphors makes the
competitive screening mechanism look like a natural and inescapable objective reality: there are dangerous waters on one side and a difficult crossing
on the other, and there is only one acceptable end point.
This set of narratives obscures the diversity of real life. Being admitted to the civil service is just a career result, not a rescue from danger;
postgraduate study is just an educational choice, not the ultimate proof of personal value; after employment, you will still face new pressure,
responsibilities and life turning points. When the metaphor of "going ashore" is repeatedly spread without restrictions, a temporary examination result
will be regarded as a complete judgment of people; alternatives such as vocational work, entrepreneurship, creative industry, local employment, taking
additional time, etc. may appear inferior or may be ignored.
Sun (2026) carried out a critical discourse analysis of Xiaohongshu's "baoyan anxiety" experience-sharing posts and, using social comparison
theory, points out that language and platform mechanisms can construct baoyan anxiety. Although the formats of Xiaohongshu and Douyin are
different, the underlying mechanism is connected: individual experience is shaped as the standard for judging others; the emotional meaning of success
is jointly created by language content and platform communication.
2.2 Social Comparison and Youth Anxiety
Social media feeds continually present selected success stories: admission letters, offers, high scores in exams, and carefully edited exam
preparation stories. These contents may be true in themselves, but their concentration may distort social perception, making success appear common
and a slower life pace appear abnormal. Tang et al. (2021) proposed that the use of social media by Chinese youth is both supportive and harmful, and
the effect depends on the mode of use, social background and platform interaction.
Jin et al. (2024) found that the use of social media affects the employment anxiety of Chinese youth through upward social comparison; online
social support and self-esteem level will regulate this impact. Xu and Li (2024) also found that the upward social comparison is related to the social
anxiety of college students through relative deprivation and ruminative thinking. These studies cannot directly prove that the word "landing" directly
creates anxiety, but they reveal a possible mechanism: repeated contact with the "success narrative" of peers, which gives rise to social comparison and
a sense of relative weakness, and makes people repeatedly think about their own situation. The metaphor of "landing" further strengthens this
mechanism, rendering the differences in ordinary life situations between people into the opposition of danger and safety.
2.3 Fear Appeals and Anxiety-Based Persuasion
Fear appeals generally consist of two parts: a threat warning and recommended action. The meta-analysis of Tannenbaum et al. (2015) shows that
fear appeals can change attitudes, intentions and behaviours, and are more effective when feasible action plans are given at the same time. Even without
frightening imagery, educational marketing will follow a similar logic: the threat is unemployment, falling behind one's peers, missing exam
opportunities, and disappointing one's family; recommended actions are to buy courses, tutoring packages, and information services.
Marketing effectiveness does not mean that the practice is ethical. Hastings et al. (2004) warned against fear-oriented marketing: it will cause
psychological pain, label individuals, and take advantage of the vulnerability of the audience to achieve business purposes. Braca and Dondio (2023)
further proposed that the persuasion effect is the product of the interaction of communication skills, audience characteristics and information
characteristics. In the context of this article, when propaganda deliberately exaggerates threats, packages consumption as the only way out of trouble,
and targets groups whose anxiety leads to a decline in rational judgment, the message raises ethical concerns.
Persuasion knowledge is equally crucial. When the audience identifies the persuasive purpose of the information, the reaction will be completely
different. Voorveld et al. (2024) pointed out that different users' cognition, acceptance and coping ability of social media advertising algorithms are
very different. Personal success stories, blogger recommendations, and seemingly well-intentioned experience posts may influence users before the
audience recognises their commercial purpose. Using popular rhetoric such as 'shang'an' can make advertising appear to be peer experience sharing and
feel less like advertising.
2.4 Platform Design and Media Responsibility
Social media ethics should not only look at the authenticity of the content of a single post. The platform ranks, repeatedly distributes, and
personalises content. Metzler and Garcia (2024) proposed that the communication results of digital media come from the interaction between social
drivers and algorithm mechanisms. Comparative and emotional content can easily gain high engagement, and the interactive data in turn further
expands the content exposure. Saura et al. (2021) believe that product design that intervenes in user behaviour needs to be evaluated ethically; platform
performance indicators will drive the system to give priority to user interaction, but ignore user welfare.
The perspective of attention economy shows the contradiction more clearly. Bhargava and Velasquez (2021) proposed that when the business
model takes attention as the core and undermines users' right to choose independently, the platform constitutes the exploitation of users. Granstedt
(2024) builds an ethical framework for social media marketing, taking into account the platform environment, multi-stakeholders and ethical standards.
From this perspective, the responsibility is shared by many parties: advertisers output propaganda content, bloggers provide social credibility, and the
platform determines the visibility of the content. Howard (2024) also proposed that content moderation is a moral obligation, not just an optional
business choice. For anxiety marketing, the key is not whether the platform created examination competition, but whether the algorithm amplifies,
disguises, and profits from harmful persuasion.
3 Methodological Approach
This study adopts a focused critical literature review, with an illustrative social media case. A critical literature review is suitable for integrating
knowledge in different fields, reflecting on existing assumptions, and developing an ethical argument, rather than estimating a population effect. Snyder
(2019) proposed that as long as the research purpose, literature screening logic and analysis path are clear, literature review itself can be used as a

research method.
The literature sources came from the academic search engine, publisher databases, and Chinese academic journal databases. The search terms
included "shang'an metaphor", "baoyan anxiety", "Chinese youth employment anxiety", "upward social comparison", "fear appeal ethics", "social
media marketing ethics", "algorithm persuasion knowledge", "platform responsibility". Priority was given to peer-reviewed literature published from
2021 to 2026; older sources were retained for foundational theory and meta-analysis. This article is a focused review, not a systematic exhaustive
review.
The illustrative case comes from a screenshot of a Douyin public post provided by the first author. The post raises the question: why more and
more people use 'shang'an' instead of plain expressions such as "passing the exam", "being admitted" and "finding a job". The post lists several
marketing expressions, including promises by training institutions to help candidates 'go ashore', and argues that the spread of the metaphor shapes
social expectations into the only acceptable life outcome. This case was chosen because it directly connects language, anxiety, platform bloggers and
training institutions; the case is used to explain ethical issues, not to count the popularity of the word on the whole network, and not to represent all
Douyin users.
In order to protect user privacy, the account name, avatar, and original screenshot are not reproduced in the document, and only short expressions
relevant to the analysis are paraphrased or translated. No private information was accessed, the post is regarded as an illustrative case rather than
representative evidence, and no conclusions are drawn about the identity or intention of the creator. The ethical analysis is organised through the Potter
Box, which examines the definition of the situation, values, ethical principles, and loyalties (Christians et al., 2020).
4 Illustrative Analysis of the Douyin Post
4.1 From Description to Moral Destination
The core observation of this post is that plain verbs are replaced by more dramatic result metaphors. Saying that an examinee passed an
examination simply states a result; saying that the examinee 'reached the shore' adds a narrative of distress and rescue, implying that they are in a
dangerous situation before success, and the examination selection brings safety. This set of additional meanings has persuasive force, turning an
achievement into emotional relief.

4.2 Anxiety as a Persuasive Resource
The post suggests that the popularity of the word reflects the deep anxiety of the times. This view comes from one user's interpretation rather than
group-level empirical data; its value lies in showing how an ordinary user recognises the emotional function of the metaphor. "Not on the shore" means
that the situation is unstable, lonely and time is tight, which creates a sense of urgency.
When a communicator first makes the audience feel left behind and then links emotional relief to consumption, anxiety becomes a persuasion tool.
The short-video feed naturally supports this logic by placing the audience's uncertainty beside a blogger's confident suggestions, students' success
testimonials, and limited-time course offers. Even if each content is not fake, the combination may create a strong impression: everyone is moving
forward, there is not much time, and buying courses is a rational choice to avoid failure. Ethical hidden dangers come from the combined narrative, not
from a certain forbidden word.
4.3 The Counter-Narrative in the Post
It is worth noting that this post itself does not sell anxiety, but criticizes the mainstream discourse. The post points out that civil servants are just
one of many professions, postgraduate education is just a qualification, and employment is just a lot of ways of working. It also notes that after reaching
an apparent endpoint, there will be new assessments, pressures and responsibilities. This set of counter-narratives restores a sense of continuity and the
diversity of life paths, and replaces the dramatic "shore" image with objective descriptions.
From the perspective of media ethics, this case shows that audiences are not necessarily passive receivers. Public criticism can expose the narrative
routine and improve the public's knowledge of persuasion. However, critical posts also run in the same attention flow system, and users' spontaneous
criticism alone cannot offset business incentives. This anti-narrative does not mean that most users will reject the 'go ashore' narrative; it is more like an
ethical reflection sample that makes a normalised communication practice visible.
5 Potter Box Ethical Analysis
5.1 Definition
The research context is educational career marketing for social media users who are burdened with exams and employment pressure. The core

International Journal of Research Publication and Reviews, Vol (7), Issue (9), September (2026), Page – 1392-1397 1395
communication practice is to use 'shang'an' and similar successful narratives to grab attention and drive users' actions. Objective facts that require
separate verification include the real pass rate of the institution, the refund restriction clause, whether the blogger receives commercial payment, the
platform content label and recommendation mechanism. The metaphor itself is just a framework tool, not evidence of deception.
5.2 Values
Multiple values conflict with each other: commercial institutions pursue communication effects, business growth, and clear delivery of product
benefits; creators care about content popularity, fan connection, and income; users yearn for hope, effective guidance, independent choice, emotional
health, and truthful and sufficient information; the platform pursues interactive data and revenue, but must also maintain public trust and ensure the
safety of users. Ethical risks occur when the priority of commercial effectiveness overwhelms the accurate context, psychological well-being, and the
free choice of the audience.
5.3 Ethical Principles
Respect for autonomy involves more than the absence of coercion, and the audience needs to fully understand business intentions, limited evidence,
and alternative options. Deliberately creating fear and hiding sponsorship relationships at the same time will undermine rational decision-making.
The principle of non-maleficence requires communicators to avoid foreseeable unnecessary harm. This does not require communicators to soften
difficult employment realities, but communicators should not deliberately amplify the threat and distort ordinary uncertainty into personal defects.
The principle of justice requires attention to unequal vulnerability. Groups with limited economic conditions, lack of family guidance, and repeated
examination failure are more likely to bear the emotional and financial costs brought by exaggerated propaganda.
The principle of truthfulness applies to both literal statements and the narrative framework. Some propaganda is literally true, but the overall
narrative is misleading: institutions can truthfully write that some students pass the exam after class, but only show successful cases, implying that the
course is a decisive factor.
The principle of social responsibility goes beyond legal compliance. Communication should promote a rational public information environment,
instead of taking widespread social anxiety as a raw material for engagement and sales.
5.4 Loyalties and Ethical Judgement
The strongest loyalty should be to the audience as independent decision-makers. Safeguarding the interests of users is not the same as being hostile
to business: the long-term development of business trust also depends on truthful communication. Bloggers are responsible to fans, and fans will regard
personal stories as sincere suggestions; the platform should not only be loyal to advertisers and shareholders, but also to the general public who provide
traffic and data.
The resulting ethical judgement is conditional: it is acceptable to use 'shang'an' as familiar shorthand, humour, or community encouragement can be
ethically acceptable. However, when communicators deliberately compress life choices, conceal commercial relationships, take advantage of high
anxiety, and make unverifiable certainty promises, it constitutes manipulation. When the platform repeatedly targets users who are anxious about exams
and employment, the platform will bear greater responsibility. The reasonable countermeasure is not to block the word, but to strengthen context
description, commercial disclosure, evidence standards, and distribution control.
6 Discussion
6.1 Persuasion Is Not Automatically Manipulation
Educational institutions have the right to introduce services, and students do need exam preparation guidance; emotional language is part of daily
communication. It is not realistic to ban all senses of urgency and motivational expressions through a one-size-fits-all rule. The key to the ethical
boundary is whether the audience has the ability to identify the persuasion relationship and whether the audience has sufficient information to evaluate.
Transparent persuasion will explain the reasons, point out the limitations, and retain multiple choices; manipulative communication will bypass and
weaken people's rational judgment.
The ethics of the 'shang'an' narrative is sensitive because it plays a role in both factual description and identity: it not only describes the result, but
also defines what kind of person is safe, decent, self-disciplined and valuable. This means that a disclaimer alone is far from enough. The advertisement
is marked with an advertising label, but the overall deterministic success-failure narrative will still create psychological pressure. On the other hand, the
frequent use of this rhetoric by non-commercial motivational bloggers may also promote unhealthy social norms. Media responsibility must
comprehensively consider the content, context, commercial relationship and algorithm distribution.
6.2 Individual Harm and Public Consequences
At the individual level, repeatedly receiving anxiety-based narratives may aggravate rumination, shame and impulsive consumption, making people
believe that an exam determines the whole life. At the family level, such messages may drive families to invest irrationally in training with uncertain
benefits. At the public level, they may individualise structural problems. The scarcity of positions, uneven educational resources, and the fierce
screening system have been rewritten as insufficient personal efforts, which can be solved by purchasing guidance.
This kind of individualisation brings about ethical changes: once a person is "still in the water", the narrative implies that the individual's efforts are
insufficient; the persuasive role of commercial institutions and the role of platform distribution are diluted. The responsible media environment allows
the discussion of real competition, but it cannot transform every uncertainty in life into personal defects or business opportunities.
6.3 Platform Amplification and Shared Responsibility
Literature shows that social factors and algorithm mechanisms are interactive and not independent of each other (Metzler & Garcia, 2024). Users
click on the "onshore" content because of cultural resonance; the algorithm captures interactive data and pushes more similar content. This does not
mean that the algorithm deliberately creates anxiety, but it suggests that a platform optimising predicted engagement may continue this pattern. Without
safeguards for user well-being, the system may continue to push highly emotionally similar content to vulnerable audiences.
Shared responsibility does not mean the dilution of responsibility. Advertisers are responsible for publicity and targeted placement; content
bloggers need to do a good job in commercial disclosure and testimonial accuracy; the platform controls recommendation, advertising placement,
complaint system and data permissions, and is most capable of identifying large-scale phenomena that cannot be seen by a single post: including
repeated targeting of anxious users and networks of marketing accounts disguised as non-advertising content.

7 Recommendations for Responsible Communication
First, education businesses: use specific and verifiable descriptions and abandon the absolute narrative of "redemption and ashore". They should
clearly state what the service actually provides, the evidence to support the effect, and which factors are not within the control of the institution. At the
same time, refund promotions should also display their restrictions prominently. Student testimonials cannot imply that the course unilaterally decides
the examination and employment results.
Second, content creators should disclose payment, free services, affiliate benefits, and coordinated promotion in a way that the audience can
understand at a glance. The disclosure hidden at the end of the long copy cannot improve the user's persuasive knowledge. Avoid using a single career
path as a ruler to judge personal value, and distinguish between personal experience and universal advice.
Third, platforms should improve educational advertising labels and establish public advertising sponsorship records. Evaluate whether the
recommendation system excessively exposes anxious commercial content, especially for users who have searched for examination failure,
unemployment, and emotional stress. Set an evidence threshold for quantified success claims, improve the reporting classification of disguised
advertisements, add friction before high-risk purchases, and reduce impulsive repeated consumption.
Fourth, universities, schools, and public institutions should include persuasion awareness in media literacy education. Teach students to identify
threat frameworks, selective success testimony, urgency routines, hidden commercial sponsorship, and algorithmic repetition. The goal is not to
completely deny the training industry, but to help the audience distinguish between effective support and content that treats anxiety as a sales tool.
Fifth, media practitioners should advocate pluralistic narrative language. To celebrate admission and employment, there is no need to put other life
statuses into drowning and suffering. Responsible communication should acknowledge setbacks, diverse career paths, multiple attempts, and changes in
life goals. This will not weaken aspiration, nor will it damage the clarity of commercial expression. It will only restore the growth process of people
more realistically.
8 Limitations and Directions for Future Research
This study has three main limitations. First of all, the study is based on a focused literature review and an illustrative Douyin public post for
conceptual media ethics analysis. The selected post helps to reveal how the metaphor of "shang'an" shapes the results of education and career
development, but it cannot explain how often such narratives appear on Douyin, or how most users interpret it. Secondly, the analysis relies on
screenshots and paraphrased translations. Some features of the original post, such as its audiovisual presentation, comment interaction, and circulation,
were not been systematically examined. Third, this study did not obtain any recommendation data, advertising contracts, creator income records, or the
actual results of promoting educational services. Therefore, the research results point out the potential ethical risks in communication practice rather
than prove the specific intentions of a creator or the causal impact of the platform algorithm.
Future research can systematically select Douyin videos and advertising samples related to examinations, postgraduate admissions and employment
for further analysis. Content analysis can compare commercial and non-commercial posts, disclosed and undisclosed promotions, and different ways of
using threats and success metaphors.
Through interviews or focus group discussions with college students, we can also explore how the audience understands the "Shang'an" narrative,
and whether the narrative affects anxiety, trust or purchasing decisions. Research involving participants or identifiable personal information should
receive institutional ethical approval, and appropriate informed consent, data minimization and anonymization measures should be taken. These
methods will test whether the ethical model found in this case is also applicable to a wider range of social media content.
9 Conclusion
The narrative of "Going ashore" compresses uncertain educational and professional experiences into a narrative movement from danger to safety.
The expression is not unethical in itself. It can carry humor, hope and solidarity. The ethical problem arises when social media marketing uses this
metaphor to amplify unease, conceal the persuasive relationship, promise unrealistic control, and compress the rich and diverse life into the only correct
destination.
Existing literature shows that upward social comparison, fear appeal, limited audience persuasion knowledge, and algorithmic traffic logic will
reinforce each other. The value of the anonymous Douyin case is to present this interactive process intuitively and provide a counter-narrative: exams,
certificates and work are just life events and choices, not the ultimate definition of a person. With the help of the Potter Box, the most defensible ethical
priority is to respect the autonomy of the audience, supported by truthfulness, avoidance of unnecessary harm, justice and social responsibility.
Educational institutions, creators and platforms should support people's aspirations, but refuse to package anxiety as a hidden and excessive persuasion
tool.
