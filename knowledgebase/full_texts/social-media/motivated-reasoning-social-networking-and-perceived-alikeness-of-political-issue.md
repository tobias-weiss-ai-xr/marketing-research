Abstract
Social media (SM) has become a major source of political inform ation in the digital age. Campaigns simplify political
issues into short terms for public communication (e.g., radical terrorism and violent extremism). Differentiation is crucial
to generating perceived value th at motivates behavior. However,  the influx of negative political news over time has
generated cynicism and partisanship. Based on the motivated rea soning theory (MRT), citizens interpret information
through a preconceived filter in the decision-making process. T his lens translates into a belief that policy terms are
approximately the same even though there are meaningful nuanced  differences (e.g., addressing income inequality and
increasing the minimum wage). Moderated mediation analysis evinced social networking interest (SNI) related to greater
perceived alikeness (PA) of issu es. SNI related to greater rigi dity (mediator). Rigidity related to greater PA of issues.
Intellect moderated the relationship between SNI and rigidity. High SNI and high intellect dampened rigidity and thereby
PA of issues. Political marketing implications discuss how to appeal to voters that view issues similarly.
Keywords: motivated reasoning theory, social networking interest, differentiation, perceived alikeness, rigidity, intellect,
social media
1. Introduction
Imagine an average working citizen that has voted over the last  ten years. Living conditions have remained stagnant.
Campaign promises went unfulfille d. Corruption and scandals are  common political news. There are incremental
improvements with some easily overlooked policy wins (e.g., net  neutrality and digital privacy acts). However, these
voters believe the current U.S. political system will fundament ally not change. This becomes a cycle of overstated
campaign messages (e.g., fearmongering) (Nai, 2021) and disappointing outcomes (e.g., legislation blocked by the Senate
filibuster) (Bajwa, 2022). Motivated reason theory (MRT) explains individuals view information through a biased filter
to confirm their preconceived notions (Radnitz, 2022). Relating to politics, MRT applies a cynicism and partisan bias in
which election outcomes make little difference (even though his torically and over the long-term this is not true) (Cotter
et al., 2020). For example, slim majorities often pass state budgets that have substantial priority differences (e.g., education
funding for common core versus funding schools based on standardized testing performance) (Bolton, 2022). While coded
in political messaging, the ability for citizens to grasp long-term consequences of elections and enacted policies are vital
for a healthy democracy. Public debate is part of the development of legislative priorities and policies (commonly viewed
online in the digital era) (Abid e t al., 2023). The purpose of this study is to investigate th e impact of social networking
interest (SNI) on the ability to distinguish between political issues.
Social media (SM) has expanded civic participation and learning opportunities (Baert & Booth, 2012). SM has become a
major source for political communication, engagement (e.g., petitions), and campaign persuasion (e.g., expressing reasons
to win over support) (Abid et al., 2023). Despite a general user aversion to political news, SNSs have enormous amounts
of political content (e.g., TikTok) (Goyanes et al., 2021). However, the constant barrage of politically motivated content
has created disparate policy positions that appear comparatively alike (Bok et al., 2022). So, while citizens may vote, they
are voting for partisan and cynical reasons (e.g., to oppose an other party) (Song et al., 2020). Arguably, rigidity (i.e.,
inflexibility, lack of open-mindedness, and preferred categoric al thinking) is a mechanism behind this pattern of
processing political content (Zmigrod, 2020). This rigidly cate gorizes policies (e.g., good or bad) with often flawed

2. Literature Review and Hypothesis Development
2.1 Motivated Reasoning Theory (MRT) and Politics
MRT explains an individuals’ goals influence their thought proc ess in a biased way to confirm their preferred beliefs
(Cotter et al., 2020). Motivated reasoning (MR) is like viewing information through a lens with preconceived assumptions
(Leeper & Slothuus, 2014). For example, if someone believes vot ing makes no difference in the outcome of elections,
they will view tallied results with this skewed belief. If a winner was decided by dozens of votes or a landslide, they will
interpret their single vote as m eaningless in the outcome of th e election. When individuals have preconceived motives
information is often interpreted to confirm existing beliefs (v ersus objective observations). MRT is particularly relevant
in how voters process political information. One’s MR lens is s haped by norms (e.g., historical defaults) and the
distribution of common resources in democratic nations (e.g., t axes to pay for various public programs and services)
(Groenendyk, 2022). Self-interests often drive individual behaviors and support for public policies (Tummers, 2019). MR
in politics is shaded by political cynicism (Radnitz, 2022). When politicians campaign on promises that are left unfulfilled,
a negative bias forms. A degree of skepticism or cynicism filters political information.
2.2 Social Networking Interest (SNI) and Perceived Alikeness (P A) of Issues
Political cynicism coexists with political news consumption whi ch contemporarily comes from SM (Song et al., 2020).
MR cynicism was observed of recent 2016 and 2020 polling data t hat underestimated Republican turnout for the U.S.
general elections (Lees et al., 2020). V oters expressed distrust in polling data, which related to perceived illegitimacy of
election results (even though other recent polls were accurate and within presented margin of errors). Cynicism crosses
the political spectrum and is based on preconceived notions. Pe rceptions are subjective and based on the observer. This
project investigates MR cynicism that crosses political affiliations on how individuals perceive the alikeness of political
issues. Discerning political policies is fundamental for an edu cated voting populace (Smith  & Tolbert, 2009). Without
discerning complex political issues, the public can be more easily manipulated to vote against their interests. Transmission
of SM information is often short and simplified, requiring low effort to consume (e.g., viewing meme compared to reading
legislation).
MRT helps to explain how individuals navigate complex political media which is coded and crafted to change someone’s
beliefs. Individuals process political information through a lens consistent with their prior beliefs and identity. Skepticism
and distrust seemingly protect someone from potential harm (e.g ., being influenced to vote against their own interests)
but means shortcutting the important thought process on policies that are more beneficial for society (Johnson, 2009). By
approaching political news with MR, it reduces the attention sp an and effort to distinguish campaign messages. One
campaign stating they support green energy production and anoth er claiming they support producing renewable energy
sources is likely to be believed they are relatively the same ( when they can be very different in policy outcomes). With
limited budgets, legislative priorities translates into less/diverted funding (e.g., natural gas production versus hydroelectric
power plants) (Lienert, 2010). We contend simplified cognitive processing (practiced with SM use) increases one’s
likelihood to view political issues alike. This can miss crucial policy differences.
When users consume SM content, they learn to process informatio n quickly to determine if it is worth their attention
(Hämäläinen et al., 2024). SM echo chambers (i.e., multiple sou rces of repeated or corroborating information) reinforce
belief systems (Jost, 2017). When users view SM content support ing a belief, they become more entrenched (Carson et
al., 2025). This narrows a MR lens to assess information first with respect to a narrative. Thereby, political issues coded
in campaign messages appear less meaningful and distinct becaus e it will be implemented by duplicitous politicians
(bought out by special interests) (Radnitz, 2022). One would ex pect the inundation of SM information to create a well-
informed public. However, while the public is cognizant of bits of information, it feeds a partisan and skeptical narrative
that reduces one’s ability to think critically about complex po litical issues (Song et al., 2020). Thus, more SM exposure
is likely to relate to deeming political issues as alike.
H1: SNI will relate to greater PA of domestic, national security, immigration, and election issues.
2.3 SM Influence on Cognitive Rigidity
MR cynicism is a form of calcifying one’s outlook to fit a preconceived narrative (Cotter et al., 2020). This closely aligns
with the psychological construct rigidity. Rigidity is having a narrow-minded belief in one’s opinions as being right and
fixed (Simms et al., 2011). For example, in political marketing, partisan voters tend to believe their political party should
be in power and enact their policies as the correct way to solve societal problems (Johnson, 2009, p. 405). Johnson (2009)
also explained that rigidity can also feed into pessimism in po litics. This appears when politicians are caught lying to
garner support from different competing interest groups. Social networking sites (SNSs) use algorithms to populate users’

2.4 Ease of Public Opinion Persuasion with Cognitive Rigidity
Public opinion persuasion is easier when individuals are cognit ively rigid. Prior research r eviewed how citizens can be
nudged to vote a certain way on ballot measures when there is l ow educational effort (Sunstein, 2015). For example,
policies can be framed as a matter of favoring choice, even tho ugh it means reducing funding for programs used (e.g.,
diverting funds into a new privatized retirement system that will cut the existing U.S. Social Security system) (Diamond,
2002; Svihula & Estes, 2008). Framing and using select words ca n disguise harmful policies into perceived favorable
terms (Blau & Abramovitz, 2010). This manipulative practice can  influence those less willing or unable to expend
cognitive effort into understanding ramifications of their voting decisions.
V oter partisanship and simplification of policies allows politi cians to take rigid ideolo gical positions to signal to
constituents they share their policy positions (e.g., antiterrorism rhetoric with minimum details) (Canes-Wrone & Shotts,
2007). This matches the simplistic thinking of voters that will support representatives that share their beliefs and will do
the necessary complex policymaking for them (Bischof & Senninger, 2018). This cognitive simplification of policy issues
allows campaigns to use rhetorical messaging even if policy details harm constituents (e.g., federal tax cuts, has resulted
in less corporate taxes to fund social programs) (Rabie, 2018, p. 75). Rigidity reduces one’s ability to comprehend
ambiguity and complexity present in politics (Zmigrod, 2020). Z migold (2020) explains that rigidity is linked to
ideological extremism and partisanship through processing infor mation for closure and certainty. A voter will evaluate
policies in contrast to their partisan identity and attachment (Jost, 2017). Thereby, rigid cognitive processing will relate
to lower perceived differentiation among political issues.
H3: Rigidity, as a mediator, will relate to greater PA of domestic, national security, immigration, and
election issues.
2.5 Flexible Intellectual Thinking to Discern Political Details
Intellect refers to one’s verbal comprehension skills and fluid  ability to solve practical problems (Lüftenegger & Chen,
2017). Intellect expands when individuals engage content with o pen-mindedness for cognitive development (versus
drawing from previous routines and assumptions to confirm repea ted patterns) (Wu et al., 2011). This is observing for
novelty and unique patterns within a given situation. Open-mind ed intelligent learning is fluid with a greater ability to
apply new information to generate novel ideas. For example, man y voters watch a political debate with an open mind
looking for new ideas that will solve societal problems (Cormier et al., 2025). Meanwhile, others watch with a MR partisan
lens looking for their favored candidate to score political points. While it may seem like an intelligent activity to watch a
political debate, how information is cognitively processed can greatly differ. Openness to experiences has an intellectual
component that is curious for semantic and abstract information (Dolbier et al., 2024). This sharply contrasts to categorical
thinking related to rigidity that seeks to reaffirm prior knowledge.
In the digital age where much political communication is online , political change will require an advanced ability to
navigate and process information on SM (Abid et al., 2023). How ever, oversimplification is a reductive bias that
negatively effects learning by building cognitive networks that promote consequential and durable misconceptions (Spiro
et al., 2012). Knowledge gaps are filled with assumptions and u nsubstantiated information. This is knowing less and
creating certainty with more subjective information. However, comprehending the impact of abstract concepts is part of
policymaking. Intellectual openness is a crucial part of discer ning political issues as well as wisely participating in the
political process.
Intellectual political intervention is important in advancing p ublic policy debate (Baert & Booth, 2012). Intellectual
contribution deepens discourse and brings innovation that often opposes an adversarial status quo. SNSs are digital public
spheres where users can voice their opinions and engage in poli tical debate (Abid et al., 2023). However, SNSs have
become places to engage with other like-minded individuals and reinforce beliefs (Goyanes et al., 2021). Higher cognitive
ability associated with a greater  ability to discern partisan m isinformation and less likely to share it across the political

3. Method
3.1 Overview of Study and Participants
A U.S. convenience sample was collected using Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) through Qualtrics (survey platform)
during the spring of 2023. High quality MTurk standards was applied towards eligibility criteria to participate in the study.
Participants must have had 100 or more assignments completed with an approval rating of 97% or above (N=992) (Keith
et al., 2017). Bootstrapping procedures and large samples (405 or larger) for path analysis was recommended to produce
satisfactory statistical power (Fritz & MacKinnon, 2007). Males composed 44.9% of the sample (see Table 1). The median
age was 37 (ranging from 18 to 79 years old). The three major p olitical affiliations in the U.S. were represented in the
sample (224 Republicans, 485 Democrats, 283 Independent/other). Identifying as a U.S. citizen that had voted in a past
general election was required to participate in the study. Afte r providing written online consent, participants completed
survey questions and answered demographic questions. Control variables and political party affiliation questions were at
the end of the survey to avoid influencing other survey questions. A diverse national sample was collected as a basis for
generalizability (Levay et al., 2016). Zip code data analysis s howed participants lived throughout the U.S. in urban,
suburban, and rural regions.

Independent variable. SNI measures an individual’s use and enga gement with SM (Sigerson & Cheng, 2018). The
validated measure used 5–items from the Oregon Avocational Interest Scales (e.g., “Participated in an online discussion
group”) (alpha=.69) (Goldberg, 2010) (see Appendix).

Mediator variable. Rigidity measures an individual’s narrow-minded belief in their opinions as being right and fixed using
10–items (e.g., “Am convinced that my way is the best way”) (alpha=.80) (Simms et al., 2011). Rigidity has a quality of
inflexibility, lack of open-mindedness, and preference for categorical thinking.
Moderator variable. Intellect meas ures someone’s self-reported cognitive ability to read and grasp ideas using 7–items
(e.g., “Have a rich vocabulary”) (alpha=.79) (Hogan, 1995). Intellect captures a degree of someone’s cognitive faculties
to learn concepts. In politics, marketing nuance has been used to obfuscate and nudge voters towards a selection (Sunstein,
2015).
Dependent variables. PA of political issues was measured on a 0 % to 100% continuous sliding scale (“Please rank the
degree the two items are the same.”) (Bok et al., 2022). Domest ic issues included seven paired terms (net neutrality and
digital privacy; entitlement benefits and retirement benefits; healthcare and health insurance; mandatory vaccines and
disease prevention; marriage equality and gay marriage; income inequality and minimum wage; and death tax and estate
tax).
National security issues included three paired terms (renewable  resources and green energy; climate change and global
warming; and radical terrorism and violent extremism). Carbon emissions and dependence on foreign oil are viewed as a
threat to national security because of involvement in foreign a ffairs to maintain regional control for this energy source
(Gainous & Merry, 2022). Climate change also poses an existenti al threat to humanity because of extreme weather
conditions that globally impact environments (e.g., drought).
Immigration issues included two paired terms (immigrant detainm ent and border security; and sanctuary cities and
immigration assimilation).
Election issues included three paired terms (electoral college and representative government; lobbyists and special interest
groups; and campaign financing and corporate financing). There was random presentation of the political terms from left
and right. Higher scores indicated a greater alikeness (e.g., 75=75% alike).
Control variables. Control variables included gender, political  party affiliation (Republican, Democrat,
Independent/other), news hours (self-reported average hours per week of read/watched news), and recency voting.

5. Discussion
5.1 General Discussion
Main effect results showed SNI related to greater PA of domestic, national security, immigration, and election issues. This
supports MRT within the context of political marketing. Greater SM consumption evinced a greater blurring of political
issues. This suggests greater cynicism in policy differences an d the ability to government to address issues. This filter
becomes a psychological barrier to communicate important policy nuances that can greatly differ in long-term outcomes.
A ruse is the ability to persuade the public into believing an issue is meaningful to them, when in fact, it can harm the
public. For example, labeling the estate tax as the death tax s uggests it effects everyone that  passes away, whereas it is
inheritance policies that effects  wealthy property owners (Scha ffner & Atkinson, 2009). The reduced tax revenue from
taxing wealthy property owners d ecreases the amount of funds av ailable to serve average citizens (e.g., funding public
schools) (Orgain, 2011). The ability to understand policies and  abstract topics are crucial to having a more competent

electorate, less likely to be manipulated to vote against their own interests. Results imply more SNI decrease one’s ability
to make policy distinctions.
In fact, mediation results showed SNI related to greater rigidi ty. This supports prior research that found more SM
consumption relating to greater rigidity (Proma, 2023). SM cons umption increases one’s set beliefs by reinforcing a
narrative (e.g., media echo chambers) (Jost, 2017). Moreover, through the mediation path, rigidity related to higher PA of
domestic, immigration, and election issues. This implies voters who are more set in their beliefs are less likely to perceive
these political issues as distinct. We attribute this to MR cynicism in the political system to implement meaningful changes
regardless of policy terms. It is perceived as rhetoric to gain support, but when elected into office, it is likely to result in
an unfulfilled promise (Radnitz, 2022). V oters care that their living conditions are improved regardless of the specific
policy. Elections grant power to politicians to hash out the policy details that create tangible positive outcomes (Rahman,
2017). We contend rigidity in political beliefs is a mixture of  having hardened policy positions shaded by a distrust in
government to enact tangible outcomes.
Meanwhile, through the mediation path, rigidity related to lowe r PA of national security issues. This was the opposite
direction of the proposed hypothesis (H3). These results indica te when voters have greater rigidity, they are more likely
to perceive national security issues as different. This suggests voters have a better understanding of national policies and
likely stronger opinions about them. This has potential benefits for society. When there is more debate and media coverage
about a topic, informed voters may be more driven to act on specific impassioned policies (Canes-Wrone & Shotts, 2007).
For example, there is a sizeable U.S. contingency that votes ba sed on environmental conservation because they view
dependence on foreign oil and oil production as a threat to nat ional security (Gainous & Merry, 2022). While counter
movements can form, it brings forth popular policies that can b enefit society (e.g., investments in renewable energy
sources to reduce dependence on fossil fuel). Public debate is part of the U.S. political process where ideas can win over
voters. V oters then elect representatives to ideally enact poli cy demands. Rigorous public debate increases awareness,
potentially forming stronger distinctions in voters’ minds.
5.2 Implications
Moderator results showed low intellect and high SNI, heightened  rigidity and reduced perceived distinctions among
political issues. When someone is less able to grasp abstract c oncepts and ideas, th ey can be particularly influenced by
SM in concretizing their political beliefs. For example, in modern politics, false conspiratorial beliefs are pervasive online
(e.g., COVID-19 vaccine mandates intended to insert microchips to monitor citizens) (Bodner et al., 2020). While
disproven citizens have politically mobilized based on misbeliefs (Carson et al., 2025). Algorithms designed to increase
views for monetarization fosters a misinformed electorate (Zimmer et al., 2019). This manipulates the MR lens citizens
view politics which becomes a warped reality (e.g., the deep state is running the government) (Radnitz, 2022). One would
imagine, if citizens were lied to and learned a source was pedaling lies, they would stop viewing. However, instead, it is
often justified by archetypal narratives related to entrenchmen t (e.g., distrust in government). Intellectual openness to
change one’s misbeliefs could lessen SM misinformation sharing behaviors.
Moreover, results expand understanding of MRT. The practice of viewing information through a biased lens effects long-
term evaluations of politics (Groenendyk, 2022). While there are clear distinctions between political issues, cynicism in
the political process can filter communication from campaigns ( Song et al., 2020). Campaigns need to win over public
support, but if voters are fixed in their political thought pro cess, it makes it more difficult to persuade votes (Carson et
al., 2025). Campaigns are advised to confront biases, so communication is not blunted and reinterpreted as usual gridlock
politics. For instance, marketing ads using phrases like “prove n leader” and “fights on behalf of working Americans”,
emphasizing character traits that bypass the minutiae of policy differences (Luntz, 2007). This suggests regardless of the
specific policy, the problem will be resolved somehow. In the m inds of many citizens, it often does not matter what
specific national security policies are implemented, but that t hey feel secure (Mueller, 2006). Historically, national
security rhetoric has generated votes (e.g., war on terrorism). In practice, this means resolving the underlying pain points
citizens seek government official s to change. Even if it means unconventional strategies, citizens want their issues
addressed. The procedures of how government enacts change are less important to average citizens than the desired policy
benefits.
5.3 Limitations and Future Research
The sample had more Democrats than other party affiliations, which could have potential issues with political party voter
overrepresentation (Gelman & Novoa, 2023). To account for potential sampling bias, analysis included a large sample of
the three major political affiliation in the U.S. (i.e., over 2 00 participants of Democr ats, Republicans, and
Independents/other). When there is adequate sample representation of different groups, there is sufficient statistical power
to compare differences (Columb & Atkinson, 2016). Additionally, 10,000 bootstrapping resampling procedures can help
minimize sampling bias for large representative samples (James et al., 2023). The inclusion of political party affiliations

Furthermore, relevant political issues change over time. Some i ssues dominate a U.S. election cycle, becoming a major
reason driving voters to the polls (Stimson, 2018). For example , specific policies around artificial intelligence (AI) and

to know politicians’ position and if they will enact legislation to better their lives (Rahman, 2017). Some political positions
can become untenable because voters have developed an aversion to them (e.g., “defund the police”) (Craig & Reid,

emerging political issues to exam the application of findings o n specific policy terms (e.g., unlimited financial
contributions). The scope of this project was to exam more nuanced political terms that are less partisan yet still designed
to articulate a distinct policy position. Policy details have m ajor societal implications but are often blurred in
communication when voters perceive information through a biased lens.
6. Conclusion
While SM has broadened the range of beliefs and ways to express users’ viewpoints, exposure skews towards content that
confirms preexisting narratives. MR offers a cognitive shortcut , lowering the effort needed to process political
communication. Campaigns often manipulate this through slogans and simplified messaging suited to typical social media
consumption. Rigidity emerged as a key mechanism linking SM use to greater PA of domestic, immigration, and election
issues. Cultivating intellectual curiosity and deeper comprehension of complex political issues may counter this dynamic.
This could shift public discourse away from partisanship and towards substantive dialogue to address underlying societal
problems.
