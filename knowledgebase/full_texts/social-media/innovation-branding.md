# Innovation Branding

Dr. Itzhak Mashiah (Ph.D.) is a researcher and lecturer with a background in
communication and marketing consulting across the public and private sec -
tors. With an interest in the tech industry, business, and corporate strategy, his
work lies at the intersection of marketing communications, innovation, and
brand management.
This book explores how “innovation” has evolved from a buzzword into a
powerful cultural, rhetorical, and strategic force.
Today, the word is more than a descriptor—it signals how brands, organiza-
tions, and even nations differentiate themselves, justify investments, and proj-
ect credibility and forward momentum. With thousands of mentions and uses
every day, around the world, the motif of “innovation” now appears in reports,
TV and print ads, digital media, organizational strategy plans, presentations,
conferences, and even in the names of companies and brands, making it a
widely recognized and popular idea. Blending theory with practice, Itzhak
Mashiah examines innovation as a communicative strategy that shapes narra-
tives across branding, policymaking, and identity. The book shows how inno-
vation functions as a mechanism of influence within advertising, PR, social
media, and other marketing channels. Drawing on insights from communica-
tion, marketing, management, and strategy, Mashiah traces innovation’s trans-
formation into a persuasive tool that positions people, organizations, and
nations as distinctive, future-oriented, and competitive.
With clear frameworks, global case studies, and practical guidance, this
book offers both a theoretical foundation and actionable insights, making it
essential reading for students and professionals in marketing, communication,
business, strategy, PR, media, and content creation.

Across the globe, and across industries and sectors, the terminology of “inno-
vation” has become nearly ubiquitous. Whether at a startup pitch, a product
launch, or a government policy announcement, the term “innovation” is con -
sistently used to signal value, credibility, and forward momentum. A business
might be strategically described as “innovative”, a national government might

This performative use of innovation is far from incidental. In today’s profes-
sional and public discourse, innovation operates as a powerful marker of rele-
vance and legitimacy. Its appeal lies in its flexibility: it can refer to disruptive
technologies, refined processes, or even a vague promise of improvement. As
such, innovation increasingly serves as a branding device, a shorthand for
progress and potential, often regardless of its actual content.
But how has innovation come to play such a powerful role? Who constructed
it this way, and through what processes did it become a marketing asset? Who
can make use of it, and how might it be implemented as a brand strategy?
What tactics can a brand use to project an image of innovation, and what are
the strengths and limitations of this approach?
This book explores the cultural and rhetorical work that innovation per -
forms. It investigates how innovation is constructed, represented, and circu -
lated through communication and marketing discourse. Rather than focusing
solely on innovation as a technical or organizational process, the emphasis here
is on how innovation is narrated, how it is framed, packaged, and deployed in
ways that shape perception and confer authority.
This is a resource for both academics and practitioners interested in adopt-
ing or understanding a multidisciplinary approach to innovation communica-
tion. The project began in practice, emerging from industry work in which
clients and organizations across various domains consistently sought to associ-
ate themselves with “innovation”. What mattered most was not necessarily
what they were doing, but how effectively they could appear innovative. This
insight led to a deeper, theory-driven academic inquiry into the discursive life
of innovation: how it is expressed across platforms, amplified through media
and marketing, and ultimately used as a form of social and symbolic capital.
Drawing on content analysis, ethnographic fieldwork, and conversations
with communication professionals, this book synthesizes the concept of
Innovation Branding (IB). This framework examines how innovation is com -

constructing legitimacy. It explores how narrative techniques, such as claims of
uniqueness, future-oriented framing, and comparative value, are used to posi-

more as a form of expression. The act of expressing and reiterating innova -

conceptual frameworks with actionable strategies.

synthesized, comprehensive foundation for the concept of Innovation Branding

Chapter 5: The Strategic Model for Innovation Branding
This chapter introduces the C.L.I.P . Model for Innovation Branding, a
research-based framework that combines theoretical insights with practical
applications. The model is built as a blueprint, combining four interrelated
components: Catalyzing, Layering, Institutionalizing, and Promotion.
Catalyzing refers to sparking and sustaining conversations around innovation,
helping to generate momentum and engagement. Layering captures the differ-
ent levels at which innovation is structured and communicated within the
organization. Institutionalizing involves embedding innovation into the orga-
nization’s culture, systems, and strategic direction. Promotion encompasses
both push and pull strategies for effectively distributing innovation messages to
audiences. Together, these components provide a structured approach to build-
ing innovation-driven brands, enabling organizations to enhance credibility,
define their market position, and foster a reputation for ongoing innovation.

while others highlight relationships, policies, heritage and tradition, or narra -

reflects novelty, breakthroughs, advancement, or progress. These stories unfold

constructed play a decisive role in shaping brand perception. Communication

resonate with audiences and support strategic objectives. This chapter dis -

and how such narratives are constructed and communicated across marketing
channels. Within this context, Innovation Branding (IB) emerges as a frame -

The Innovation Image
Innovation has become one of the most powerful and desired attributes of con-
temporary brands. In a fast-moving and highly competitive world, being per -
ceived as innovative is no longer optional; it is increasingly central to strategy,
legitimacy, and growth. From tech startups and universities to governments,
banks, NGOs, cities, nations, and even public figures, a wide range of actors seek
to associate themselves with innovation. This ambition is not limited to what
organizations do internally but extends to how they present themselves externally.
Innovation today is as much a matter of communication as it is of capability.
At the heart of this communication lies perception. Brands are not defined
solely by their actions, but by how those actions are interpreted, remembered,
and discussed by others. Being seen as innovative is therefore not an end, but a
means of achieving broader strategic objectives. An innovation-oriented image
can generate interest, stimulate demand, and confer prestige. For nations, it
can attract investors and multinational companies. For businesses, it can open
new markets and expand customer bases. For universities, it can help recruit
top researchers and students while fostering pride among alumni. Financial
institutions can use it to engage younger and more diverse audiences, NGOs
can build trust and legitimacy, and public figures can position themselves as
modern, capable, and forward-looking. Across these diverse contexts, innova-
tion functions as a powerful reputational resource.
This widespread use of innovation as a reputational signal explains why the
image of innovation has become one of the most sought-after assets of our time.
Innovation messaging is now ubiquitous. Y et this ubiquity raises important ques-
tions. If everyone claims to be innovative, how can such claims remain credible and
effective? How should innovation be communicated so that it genuinely strength-
ens a brand’s reputation rather than dissolving into noise? What strategies and tac-
tics are required, how often should innovation be communicated, and how can
consistency be maintained over time? Just as importantly, what risks, limitations, or
unintended consequences accompany the pursuit of an innovation-driven image?

A key part of the answer lies in understanding how branding works.
Branding is rarely about single messages or one-off campaigns. It operates
through repetition with intention. People seldom remember what they encoun-
ter once; they remember what they see, hear, and experience consistently over
time. Effective brands reiterate their core ideas repeatedly, while varying the
form, context, and storytelling through which those ideas are expressed (Grant,
2011; Kapferer, 2008). Innovation branding (IB) follows the same logic. It is
not about declaring innovation once, but about reinforcing it through repeated
signals, narratives, and experiences that accumulate into a coherent image.
Reiteration, when guided by strategy and grounded in substance, is what allows
innovation to become part of a brand’s identity rather than a passing slogan.
This makes IB both intuitive and demanding. At its core, it involves using
specific stories, symbols, and ideas to communicate that a brand is dynamic,
evolving, and oriented toward the future. Y et projecting an innovative image
cannot be achieved through communication alone. It requires an underlying cul-
ture that supports experimentation, learning, and change. Without this founda-
tion, innovation messaging risks becoming hollow. Developing and sustaining
such an image demands hard work, openness, and a carefully crafted strategic
plan, followed by consistent execution over time (Bellini & Pasquinelli, 2015).
Many organizations engage in IB for pragmatic reasons. A bank may launch
a new digital product and emphasize its technological sophistication to attract
attention and signal relevance. An institution might adopt artificial intelligence
capabilities and communicate this development to shareholders as evidence of
progress. Similar patterns can be found across sectors. In this sense, innova -
tion-oriented communication is not only understandable but often expected.
Innovation has become universal rather than exceptional. It can be invoked by
banks seeking clients, universities seeking students, factories seeking partners,
cities and nations seeking investment, or political figures seeking electoral sup-
port. Unlike other types of messaging that may quickly feel exhausted or
superficial, innovation has the potential to evolve alongside real practices and
societal change, embedding itself more deeply into organizational culture when
supported by genuine action.
Brands that recognize the signals associated with innovation and integrate
them thoughtfully into their communication can benefit from significant mar-
keting value. The motivation to adopt an innovative look and feel depends
largely on how willing a brand is to invest effort in identifying what counts as
innovation within its social and cultural context, and in highlighting this con-
sistently. IB is therefore not a shortcut, but a long-term commitment that relies
on repetition, relevance, and credibility.
Although IB is increasingly visible in practice, it remains only partially
articulated in academic and professional discourse. There is no single, stan -
dardized definition, yet evidence of the phenomenon appears across marketing
research and case studies. In place branding, for example, cities and nations

have positioned themselves as hubs of innovation to attract global attention
and investment, transforming technological and scientific progress into narra-
tives of modernity and aspiration (Pasquinelli, 2014). In such cases, innovation
functions not merely as an economic indicator but as a symbolic and commu-
nicative device. Scholars have shown how innovation messaging has become a
cornerstone of place and national branding, used to signal creativity, progress,
and forward-thinking governance (Bellini & Pasquinelli, 2015).
Despite these insights, the relationship between branding and innovation
remains underexplored. Research has long acknowledged that both are vital to
organizational success, yet their integration has received limited systematic
attention. As Brexendorf and colleagues note, “the interrelationship between
branding and innovations is still relatively under-researched”, even though
brand and innovation management clearly benefit from deeper integration
(Brexendorf et al., 2015, p. 548). Some scholars have begun to address this gap
by examining how brands signal innovation through recognizable markers or
“innovation trademarks” (Flikkema et al., 2019), by proposing strategic typol-
ogies that unite branding and innovation as complementary forces (Paswan et
al., 2021 ), or by defining marketing innovation as the development of new
ideas, processes, or tools specifically for branding purposes (Narayanan & Das,
2022). Others demonstrate how innovation and branding together contribute
to sales growth and competitive advantage ( Elezaj & Ramaj, 2023 ). Still, the
field remains fragmented, with studies scattered across disciplines and lacking
a unifying framework.
One notable limitation of existing research is that innovation is often treated
as a driver of organizational performance rather than as an object of brand -

Defining Innovation Branding (IB)
Innovation, especially technological innovation, is no longer driven only by
engineers or technical experts. The rise of artificial intelligence and no-code/
low-code platforms has turned digital development into a faster, simpler, and
more accessible business capability. As Rokis and Kirikova (2022)  note, no-
code/low-code development allows software to be created with minimal coding
and enables non-programmers to participate in development. From a manage-
ment perspective, this means that individuals and organizations with limited
technical expertise can now turn ideas into market-ready products and ser -
vices, reducing traditional barriers to innovation.
The growing availability of development tools is changing how innovation
is managed across organizations and markets. As these tools become more
accessible, more people can contribute to innovation and value creation
(Arocena & Sutz, 2017 ; Karlovitz, 2020). For companies, this enables faster
product development, greater independence, and increased competition. At
the same time, the rising number of new offerings makes clear positioning and
effective communication more important than ever.
As technology becomes a standard part of business operations, innovation has
also become a central element of brand strategy. Organizations increasingly pres-
ent their innovative capabilities as part of their brand identity, using innovation-
focused messages to signal relevance, differentiation, and problem-solving

strength (Mashiah, 2024, 2025a, 2025b). In this context, innovation is not only
about developing new solutions, but also about how those solutions are pre -
sented to the market. Understanding how both tech and non-tech innovation
is branded and communicated, therefore, becomes essential, making IB a core
managerial skill.
At this point, an important distinction should be made. This book is not
about innovation in branding (the use of new technologies or tools in brand
communication). While this topic is important and widely discussed, particu -
larly in relation to digital transformation and AI, it is not the focus here.
Instead, this book examines IB: the strategic practice of making innovation
narrative central to the brand. In this view, innovation is not only what compa-
nies do, but what they stand for. It becomes a brand promise, a positioning
strategy, and a source of long-term competitive advantage. Building on previ-
ous work (Mashiah, 2021, 2023, 2024, 2025b, 2026), including academic
research, industry practice, and teaching in marketing and management, the
following definition is proposed:
Innovation Branding (IB) is a strategic, integrated, multidisciplinary, and
research-based process involving assessment, content creation, and dissemi-
nation, aimed at positioning an entity in an innovative, positive, progressive,
and empowered light. It entails crafting or shaping a brand’s reputation as
one that is evolving toward advancement and contributing to its environ -
ment and stakeholders, whether through advanced technologies or other
innovative methods and ideas.
The IB process encompasses the strategic amplification of innovation-related
messages and symbols, both rhetorical and visual, across diverse marketing
channels, both traditional and contemporary, while tailoring communica -
tion to specific target audiences on each platform.
Moreover, IB is a marketing paradigm that aligns with and supports concur -
rent organizational processes, grounded in the belief that innovation can
emerge from any domain. It is a universal, multi-field approach suitable for
entities of various sizes, sectors, and stages, ranging from individuals (such
as managers or entrepreneurs) to businesses, organizations, corporations,
and even entire nations or geographic regions seeking to cultivate an image
of innovation.

Aaker (2007, p. 8) observed, “nearly every firm will include innovation as one of

expanded his framework to apply well beyond economics. Today, innovation is
widely recognized as a critical asset for companies, institutions, and brands aim-
ing to remain relevant, credible, and competitive in a rapidly evolving landscape.

Signaling theory shows that communication, marketing, and psychological
processes often involve three key elements: the signaler (who sends the mes -
sage), the receiver (the audience), and the signal itself, an action or cue that
gives credibility to the sender’s message.
 a) The Signaler: The Brand
 b) The Receiver: The Audience
 c) The Signal: The Message
The relationship and process among the three factors is naturally uneven, since
the signaler usually has more information, stronger motivation, and a clear
interest in shaping the receiver’s perception. Rooted in sociology, communica-
tion, and marketing, signaling theory explains how one party can strategically
provide evidence to strengthen their position in an exchange ( Boateng, 2018;
Connelly, 2011; Dunham, 2011; Herbig & Milewicz, 1995).
In the context of IB, the signaler (e.g., the brand or organization) must
undertake and lean on concrete actions that reinforce the message of innova -
tion and establish the authenticity of its future campaign. This could include
investment in R&D, tech partnerships, high-profile innovation programs, or
targeted marketing efforts aimed at specific audiences. Such practical initia -
tives function as credible signals; they are costly, visible, and therefore difficult
to fake, helping to convince audiences that the brand is genuinely innovative,
progressive, and forward-thinking. These actions move beyond empty rhetoric,
distinguishing brands that merely claim innovation from those that actively
signal it through actual behavior. Even when marketing campaigns are not
directly tied to specific tech acts, the existence of such foundational innovation
efforts reinforces the overall credibility of the brand narrative.

in the past and now seek to leverage them for reputation-building and strategic
positioning within the global or local innovation landscape.
Either way, understanding the mechanisms behind these dynamics, how
innovation is branded through concrete actions, is essential for anyone involved,
whether at the level of the state, the organization, or the individual.
Brand Actions and Audience Perception
In the influential book Positioning, published several decades ago ( Trout &
Ries, 1986), the authors explore the concept of brand positioning in depth.
They argue that effective branding is not about physical attributes, but about
shaping perceptions. In an environment saturated with messages, a brand must
carve out a clear and unique space in the consumer’s mind. This can be achieved
either by being the first to introduce a compelling idea or by presenting a dis -
tinct and differentiated angle. To succeed, a brand must be not only different,
but also consistent and engaging.

According to Thompson, positioning includes four key areas of focus
(Thompson, 2003, pp. 4–5): (a) Relevance—“Strong brands connect with cus-
tomers. They meet functional needs and tap into, and satisfy, emotional needs
and desires”. (b) Differentiation—“Strong brands add value, which makes
them stand out from their competitors”. (c) Credibility—“The brand must be
true to itself and keep the promises it makes”. (d) Stretch—“A brand’s contin-
ued success lies in its ability not only to remain relevant in a changing world
but also to foster innovation and to bring new products and line extensions
into its value proposition”.
In branding processes, differentiation is a widely discussed and essential
concept. A brand must identify a theme or element that distinguishes it from
its competitors. Often, from a comparative perspective, brands seek to empha-
size their superiority along a specific dimension—such as innovation or tech -
nological advancement. However, one of the key challenges in managing brand
equity is the tendency to focus exclusively on innovation as the primary point
of differentiation. According to Aaker, relying solely on innovation-based
comparisons can be problematic, as competitors frequently make similar
claims (Aaker, 2009). Consequently, if innovation is not communicated and
implemented in a strategic and sophisticated manner, it may be perceived by
consumers as a weak or unconvincing source of differentiation.
This is the time to remember that over the years, branding has been recog -
nized as a systematic, long-term process, grounded in strategic actions taken by
the brand. As part of this process, many brands today use innovation as a
central element to strengthen their image and employ it as a key positioning
concept (Aggarwal & Commuri, 2023).
Effective brand equity management requires strategic and thoughtful
approaches. According to Y ohn, strong branding is rooted in the organiza -
tion’s internal culture, beginning with clearly defined core values and the con-
sistent behavior of its people. Furthermore, successful brands go beyond
functional benefits; they cultivate emotional connections, promote shared val-
ues, and deliver meaningful experiences to their audiences ( Y ohn, 2013).
Wheeler states that “branding is a disciplined process used to build awareness,
attract new customers, and extend customer loyalty. Positioning a brand to be
irreplaceable requires a daily desire to be the best” ( Wheeler, 2017, p. 6). This
notion is grounded in core concepts related to the development and strategic
use of brand assets. For example, in the 1990s, Aaker introduced the concept
of brand equity, defining it as a set of brand-related assets—such as brand
loyalty, awareness, perceived quality, and brand associations—that create value
for both consumers and the firm by enhancing marketing effectiveness, enabling
premium pricing, and fostering long-term competitive advantage ( Aaker,
1992). According to Tuominen ( 1999, pp. 71–72) “Brand equity includes not
only the value of the brand, but also implicitly the value of proprietary tech -
nologies, patents, trademarks, and other intangibles such as manufacturing

know-how”. In other words, there is a strong connection between the brand’s
image, reputation, and communicated values, and the actual perceptions held
by consumers, particularly as they relate to tangible assets in the spheres of
innovation and tech capabilities.
Indeed, in the fields of marketing and branding, much attention is often
placed on what a brand does or should do to persuade its target audience.
However, an equally critical dimension lies in understanding how the audience
perceives the brand. This consumer perspective is a foundational aspect of
contemporary marketing thinking, and it is precisely this viewpoint that is cap-
tured in customer-based conceptual models in branding (Fayrene & Lee, 2011;
Keller, 1993; Lassar et al., 1995). The Customer-Based Brand Equity (CBBE)
theory provides a strategic framework for building strong brands by focusing
on how customers think, feel, and respond to a brand (Keller, 2001). It outlines
a hierarchical model consisting of four stages: (a) Brand identity (salience), (b)
Brand meaning (performance & imagery), (c) Brand response (judgments &
feelings), and (d) Brand resonance.
In brand salience, the stage at which the brand’s visibility and prominence
serve as cues that remind the consumer of its existence. Prior to this stage, the
brand must engage in consistent marketing efforts to ensure that the audience
is aware of its presence and offerings. The key objective at this level is for the
brand to be top-of-mind when the consumer is in a purchasing situation or in
need of a relevant product or service.
The next level in the pyramid involves performance and imagery. At this
stage, the brand must deliver its promised product or service effectively and
reliably, ensuring quality and authenticity. Through these performance attri -
butes, the brand builds a set of positive associations in the consumer’s mind. A
brand that consistently performs well is perceived as relevant and aligned with
the consumer’s cultural and personal expectations.
Following this is the judgments and feelings stage. Here, the consumer
begins to develop emotional and evaluative responses to the brand. These can
include sentiments such as joy, nostalgia, pride, or admiration. This stage reflects
the brand’s success in creating an emotional layer to its identity. For example,
Apple has cultivated a strong emotional connection with its consumers, who
often feel pride and belonging within the brand’s ecosystem.
At the top of the pyramid is the resonance stage. This represents the culmina-
tion of the previous stages, wherein the brand achieves deep psychological bond-
ing with the consumer. Resonance is marked by strong brand loyalty, a sense of
community, and a consistent preference for the brand. Consumers at this stage feel
a strong personal connection and see themselves as part of the brand’s identity.
Overall, by mapping how consumers build relationships with brands, the
CBBE model shifts the analytical focus from brand-driven actions to audience
perceptions, offering a consumer-centric framework for understanding and
managing brand equity.

The Democratization of Innovation
Development processes related to innovation and technology are often resource-
intensive, requiring substantial investment in terms of budget, expertise, personnel,

28 Innovation Branding
and time before an idea matures into a viable and valuable solution for its
intended audience. This raises a fundamental question: why should we engage
with innovation, and is it truly accessible to all?
As previously defined, innovation is both a practical act—a form of behav-
ior and action—and, as this book elaborates, a discursive phenomenon—
something that is talked about, promoted, and marketed ( Vinsel & Russell,
2020). There is a close relationship between the act of innovation and the dis -
course surrounding it, yet a crucial issue emerges: who possesses the capacity
to bring innovation to maturity and transition it from practice to narrative,
from product to promotional identity, ultimately acquiring the image of being
an innovative and forward-thinking entity?
This question leads us to the concept of the democratization of innovation
and technology (Arocena & Sutz, 2017 ; Spies, 2014). A common critique of
advanced technological and innovation-driven industries is that they often
operate as exclusive or selective enclaves—“closed clubs” into which certain
populations struggle to gain entry. This critique is evident across various global
contexts, especially in marginalized or developing regions where social and
economic disparities limit access to technological resources.
One response to this issue lies in the social process of democratization of
innovation and technology. This refers to the evolving reality in which tools
(e.g., artificial intelligence), knowledge, and access to innovation are becoming
more widely available—not limited solely to experts, large corporations, or
powerful institutions. Democratization, in this sense, is a socio-technical pro -
cess through which resources, tools, and capabilities—once reserved for elites—
are increasingly accessible to individuals, communities, and a broader array of
organizations. This shift allows for more diverse participation in the creation,
design, and use of innovation, thereby reshaping the boundaries of creativity,
power, and influence in the economic and social spheres.
Democratization of innovation operates on both micro and macro levels: (a)
At the micro level, democratization can occur within an individual organiza -
tion. Whereas innovation may have once been confined to expert teams or
R&D departments, it can now become a shared resource within the organiza-
tion. Employees from various departments are invited to contribute to innova-
tion processes, thus embedding innovation more deeply across the organization’s
culture. (b) At the macro level, democratization refers to broader societal pro-
cesses. For instance, across a country, region, industry, or population segment,
access to innovation and technology becomes more widespread. Groups that
were previously excluded or disadvantaged in this regard now find themselves
with new opportunities to participate.
One clear manifestation of this democratization is the rise of no-code and\
or low-code platforms ( Beranic et al., 2020 ; Hurlburt, 2021; Martins et al.,
2023). These tools enable individuals without traditional programming skills
to develop digital products or take part in innovation and technology building.
