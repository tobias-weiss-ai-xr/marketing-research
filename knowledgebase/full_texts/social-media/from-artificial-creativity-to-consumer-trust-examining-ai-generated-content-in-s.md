ABSTRACT: This study examines the relationship between the disclosure of AI-generated content, perceived content authenticity,
and consumer trust in social media marketing, while also evaluating the moderating role of consumer skepticism toward AI.
Grounded in Signaling Theory and the Persuasion Knowledge Model, the study conceptualizes consumer trust as an outcome of
transparency regarding technological origins and the assessment of content authenticity, wherein skepticism shapes how
consumers interpret disclosure signals. Employing a quantitative cross-sectional research design, data were collected via a 5-point
Likert scale questionnaire from 385 active social media users in Vietnam who had encountered AI -generated or AI -assisted
marketing content. Data were analyz ed using IBM SPSS version 26 for reliability assessment, exploratory factor analysis, and
multiple linear regression, while Model 1 of the PROCESS Macro (developed by Hayes) was applied to test the moderating effect .
The results indicate that both the disc losure of AI-generated content (β = 0.625) and perceived content authenticity ( β = 0.576)
significantly contribute to strengthening consumer trust in social media marketing. Disclosure exerts a slightly stronger dir ect
impact, highlighting the importance o f clearly communicating AI's involvement in the content creation process. Furthermore,
consumer skepticism toward AI significantly weakens the positive relationship between AI content disclosure and consumer trust,
as evidenced by a negative interaction coefficient of -0.499. These findings confirm that mere disclosure is insufficient to build trust
among highly skeptical consumers. Brands must combine genuine transparency with authentic content, information verification,
clear accountability, and human oversight to maintain trust in social media marketing that utilizes AI-generated content.
KEYWORDS: Consumer trust in social media marketing; AI generated content disclosure; perceived content authenticity; consumer
AI skepticism; AI generated marketing content.

1. INTRODUCTION
Consumer trust in social media marketing describes the readiness of people to view brand messages on these platforms as
believable honest and reliable. This trust grows more vulnerable as generative artificial intelligence creates convincing material in
large volumes while obscuring who produced it what purpose it serves and whether the details hold true. The issue demands
attention since trust encourages people to accept marketing statements and remain loyal customers yet any sense of being misled
harms interaction and the connection with the brand. Studies reveal that material from generative artificial intelligence may lower
the sense of brand genuineness and provoke negative reactions especially when machines take over instead of supporting human
work (B rüns & Meißner, 2024). Transparency offers no straightforward solution. Sharing that content comes from artificial
intelligence can show openness yet it may also stir dislike of algorithms and reduce believability. Grigsby et al. (2025) sho w that
the impact of such sharing changes according to how intangible the service is while Bui et al. (2024) report that a genuine feel in
artificial intelligence created images builds trust. These mixed results highlight a key limiting factor. Scholars therefore need to
examine if disclosure strengthens or weakens trust how authenticity carries credibility and if consumer doubt about artificia l
intelligence heightens unfavorable views.
Prior research generally shows that involving artificial intelligence tends to lower how consumers judge brands even though t he
results stay mixed. Brüns & Meißner (2024) observed that generative artificial intelligence material decreased the sense of brand
genuineness especially when people thought machines had taken the place of human makers. Bui et al. (2024) by contrast
demonstrated that visuals created by artificial intelligence could bolster trust and buying intent once viewers saw the material as
fresh and true to life. Work on disclosure reveals a parallel conflict. Grigsby et al. (2025) noted that revealing artificial in telligence

2. LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1. Consumer Trust
Consumer trust describes the conviction that an exchange partner proves reliable truthful skilled and able to meet expected duties
(Hajli et al., 2017). Within marketing this trust lessens felt uncertainty and lets consumers depend on information while bui lding
attitudes connections and buying choices. The role grows stronger on social media where people judge material from brands
influencers peers and automated tools without any direct check. Hajli et al. (2017) indicate that trust encourages informatio n
search familiarity social presence and purchase intent in social commerce. In the same way Lou & Yuan (2019) show that
informative worth and source believability build trust toward branded social media material. Trust further links sensed exper tise
and genuineness to loyalty and wider marketing results which affirms its key relational place (Kim & Ki m, 2021). Still consumer
trust in social media marketing stays fragile since persuasive aims commercial backing false details and unclear origins can lower
content credibility. Chen and Cheng (2020) uncover that persuasion knowledge guides how consumers judge false brand details
and then revise brand trust. Generative artificial intelligence intensifies the issue by hiding human roles and sparking ques tions
about originality and responsibility. Consumer trust must therefore be seen as a conditional assessment formed by openness
sensed genuineness and consumer doubt rather than a simple result of ongoing digital contact.
2.2. Critical Analysis of Theoretical Frameworks

Signaling Theory introduced by Michael Spence (1973) accounts for the way an informed party conveys visible information to
another under information asymmetry. Signals allow receivers to judge qualities that stay hidden yet their impact hinges on clarity,
consistency, credibility and match with later actions. Current marketing studies draw on this logic for settings where consumers
cannot fully confirm brand motives production methods or message truth. Shahid et al. (2024) establish that signaling process es
matter strongly in marketing as firms deploy communication cues to ease uncertainty and shape consumer judgments across varied

When consumers read disclosure as chosen honesty , it may convey openness , accountability and regard for informed choice.
Cambier & Poncin (2020) show that transparency signals can raise views of brand integrity by letting consumers conclude that a

Theory thus frames disclosure as an informative cue without assuming its trust i mpact stays positive. Perceived content

and aims o f its source. Material seen as original , sincere and consistent with prior brand conduct offers clear evidence that the
message merits trust. Lou & Yuan (2019) indicate that message worth and source credibility build consumer trust in branded social
media material showing how visible message traits guide views of otherwise uncertain sources. In the suggested model ,
authenticity adds real substance to the disclosure signal. Open disclosure paired with authentic material may display careful
artificial intelligence use while disclosure joined to formulaic or emotionally hollow material may heighten doubt. Signaling Theory
therefore links disclosure of artificial intelligence generated content and perceived content authenticity to consumer trust by

underpins the proposed moderation by clarifying why identical disclosure signals can yield varied trust judgments among
consumers.
This study assumes that consumers hold incomplete knowledge about the creation of social media marketing content and whether
its claims mirror true brand aims. Disclosure of artificial intelligence generated content and perceived authenticity supply visible
signals through which consumers judge honesty accountability and credibility. Consumers actively decode these signals instead of
reacting in the same way. Their artificial intelligence skepticism shapes the meaning of the signal so that disclosure builds  trust
when seen as openness or reduces trust when read as proof of manipulation.
2.2.2. The Persuasion Knowledge Model
The Persuasion Knowledge Model, developed by Marian Friestad and Peter Wright, (1994) accounts for the manner in which people
notice, interpret, judge and respond to efforts aimed at influencing them. Over time, consumers build understanding of persuasive
agents, tactics and their personal coping strategies through repeated marketplace encounters. When a message cue highlights
commercial aims or a persuasive method, consumers may call up this knowledge and revise their views of both the message and
its origin. The framework fits social media marketing  especially well since commercial content often sits inside entertainment
personal stories influencer posts and everyday conversation. Such forms can hide persuasive aims and turn disclosure into a key
recognition signal. Waltenrath (2024) notes that advertising disclosures can heighten awareness of commercial persuasion while
creating varied effects on current and later social media involvement. In this research, disclosure of artificial intelligence generated
content tells consumers that artificial intelligence took part in crafting the message. The detail can trigger conceptual persuasion
knowledge by helping consumers spot the production method and attitudinal persuasion knowledge by prompting views on
whether that method seems fitting believable or manipulative. Wortel et al. (2024) indicate that artificial intelligence disclosures
in Instagram advertising can shape advertising attitudes and spark assessments tied to persuasive aims though the impact diff ers
across marketing results. The model thus clarifies why disclosure may lower consumer trust even as it raises transparency. After
consumers realize a marketing message arose through artificial intelligence, they may wonder whether personalization, emotional
wording images or seeming creativity shows true brand expression or deliberate calculation. Koning & Voorveld (2025) offer clear
proof that artificial intelligence disclosures can raise persuasion knowledge and then lower trust in advertisements and
organizations. Additionally, perceived content authenticity  gains theoretical weight because consumers handle persuasive
communication by more than simply spotting its production approach. They also judge whether the message seems sincere original
truthful and consistent with the communicator identity. Authentic material can lessen defensive reactions by reassuring consumers
that technological help has not replaced genuine human purpose. By contrast, material viewed as artificial or carefully staged may
heighten persuasion knowledge and foster resistance. This logic backs the direct impact of perceived content authenticity on
consumer trust in social media marketing. The model further supplies a clear account of the moderating part played by consumer

2.3. The Impact of Artificial Intelligence Generated Content Disclosure
Artificial intelligence generated content disclosure means a clear statement or label that tells audiences artificial intelligence took
part in creating modifying or showing marketing text images audio or video. It makes technological authorship plain and shows
how far nonhuman effort entered content production (Kirkby et al., 2023). Conceptually, disclosure works as an informational tool
that lessens the gap between marketers who know the production method and consumers who cannot see that process directly.
Signaling Theory indicates that freely sharing details that might stay hidden can convey honesty and accountability. Organizational
transparency likewise builds sensed trustworthiness when the shared details stay clear accurate and open to key stakeholders
(Schnackenberg & Tomlinson, 2016). In social media , marketing disclosure safeguards consum ers ability to separate synthetic
messages from human expression and to form informed views about credibility. This function has grown especially vital because
generative artificial intelligence can copy persuasive wording realistic pictures and emotional signals without audiences noticing
its role. Kirkby et al. (2023) observed that revealing an artificial intelligence source left perceived brand voice authentic ity and
brand attitude unchanged suggesting audiences may accept technological authorship when t he material stays true to brand
identity. Wang & Qiu (2024) likewise show that artificial intelligence transparency can yield positive consumer reactions by lifting
views of the disclosed digital agent. From a normative philosophical standpoint, disclosure shows respect for consumer autonomy
since it allows informed reading rather than hiding key production facts. From an interpretivist standpoint , however, disclosure
carries no fixed social sense. Consumers form its meaning through earlier experiences cultural norms technological familiarity and
views on creative duty. Higher artificial intelligence generated content disclosure may lift consumer trust in social media marketing
when audiences read openness as proof that a brand hides nothing. Clear disclosur e can mark ethical duty lessen doubt about
content source and let consumers judge marketing claims with more assurance. This view matches a critical realist stance.
Disclosure stands as a visible marketing act yet its impact arises through deeper psycholog ical processes such as sensed honesty
accountability authenticity and skepticism. The favorable impact may prove strongest when disclosure stays noticeable easy to
grasp and joined by signs of human supervision. Schnackenberg et al. (20 19) maintain that transparency gains trustworthiness
when the shared information aids stakeholder judgments of organizational behavior. Still empirical work shows ongoing
disagreement. Marked negative impacts appear in Schilke & Reimann (2025) whose thirteen experiments revea led that parties
disclosing artificial intelligence use gained less trust because observers credited them with lower effort and skill. Grigsby  et al.
(2025) also found that artificial intelligence disclosure cut trust and advertising attitudes especially w hen services stressed
intangible traits. Baek et al. (2026) note that disclosure lessened prosocial advertising judgments through lower credibility  and
sensed empathy. By contrast Kirkby et al. (2023) reported a neutral outcome since an artificial intelligence source did not notably
weaken brand voice authenticity or brand attitude. These mixed findings show that disclosure is neither automatically trustworthy
nor automatically harmful. A postpositivist view therefore treats its impact as probabilistic and open to empirical test rather than
fixed in every case. In this research the positive forecast stays theoretically sound because transparent disclosure can display
honesty aid informed judgment and ease worries about hidden technological influence. When applied in a steady and responsible
manner stronger artificial intelligence generated content disclosure should thus raise consumer confidence in the integrity of social
media marketing communication. Therefore, the following hypothesis is advanced:
H1: AI generated content disclosure has a positive impact on consumer trust in social media marketing.

2.4. The Impact of Perceived Content Authenticity
Perceived content authenticity refers to a consumer's subjective assessment that marketing content is genuine, unique, honest ,
and consistent with the source's identity, values, and intention s (Pérez, 2019; Lee & Eastin, 2021). In the context of AI -generated
communications, authenticity also hinges on whether the synthetic content faithfully reflects the people, products, experienc es,
or brand realities it depicts. Bui et al. (2024) distinguish between uniqueness and representa tional fidelity, demonstrating that
audiences can perceive AI-generated images as authentic even when they lack traditional, direct human creativity. Consequently,
authenticity is a matter of perception rather than an objective attribute defined solely by the production origin. From a Signaling
theory, authentic content provides observable evidence that allows consumers to infer sincerity, integrity, and consistency amidst
information asymmetry. It enables audiences to differentiate between meaningful brand expressions and calculated, imitative, or
technologically simulated persuasive behaviors. Audrezet et al. (2020) indicate that passion and transparent communication he lp
social media influencers maintain authenticity when commercial partnerships threaten their credibility. Similarly, Liao & Chen
(2024) demonstrate that managing authenticity through information disclosure and the expression of passion shapes engagement
levels with sponsored videos. Perceived content authenticity is particularly crucial in social media marketing, as bran d messages
often appear alongside personal stories, peer -to-peer communication, and user -generated content. Consequently, consumers
evaluate not only factual accuracy but also whether the message conveys sincerity and consistency, and aligns with the socia l
context. An interpretivist perspective views authenticity as a meaning constructed through the interplay of content characteristics,
audience expectations, platform norms, and cultural understanding. However, a critical realist perspective acknowledges t hat
underlying factors , such as factual consistency, source transparency, human oversight, and brand alignment  can shape these
perceptions, even if audiences experience and interpret them in diverse ways.
A high level of perceived content authenticity reinforces consumer trust in social media marketing; content perceived as sinc ere
and honest reduces uncertainty regarding the communicator's motives and the message's credibility. Authenticity signals that a
brand is demonstrating genuine commitment rather than merely manufacturing emotional appeal to manipulate consumer
behavior. This fosters trust in marketing claims, mitigates feelings of manipulation, and strengthens long -term confidence in the
source. Kim & Kim (2021) demonstrated that influencer authenticity bolsters trust, thereby driving loyalty and yielding positive
marketing outcomes. Similarly, Bui et al. (2024) found that perceived authenticity in AI-generated visual content increases purchase
and advocacy intentions, indicating that a synthetic origin does not preclude positive responses when the content conveys a sense
of uniqueness and honesty. Further compelling evidence is provided by Liao & Chen (2024); they demonstrate that authenticity
management strategies significantly enhance engagement with sponsored content, particularly when supported by influencer
competence and brand fit.
However, this relationship remains a subject of debate. Andonopoulos  et al. (2023) found that authenticity boosts purchase
intentions via perceived trustworthiness but does not significantly influence non -purchase-related behaviors. Their experiments
also revealed that influencers with lower levels of authenticity could sti ll drive purchasing behavior by evoking inspiration. This
neutral or compensatory impact model suggests that factors such as entertainment value, novelty, aspirational appeal, and visual
attractiveness can sometimes outweigh authenticity. Consequently, fr om a post-positivist perspective, the proposed relationship
is probabilistic rather than a universal law. Perceived authenticity is expected to foster consumer trust, although the magnitude of
this effect may vary depending on content type, technological p erceptions, and platform context. From a practical standpoint,
authenticity remains valuable because consumer responses are driven by a combination of honesty, relevance, and consistency.
Therefore the following hypothesis is advanced:
H2: Perceived content authenticity has a positive impact on consumer trust in social media marketing.
2.5 The Moderating Role of Consumer AI Skepticism
Consumer AI skepticism refers to the tendency of consumers to question the competence, reliability, authenticity, persuasive
motives, and ethical acceptability of artificial intelligence when it contributes to marketing communications  (Castelo et al., 2019;
Puntoni et al., 2021; Kirkby et al., 2023 ). It reflects critical doubt rather than outright rejection, as skeptical consumers may still
accept AI when its contributions are accurate, useful, verifiable, and supported by meaningful human oversight. Pierre et al. (2026)
conceptualize consumer aversion to AI in marketing communications as discomfort with its involvement in both communication
strategy and content execution, which subsequently influences brand perceptions and purchase intentions. Consumer skepticism
toward AI is closely related to algorithmic aversion but is conceptually broader. Algorithmic aversion describes a reluctance to rely
on algorithmic judgments , particularly after observing algorithmic errors , even when those algorithms perform more accurately
than human decision-makers (Dietvorst et al., 2015). Consumer AI skepticism also encompasses doubts regarding synthetic
authorship, emotional sincerity, content authenticity, commercial manipulation, data practices, and accountability. Castelo e t al.
(2019) demonstrate that consumers are particularly hesitant to trust algorithms i n subjective tasks requiring creativity, intuition,

Consumer skepticism toward AI  serves a vital protective function by encouraging consumers to verify claims, question the

supports this context-dependent interpretation. From a post-positivist perspective, the moderating impact of skepticism must be

Consumer skepticism toward AI is expected to negatively moderate the relationship between the disclosure of AI -generated
content and consumer trust in social media marketing. When skepticism is low, disclosure can serve as a signal of transparenc y,
demonstrating honesty, accountability, and respect for the consumer's informed judgment. However, when skepticism is high, the
same disclosure may highlight automation, reduced human effort, artificial emotion, questionable originality, and uncertain
accountability. Consequently, the positive impact of disclosure on trust diminishes as skepticism rises. Wortel et al. (2024) found
that AI disclosure reduced positive brand attitudes among consumers with high levels of AI aversion, indicating that prior attitudes
toward artificial intelligence shape reactions to disclosure. Similarly, Qiu et al. (2025) found that AI aversion heightened skepticism

evidence was also provided by Schilke & Reimann (2025); across thirteen experiments, they consistently demonstrated that
disclosing AI usage reduced trust, as observers perceived lower levels of effort and execution competence.
However, empirical evidence remains mixed. Wortel et al. (2024) found no significant moderating effect of AI aversion on attitudes
toward advertising or source credibility, suggesting a neutral moderating role regarding certain outcomes. Zehnle et al. (202 5)
further demonstrated that consumer aversion varies significantly depending on the type of AI and the consumption context. From
a practical standpoint, skepticism may be mitigated when AI delivers proven accuracy, useful personalization, clear human
oversight, and content aligned with brand identity. Despite these conditional findings, social media marketing inherently involve s
subjective creativity and persuasive intent; consequently, skeptical consumers are particularly prone to viewing AI involveme nt
negatively. On this basis, the following hypothesis is proposed:
H3: Consumer AI skepticism negatively moderates the relationship between AI generated content disclosure and consumer trust in
social media marketing.

3. METHODS
3.1. Sampling Strategy
This research employed a quantitative cross -sectional design to investigate how AI generated content disclosure and perceived
content authenticity influence consumer trust in social media marketing while examining the moderating effect of consumer AI
skepticism. Vietnam was chosen given its consumers’ active engagement with social media and growing exposure to marketing
material created or altered by generative artificial intelligence. The quantitative method enabled rigorous hypothesis testin g and
statistical assessment of both direct and interaction effects through standardized survey data. The cross -sectional approach
allowed efficient capture of consumer views on AI disclosure, content authenticity, AI skepticism, and trust at one moment in time
(Bryman, 2016). Individual social media users formed the unit of analysis. Purposive sampling recruited participants with relevant
experience who could evaluate AI generated marketing content meaningfully. Eligible respondents were at least 18 years old,
living in Vietnam, actively used at least one social media platform, and had encountered advertising or branded material identified
as created, modified, or assisted by artificial intelligence within the prior six months. Screening covered users of Facebook, TikTok,
Instagram, YouTube, and other widely adopted platforms. Participants were drawn from varied age groups, occupations,
educational backgrounds, regions, and levels of social media experience to promote sample diversity.
Data collection relied on a structured self -administered questionnaire built from established academic scales. AI generated
content disclosure measured the degree to which consumers perceived that social media marketing clearly, visibly,
understandably, and honestly signaled artificial intelligence involvement in content creation, drawing on studies of disclosure
recognition and responses to AI in digital advertising (Wortel et al., 2024; Koning & Voorveld, 2025). Perceived content authenticity
assessed whether respondents viewed AI generated marketing material as genuine, truthful, original, sincere, and aligned with
the brand’s identity and values, with items adapted from authenticity research in social media and AI communication (Lee &
Eastin, 2021; Bui et al., 2024). Consumer AI skepticism captured the tendency to doubt the competence, reliability, authenticity,
ethical standing, and persuasive intent of artificial intelligence in marketing, reflecting work on skepticism toward social media
information, algorithm aversion, and resistance to AI in marketing (Pelau et al., 2021; Pierre et al., 2026). Consumer trust in social
media marketing evaluated whether branded social media messages were seen as credible, honest, reliable, dependable, and
worthy of conf idence, adapted from research on trust in branded social media content and social commerce (Hajli et al., 2017;
Lou & Yuan, 2019). All items employed a five point Likert scale from 1 for strongly disagree to 5 for strongly agree. Academi c
experts in market ing, consumer behavior, artificial intelligence, and quantitative methods reviewed the instrument for content
relevance and clarity of wording. Marketing practitioners and experienced social media users further checked whether items
mirrored realistic encounters with AI generated content.
Because the questionnaire was administered in Vietnamese, translation followed by independent back translation ensured
conceptual equivalence with the original English measures. The final online questionnaire was shared through universities,
professional networks, consumer communities, social media groups, and digital channels. Participation remained voluntary and
anonymous. Respondents received details on the academic purpose, confidentiality safeguards, and the right to withdraw.

3.2. Data analysis
Analysis of the data relied on SPSS version 26 together with Hayes’ Process macro. Descriptive statistics first outlined the
respondents’ demographic profiles, patterns of social media use, and encounters with AI generated marketing content. Reliability
of the scales was checked through Cronbach’s alpha, where coefficients of 0.70 or above and corrected item total correlations
greater than 0.30 confirmed adequate internal consistency (Tavakol & Dennick, 2011). Exploratory factor analysis followed,
employing principal component extraction and Varimax rotation. Items were kept only when their loadings surpassed 0.50 and
displayed no notable cross loadings (Hair et al., 2009). Multiple linear regression then assessed the direct influences of AI
generated content disclosure and perceived content authenticity on consumer trust in social media marketing, with significance
judged at the conventional level of p below 0.05. Hayes’ Process Model 1 was applied last to test whether consumer AI skepticism
moderated the link between ai generated content disclosure and consumer trust in social media marketing (Hayes, 2022) .

5. DISCUSSION
5.1. Result Summary
AI-generated content disclosure and the perceived authenticity of that content both demonstrate a significant positive impact on
consumer trust in social media marketing, with standardized regression coefficients of 0.625 and 0.576, respectively. These
findings indicate that clearly communicating the involvement of artificial intelligence in content creation and presenting marketing
content perceived as genuine, truthful, original, and consistent with brand identity significantly enhance consumer trust.
Furthermore, consumer AI skepticism acts as a significant negative moderator, weakening the relationship between AI content
disclosure and consumer trust; this is evidenced by an interaction coefficient of -0.499 and a confidence interval ranging from -
0.574 to -0.424. These findi ngs suggest that the impact of disclosure on trust diminishes when consumers question the
competence, reliability, authenticity, motives, and ethical standards of AI. Overall, the research results provide robust emp irical
support for all three hypotheses and validate the theoretical framework linking AI content disclosure, perceived content
authenticity, consumer skepticism toward AI, and consumer trust in social media marketing.
Regarding the research questions, the findings address the first question by confirming that disclosing information about AI -
generated content significantly strengthens consumer trust in social media marketing. The study also addresses the second
research question by demonstrating that the perceived authenticity of content exerts a significant positive impact, although the
standardized effect size of this factor is slightly lower than that of information disclosure. The third research question is answered
by showing that high consumer skepticism toward AI significantly diminishes the positive impact of disclosing AI -generated
content on consumer trust. Collectively, these findings confirm that brands need to combine transparent disclosure with authentic
content, while recognizing that disclosure alone may be insufficient to build trust among highly skeptical consumers.
Consequently, effective social media marketing requires clearly explaining the role of AI, ensuring content credibility, maintaining
rigorous and meaningful human oversight, and employing communication strategies that alleviate consumer concerns regarding
issues such as information manipulation, the nature of artificial creativity, and accountability .
5.2. Theoretical Implications
The empirical findings strongly support H1 because AI generated content disclosure significantly increases consumer trust in social
media marketing with β = 0.625. This outcome aligns with Cambier & Poncin (2020), who argue that transparent communication
functions as an observable signal of brand integrity, and with Schnackenberg & Tomlinson (2016), who associate transparency
with stronger stakeholder trust. It also complements Kirkby et al. (2023), whose findings indicate that revealing AI authorship does
not necessarily damage perceived authenticity or brand attitudes. However, the result contrasts with Schilke & Reimann (2025) ,
Koning & Voorveld (2025), and Grigsby et al. (2025), who report that AI disclosure can reduce trust by highlighting limited h uman
effort, manipulative potential, or weak emotional capability. The strong positive coefficient found here suggests that consum ers
may interpret clear disclosure primarily as evidence of honesty and accountability when evaluating social media marketing.
Nevertheless, the finding does not support a purely deterministic view that disclosure universally produces trust, because its
meaning depends on consumer interpretation and contextual conditions. Theoretically, the study extends Signaling Theory by
conceptualizing AI disclosure as a potentially credible transparency signal that reduces information asymmetry between marketers
and consumers regarding technological authorship.
The findings also support H2 because perceived content authenticity positively affects consumer trust in social media marketi ng
with β = 0.576. This result agrees with Kim & Kim (2021), who demonstrate that authenticity strengthens trust and subsequent
marketing outcomes, and with Audrezet et al. (2020), who emphasize sincerity, transparency, and genuine commitment as
foundations of authenticity in social media communication. It also supports Bui et al. (2024), whose concept of AI authentici ty

subject. Similarly, Liao & Chen (2024) show that effective authenticity management improves audience responses to sponsored
social media content. However, the finding only partly corresponds with Andonopoulos et al. (2023), who find that authenticity
supports purchase intention through trustworthiness but does not significantly influence certain nonpurchase responses.
Although inspirational or entertaining content may compensate for limited authenticity in some situations, the substantial direct

authenticity research by demonstrating that authenticity is not restricted to human authorship. Instead, it represents a consumer
judgment concerning genuineness, truthfulness, originality, and consistency that remains relevant when marketing content is
generated or assisted by artificial intelligence.
The empirical findings further support H3 because the negative interaction coefficient of −0.499 shows that Consumer AI
skepticism weakens the positive relationship between AI generated content disclosure and consumer trust in social media
marketing. This result aligns with Wortel et al. (2024), who find that AI disclosure produces less favorable brand attitudes among
consumers with strong AI aversion. It also supports Qiu et al. (2025), who demonstrate that AI disclosure can activate persuasion
knowledge and skepticism, particularly among consumers predisposed to resist artificial intelligence. Castelo et al. (2019) provide
further theoretical support by showing that consumers distrust algorithms more strongly when tasks involve subjective qualiti es
such as creativity, intuition, and emotional understanding. Nevertheless, the result differs from evidence of neutral moderation
reported by Wortel et al. (2024) for source credibility and advertising attitude. It also qualifies Logg et al. (2019), who s how that
consumers may sometimes prefer algorithmic judgment over human judgment. The strong negative interaction observed in this
study suggests that social media creativity represents a context in which technological skepticism remains especially influen tial.
Theoretically, the research extends the Persuasion Knowledge Model by positioning consumer AI skepticism as a boundary
condition that determines how disclosure cues are interpreted. Disclosure may signal honesty to consumers with low skepticism
but may emphas ize synthetic persuasion, diminished human contribution, and uncertain accountability to highly skeptical
consumers. Accordingly, consumer AI skepticism explains why identical transparency practices generate divergent trust outcomes.
5.3. Practical Implications
First, the empirical findings strongly support H1 and demonstrate that AI generated content disclosure significantly strength ens
consumer trust in social media marketing with β = 0.625. This result indicates that brands should regard disclosure as a substantive
communication practice rather than a minimal compliance label. Marketers should clearly indicate whether AI created, modified ,
or assisted the text, images, audio, or video and explain the degree of human involvement. Detailed disclosure can reduce
information asymmetry and communicate accountability, whereas vague labels may create further uncertainty. Cambier & Poncin
(2020) demonstrate that transparent marketing communication can strengthen perceived brand integrity. Pierre et al. (2026)
similarly indicate that detailed disclosure may reduce perceived manipulation more effectively than superficial disclosure.
Nevertheless, managers should not assume that disclosure automatically creates trust because consumers may interpret it as
evidence of reduced  human effort or capability (Schilke & Reimann, 2025). Brands should therefore combine disclosure with
factual verification, editorial review, creative supervision, and clear responsibility for errors.
Second, the empirical findings support H2 and show that perceived content authenticity positively affects consumer trust in social
media marketing with β = 0.576. Brands must therefore ensure that AI generated content remains genuine, truthful, original, and
consistent with their identity and values. Marketing teams should avoid generic content, unrealistic imagery, fabricated
testimonials, and emotionally artificial narratives. Instead, AI should support human creativity and production efficiency wi thout
replacing meaningful brand judgment. Audrezet et al. (2020) emphasize that genuine commitment and transparent
communication help preserve authenticity in commercial social media relationships. Bui et al. (2024) also show that AI generated
visual content can  produce favorable consumer responses when it appears original and faithfully represents its subject. Firms
should establish review procedures covering factual accuracy, visual realism, cultural appropriateness, emotional credibility , and
brand consistency . Social listening and consumer feedback should also identify content perceived as artificial, misleading, or
inconsistent before widespread publication.
Finally, the empirical findings firmly support H3 and reveal that consumer AI skepticism significantly weakens the positive
relationship between AI generated content disclosure and consumer trust in social media marketing, as demonstrated by the
interaction coefficient of −0.499. A uniform disclosure strategy is therefore unlikely to  influence all consumers equally. Brands
should segment audiences according to AI familiarity, perceived technological risk, and skepticism. Concise disclosure may be
sufficient for consumers with low skepticism. Highly skeptical consumers require further explanation of why AI was used, how
humans supervised the process, how claims were verified, and who remains accountable. Castelo et al. (2019) show that
consumers resist algorithms more strongly when tasks involve subjective creativity and emotional judgment. Wortel et al. (2024)

should therefore preserve visible human involvement, offer accessible correction channels, and provide verifiable evidence
supporting AI generated claims. The practical objective should not be to suppress skepticism but to address it through meaningful
transparency, consumer control, and accountable human oversight.
5.4. Limitations
This study has a few limitations. The cross -sectional design captures perceptions at a single point in time and cannot determine
how trust levels evolve with repeated exposure to AI-generated marketing content. The use of purposive sampling and a focus on
social media users i n Vietnam may limit the generalizability of the findings to other cultures, platforms, and consumer groups.
Self-reported responses may also be subject to recall bias, social desirability bias, and variations in participants' ability to i dentify
AI-generated content. The study measures information disclosure as a perception -based variable rather than experimentally
manipulating the format, level of detail, or placement of such information. Furthermore, the model considers only two direct
predictors and one moderator, thereby excluding other potential mechanisms from the empirical scope , such as perceptions of
manipulation, AI literacy, human oversight, content type, brand familiarity, and privacy concerns.
5.5. Future Research Directions
Future studies should employ longitudinal and experimental designs to determine whether the impact of AI disclosure and
authenticity remains stable over repeated exposures. Experiments could compare concise labels, detailed explanations, platform-
generated notifications, and brand-initiated disclosures across text, image, audio, and video formats. Cross-cultural studies should
examine whether this model differs between emerging and developed markets or between collectivist and individualist societies.
Researchers should also consider perceived manipulative intent, brand integrity, and emotional trustworthiness as mediating
factors. AI knowledge, brand familiarity, content engagement levels, platform type, and perceived human oversight could serve
as additional moderating factors. The use of probability sampling and behavioral outcomes , such as clicks, shares, purchases, or
unfollowing, would strengthen external validity. Future research could also distinguish skepticism from AI -related anxiety,
aversion, perceived risk, and general advertising skepticism.
5.6. Conclusion
This study examined how AI generated content disclosure and perceived content authenticity shape consumer trust in social media
marketing and whether consumer AI skepticism conditions the disclosure effect. Both predictors significantly increased trust, with
disclosure producing the stronger standardized effect. However, consumer AI skepticism negatively moderated the disclosure and
trust relationship, indicating that transparency becomes less effective among consumers who strongly question AI competence,
authenticity, motives, and accountability. The findings integrate Signaling Theory and the Persuasion Knowledge Model by showing
that disclosure can communicate honesty whi le simultaneously activating critical resistance. Overall, brands cannot build trust
through labeling alone. Trustworthy AI generated marketing requires clear disclosure, authentic content, factual verification, visible
human oversight, and communication strategies responsive to differing levels of consumer skepticism.
