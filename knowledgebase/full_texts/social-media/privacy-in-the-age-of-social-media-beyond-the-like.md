Privacy in the Age of Social Media: Beyond the Like explores the hidden dynamics
of social media and the ways our online behavior is tracked, analyzed, and
 monetized, offering an accessible overview of the interaction between social media
engagement and personal data privacy.
This book dives into the heart of social media’s greatest paradox: the pursuit
of connection versus the protection of personal privacy. It reveals how every‑
day online interactions drive engagement, targeted advertising, and predictive
analytics; build detailed user profiles; shape the content we encounter; and influ‑
ence how platforms operate.
This book explores ethical considerations, regulations, and practical strategies
for protecting personal and organizational data, while identifying emerging trends
in social norms and technology that may shape the future of privacy and social
media. Through case studies, expert insights, real‑world examples, and  accessible
explanations, readers are equipped to understand the hidden mechanics of social
media, engage online with awareness, and safeguard their digital footprint. The
authors also illustrate how the simple “Like” button transformed the digital
 landscape—what began as a quick tap of approval has become one of the most
powerful tools in the online ecosystem.
This compelling book is essential reading for marketers, business professionals,
and those working in the fields of social media, cybersecurity, and data privacy,
as well as organizations seeking to understand how platforms operate behind the
scenes. It will also be of interest to students and researchers in the fields of social
media, marketing, cyberpsychology, data privacy, digital media, and business
management.

Foreword
I have spent a great deal of my career studying why people behave the way they
do online: what draws them in, what they share without thinking, and what they
later wish they hadn’t. What strikes me most, still, is not the complexity of the
 technology. It is the gap between what users believe is happening to their data and
what is actually happening. That gap is not accidental. It is, in many cases, designed.
Drawing from my earlier research on social media privacy (or the lack of it), one
theme surfaced repeatedly across contributions from researchers in  cybersecurity,
psychology, law, and public health: we have built extraordinarily sophisticated
 systems for capturing human behavior, and extraordinarily poor ones for helping
people understand what that means for their lives. Social media platforms know
more about our habits, preferences, and vulnerabilities than most of our closest
friends, and they know it in real time, at scale, and with commercial intent. Privacy,
in this context, is not simply a matter of adjusting settings. It is a matter of literacy,
power, and rights.
This is precisely why this book matters. It takes a subject that has too often
been the preserve of specialists: data scientists, regulators, and legal scholars, and
opens it to the rest of us. It asks what really happens when we tap a like button,
accept a cookie notice, or share a location. It traces the journey from that small,
instinctive gesture to the vast data economy it feeds. And it does so without either
 sensationalizing the risks or retreating into technicality.
What I find particularly valuable here is the pairing of two perspectives
that rarely appear together in a single volume: the technical and the human.
 Cybersecurity research tends toward systems and threats; marketing practice tends
toward behavior and influence. Together, they shed some light onto something
neither fully  captures alone: the experience of being a user inside a machine that
was not designed with your interests as its primary concern. Understanding that
 experience, clearly and honestly, is the first condition for changing it.
We are at a moment when the questions this book raises are moving from
academic concern to urgent public debate. Regulators are catching up, slowly,
imperfectly, but genuinely, with the realities of surveillance capitalism. Artificial
intelligence is reshaping what platforms can infer from the data they hold. Users,
in growing numbers, are asking harder questions about the bargains they have
 implicitly accepted. This book equips its readers to participate in that conversation
with knowledge, confidence, and, crucially, agency.

Since the early 2000s, the rapid rise of social media has reshaped how people  interact,
communicate, and consume information—and the shift has added enormous value
globally. As of 2025, over 5 billion people use social media worldwide and billions
more benefit from the economic impact: social media advertising alone has grown
into a multibillion‑dollar industry, with global ad spend surpassing US$275 billion
and still climbing.
However, despite the benefits, it is also evident that this digital transformation
has come with some serious costs. Its use can impact mental health, well‑being, and
social connection, increase exposure to misleading or polarizing content on social
media, influence politics and policies, and threaten our privacy and human rights.
Social media platforms operate within a data‑driven economy, a system in
which personal information is treated as a valuable resource that fuels business
models and drives revenue. Every interaction—from likes and shares to clicks and
 comments—generates data that is collected, analyzed, and monetized, often in
ways users cannot see or control. Even simple online actions contribute to detailed
 profiles that companies and organizations use to predict interests,  influence
 decisions, and shape the content users encounter.
This was not part of the vision for the web, as inventor of the World Wide Web,
Tim Berners‑Lee intended. In his book, This is for Everyone, he said:
I had known for a while that something was wrong with the web. What was
intended to be a tool for creativity and collaboration had become divisive,
polarizing and toxic. I had often talked publicly of the two Cs of the
web:  creativity and collaboration. After 2016, I began to talk of a third C:
 compassion. I was greatly concerned that the human element of the web was
beginning to disappear. In its place we had large, faceless systems which
spied on and manipulated the user.
Today, the reality is dominated by massive social media networks with  billions
of users worldwide, where personal data has become the cornerstone of a
 multibillion‑dollar industry. The scale and concentration of power in the hands of
a few platform leaders, combined with the global reach of these networks, means
that the ways data is collected and used affect people on an unprecedented scale.

For individuals, this makes privacy critically important. It’s not just about
deciding what to share, but about understanding the consequences of your online
actions—how seemingly small interactions reveal patterns, habits, and preferences
that can affect your personal, financial, and professional life. For organizations,
privacy matters because mishandling user data can damage trust, harm reputations,
and lead to legal and financial consequences.
Social media and digital technologies have transformed privacy. Data is
 collected constantly and at an unprecedented scale, stored indefinitely, and used in
ways that are often invisible to users.
In this connected world, being aware of how your online behavior  generates
information—and why that information matters—is essential for protecting
 yourself and for holding organizations accountable for the ways they use it.
Yet, most users remain unaware of the full extent of how their data is collected,
analyzed, and used; and this raises important ethical dilemmas: platforms profit
from users’ personal information through mechanisms that are often invisible or
poorly understood, raising questions about consent, transparency, and fairness.
The scale and complexity of these networks also make it difficult for  governments
and regulators to manage how data is collected and used. When users do not fully
understand how social media platforms operate within a data‑driven economy,
they cannot make informed choices about their online actions or the information
they share.
Research shows that this lack of understanding is widespread. Only a  minority
of social media users are aware of how their data is collected, processed, and
 monetized, and many underestimate the degree to which even passive interactions
contribute to profiling and targeted content. At the same time, surveys indicate that
concern about data privacy is high: a 2023 Pew Research Center study found that
81% of adults are worried about how companies use their data, while 67% report
having little to no understanding of what happens to their personal information
once it is collected. Academic studies also show that lower awareness of privacy
risks is associated with greater self‑disclosure on social media, meaning people
often share more than they might realize.
This gap between concern and understanding highlights why education about
data practices, ethical considerations, and privacy is increasingly essential—not
just for individuals, but for society as a whole.
By exploring these issues, this book takes readers beyond the like—uncovering
what happens behind the scenes when you interact online, how your data is
 collected and used, and the ethical and privacy implications and the challenges of
managing these complex systems at scale—so that you can navigate social media
with greater awareness, control, and confidence.

A Call to Action
Privacy in the digital age requires active participation. By understanding the

 policies,  individuals and organizations can build a more secure and ethical digital

You’ll see the word “manipulation” used periodically within this book. That
term can sound heavy or threatening, and that’s understandable—in everyday
 language it often carries negative connotations. But in the context of digital privacy
and social media, we use it in a specific, descriptive, and practical way.
In Manipulation: What It Is, Why It’ s Bad, What to Do About It, Cass Sunstein—
drawing on decades of work in behavioral science—offers a definition tailored to
our digital age. He explains that manipulation refers to ways in which information,
algorithms, and design can steer choices or behaviors behind the scenes, potentially
undermining individual autonomy or well‑being. Importantly, this isn’t about
suggesting everyday use of social media is inherently harmful—it’s about recognizing
how certain unseen influences can shape decisions and why awareness matters.
