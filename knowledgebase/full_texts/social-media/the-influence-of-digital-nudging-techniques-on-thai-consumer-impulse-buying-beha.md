Abstract
Aim/Purpose: This study examined the impact of digital nudging on the online purchasing behavior of
Thai fashion consumers. It identifie d which of four digital nudging techniques, such as social proof,
scarcity, default settings, and visual cues, were the most effective in driving behavior. Additionally, it
investigated whether perceived persuasiveness mediates the relationship between these nudges and
impulse buying.

Introduction/Background: As more Thai consumers interact with online shopping sites such as Shopee
and TikTok  Shop, digital nudge strategies based on cues (social proof , scarcity) are used to drive
impulse purchasing. Online fashion retailers in Thailand are increasingly us ing social commerce
platforms and mobile applications to engage consumers through visually appealing and interactive
digital environments. These platforms incorporate digital nudging techniques  to influence consumer
decisions and encourage purchase behavior w ithout restricting consumer choice.  Although digital
nudge strategies have been shown to be successful in Western markets, there is limited research on
their impact on Thai fashion shoppers. The current study examine d how digital nudge strategies
influence impulse purchases among Thai fashion shoppers, using a mediation model that incorporates
perceived persuasiveness.

Methodology: A quantitative research design was used in this study. A total of 400 Thai online fashion
consumers who had experience purchasing clothing products through social commerce platforms such
as TikTok Shop and Shopee within the previous 90 days  were sampled via convenience sampling; an
online questionnaire (Google Form) was used to collect data. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s
alpha, and all constructs exceeded the recommended threshold of .70, indicating strong internal
consistency. Construct validity was supported through the use of previously validated measurement
scales and expert review procedu res to ensure content relevance and clarity.  Descriptive statistics,
correlation analysis, multiple regression, and PROCESS mediation analysis were used to analyze data
collected from the survey. PROCESS (Model 4) was used to test the mediation effect.

Findings: First, the analysis revealed that the perceived persuasiveness of a platform was significantly
and positively correlated with both social proof (β = .355, p < .001) and visual cues (β = .437, p < .001);
visual cues had the largest impact on perce ived persuasiveness. Second, the findings indicated that
scarcity (β = .029, p = .509) and default settings (β = −.010, p = .810) did not have a direct effect on
perceived persuasiveness, suggesting these nudging techniques do not affect how Thai fashion
shoppers evaluate a platform’s persuasiveness. Third, the study found that digital nudging had a direct
effect on impulse buying (b = .604, p < .001), as this technique triggered unplanned purchases among
consumers. Finally, while the results of digital nudging affect how persuasive a platform is to be
perceived by consumers, this perception did not translate into actual shop ping behavior. Therefore,
the mediation effect was not supported , as the confidence interval for the indirect effect included
zero (indirect effect = .060, 95% CI [−.02, .13]).

Contribution/Impact on Society: This theoretical research builds on persuasive technology models by
demonstrating a “null mediation” relationship. It illustrate d how digital nudges may elicit impulse
purchases both directly and  intuitively from an individual’s perspective, without using a person’s
perception of persuasion as a media tor. Therefore, the notion that people must be convinced by a
platform of its ability to affect them prior to acting has been challenged. In addition, since these nudge
triggers can directly stimulate specific types of consumer behavior, more rigorous ethi cal standards
need to be developed so that platform design architects create choice architectures that support the
achievement of United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 12 (Responsible Consumption and
Production).

Recommendations: Retailers should focus on using visual cues and social evidence to enhance
consumer engagement and impulse purchases, as well as on creating aesthetically pleasing, easy -to-
navigate interfaces, rather than relying on persuasive messaging. At the same time, designers need to
incorporate transparent ethics into their designs to not encourage overconsumption. Researchers
should examine potential mediators (i.e., emotional arousal, trust, and fear of missing out) in future
studies. The use of cross-cultural studies would provide an opportunity for researchers to study how
digital nudges can be used to promote sustainable consumption.

Research Limitations: The current study had limitations due to its concentration on the Thai apparel
manufacturing industry. The study also ha d an age bias toward young people. Therefore, this could
limit the applicability of the results to some degree for other industries or demographics.

Future Research: Further studies could examine how the dynamic described above relates to various
product lines and cultural environments. Future study is needed to understand the other potential
mediating factors (i.e., emotional arousal) and moderating variabl es (i.e., FOMO, trust) in digital
nudging to further clarify the psychological processes behind this. In addition to cross-cultural studies,
future studies would also be beneficial for examining various configurations of nudge and consumer
response in a variety of markets and behaviors.  Finally, future studies shou ld also integrate UN
Sustainable Development Goal 12 (Sustainable Consumption) to better assess the ethical implications
of digital nudging.

Introduction
Over the last decade, consumers of Thai fashion have dramatically changed their purchasing
behavior from in-store to a much more interactive online experience. Apps such as Shopee, Lazada,
Instagram Shop, and TikTok Shop have turned the fashion-consumption experience into a fast-paced
visual search for the latest trends. Fashion consumers no longer browse products; they navigate digital
retail environments designed to prompt rapid purchases.
Retailers in Thailand are increasingly turning to online platforms  to reach consumers who use
smartphones and access social media. Design in online commerce has evolved to include both the
aesthetic form and functional elements of a website or mobile app. Design can also encourage
purchase through features such as "Only 3 items left," "Best Seller," "X people viewing right now," and
bundled products. Behavioral economists have described these features as "nudges" that shape
behavior through design without being coercive. The term "digital nudge" was first used by Weinmann
et al. (2016) to describe how digital interfaces, including colors, buttons, social cues, timing/urgency
messages, and default settings, can influence consumers' choices on digital platforms.
The models developed by Schürmann et al. (2023), on which digital nudges for sustainable fashion
in Western countries  were based , are likely to be limited when transferred to Southeast Asia. As
argued by Suciati et al. (2025), the digital market in this region is characterized as an "integrated"
space where social interactions take place seamlessly during purchases, payment systems are easy to
use, and exclusive offers are the norm. Therefore, it can be questioned whether the same forms of

While much has been written about digital nudging in the Western e-commerce space, most prior
studies have assumed a cognitive mediation process; therefore, if a digital nudge is to effectively
prompt an action from a consumer, it must first be perceived by the consumer as a persuasive
element.
In contrast, the fast-paced, entertainment-based nature of "social commerce" in Southeast Asia -
such as TikTok Shop and Shopee Live creates a setting in which the distinction between browsing and
purchasing becomes increasingly ambiguous. As a result, there is a significant gap in the body of
knowledge regarding whether digital nudges within this cultural context act as explicit influences
toward behavior or as direct, non-conscious behavioral triggers. The purpose of this study is to fill that
void by testing a mediation model that questions the requirement for “perceived persuasiveness” to
stimulate impulse purchases.
According to Nyrhinen  et al. (2024), t he digital nudge effect that causes consumers to make
impulse buys, which are defined as "immediate and spontaneous reactions" to environmental stimuli,
is further amplified in the context of recreational fashion due to both social proof a nd visual
stimulation. It also remains unclear whether such digital nudges use a cognitive persuasive method or
serve as an intuitive trigger for the behavior. Zhang et al. (2024) suggest ed that when making
purchasing decisions on platforms using high velocity live e-commerce, many consumer decisions may
be made before hav ing had time to reflect upon the platform's persuasive ability. Therefore,
determining the degree to which a customer perceives a platform to be persuasive would provide
insight into how social commerce operates today.
To stimulate engagement and purchase intentions, online fashion retailers in Thailand have
increasingly relied on interface-related strategies. However, in the absence of empirical clarity on why
these nudges work, firms either underutilize effective strategies or resort to  overused manipulative
tricks. In a digital economy where purchasers, especially th ose who are younger, are constantly
bombarded with persuasive triggers, questions of ethically and ecologically consciou s nudging are
exceedingly pressing.
Therefore, this research aimed to explore these three factors in Thai online fashion. First, it tested
how particular techniques of digital nudging, such as social proof, scarcity cues, default settings, visual
reminders, or their manipulations may influence perceived persuasiveness. Second, it examine d
whether digital nudging directly affects impulse buyi ng behavior. Third, it tested perceived
persuasiveness by examining  whether the relationship between total digital nudging and impulse
purchasing among Thai consumers was mediated by perceived persuasiveness. In response to these
questions, the study provi des empirical evidence from the emerging digital economy and examines
the mediating role of perceived persuasiveness on digital choice architectures and their impact on
consumer behavior in social commerce systems.
Three key contributions are offered by th is study. Firstly, it shows that in Thai social commerce,
visual and social nudges use System 1 processing to bypass cognitive evaluation of persuasiveness on
platforms. Secondly, it extends nudge theory to Southeast Asia and demonstrates that 'social proof' is
a more effective tool than Western -centric nudges such as 'scarcity' or 'defaults'. Thirdly, it links the
economic efficacy of nudge theory with United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 12, advocating
for the use of the responsible choice architec ture framework to address ethical risks associated with
direct behavioral triggers.

Research Objectives
This research study attempted to reach three specific goals: first, to assess how well four different
types of digital nudge tactics (social proof, scarcity, default setting, and visual cue) compared to each
other as far as increasing the likelihood that consumers in Thailand w ould make purchases from a
fashion-related social commerce platform. Second, to determine if these tactics influence consumer
perception of the digital platform ’s persuasiveness . Third, to identify through mediation analysis

Literature Review
Digital Nudging in Online Environments
The nudge strategy, which is based on behavioral economics, describes the deliberate design of
choice architecture to guide consumers to certain behaviors , but does not restrict their freedom of
choice (Hettler et al., 2025). While traditionally utilized for government policy, the shift toward digital
interfaces has transformed nudging into an important tool for behavior. Hettler et al. (2025) point ed
out that digital nudge strategies leverage interface -based design elements , such as placement of
buttons, color highlighting, and default options , to change user behavior. Although these design
elements may seem trivial, they have a great impact on how users interact with websites and make
complex decisions in digital environments.
Mirsch et al. (2017) identified four primary forms of digital nudging , specifically: social proof,
scarcity cues, default settings, and visual cues. In the case of social proof signaling (e.g. , "best-seller"
labels, large numbers of reviews), an individual's reliance on conformity bias is targeted. When using
scarcity cues (e.g., limited time/availability), urgency is created. In addition, the selection of default
options incurs less cognitive effort when making decisions.  Finally, visual cues used in website design
guide users to certain areas of the website (Jachimowicz et al., 2019; Schneider et al., 2018).
Most research on Digital Nudges has taken place within Western laboratories,  and very little
research exists on their efficacy within emerging Asian digital economies (such as Thailand) that utilize
social commerce, mobile -first marketing strategies, and influencer content. Thailand presents a
unique market environment as social commerce and mobile commerce platforms provide both
entertainment and social intera ction. While most recent digital nudge literature has been based in
Western settings, Thailand's developing digital economy provides a new and exciting marketplace for
social commerce to combine the elements of entertainment and the use of mobile-first technologies.
Website Aesthetics and Social Media Connectivity are significant precursors that reduce transaction
costs and cue purchases, thereby increasing impulse purchasing behavior among younger consumers
(Ngo et al., 2025; Nyrhinen et al., 2024).

Impulse Buying in Online Fashion Retail
Impulsive purchases are defined as strong and long-lasting desires to buy something immediately
that do not allow for rational evaluation (Zahoor et al., 2026). The ease of instant gratification in
mobile applications is a major contributor to impulse purchasing in the digital fashion industry. While
previous studies have traditionally focused on individual characteristics that contribute to impulsive
purchasing decisions, this study evaluated whether high -velocity digital environment choice
architectures triggered impulsive response behaviorally or were cognitively mediated by perceived
persuasiveness.

Perceived Persuasiveness as a Psychological Mechanism
User perception of a system as persuasive represents how users perceive its impact (Wiafe et al.,
2022). Aesthetic design enhances credibility and mediates usage; however, people often use either
"quick and dirty" heuristics or "deliberate," "systematic" thinking when making decisions (Zhang et al.,
2014). When high levels of arousal  are experienced, one’s automatic responses eliminate reflective
thought; thus, while perceived persuasiveness may lead  to intentional reasoning, it is typically
circumvented in fast-paced, impulsive situations.
The dual-process theory provides a basis for describing how consumers interact with the design
of their choices. According to Kannengiesser & Gero (2019), decisions are made by two systems. The
first is called System 1, and it is fast, intuitive, and non -conscious. The second is called System 2, and
it is slower, more analytical, and conscious. When an individual is in a highly active digital environment,
such as shopping online, there may be so many stimuli that they use System 1 when making purchasing

The Thai Online Fashion Context
Over the last decade, Thailand has experienced an explosion of fashion e -commerce, primarily
through social media platforms that integrate commerce (Shopee, Lazada) with social media,
influencers, and entertainment (Instagram Store & TikTok Store). Thai consumers, particularly those
aged 18 to 35, drive the Thai market due to social validation from peers and the ability to conduct live-
commerce streaming. The West focuses on generating urgency through limited supply and tim e-
sensitive purchases, but in Southeast Asia, especially Thailand, a 'social commerce' model is built upon
collective trust (Purboyo et al., 2025) and as such may induce 'scarcity-fatigue.' This means that rather
than creating an unstable behavior through manufactured urgency, social proof is a more consistent
and reliable influence for these consumers.

Conceptual Framework and Research Hypotheses
The conceptual framework shown in Figure 1 combines Nudge Theory with Dual-Process Theory
to explore the conflict that exists between automatic (System 1) and deliberate (System 2) thought
processes. Using "Perceived Persuasiveness" as a mediator, the conceptual framework tested whether
consumers needed to engage their conscious cognitive abilities (System 2) to be influenced by digital
nudge strategies, or whether digital nudge strategies were able to directly trigger impulsive behaviors
(System 1). Thus, a potential "mediation gap"  was examined to see if consumer behavior may result
from platform influence without their conscious awareness.
This mediation gap study was grounded in the Persuasive Technology Model; therefore, it
examined perceived persuasiveness as a mediator to determine whether digital interfaces must be
evaluated as "persuasive" or cognitive in nature to affect an individual's behavior. The study
particularly focused on addressing the transferability of Western laborat ory findings to social
commerce in Southeast Asia. The study attempted to examine the mediation gap for indirect effects.
A lack thereof would indicate that Thai social commerce trigger mechanisms work at the System 1
level (intuitive), rather than working at the System 2 level (deliberate evaluation of persuasion).
Based on the theoretical discussions presented above, the following hypotheses are proposed:
Hypothesis 1: The effect of digital nudging techniques on perceived persuasiveness is positive
among Thai online fashion consumers.
H1.1: Social Proof has a significant positive effect on Perceived Persuasiveness.
H1.2: Scarcity positively influences Perceived Persuasiveness.
H1.3: Default Settings are positively related to Perceived Persuasiveness.
H1.4: Visual Cues are positively related to Perceived Persuasiveness.
Hypothesis 2: Digital Nudging Strategies have a positive impact on Impulse Buying of Thai online
fashion consumers.
Hypothesis 3: Perceived Persuasiveness mediates the relationship between overall Digital Nudging
and Impulse Buying behaviors among Thai online fashion consumers.

Methodology
Research Design
In this study, an explanatory, quantitative, cross-sectional survey methodology was used based on
a deductive research model. It utilized Nudge Theory and Dual -Process Theory to test hypotheses. A
primary advantage of this design type is that it can be used to identify the causal relationships and
mediating effects of nudge stimuli (digital st imuli) on impulse purchase behavior. Due to data
collection at a single point in time, the findings represented a static snapshot of how consumers react
to digital stimuli (nudges) within fast-paced environments such as social commerce.

Target Population and Sampling
The target group was composed of Thai customers between the ages of 18 and 35 years who had
bought clothing products through social media shopping sites (i.e., TikTok Shop, Shopee) over the
previous 90 days. The sample size was concentrated on  the 20–29-year-old segment to reflect the
major customer base for digital natives who purchase fashion items through e-commerce in Thailand
(Purboyo et al., 2025).
Convenience sampling was used in this study because there was no single source to sample; this
is a form of non-probability sampling. Strict selection criteria (screening) were employed to ensure the
sample was representative of the behaviors of Thai consumers engaged in social commerce. The use
of such sampling methods provided internal validity related to the behavior of high-frequency online
shoppers; however, they also limited generalizability across age groups.

Research Instruments
The assessment tool for this research study was constructed by modifying valid scales previously
used in academia so that the variables would have good content validity. A twelve -item scale, based
on the work of Schneider et al. (2020), was created to measure Digital Nudging as an overarching
construct and its four dimensions (social proof, scarcity cues, default settings, and visual reminder).
The mediating variable, Perceived Persuasiveness, was measured with a five-item scale adapted from
Kaptein and Eckles (2012), and the dependent variable of Impulse Purchase was also measured with
a six-item scale adapted from Zahoor et al. (2026).
All items used a 5 -point Likert scale to maintain consistent evaluation. Data integrity was
established using Cronbach's Alpha, and all constructs exceeded the .70 threshold (internal reliability).
Construct validity was supported through the adaptation of established measurement scales and
expert review procedures to ensure content appropriateness.

Data Collection Procedures
The questionnaires were made available on social media sites and fashion-related interest groups
for four weeks. Remedies for Common Method Bias were implemented in this research: ( a)
respondents' anonymity w as ensured to avoid Social Desirability Bias; ( b) scales w ere counter-
balanced; and ( c) there was a c lear distinction psychologically separating measures of independent
and dependent variables. In addition, Harman's Single Factor Test demonstrated that no single factor
accounted for more than 50% of the variance.

Data Analysis
Data was analyzed through SPSS v.28 to create descriptive statistics and to correlate data. The
PROCESS Macro from Hayes (2018) (Model 4) was used to assess the mediation model (Hypothesis 3).
A total of 5,000 bootstrap samples were employed. Bootstrap sampling is a non -parametric
resampling technique that has been shown to have greater statistical power than traditional tests
when assessing indirect effects. Thus, this method was selected as it provide d a robust means of
testing for the proposed "mediation gap" in Thai consumer behavior.

Results
Respondent Demographics
For this study, 400 usable responses were collected. Participants were largely female (76.0%), with
a smaller number of males (17.8%) and LGBTQ+ (6.2%). The largest age group was 20 –29 years old
(59.8%), followed by 30 –39 (12. 4%), 40 –49 (13.8%), and 50+ (14.0%). Most participants held an
academic qualification equivalent to a bachelor’s degree (71.3%), with 20% holding a higher
qualification and 8.8% a lower qualification. Respondents were mainly comprised of students (52.8%),
civil servants (19.8%), and private employees (14.8%). Monthly income varied: 35.3% earned less than
10,000 Baht, and 16.3% earned more than 50,000 Baht. Thus, the respondents were mostly digitally
active younger females who represent Thailand's primary  target demographic for online fashion
shopping. For these participants, most of their shopping occurred via digital platforms such as Shopee
(61.5%) and TikTok (22.8%). Also, 52.3% of respondents reported learning about products via social
media, followed by search engines (19.8%), influencer marketing (14.5%), and advertising (13.5%).
These results demonstrate how user-generated/organic content can shape consumer knowledge and
purchasing intentions.

Mediation Analysis
The mediation model indicated that overall digital nudging techniques directly predicted
perceived persuasiveness (b = .6518, p < .001). Digital nudging also had a significant direct impact on
impulse purchase behavior ( b = .6035, p < .001). When both digital nudging and perceived
persuasiveness were entered simul taneously into the model, perceived persuasiveness did not
statistically predict impulse buying (p = .063), suggesting the absence of a mediation effect. The 95%
bootstrap confidence interval for the indirect effects included zero, indicating that mediation was not
evident.

Discussion
The ov erall model showed an overall measure of how well digital nudge tactics explained (or
accounted for) the variation in how persuasive a given digital platform was to consumers; these results
(R2 = .495, p < .001) indicated that both visual design and social  validation had significant effects on
the way consumers perceive the persuasion of digital platforms. Overall, it appears that consumers
tend to find platforms with more prominent visual cues and fewer structural elements, such as default
settings, more persuasive.

The results demonstrate how Thailand differs from the typical Western retail model, which uses
scarcity and pressure as triggers to induce impulsive behavior. On the other hand, it was clear from
the research that Thai customers do not seem to be impacted by scarcity cues and are likely saturated
with artificial scarcity (and therefore skeptical). Instead, they appear to rely on social proof and visual
cues as indicators of collective approval and aesthetic credibility, rather than  pressure-driven cues
designed for individual consumers.

Digital Nudge Techniques on Perceived Persuasiveness
This study’s findings suggested that both Social Proof and Visual Cues were significant predictors
of perceived persuasiveness. This implied that Thai fashion consumers utilize these two cues as mental
shortcuts. High-quality visuals indicate professionalism; Social Proof may serve as a cognitive heuristic
to reduce uncertainty and ultimately mitigate the risk of making a wrong purchase decision. As such,
the "Seeing is Believing" environment as posited by Huang et al. (2025) w as confirmed. The authors
suggested that social validation in video-based formats provides confirmation for purchasing decisions
and encourages consumers to act quickly. Additionally, Filieri et al. (2021) determined that visual cues
were the most influential variable affecting behavioral intentions because they immediately present
the product attributes that allow for quick evaluation. Therefore, this research supports ho w Nudge
Theory can be developed. Inspirational nudge (social proof/visuals) outperformed pressure type
nudges (scarcity) in this context. This shows that cognitive persuasion in Southeast Asia primarily
depends on visual credibility and collective trust rather than fabricated urgency.
On the other hand, neither scarcity nor default settings were statistically significant as predictive
variables of perceived persuasiveness. The findings indicate d a "habituation effect" , where Thai
consumers' habitual exposur e to artificial urgency increase d their marketing skepticism towards
scarcity. Barton et al. (2022) also show ed that scarcity effects depend on the level of consumer
skepticism and lose their effectiveness when the market is saturated . Jachimowicz et al. ( 2019)
showed that default settings operate subliminally; instead of acting through explicitly persuasive
mechanisms, they act by influencing behavior via passive effort heuristics. Together, these results
provide a critical boundary condition for Persuasiv e Technology Theory; while structural/pressure -
based nudges may influence behavior within a behavioral domain, they tend to have little to no
influence over a cognitive realm, such as how consumers perceive the persuasiveness of a platform.

Digital Nudge Techniques on Impulse Buying
Impulse shopping triggered by digital nudges has been shown to have a very significant direct
effect statistically in digital environment s. Thus, digital nudges trigger spontaneous purchasing
behavior. In Thailand, nudg ing creates an easy way to make a purchase when consumers use their
mobile devices to browse for entertainment purposes, thus eliminating rational thought processes
and replacing them with emotional ones. Additionally, M irbabaie et al. (2023) created the term
"Nudge Stacking", which means a combination of several different types of digital nudges increases
the amount of impulsive behavior.
In addition, Parvez and Kaushik (2026) have shown that digital cues are the base driver s of
unplanned shopping. This supp orts the Stimulus Response (S -R) theory of Social Commerce from a
theoretical standpoint by showing that digital nudges act as direct stimulation (S) and cause consumer
responses (R). This demonstrates that in fast-paced retail settings, consumer environments often have
greater influence than reason.

Perceived Persuasiveness as a Mediator
One of the most important findings from this study is that there is a “mediation gap”, where digital
nudges substantially improve perceived persuasiveness but do  not increase impulse buying. This
suggests that System 2 analytical evaluations process for credibility and trust, but System 1 heuristic
impulses lead to purchase behavior. As Xie et al. (2026) have noted, with high velocity e -commerce

As such, the Direct Stimulus -Response (S-R) Pathway is suggested as an alternative theoretical

stimulus/response interaction process . T his supports the "scroll, stop, shop" paradigm, in which

Therefore, this clearly defines a pri mary boundary condition for Persuasive Technology Theory and

purchasing decisions, it will be circumvented in lower involvement/high velocity situations where the
'persuasion filter' will be replaced by immediate emotional responses.

Conclusion and Implications
A key conclusion from this study is that digital nudge s significantly affect the impulse -purchase
behavior of Thai online consumers who shop for c lothing. The primary way this occurs is via a direct
behavioral pathway; however, cognitive mediation was less relevant. It appears that Visual Cues and
Social Proof were the two most successful types of nudges examined in this study; these illustrate how
effective interface design and social validation can significantly influence consumer purchase
behaviors on social commerce platforms.
This demonstrates a mediation gap in social commerce. The lack of a significant mediation effect
of perceived persuasiven ess on the nudge to impulse buying relationship indicates that digital
stimulation may influence behaviors independently of conscious evaluation. Thus, the study has
challenged the Persuasive Technology Model as well as its most common use case, user inter action
with an interface, and proposed instead a Direct Trigger Model for high-velocity digital environments
where behavior is decoupled from conscious evaluation.
Retailers should therefore invest in high-quality images and social media-based visual marketing,
as well as a strong social aspect , instead of using countdown timers or default options. The research
shows policymakers the "hidden power of digital nudge". Because they can influence behavior directly
without having an impact on the consumer's awareness, they have the potential to lead to excessive
consumption. Therefore, the study is one example of why there is a  demand for ethical design
standards in Thailand, which would protect younger consumers' cognitive vulnerability and provide a
foundation for their long-term contribution to global sustainable development.
Lastly, the study provides important evidence for the importance of ethical design f or digital
systems. Specifically, if designers rely too heavily on urgency cues and other high-arousal strategies in
their digital products, they risk creating conditions that foster excessive consumption and lead to
negative post-purchase consumer outcomes . As such, incorporating transparency and sustainable
consumption principles in to the development of digital products is crucial for promoting a balance
between the commercial success of a product or service and the well-being of its users.

Limitations and Future Research Directions
Several constraints limit the generalizability of these results. The first is that using a convenience
sample limits the results to an 18 –35-year-old age group, which excludes other possible market
segments. Secondly, the cross -sectional nature of the study provides a single moment in time (a
snapshot) rather than allowing for causal relationships to be definitively established. Thirdly, since the
data was self-reported, there may be potential response biases associated with measuring impulsive
behaviors. Fourthly, as the study focused on the Thai fashion apparel industry, its results may not be
generalizable to products that have higher involvement characteristics or different cultural
backgrounds.
Future research needs to look at the "mediation gap" using affective mediators, including
emotional arousal, fear of missing out (FOMO), and trust, that may be a better representation of non-
conscious "System 1" decision -making processes. Research into "scarcity fatigue" and consumer
marketing skepticism in Southeast Asia is necessary due to the lack of significa nce of scarcity and
