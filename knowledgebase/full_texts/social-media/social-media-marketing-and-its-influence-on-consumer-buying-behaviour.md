Abstract
Social media has become one of the main ways firms reach, persuade and keep customers. This study asks how social
media marketing (SMM) shapes consumer buying behaviour. A structured questionnaire was given to 200 social media
users, covering four aspects of SMM exposure (engagement with brand content, influencer credibility, peer reviews or
electronic word -of-mouth, and brand trust) along with a purchase behaviour construct. Every multi -item scale was
reliable, with Cronbach's alpha between 0.755 and 0.787. A chi-square test showed that age group and purchase action
after SMM exposure are significantly associated, χ ²(3, N = 200) = 11.28, p = .010, Cramér's V = 0.24, with younger
respondents the most likely to have bought something. A one-way ANOVA found that purchase behaviour differed by
the platform people used most, F(3, 196) = 3.42, p = .018, η² = 0.050, and Tuke y's HSD showed that Instagram users
scored higher than WhatsApp/Other users. In the multiple regression, the four SMM factors together explained 11.1%
of the variance in purchase behaviour, F( 4, 195) = 6.09, p < .001. Influencer credibility (β = .246), peer reviews (β =
.162) and brand trust (β = .149) all had significant positive effects, while engagement with brand content did not (β =
.088, p = .196). In short, credibility and social proof count for more than simple exposure. The paper recommends that
marketers invest in credible influencer partnerships, genuine customer reviews and trust-building.
Keywords: social media marketing, consumer buying behaviour, influencer marketing, electronic word-of-mouth, brand
trust, purchase behaviour
1. Introduction
1.1 Background of the Study
Smartphones and cheap internet access have changed how people find, weigh up and buy products. Instagram, Facebook,
YouTube and WhatsApp are no longer just places to keep in touch; they have also become spaces for information,
entertainment and, often, a s ales pitch. Social media marketing (SMM) means using these platforms to create,
communicate and deliver offerings of value to customers, whether through paid advertising, brand pages, influencer
collaborations, short videos or conversations with customers (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010; Mangold & Faulds, 2009).
Traditional marketing mostly sent messages one way, from the firm to the consumer. Social media turns that into a two-
way, networked exchange. Consumers comment on, share and remix brand content, and they often trust other consumers
and creators more than the brand itself. The consumer decision journey runs from need recognition through information
search, evaluation and purchase to post-purchase behaviour (Kotler & Keller, 2016), and today part of it plays out inside
social media feeds.
1.2 Statement of the Problem
Firms keep raising their social media budgets, but it is still unclear which parts of SMM actually change what consumers
buy. Likes, views and follower counts are easy to measure, yet they do not always lead to sales. Marketers therefore
need evidence on t wo things: whether consumer characteristics such as age, and the choice of platform, make a
difference, and which SMM factors best predict purchase behaviour.
1.3 Objectives of the Study
• To describe the profile of social media users and their level of exposure to social media marketing.
• To test whether age group is associated with taking a purchase action after seeing social media

2. Review of Literature and Hypotheses
2.1 Social Media Marketing
Mangold and Faulds (2009) described social media as a hybrid element of the promotion mix: firms use it to talk to
consumers, but consumers also use it to talk to each other, and firms cannot fully control that conversation. Kaplan and
Haenlein (2010) sort ed social media into collaborative projects, blogs, content communities, social networking sites,
virtual game worlds and virtual social worlds, and argued that success depends on picking the right medium and taking
part authentically. Erdoğmuş and Çiçek ( 2012) found that brand content strengthens brand loyalty when it is relevant,
interactive and popular.
2.2 Influencer Credibility
Influencers act as opinion leaders, and their recommendations feel personal. Lou and Yuan (2019) showed that how
informative an influencer's content is, and how credible the influencer seems (trustworthy and expert), shape consumers'
trust in the branded content, which in turn affects purchase intention. On this view, credibility should matter more than
the size of an influencer's audience.
2.3 Peer Reviews and Electronic Word-of-Mouth (eWOM)
Hennig-Thurau et al. (2004) identified consumers' desire for social interaction, their concern for others, and the chance
to enhance their own self-worth as motives for sharing opinions online. Reviews, ratings and comments work as social
proof and lower perceived risk, especially for products that cannot be inspected before purchase.
2.4 Brand Trust
In the commitment-trust theory of relationship marketing, trust is a key ingredient of long-term exchange relationships
because it reduces uncertainty and encourages commitment (Morgan & Hunt, 1994). On social media, trust grows
through consistent, honest communication and responsive service, and it should make consumers more willing to act on
what brands tell them.
2.5 Consumer Buying Behaviour
Buying behaviour covers the decisions and actions consumers take when they select, purchase and use products (Kotler
& Keller, 2016). The theory of planned behaviour explains action through intentions, which are shaped by attitudes,
subjective norms and perceived control (Ajzen, 1991). Influencer endorsements and peer reviews work mostly through
subjective norms and attitudes, and that is what links the SMM factors in this study to purchase behaviour.

2.6 Research Gap
Many studies look at a single SMM factor, or measure only purchase intention. Fewer test, within one design, (a) whether
demographic groups differ in their purchase action, (b) whether platform choice matters, and (c) how much each of
several SMM factors contributes to purchase behaviour. This study aims to fill that gap.
2.7 Conceptual Framework
The framework proposes four independent variables (engagement with brand content, influencer credibility, peer
reviews/eWOM and brand trust) that influence one dependent variable, purchase behaviour. Age group and most -used
platform are treated as grouping variables.

H1: There is a significant association between age group and taking a purchase action after exposure to social media
marketing.
H2: Mean purchase behaviour differs significantly across the platforms consumers use most.
H3: Engagement with brand content has a significant positive effect on purchase behaviour.
H4: Influencer credibility has a significant positive effect on purchase behaviour.
H5: Peer reviews (eWOM) have a significant positive effect on purchase behaviour.
H6: Brand trust has a significant positive effect on purchase behaviour.

4. Analytical Framework
4.1 Chi-Square Test of Independence
The chi-square test compares the observed cell frequencies (O) with the frequencies expected if the two variables were
independent (E): χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E, with (r − 1)( c − 1) degrees of freedom. The null hypothesis of independence is
rejected when the p-value falls below 0.05. Cramér's V measures how strong the association is. The test assumes that
expected counts are large enough, typically at least 5.
4.2 One-Way ANOVA
One-way ANOVA tests whether the means of a continuous variable differ across three or more independent groups, by
comparing between-group variance (MSB) with within -group variance (MSW): F = MSB / MSW. Eta -squared (η² =
SSB / SST) gives the share of variance explained by group membership. A significant F only says that at least one mean
differs, so Tukey's honestly significant difference test (Tukey, 1949) is used to compare pairs of groups.
4.3 Multiple Linear Regression
The regression model is PB = β0 + β1(EN) + β2(INF) + β3(EW) + β4(TR) + ε. Coefficients are estimated by ordinary
least squares. The model as a whole is tested with an F statistic and each coefficient with a t statistic, and explanatory

6. Findings and Discussion
6.1 Major Findings
• 51.5% of respondents had bought something after seeing social media marketing, and mean scores on
every construct were above the scale midpoint.
• Age group is significantly associated with purchase action (χ² = 11.28, p = .010), and the 18 to 24 group
is the most likely to buy (67.7%).
• Purchase behaviour differs by platform (F = 3.42, p = .018): Instagram users score highest and
WhatsApp/Other users lowest.
• Influencer credibility, peer reviews and brand trust significantly predict purchase behaviour;
engagement with brand content does not.
• The model explains 11.1% of the variance in purchase behaviour, so other factors clearly matter too.
6.2 Discussion
Younger consumers were more likely to act on social media marketing, which fits their heavier use of visual, mobile -
first platforms and their comfort with in-app shopping. The platform effect suggests that format matters. Visually rich,

The regression results show that credibility and social proof matter more than exposure. Once credibility, reviews and

fits the idea that influencers work as trusted peers rather than as advertisers.
The modest R² tells us something too. Social media marketing is one influence among many, so marketers should not
expect it to explain the whole purchase decision.
7. Recommendations, Limitations and Conclusion
7.1 Managerial Recommendations
• Prioritise credibility over reach.
Choose influencers for their expertise, authenticity and audience fit, not just their follower counts, and require clear
disclosure of paid partnerships.
• Build social proof.
Encourage verified customer reviews, user-generated content and visible ratings, and answer comments and complaints
promptly.
• Invest in trust.
Communicate honestly, keep the promises made on social media, and protect customer data.
• Target by age and platform.
Aim conversion-focused campaigns at younger users and at platforms such as Instagram, and use messaging channels
mainly for service and retention.
• Measure outcomes, not only engagement.
Track conversions, repeat purchases and referrals instead of treating likes and views as success.
7.2 Limitations
The study has several limitations. The sample is limited to 200 respondents from [city/region], and the sampling method
may restrict how far the results can be generalised. The data are self -reported, so recall and social desirability bias are
possible. The design is cross -sectional, so the regression shows association, not proof of causation. Several relevant
variables, including income, product category, price sensitivity and platform -specific advertising formats, were not
measured. [Add limitations that apply to your actual data collection.]
7.3 Scope for Future Research
Future studies could use larger and more diverse samples, compare product categories, adopt longitudinal or
experimental designs, and add moderators such as income or gender. Structural equation modelling could also be used
to test mediation, for example whether trust mediates the effect of influencer credibility on purchase.
7.4 Conclusion
This study examined how social media marketing influences consumer buying behaviour, using chi -square, ANOVA
and multiple regression. Purchase action varies with age, and purchase behaviour varies with platform. Three of the four
SMM factors (influencer cr edibility, peer reviews and brand trust) significantly predict purchase behaviour, while
engagement alone does not. Taken together, the results suggest that being believed counts for more than being seen.
Firms that build credibility, encourage authentic peer voices and earn trust are in a better position to turn social media
attention into purchases.
