Introduction
“… the system of consumption is in the last instance based not on need and enjoyment but on a code of signs (signs/object)
and differences ” (Baudrillard, 1998 ). The statement above reflects the transformation of modern society , in which
consumption is no longer oriented toward use value but rather toward sign value . In modern consumer society,
individuals consume objects, activities, and lifestyles to construct self-image, social status, and symbolic recognition
in the public sphere ( Baudrillard, 1998 ). This phenomenon is increasingly evident in contemporary urban sports
practices, including padel, which has grown rapidly in Indonesia over the past few years. Sports are no longer merely
understood as physical activities for physical health but have shifted into an arena for the representation of identity,
lifestyle, social prestige, and symbols or signs indicating one ’s middle-class status and contributing to psychological
phenomena such as Fear of Missing Out (FOMO).
In the global context, padel has experienced significant growth. The 2026 Padel Global Search Report identifies
Indonesia as one of the countries with the fastest-growing interest in padel searches in Southeast Asia, with search
volume increasing by approximately 887% over the past year (Rowe, 2026). This trend is reflected in the rising number of
padel courts in major Indonesian cities such as Jakarta, Banten, Bali, and Yogyakarta. Jakarta has emerged as the primary
hub for the padel industry in Indonesia, with rapid expansion in courts, communities, and tournaments. National media
report that by 2025, the number of padel courts in Indonesia has reached several hundred units and is projected to continue
increasing as urban residents ’ interest in the sport grows. This development suggests that padel has evolved beyond a
conventional sport and has become an integral part of contemporary urban culture.
The significance of the padel phenomenon extends beyond industry growth to encompass its embedded social meanings.
According to Pierre Bourdieu (1984), cultural consumption practices, including sports, are closely linked to habitus, class
tastes, and the accumulation of economic, social, and cultural capital. The selection of a particular sport is not solely a
matter of individual preference but also reflects social position within the broader societal structure. Bourdieu (1978)
further argues that certain sports function as symbols of social class differentiation, as they require specific economic
resources, social networks, and cultural competencies. Within this framework, padel can be understood as a lifestyle
practice of the urban middle class that demonstrates social distinction through sport.
The high costs associated with playing padel illustrate its exclusivity. In Jakarta and Bali, padel court rental prices range
from Rp300,000 to over Rp400,000 per hour. Premium padel rackets may cost several million rupiah. Additional
expenses include branded sportswear, personal trainer fees, community memberships, and participation in tournaments,
which are increasingly prevalent in major cities. These factors indicate that padel is not a fully inclusive sport but is
predominantly accessible to individuals with significant accumulated capital. Thus, participation in padel reflects both
economic capacity and access to the social and cultural capital characteristic of Indonesia ’s urban middle class.
This phenomenon supports Stempel’s (2005) argument that participation in specific sports can serve as a form of cultural
capital. Sports function as a medium for expressing social identity and symbolic legitimacy among different social
groups. Wilson (2002) further asserts that involvement in modern sports is closely associated with social class, as certain
athletic activities are linked to social status and prestige. Consequently, padel should be understood not only as a physical
activity but also as a form of symbolic consumption that reveals class position within urban society.
In addition to its connections with capital and social class, the development of padel is closely linked to social media
culture and the digital economy. In contemporary digital society, identity is increasingly constructed through visual
performativity on social media platforms. Sports activities become integral to the production of self-image presented to
the digital public. Goffman (2008) describes this process as the “presentation of self, ” in which individuals display a
specific identity to a social audience. Within this context, playing padel serves not only as physical exercise but also as an
activity for posting, showcasing, and visual consumption on platforms such as Instagram and TikTok.
This is demonstrated by the proliferation of social media posts that present padel as part of a modern, healthy, aesthetic,
and exclusive lifestyle. Images of athletic attire, premium courts, and interactions within the padel community contribute
to the digital identity construction of the urban middle class. Marwick (2015) notes that social media has established an
“attention economy, ” in which individuals cultivate their social existence through the visualization of lifestyle and
symbolic consumption. In this context, padel functions as a performative medium for attaining social recognition and
digital presence. Abidin (2016) further supports this view by showing that visual practices on social media are frequently
associated with efforts to construct self-image, social status, and symbolic legitimacy in contemporary digital culture.
Representations of urban lifestyles within the padel phenomenon are highly prominent on social media. Digital content
related to padel frequently highlights athletic activities, healthy lifestyles, visual aesthetics, and social interactions that

From the perspective of consumer culture, the body and lifestyle are conceptualized not merely as biological entities or
physical activities but as ongoing identity projects that are produced and displayed ( Featherstone, 2007).
The development of padel also illustrates the commodification of sports. The padel industry is expanding through the
construction of premium courts, the sale of branded rackets and apparel, coaching services, tournaments, sponsorships,
and the creation of social media content. These developments demonstrate that sports have become integral to the urban
lifestyle and leisure industry. Horne (2006) asserts that in modern consumer culture, sports are transformed into
entertainment commodities produced and marketed according to the logic of global capitalism. Rojek (2010) further
observes that leisure activities in contemporary society are increasingly associated with symbolic consumption and the
construction of social identity.
The emergence of padel in Indonesia exemplifies the operation of cultural globalization through sports. Maguire (1999)
contends that modern sports are embedded within the global flow of popular culture, transmitting values related to
lifestyle, consumption, and transnational identity. Within this framework, padel is positioned not only as an imported
sport but also as a symbol of cosmopolitanism among Indonesia ’s urban middle class. The establishment of padel courts
in elite urban neighborhoods, lifestyle hubs, coffee shops, and entertainment centers further illustrates the integration of
sports into urban consumer culture.
Research on sports and social class has developed extensively within the field of the sociology of sport. Studies by
Bourdieu (1978, 1984) , Coakley (2021), Giulianotti (2005) , and Wheaton (2004) demonstrate that modern sports are
closely linked to issues of identity, lifestyle, and social class differentiation. However, scholarship on padel in Indonesia
remains limited, especially regarding its role as a practice of consumer culture and a site for the construction of urban
middle-class identity. Most existing research and media coverage focus primarily on the growth of the sports industry and
the rising popularity of padel. Nevertheless, this phenomenon encompasses more complex social and cultural issues,
including symbolic consumption, digital performativity, and the reproduction of social class.
Therefore, this study investigates how the sport of padel is interpreted as both a lifestyle and a sign value by the urban
middle class in Indonesia. It also analyzes the commodification of padel through various consumption mechanisms,
including courts, rackets, apparel, coaching, communities, and tournaments. Additionally, the study examines how
playing padel serves as a medium for the formation of social identity and digital presence within the public sphere of
social media. The findings are expected to contribute theoretically to the fields of sports sociology, consumer culture,
digital media, and urban middle-class studies in contemporary Indonesia.
Methodology
This study uses a qualitative approach with Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA). This methodology is selected to examine
padel as both a sports phenomenon and a social and cultural practice involving power relations, ideology, symbolic
consumption, and the construction of urban middle-class identity. Critical Discourse Analysis facilitates the investigation
of how meanings related to padel are produced, disseminated, and normalized through media texts, social media, and
digital representation practices.
Epistemologically, Critical Discourse Analysis conceptualizes language and representation as arenas of meaning
production intrinsically linked to power, ideology, and social practices ( Fairclough, 1995 ). Within this framework,
discourses surrounding padel are interpreted as media that reproduce urban lifestyles, symbolize social status, and
construct middle-class identity through cultural consumption. Accordingly, the analysis addresses not only the content of
discourse about padel but also its modes of representation, the values ascribed to it, and the socio-cultural interests
underpinning these representations.
The subject of this study is discourse on the sport of padel in Indonesia produced and circulated through social media,
particularly TikTok. The corpus consists of four TikTok posts from the de-identified accounts User A, User B, User C,
and User D. User C’s post served as supplementary evidence to triangulate the analysis of User B’s account. Accordingly,
no detailed description of User C ’s content. The four posts were purposively selected for their relevance to the research
focus and their depiction of padel as an element of urban lifestyle consumption. On social media, padel is represented as a
symbol of a modern and exclusive social identity associated with the leisure class. The selection of this corpus enabled
data triangulation while demonstrating how padel is constructed within urban consumer culture.

Further details are provided in the Ethical Statement.
Data collection was conducted using digital documentation techniques. The researcher gathered various texts, images,
captions, short videos, audio, and promotional materials related to padel from 2025 to 2026. Data were selected
purposively based on their relevance to the research focus, namely the representation of padel as a lifestyle, a status
symbol, and an identity of the urban middle class.
The analysis employs Norman Fairclough’s (1995) Critical Discourse Analysis model, which encompasses three
dimensions: (1) text analysis, (2) discursive practice, and (3) social practice. At the text level, the analysis focuses on
the use of language, visuals, symbols, narratives, and representations in constructing the image of padel as a modern urban
sport. At the discursive practice level, the study examines how padel discourse is produced and consumed through social
media, mass media, communities, and the sports industry. At the social practice level, the analysis explores the
interconnections among the padel phenomenon, consumer culture, urban capitalism, digital media, and the reproduction
of social class in contemporary Indonesia.
Jean Baudrillard’s concept of “sign value” serves as the primary analytical framework for interpreting the consumption of
padel beyond its utilitarian function as a physical sport, with emphasis on its symbolic value. Baudrillard (1998) argues
that in consumer societies, individuals consume signs, images, and social symbols to achieve recognition of identity and
social status. Within this framework, padel is conceptualized as a symbol of urban lifestyle, representing modernity,
exclusivity, health, and a particular social class. Thus, the practice of playing padel extends beyond physical activity and
functions as a medium for social existence and the performativity of identity in digital public spaces.
Mike Featherstone’s perspectives on lifestyle and identity construction further inform the analysis. Featherstone (2007)
asserts that in postmodern consumer culture, lifestyle constitutes the primary means by which individuals express social
identity. The consumption of activities, fashion, the body, and leisure contribute to the production of self-image in
contemporary urban society. Featherstone ’s framework is applied to analyze how padel is constructed as an urban
middle-class lifestyle through body aesthetics, sports attire, social communities, and social media representations.
Identity is therefore understood as dynamic and continuously produced through consumption practices and digital
visualization.
The integration of Fairclough ’s Critical Discourse Analysis, Baudrillard ’s theory of sign value, and Featherstone ’s
lifestyle perspective demonstrates that padel in Indonesia constitutes a cultural practice extending beyond its role as a
physical sport. Padel functions as an arena for the production of meaning, symbolic consumption, and the construction of
urban middle-class identity within contemporary digital society.

This phenomenon can be examined through Jean Baudrillard’s concept of “sign value.” Baudrillard (1998) argues that in

In this context, padel serves as a representation of urban social class. Participation in padel demonstrates not only athletic
ability but also the economic capacity to adopt a particular lifestyle. This observation aligns with Bourdieu’s (1984)
concept of distinction, which describes how consumption practices differentiate social classes. A preference for specific
sports, such as padel, can function as symbolic capital, enhancing an individual ’s social image in both public spaces and
on social media.
In addition, the caption “Siapa yang mulai hobby baru langsung beli keperluan semunya? (Who starts a new hobby and

with an activity to be recognized as authentic participants in that trend. Thus, the identity of a “padel kid” is constructed
not solely through participation in the sport, but also through the visible ownership of consumable accessories that
reinforce this image.
Another notable aspect of this post is the use of the song“Tampar” by Juicy Luicy, featuring lyrics that read: “Let the rain
mask the downpour, wipe away the tears, accompany my long-held disappointment. ” The selection of this song
introduces irony into the content. While the video presents a sports lifestyle that appears luxurious and enjoyable, the
lyrics suggest underlying “pain” or “disappointment” resulting from the considerable expenses involved. The inclusion
of this music reinforces the notion that participation in the padel trend necessitates substantial economic sacrifice, yet
individuals continue to pursue it to achieve specific social and symbolic experiences.

FOMO, Symbolic Consumption, and Social Existence in the Padel Trend on the TikTok Account User
B and User C
A TikTok post from the User B account dated June 16, 2025, as presented in Table 2, demonstrates the close association
between the padel phenomenon on social media and FOMO ( Fear of Missing Out ) culture. The post explicitly uses the
phrase “FOMO with capital,” suggesting that participation in the padel trend requires substantial financial resources. By
detailing expenses such as court rental fees ranging from Rp400,000 to 700,000 per hour, racket prices reaching up to
Rp10 million, sports attire, footwear, and post-game social activities, the post constructs an image of the padel trend as
emblematic of modern urban lifestyle consumption.
In digital culture, FOMO refers to the fear of missing out on significant social experiences. Przybylski et al. (2013) argue
that FOMO stems from a psychological motivation to remain connected and involved in activities valued by particular
social groups. In the analyzed post, the choice to engage in padel is driven not solely by interest in the sport, but also by the
aspiration to participate in an emerging social trend. Consequently, padel serves as a vehicle for attaining social
recognition and constructing identity within digital environments.
The text “but it’s fun, addictive ” in the caption indicates that playing padel is not merely a sporting activity but also a
social experience that provides symbolic satisfaction. Individuals feel they gain social value when they can keep up with
popular trends. From Baudrillard’s (1998) perspective, consumption in modern society is no longer oriented toward
functional needs but rather toward the search for symbolic meaning. People engage in an activity or consume an object
because of the social meaning attached to it, not merely for its practical benefits.
This phenomenon is clearly evident in the spending details shown in the post. From “cute” outfits and designer shoes to
expensive rackets and post-game hangouts, it’s clear that consumption surrounding the sport of padel is an integral part of
the social experience itself. Sports activities do not stop on the court but extend into lifestyle practices encompassing
fashion, hangout spots, and social media content. Thus, padel is presented as a lifestyle package that must be fully
embraced for someone to be considered a true part of the community.
Furthermore, these posts also demonstrate how social media functions as a space for the reproduction of a culture of
consumption. TikTok is not merely a platform for sharing sports experiences but also a space for showcasing lifestyle and
social status. When someone posts about playing padel, complete with expensive outfits and gear, that content indirectly
creates new social standards regarding how the modern lifestyle should be lived. This aligns with Featherstone’s (2007)
argument that modern consumer culture is deeply tied to self-image and the representation of identity through symbolic
consumption.

Glocalization, the Commodification of Local Culture and Lifestyle Experiences in TikTok Content by
User D
A TikTok post from the User D account, dated September 17, 2025, and referenced in Table 3 , presents padel in
conjunction with Yogyakarta ’s local cultural identity. The content highlights “Joglo Padel, ” a padel court whose
architecture blends modern industrial elements with the traditional Javanese joglo house. Features such as a gazebo,
market snacks, a prayer room, a wudu area, and gamelan music as background audio collectively construct an atmosphere
that is distinctly representative of Yogyakarta. In this context, padel, as a global sport, is adapted to local culture, resulting
in a new identity that resonates more closely with the local community.
This phenomenon is best interpreted through the concept of glocalization. According to Robertson (1995), glocalization
refers to the process by which global culture is adapted and integrated with local elements to create a new, hybrid cultural
form. Padel, initially associated with a global, urban, and exclusive lifestyle, is depicted in this post through the use of
local Yogyakarta symbols. The inclusion of a Joglo house, a traditional gazebo, a small mosque, and market snacks
demonstrates that local culture is not marginalized but rather becomes the central feature in shaping the padel playing
experience.
Captions such as “archipelago vibes,”“ Jogja vibes,” and “hidden gem in Jogja” further reinforce the construction of local
identity. The content serves not only to promote sports facilities but also to market a distinctive cultural experience.
Consequently, the padel experience is framed not solely as a physical activity but also as an aesthetic and emotional
engagement with the local environment and social comfort.
The spatial visualization in these posts illustrates how sports venues are constructed as lifestyle spaces. Features such as a
comfortable waiting area, a gazebo for socializing and dining, a shower area equipped with air conditioning and a hair
dryer, and an aesthetically designed semi-outdoor concept indicate that supporting facilities are integral to the consump-

social venues and representations of lifestyle (Featherstone, 2007). As a result, comfort, aesthetics, and visual appeal have

Conclusion
The phenomenon of padel in Indonesia illustrates that contemporary sports extend beyond physical activities for health
maintenance and have transformed into practices embedded in consumer culture, social status symbols, and mechanisms
for constructing urban middle-class identity. The research findings indicate that the rapid growth of padel in major cities
such as Jakarta, Banten, Bali and Yogyakarta exemplifies the integration of sports with consumer capitalism, digital
culture, and modern urban lifestyles.
The study demonstrates that padel serves as a marker of social distinction, signifying the position of particular classes
within Indonesian urban society. The substantial costs associated with padel, including court rentals, premium equip-
ment, sports attire, coaching, and participation in communities and tournaments, suggest that the sport is primarily
accessible to groups possessing significant economic, social, and cultural capital. Consequently, padel functions as a
symbolic arena that differentiates the urban middle class from other social groups. This finding supports Pierre
Bourdieu’s argument that cultural consumption practices, including sports, are intrinsically linked to habitus and the
reproduction of social class.
Beyond serving as a venue for social differentiation, this study also reveals that padel has undergone a process of
commodification through sport marketing mechanisms and digital culture. Padel is marketed not merely as a sport, but as
a modern, healthy, aesthetic, and exclusive lifestyle. Sports marketing strategies leverage social media, influencers, urban
communities, and lifestyle visualizations to foster social aspirations among the urban middle class. In this context, sports
become part of the lifestyle industry that sells social experiences, self-image, and symbols of exclusivity.
From Jean Baudrillard’s perspective, the phenomenon of padel exemplifies how consumer society operates through the
logic of sign value. Individuals engage in padel not only for physical benefits but for the symbolic value embedded in the
activity. Padel signifies urban modernity, health, networking, cosmopolitanism, and social prestige. The focus of
consumption shifts from the sport itself to the social image and representation generated through participation. As a
result, social media posts about padel frequently highlight visual aesthetics, sport outfit, court atmosphere, and social
interactions rather than technical aspects. This trend indicates that padel has become part of a culture of hyperreality,
where representation supersedes reality.
The study further finds that padel plays a significant role in shaping urban middle-class identity within digital spaces.
Social media emerges as the principal platform for individuals to construct self-image through sports activities.
Participation in padel functions as a form of self-branding and performative social identity. Individuals portray
themselves as modern, healthy, active, productive, and cosmopolitan through visual representations on social media.
In this context, padel operates not only as a sport but as a medium for social existence in contemporary digital culture.
In summary, the study affirms that the phenomenon of padel in Indonesia reflects broader cultural shifts in contemporary
urban society, which is increasingly oriented toward symbolic consumption, lifestyle, and digital existence. Padel has
evolved from a sport into a cultural practice that generates identity, prestige, and social legitimacy. The phrase “I Play
Padel, Therefore I Am ” serves not only as a symbolic title for this study but also encapsulates the logic of modern

Ethical statement
This study employed content analysis of publicly posted TikTok content and did not collect research data through
interviews, surveys, experiments, or other forms of intervention. Direct communication with the content creators was
limited to obtaining permission to reference and analyze their previously published content. As an additional ethical
safeguard, the researchers obtained documented written informed consent from all four account owners whose content
was used in the study: User A, User B, and User D. User A confirmed consent through a WhatsApp message; User B was
contacted through TikTok direct message and subsequently signed and returned a formal consent document by email;
D confirmed consent through TikTok direct messages. Whereas, contact with User C was similarly attempted via
WhatsApp and TikTok direct message, however, no response has been received from the content creator. No verbal
consent was used.
For Users A, B, C, and D, messaging-based consent was used because no email addresses were listed on their respective
social media profiles and they chose to record their agreement through their preferred communication platforms.
Although formal consent letters were provided to them, these two of the creators confirmed their agreement through
WhatsApp or TikTok direct messages rather than returning signed copies. The researchers retained these written
communications as documentation of informed consent.
In all cases, the researchers explained the purpose of the study, the intended use of the data, and the plan for academic
publication prior to obtaining consent; the variation in format reflects accommodation of each informant ’s preferred
communication mode, consistent with flexible consent practicesin social media research (e.g., AoIR Ethical Guidelines 3.0).
Although formal identification documents were not verified for any informant, profile photographs, subject of the
content, and biographical information publicly displayed on their respective accounts indicated that all were adults, with
no indication of minor status.
Notwithstanding these permissions, the authors intentionally de-identified all social media content creators referenced in
the manuscript-including User C-by removing usernames, profile images, and other identifying information. For User A,
B, dan D, this measure was applied as an additional ethical safeguard beyond the consent already granted. For User C,
given the absence of confirmed response, de-identification serves as the primary protective measure to respect the content
creator privacy in the absence of explicit consent. This approach was adopted as an ethical safeguard to protect individual
privacy, reduce potential reputational risks associated with scholarly interpretation, and ensure consistent treatment of all
research materials. Any reproduced materials have been limited to the minimum necessary to support the scholarly
analysis. In addition, identifiable information was de-identified because contact with User C remained unanswered at the
time of manuscript submission. To respect the content creator’s privacy and safeguard their data in the absence of explicit
consent, the authors applied the same de-identification measure to all social media content included in the manuscript.
Any reproduced materials have been limited to the minimum necessary to support the scholarly analysis.
Should the editor require additional documentation, records of the permission communications between the researchers
and the content creators can be provided confidentially upon request.
