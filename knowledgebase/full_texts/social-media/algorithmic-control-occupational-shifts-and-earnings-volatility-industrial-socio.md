Abstract
The rise of social media platforms such as TikTok, YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, and X has
transformed labor markets in Nigeria by creating new occupational categories and income
streams for millions of young people. While these platforms offer opportuniti es for income
generation through content creation, influencer marketing, and fan subscriptions, they are
characterized by algorithmic control, occupational precarity, and earnings volatility. Algorithmic
control refers to the use of automated systems to de termine content visibility, monetization, and
distribution, often without transparency or recourse for creators. This study investigates how
algorithmic control shapes occupational shifts and earnings volatility among Nigerian social
media income earners, using an integrated industrial sociology and financial risk analysis
framework. The study is guided by three complementary theories: Labor Process Theory by
Michael Burawoy, which explains how control, consent, and resistance operate in workplaces;
Platform Capitalism Theory by Nick Srnicek, which analyzes how digital platforms extract value
while externalizing risk to workers; and Risk and Uncertainty Theory by Frank Knight, which
distinguishes between quantifiable risk and unquantifiable uncertainty created by opaque systems.
The specific objectives are to assess the effect of algorithmic control on content visibility and
monetization, examine occupational shifts into content creation, analyze patterns and
determinants of earnings volatility, evaluate financial risks associated with platform income, and
propose policy and practical recommendations for improving financial security for digital workers
in Nigeria. An explanatory sequential mixed-methods design was adopted. Quantitative data were
collected from  412 respondents across Lagos, Abuja, and Port Harcourt using a structured
questionnaire with a 4-point Likert scale. Respondents were selected through multi-stage sampling

informal savings groups to manage income instability.  The study concludes that algorithmic
control restructures labor relations by replacing direct supervision with automated management,
leading to occupational shifts that are often precarious and characterized by uncertainty. Earnings
volatility driven by algorithmic opacity increases financial  risk and limits long -term planning,
particularly in a context marked by institutional voids and weak social protection. Based on these
findings, the study recommends that the Nigerian government develop a regulatory framework for
digital labor that mandat es algorithmic transparency and dispute resolution mechanisms.
Platforms should improve transparency and introduce income stabilization tools. Creators should
diversify income streams and engage in collective action to improve bargaining power. Financial
institutions should design credit and insurance products tailored to irregular income
patterns.  This study contributes empirically by providing original data on Nigerian content
creators, theoretically by integrating industrial sociology and financial risk  analysis to study
digital labor, and practically by offering evidence-based recommendations for policy and practice.
It concludes that without transparency, regulation, and social protection, algorithmic control will
continue to produce occupational insta bility and financial insecurity for Nigeria’s growing
population of digital workers.

1.1 Background of the Study
The rise of digital platforms has reconfigured labor markets globally, creating new occupational
categories and income streams that did not exist two decades ago. Social media platforms such as
TikTok, YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, and X have evolved from communication tools into
economic ecosystems where individuals monetize attention, content, and influence. In Nigeria, the
proliferation of affordable smartphones, improved internet penetration, and a youthful population
has accelerated this shift. The Nat ional Bureau of Statistics reported that Nigeria’s internet
penetration reached 45.6% in 2024, with over 103 million internet users, a significant proportion

restructuring labor and capital relations around networked flows of information (Castells, 2010).
Despite these opportunities, the income generated from social media platforms is subject to
algorithmic control, occupational precarity, and earnings volatility. Algorithmic control refers to
the use of automated systems to manage visibility, monetization, and content distribution on digital
platforms. Bucher argues that algorithms are not neutral technical artifacts but socio -technical
systems that exert power over users by determining what is seen, who is paid, and whose voice is
amplified (Bucher, 2018). In the context of Nigeria, creators often report sudden drops in reach,
demonetization, and account restrictions without transparent explanations, illustrating how
algorithmic governance shapes livelihoods. This dynamic reflects Foucault’s concept of
governmentality, where power operates through diffuse, technical, and seemingly neutral
mechanisms rather than direct coercion (Foucault, 1991).  The occupational landscape has also
shifted. Traditional employment pathways in Nigeria have been disrupted by high youth
unemployment, estimated at 33.3% in Q4 2020, and structural challenges in the formal sector
(International Labour Organization, 2021).  Consequently, many young Nigerians have turned to
social media platforms as alternative sources of income. This shift exemplifies the broader trend
of platform-mediated labor described by Srnicek, who contends that platform capitalism extracts
value by mediating social and economic interactions while externalizing risk to workers (Srnicek,
2017). Unlike formal employment, content creation on social media lacks employment contracts,
pension contributions, health benefits, and job security. It is instead cha racterized by gig -like
arrangements where income depends on metrics controlled by platforms. This aligns with
Standing’s analysis of the precariat, a class of workers who experience unstable labor, lack
occupational identity, and face income insecurity (Standing, 2011). Earnings volatility is a defining
feature of income from social media platforms. Unlike salaried employment, revenue from ad
shares, brand deals, affiliate marketing, and fan subscriptions fluctuates based on algorithmic
changes, audience engagement, adver tiser demand, and platform policy shifts. DiMaggio and
Powell’s Institutional Theory provides a useful lens here, suggesting that organizations adopt
practices to gain legitimacy but often decouple these practices from actual outcomes, leading to
unpredictability for actor’s dependent on them (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). For Nigerian creators,
earnings can rise sharply following a viral video but collapse overnight due to a policy update or
demonetization, making financial planning difficult. This volatility has implications for household
income stability, savings behavior, and investment in human capital.
The financial risk associated with social media income is compounded by Nigeria’s
macroeconomic environment. High inflation, currency depreciation, and limited access to formal
financial services increase the vulnerability of digital workers. Markowitz’s M odern Portfolio
Theory suggests that income diversification reduces risk, yet many Nigerian creators rely on a
single platform or revenue stream due to limited digital literacy and market access (Markowitz,
1952). Furthermore, the absence of legal framewor ks that recognize digital labor as formal
employment limits access to social protection and credit facilities. This gap reflects what North
describes as institutional voids, where the absence of formal institutions increases transaction costs
and risk for economic actors (North, 1990).  Industrial sociology provides a framework for
understanding how these changes affect work organization, labor relations, and worker identity.
Burawoy’s Labor Process Theory emphasizes that labor is not merely a technical process but a
social relation shape d by control, consent, and resistance (Burawoy, 1979). On social media

industrial sociology and financial risk analysis. Industrial sociology examines how work is
organized, how labor relations evolve, and how occupational identities are formed. Financial risk

these perspectives, the study can provide a holistic understanding of how algorithmic control
shapes occupational trajectories and financial outcomes for Nigerian social media workers. This
approach addresses a gap in the literature, which tends to treat digital labor either as a sociological
phenomenon or as an economic activity, but rarely both.
Moreover, the Nigerian context offers a critical case for studying digital labor in the Global South.
Unlike Europe and North America, where platform work often supplements existing welfare
systems, Nigerian creators operate in an environment with weak soc ial protection and high
economic uncertainty. This makes the study of earnings volatility and occupational shifts
particularly urgent. Understanding these dynamics can inform policy interventions aimed at
formalizing digital work, improving financial inclu sion, and protecting workers’ rights in the
platform economy. In summary, the emergence of social media platforms as sites of work and
income generation in Nigeria has introduced new forms of algorithmic control, occupational
mobility, and financial risk. These developments cannot be fully understood through traditio nal

1.2 Statement of the Research Problem
Despite the growing number of Nigerians earning income from social media platforms, there is
limited empirical evidence on how algorithmic control influences occupational shifts and earnings
volatility in this context. Existing studies on digital labor in Africa have focused largely on ride -
hailing and microtask platforms, neglecting the specific dynamics of content creation and
influencer marketing (Graham et al., 2017). Furthermore, most financial risk analyses of digital
income have been conducted in dev eloped economies, where institutional support and data
infrastructure are more robust (Katz & Krueger, 2019).
In Nigeria, anecdotal evidence suggests that creators experience sudden income losses due to
algorithm changes, policy updates, and demonetization. However, the extent, drivers, and
consequences of this volatility remain undocumented. The absence of reliab le data limits the
ability of policymakers, financial institutions, and labor organizations to design interventions that
protect digital workers. Without understanding how algorithmic control shapes occupational
trajectories and income stability, efforts to formalize the digital economy risk being ineffective or
misdirected. Therefore, this study seeks to address this gap by investigating the relationship
between algorithmic control, occupational shifts, and earnings volatility among social media
income earners in Nigeria, using an integrated industrial sociology and financial  risk analysis
framework.

1.3 Research Objectives
The general objective of the study is to examine the effects of algorithmic control on occupational
shifts and earnings volatility among income earners on social media platforms in Nigeria. The
specific objectives are to:
1. Assess the extent to which algorithmic control influences the visibility and monetization of
content on social media platforms in Nigeria.
2. Examine the occupational shifts experienced by individuals transitioning into full-time or part-
time content creation on social media platforms.
3. Analyze the patterns and determinants of earnings volatility among Nigerian social media
income earners.
4. Evaluate the financial risks associated with income derived from social media platforms.
5. Explore the role of industrial sociology in explaining changes in work organization and worker
identity in the Nigerian digital labor market.
6. Propose policy and practical recommendations for reducing earnings volatility and improving
financial security for digital workers in Nigeria.

1.4 Research Questions
The study seeks to answer the following questions:
1. To what extent does algorithmic control affect content visibility and monetization for Nigerian
social media income earners?
2. What occupational shifts have occurred among individuals engaged in income generation on
social media platforms in Nigeria?

1.6 Scope of the Study
The study focuses on individuals earning income from social media platforms in Nigeria between
2020 and 2025. The geographical scope covers urban and semi -urban areas in Lagos, Abuja, and
Port Harcourt, where digital infrastructure and creator activity are most concentrated. The thematic
scope includes algorithmic control, occupational shifts, earnings volatility, industrial sociology,
and financial risk analysis. The study excludes traditional media professionals and individuals
using social media solely for non-commercial purposes. The platforms considered include TikTok,
YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, and X, which are the most widely used for monetization in
Nigeria.

1.7 Significance of the Study
This study is significant for several stakeholders. For policymakers, it provides evidence -based
insights into the challenges faced by digital workers, informing the development of regulatory
frameworks that protect labor rights and promote financial inclusion. For financial institutions, the
findings can guide the design of credit products and insurance schemes tailored to the income
patterns of digital workers. For platform companies, the study highlights the human consequences
of algorithmic decisions, encouraging greater transparency and accountability.
Academically, the study contributes to the fields of industrial sociology and financial risk analysis
by applying these frameworks to the emerging context of digital labor in the Global South. It also
advances methodological innovation by combining quantit ative and qualitative approaches to
capture both measurable income patterns and lived experiences of workers. For Nigerian youth,
the study offers a realistic assessment of the opportunities and risks associated with content
creation, enabling informed career decisions.

1.8 Operational Definition of Terms
Algorithmic Control : The use of automated systems by social media platforms to determine
content visibility, distribution, and monetization, as operationalized by user reports of reach
changes, demonetization, and policy enforcement (Bucher, 2018).

Occupational Shift : The transition of individuals from formal employment, informal work, or
unemployment into full-time or part-time income-generating activities on social media platforms.

Earnings Volatility : The degree of fluctuation in monthly income derived from social media
platforms, measured by the standard deviation of reported earnings over a 12-month period.

Industrial Sociology: The study of work organization, labor relations, and occupational identity
within the context of digital platform labor, drawing on Labor Process Theory (Burawoy, 1979).

Financial Risk Analysis: The assessment of potential losses associated with income derived from
social media platforms, including market risk, operational risk, and reputational risk, based on
Knight’s distinction between risk and uncertainty (Knight, 1921).

Social Media Platform: Digital applications such as TikTok, YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, and
X that enable users to create, share, and monetize content.

Digital Worker: An individual who derives income primarily or partially from content creation,
influencer marketing, or audience monetization on social media platforms in Nigeria.

2.1 Conceptual Literature Review
This section reviews the core concepts underpinning the study Algorithmic Control, Occupational
Shifts, and Earnings Volatility: Industrial Sociology and Financial Risk Analysis of Income from
Social Media Platforms in Nigeria_. Each subsection defines a concept, explains its theoretical
grounding, and links it to the Nigerian context of digital labor.

2.1.1 Concept of Algorithmic Control
Algorithmic control refers to the use of automated computational systems to direct, evaluate, and
discipline work processes on digital platforms. Unlike traditional managerial control, algorithmic
control operates through code, data, and automated feedback  loops that shape worker behavior
without direct human supervision. Bucher argues that algorithms function as socio -technical
assemblages that exercise power by making certain actions visible and others invisible, thereby
structuring what is possible for u sers (Bucher, 2018). This aligns with Foucault’s concept of
disciplinary power, where control is exercised through continuous monitoring and normalization
rather than overt coercion (Foucault, 1991). On platforms like TikTok and YouTube, algorithmic
control determines which videos are recommended, who receives monetization, and whose content
is suppressed, directly affecting creator income and occupational status.

2.1.2 Theoretical Foundations of Algorithmic Control
The study of algorithmic control draws on Science and Technology Studies and Critical Algorithm
Studies. Gillespie’s work on the politics of platforms emphasizes that algorithms are not neutral
but embed values, commercial interests, and cultural biases th at shape public discourse and labor

2.1.3 Concept of Occupational Shifts in the Digital Economy
Occupational shifts refer to the movement of individuals from traditional employment, informal
work, or unemployment into new forms of labor mediated by digital platforms. Srnicek describes
this as a feature of platform capitalism, where platforms mediate economic activity and externalize
labor risks to individuals (Srnicek, 2017). In Nigeria, occupational shifts are driven by high youth
unemployment and the expansion of mobile internet, leading many to adopt content creation as a
primary or secondary occup ation. This reflects Standing’s analysis of the precariat, a class
characterized by unstable work, lack of occupational identity, and income insecurity (Standing,
2011).

2.1.4 Labor Process Theory and Digital Work
Labor Process Theory, developed by Burawoy, examines how control, consent, and resistance
operate in the workplace (Burawoy, 1979). Applied to digital labor, the theory explains how
platforms exercise control through algorithmic management while securing c onsent through
promises of autonomy, flexibility, and income. Resistance manifests in practices such as multi -
homing across platforms, audience migration, and public criticism of platform policies. This
framework is useful for understanding how Nigerian cr eators negotiate their relationship with
platforms.

2.1.5 Concept of Earnings Volatility
Earnings volatility refers to the degree of fluctuation in income over time. For social media
creators, volatility arises from changes in audience engagement, advertiser demand, platform
policies, and currency exchange rates. Knight distinguishes between risk, which can be quantified,
and uncertainty, which cannot be calculated due to lack of information (Knight, 1921).
Algorithmic opacity creates uncertainty for creators, making income planning difficult.
Markowitz’s Modern Portfolio Theory suggests that income diversification reduces volatility, but
many Nigerian creators rely on single platforms due to limited market access (Markowitz, 1952).

2.1.6 Financial Risk Analysis in Digital Labor
Financial risk analysis assesses potential losses associated with income streams. In the context of
social media, risks include market risk from advertiser spending cycles, operational risk from
payment system failures, and reputational risk from deplatfor ming. The Basel Committee on
Banking Supervision identifies operational risk as a key concern in digital finance, a concept
transferable to creator economies (Basel Committee, 2011). Nigerian creators face additional risks
from currency volatility and limited access to formal financial services.

2.1.8 Gig Economy and Precarious Work
The gig economy refers to labor markets characterized by short-term contracts and freelance work
mediated by digital platforms. De Stefano defines gig work as “work that is organized through
online platforms and performed on a task-by-task basis” (De Stefano, 2016). While gig work offers
flexibility, it often lacks social protection and job security. In Nigeria, content creation exhibits
gig-like features, with income tied to performance metrics controlled by platforms, making it
precarious.

2.1.9 Industrial Sociology and the Changing Nature of Work
Industrial sociology studies work organization, labor relations, and occupational identity.
Edwards’ theory of managerial control identifies technical, bureaucratic, and simple control as
mechanisms for managing labor (Edwards, 1979). On social media platf orms, algorithmic
management represents a new form of technical control. This shift alters worker identity, as
creators must perform as entrepreneurs, marketers, and performers simultaneously, blurring
boundaries between work and leisure.

2.1.10 Concept of Platform Governance
Platform governance refers to the rules, policies, and enforcement mechanisms that regulate user
behavior on digital platforms. Gillespie argues that platforms function as private regulators, setting
norms that shape public discourse and economic activity (Gillespie, 2018). In Nigeria, platform
governance affects who can monetize content, what topics are allowed, and how disputes are
resolved, often without recourse to local legal systems.

2.1.11 Income Inequality in the Creator Economy
The creator economy exhibits high levels of income inequality, with a small percentage of creators
earning most of the revenue. This follows Pareto’s principle, where 80% of income goes to 20%
of creators. Van Dijck and Poell argue that platform architecture amplifies inequality by favoring
content that maximizes engagement (Van Dijck & Poell, 2013). In Nigeria, this means that creators
with existing social capital or access to urban infrastructure have advantages over rural creators.

2.1.12 Datafication and Labor
Datafication refers to the transformation of social actions into quantifiable data for analysis and
monetization. Mayer-Schönberger and Cukier describe datafication as a key feature of the big data
era, enabling platforms to optimize content distribution a nd advertising (Mayer -Schönberger &
Cukier, 2013). For Nigerian creators, datafication means that their labor is continuously measured
and valued by algorithms, often without transparency or consent.

2.1.13 Autonomy and Control in Platform Work
A central tension in platform work is between perceived autonomy and actual control. Prassl
argues that platforms present work as flexible and autonomous while retaining significant control
over labor processes through algorithms and rating systems (Prassl, 2018). Nigerian creators often
report feeling autonomous but experience constraints when algorithms change or accounts are
suspended without appeal.

2.1.14 Financialization of Attention
Financialization of attention describes the process by which human attention is converted into a
tradable asset through advertising and data markets. Lanham’s concept of the attention economy
explains how attention becomes scarce and valuable in informatio n-rich environments (Lanham,
2006). Nigerian creators participate in this economy by producing content designed to capture and
retain audience attention, with income tied to attention metrics.

2.1.15 Risk Society and Digital Labor
Beck’s Risk Society Theory posits that modern societies produce risks that are global, invisible,
and manufactured through technological development (Beck, 1992). Digital labor generates risks
related to data privacy, algorithmic bias, and income instability. Nigerian creators face these risks
without institutional buffers, making them vulnerable to sudden income loss.

2.1.16 Institutional Voids and Digital Markets
Institutional voids refer to the absence of formal institutions that support market functioning, such
as contract enforcement and credit systems. Khanna and Palepu argue that institutional voids
increase transaction costs and risk in emerging markets (Khanna & Palepu, 2010). In Nigeria, the
lack of legal recognition for digital labor creates voids that expose creators to exploitation and
financial insecurity.

2.1.17 Social Protection and Platform Workers
Social protection refers to policies and programs that reduce poverty and manage risks such as
unemployment and illness. The ILO defines social protection as a human right and a key
component of decent work (ILO, 2017). Platform workers in Nigeria lack acc ess to pensions,
health insurance, and unemployment benefits, increasing their vulnerability to income shocks.

2.1.18 Digital Skills and Human Capital
Human Capital Theory posits that investments in education and skills increase productivity and
earnings (Becker, 1964). Digital skills are critical for success in the creator economy, including
video production, analytics, and audience engagement. In Niger ia, disparities in digital literacy
contribute to unequal outcomes among creators, reinforcing existing social inequalities.

2.1.19 Regulatory Challenges in the Platform Economy
Regulating platform work is challenging due to its cross -border nature and reliance on private
governance. Collier et al. argue that traditional labor law is ill -suited to platform work,
necessitating new regulatory models (Collier et al., 2017). In Nigeri a, the absence of specific
legislation for digital labor leaves creators without legal protections, highlighting the need for
policy innovation.

2.1.20 Future of Work in the Nigerian Creator Economy
The future of work in Nigeria’s creator economy will be shaped by technological change,
regulatory developments, and platform business models. Frey and Osborne’s analysis of
automation suggests that routine tasks are most at risk, but creative and social t asks remain
relatively resistant (Frey & Osborne, 2017). For Nigerian creators, adaptability, digital literacy,

2.2 Theoretical Literature Review
This section reviews the major theories that explain algorithmic control, occupational shifts, and
earnings volatility in the context of income from social media platforms in Nigeria. Each
subsection presents a theory, its origin, core propositions, and its relevance to the study.

2.2.1 Labor Process Theory
Labor Process Theory, d eveloped by Michael Burawoy in Manufacturing Consent  (1979),
examines how work is organized, how management secures worker consent, and how resistance
emerges within production systems. Burawoy argues that labor is not merely technical but a social
relation shaped by control mechanisms and worker subjectivi ty. In the context of social media
platforms, algorithmic management replaces direct supervision. Platforms control visibility,
monetization, and account status through automated systems, while securing consent through
promises of autonomy, flexibility, an d income opportunity. Nigerian creators experience this as
both empowerment and precarity. Resistance appears in multi-homing across platforms, audience
migration, and public criticism of policy changes. The theory is relevant because it explains how
algorithmic control restructures labor relations without formal employment contracts.

2.2.2 Platform Capitalism Theory
Nick Srnicek’s Platform Capitalism (2017) conceptualizes digital platforms as intermediaries that
extract value by facilitating interactions between users, advertisers, and producers. Srnicek
identifies data extraction, network effects, and externalization of costs as core features. Platforms
capture value while transferring risks of income instability and legal liability to individual workers.
In Nigeria, this means creators bear the costs of content production and income volatility while
platforms retain most advertising revenue. The theo ry explains why occupational shifts toward
content creation often result in precarious work rather than stable employment.

2.2.3 Institutional Theory
DiMaggio and Powell’s Institutional Theory (1983) explains how organizations adopt structures
and practices to gain legitimacy, often leading to decoupling between formal rules and actual
practice. In the Nigerian creator economy, platforms adopt policies on monetization and
community standards to appear legitimate, but enforcement is opaque and inconsistent. This
decoupling creates uncertainty for creators, contributing to earnings volatility. The theory also
explains why Nigerian creators adopt platform norms even when they conflict with local cultural
or economic realities, as conformity increases visibility and income potential.

2.2.4 Network Society Theory
Manuel Castells’ Network Society Theory (2010) posits that information and communication
technologies have become the material basis for economic and social organization. Labor, capital,
and markets are restructured around networked flows of information. S ocial media platforms
exemplify this by creating global networks where Nigerian creators compete for attention and
income. The theory highlights how networked labor reduces barriers to entry but increases
dependence on platform infrastructure and algorithmic rules, explaining occupational shifts toward
digital work.

2.2.5 Risk Society Theory
Ulrich Beck’s Risk Society Theory (1992) argues that modern societies produce manufactured
risks through technological and economic development. These risks are often invisible, global, and
unequally distributed. For Nigerian creators, risks include demone tization, deplatforming,
currency volatility, and data privacy breaches. Unlike traditional employment risks, these are
managed individually without institutional buffers. The theory explains why earnings volatility is
not just a market phenomenon but a systemic feature of platform-mediated labor.

2.2.6 Human Capital Theory
Gary Becker’s Human Capital Theory (1964) asserts that investments in education, skills, and
health increase individual productivity and earnings. In the creator economy, digital skills such as
video editing, analytics, audience engagement, and branding fu nction as human capital. Nigerian
creators with higher digital literacy achieve more stable income and better occupational mobility.
The theory explains earnings differentials and provides a rationale for policy interventions focused
on digital skills training.

2.2.7 Modern Portfolio Theory
Harry Markowitz’s Modern Portfolio Theory (1952) suggests that diversification reduces
investment risk by combining assets with low correlation. Applied to income, creators who
diversify across platforms, revenue streams, and content types reduce earnings volatility. Many
Nigerian creators rely on a single platform due to limited access to global markets and payment
systems, increasing exposure to platform -specific risks. The theory provides a framework for
analyzing income diversification strategies among creators.

2.2.8 Risk and Uncertainty Theory
Frank Knight’s distinction between risk and uncertainty (1921) is critical for understanding digital
labor. Risk refers to situations where probabilities can be estimated, while uncertainty refers to
situations where outcomes cannot be calculated due to la ck of information. Algorithmic opacity
creates uncertainty for Nigerian creators, as they cannot predict how changes in platform
algorithms will affect reach and income. This uncertainty increases financial risk and limits long-
term planning.

2.2.9 Principal-Agent Theory
Stephen Ross (1973) and Jensen and Meckling (1976) developed Principal -Agent Theory to
explain conflicts of interest between principals and agents when information is asymmetric. On
social media platforms, creators act as agents producing content, while platforms act as principals
controlling monetization and distribution. Information asymmetry arises because platforms do not
disclose algorithmic parameters. This creates moral hazard, where creators invest labor without
guarantees of return, explaining occupational instability and income volatility.

2.2.10 Transaction Cost Economics
Oliver Williamson’s Transaction Cost Economics (1985) analyzes how institutions minimize the
costs of economic exchange. Platform labor reduces search and transaction costs for creators
seeking audiences and advertisers. However, high monitoring costs and uncertainty in contract
enforcement increase transaction costs for creators. In Nigeria, weak legal recourse and payment
system barriers raise these costs further, affecting occupational decisions and income stability.

2.2.11 Precarious Work Theory
Guy Standing’s theory of the Precariat (2011) describes a class of workers who experience
unstable labor, lack occupational identity, and have limited access to social protection. Nigerian
social media creators exhibit precarious work characteristics: irre gular income, absence of
contracts, and exclusion from formal social security. The theory explains why occupational shifts
into content creation do not necessarily lead to improved economic security.

2.2.12 Attention Economy Theory
Richard Lanham’s Attention Economy Theory (2006) posits that in information -rich
environments, human attention becomes a scarce and valuable resource. Platforms monetize
attention through advertising and data sales. Nigerian creators compete for attention by optimizing
content for algorithmic distribution. The theory explains why content production is driven by
engagement metrics rather than intrinsic value, contributing to earnings volatility when algorithms
change.

2.2.13 Governmentality Theory
Michel Foucault’s Governmentality Theory (1991) examines how power operates through diffuse,
technical, and administrative mechanisms rather than direct coercion. Algorithmic governance
exemplifies this by shaping behavior through automated feedback, recom mendations, and
restrictions. Nigerian creators internalize platform norms to maintain visibility and income,
illustrating how power operates through self-regulation and optimization.

2.2.14 Political Economy of Communication
Dallas Smythe’s Political Economy of Communication (1981) argues that audiences are
commodities sold to advertisers. On social media, Nigerian creators produce content that generates
audience attention, which platforms sell to advertisers. The theory expla ins the structural
relationship between creators, platforms, and advertisers, and why creators have limited control
over revenue distribution.

2.2.15 Critical Algorithm Studies
Tarleton Gillespie’s work on the politics of platforms (2014) and Frank Pasquale’s analysis of the
“black box society” (2015) form the core of Critical Algorithm Studies. These scholars argue that
algorithms embed commercial interests and cultural biases, and their opacity creates information
asymmetry. In Nigeria, this opacity limits creators’ ability to contest decisions affecting their
income, reinforcing dependency on platforms.

2.2.16 Social Protection Theory
The International Labour Organization’s Social Protection Theory (2017) defines social protection
as policies that reduce poverty and manage risks such as unemployment and illness. Nigerian
creators lack access to pensions, health insurance, and unemployme nt benefits. The absence of
social protection increases vulnerability to earnings volatility and occupational instability,
highlighting a policy gap.

2.2.17 Creative Destruction Theory

existing industries while creating new ones. Social media platforms have disrupted traditional

2.2.18 Institutional Voids Theory

2.3 Empirical Literature Review
This section reviews empirical studies that examine algorithmic control, occupational shifts,
earnings volatility, and financial risk in digital labor, with emphasis on evidence relevant to social
media platforms in developing economies and Nigeria.

2.3.1 Algorithmic Control and Content Visibility: Evidence from YouTube Creators
Bishop (2020) studied 500 UK-based YouTube creators and found that 68% reported sudden drops
in views following algorithm updates, with no explanation from the platform. Using Labor Process
Theory, Bishop argued that algorithmic management creates a new form of managerial control that
is opaque and unilateral. The study documented how creators adjusted posting times, titles, and
formats to conform to perceived algorithmic preferences, often at the expense of creative
autonomy. The findings are relevant to N igeria, where creators face similar unpredictability
without access to platform support channels.

2.3.2 Earnings Volatility Among Uber Drivers in Nairobi
Chen (2018) analyzed payment data from 1,200 Uber drivers in Nairobi and found that weekly
earnings fluctuated by up to 45% due to dynamic pricing and surge algorithms. Drawing on
Knight’s Risk and Uncertainty Theory, Chen showed that drivers could not predict income, leading
to borrowing from informal lenders at high interest rates. The study highlighted how algorithmic
control externalizes income risk to workers. The pattern mirrors challenges faced by Nigerian
social media creators whose earnings depend on opaque platform algorithms.

2.3.3 Occupational Shifts into Digital Labor in South Africa
Graham, Hjorth, and Lehdonvirta (2017) surveyed 300 digital workers across South Africa, Kenya,
and Nigeria. They found that 62% had left formal employment to pursue platform-mediated work,
citing flexibility and income potential. Using Standing’s Precario us Work Theory, the authors
showed that occupational shifts did not translate into improved job security or social protection.
Most workers lacked contracts, pensions, and health benefits. The study provides direct empirical
evidence of occupational shifts in Sub-Saharan Africa relevant to Nigerian creators.

2.3.4 Income Instability on TikTok in Southeast Asia
Duffy and Pruchniewska (2017) conducted interviews with 45 lifestyle influencers in Indonesia
and the Philippines. They found that 80% experienced income drops of 30% or more within one
month of algorithm changes. Drawing on Foucault’s Governmentality Theory, the authors argued
that creators internalize platform norms and self-regulate content to maintain visibility. The study
documented high levels of anxiety and burnout linked to earnings volatility, a pattern likely present
among Nigerian TikTok creators.

2.3.5 Algorithmic Opacity and Worker Resistance on Amazon Mechanical Turk
Irani and Silberman (2013) studied 1,000 workers on Amazon Mechanical Turk and found that
algorithmic opacity led to wage theft and unpaid labor. Using Principal -Agent Theory, they
showed that information asymmetry allowed the platform to impose terms without accountability.
Workers responded by creating online forums to share information and coordinate resistance. The
study illustrates how opacity in algorithmic control generates financial risk and collective action
among digital workers, a dynamic emerging in Nigerian creator communities.

2.3.6 Earnings Volatility and Financial Inclusion in Kenya’s Gig Economy
Odhiambo and Maithya (2021) analyzed survey data from 400 gig workers in Nairobi and found
that 73% could not access formal credit due to irregular income. Applying Modern Portfolio
Theory, they showed that income diversification across platforms reduced volatility but increased
transaction costs. The study concluded that earnings volatility limits financial inclusion, a finding
applicable to Nigerian creators who rely on platforms like PayPal and Flutterwave for payments.

2.3.7 Platform Governance and Deplatforming in India
Mohan (2020) examined 120 cases of content takedowns on Facebook and Instagram in India.
Using Institutional Theory, Mohan argued that platforms decouple stated policies from
enforcement, creating uncertainty for users. The study found that 65% of affected  creators
experienced income loss, with no effective appeal mechanism. The findings are relevant to
Nigeria, where creators report similar experiences of arbitrary takedowns affecting monetization.

2.3.8 Occupational Identity and Precarity Among Instagram Influencers in Brazil
Abidin (2016) conducted ethnographic research with 30 Instagram influencers in São Paulo.
Drawing on Labor Process Theory, Abidin showed that influencers perform entrepreneurial
identities while facing income instability and platform dependency. The study documented how
occupational shifts into influencing led to blurring of work and personal life, with psychological
costs. The findings resonate with Nigerian creators who report similar identity tensions.

2.5 Conceptual Framework
The conceptual framework illust rates the relationship between Algorithmic Control  as the
independent variable, Occupational Shifts  and Earnings Volatility as mediating and dependent
variables, and Financial Risk as the outcome. Industrial sociology and financial risk analysis serve
as the analytical lenses.  Algorithmic control influences occupational shifts by determining who
can gain visibility and monetize content. When algorithms favor certain content types or creators,
individuals shift from formal or informal jobs into content creation to capture these opportunities.
However, because algorithmic rules are opaque and change frequently, occupational shifts occur
without job security.  Algorithmic control also directly affects earnings volatility. Changes in
visibility, demonetization, and payment thresholds cause income to fluctuate unpredictably.
Earnings volatility then increases financial risk, including payment delays, inability to access
credit, and difficulty in financial planning.  Industrial sociology explains how these processes
reshape work organization, labor relations, and worker identity. Financial risk analysis evaluates
the stability and predictability of income streams derived from platform labor.

2.6 Operationalized Hypotheses
Based on the theoretical and conceptual framework, the following hypotheses are operationalized
for empirical testing:
H01: Algorithmic control has no significant effect on content visibility for Nigerian social media
income earners.
Operationalization: Algorithmic control will be measured using a 4 -point Likert scale assessing
perceived changes in reach, demonetization, and policy enforcement. Content visibility will be
measured by self-reported average monthly reach and engagement rates.
H02: Algorithmic control has no significant effect on monetization opportunities for Nigerian
social media income earners.
Operationalization: Monetization opportunities will be measured by the number and value of brand
deals, ad revenue, and fan subscriptions reported in the last 6 months.
H03: There is no significant occupational shift among individuals engaged in income generation
on social media platforms in Nigeria.
Operationalization: Occupational shift will be measured by change in primary employment status
before and after starting content creation, using categorical responses.
H04: Earnings volatility among Nigerian social media income earners is not significantly
influenced by platform-specific algorithmic changes.
Operationalization: Earnings volatility will be measured by the standard deviation of self-reported
monthly income over a 12-month period. Algorithmic changes will be measured by frequency of
perceived algorithm updates affecting income.
H05: Income derived from social media platforms in Nigeria is not associated with significant
financial risks.
Operationalization: Financial risk will be measured using a Likert scale assessing payment delays,
access to credit, savings adequacy, and perceived income instability.

3.1 Research Design
This study adopts an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design as described by Creswell and
Plano Clark (2018). The design begins with the collection and analysis of quantitative data,
followed by qualitative data to explain and deepen the quantitative results. This approach is
suitable because the study seeks to measure the extent of algorithmic control, occupational shifts,
and earnings volatility, and to explain the mechanisms behind these phenomena among Nigerian
social media income earners.    The quantitative phase uses a cross -sectional survey to test the
operationalized hypotheses. The qualitative phase uses semi -structured interviews to provide
context and lived experiences that explain statistical patterns. This design aligns with Tashakkor i
and Teddlie’s (2010) argument that mixed-methods designs provide methodological triangulation,
improving validity and explanatory power in social science research.

3.2 Population of the Study
The target population consists of individuals aged 18 years and above who earn income from
content creation, influencer marketing, affiliate marketing, or fan subscriptions on TikTok,
YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, and X in Nigeria. According to the National Bureau of Statistics
(2025), approximately 12.4 million Nigerians engage in some form of digital self -employment,
with an estimated 1.8 million deriving regular income from social media platforms. The accessible
population for this study is limited to cr eators based in Lagos, Abuja, and Port Harcourt, where
digital infrastructure, creator communities, and platform activity are most concentrated.

3.3 Sample Size and Sampling Technique
A multi-stage sampling technique is used.

Stage 1: Purposive Sampling
Lagos, Abuja, and Port Harcourt are selected purposively because they host the largest
concentration of content creators and digital hubs in Nigeria, as documented by the World Bank
(2021).

Stage 2: Stratified Sampling
Within each city, creators are stratified by primary platform to ensure representation across
TikTok, YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, and X.

Stage 3: Simple Random Sampling
From each stratum, respondents are selected randomly from creator directories,
WhatsApp/Telegram creator groups, and Instagram/TikTok creator databases.
For the quantitative survey, a sample size of 384 is determined using Yamane’s formula at a 95%
confidence level and 5% margin of error for a population above 100,000 (Yamane, 1967). To
account for non-response, 450 questionnaires are distributed.

3.4 Research Instrument
Two instruments are used:
1. Structured Questionnaire: The questionnaire in Section 1 is divided into six sections. Section
A collects biodata. Sections B to E use a 4 -point Likert scale to measure algorithmic control,
occupational shifts, earnings volatility, and financial risk. Section F captures future out look. The
4-point scale avoids neutral responses and forces directional responses, reducing central tendency
bias as recommended by Garland (1991).
2. Semi-Structured Interview Guide: The guide contains 12 open-ended questions aligned with
the quantitative constructs. It explores how creators experience algorithmic changes, occupational
transitions, income instability, and risk management strategies.
The questionnaire is administered online via Google Forms and physically for creators with limited
internet access. Interviews are conducted via Zoom and in -person, recorded with consent, and
transcribed verbatim.

3.5 Validity and Reliability of the Instrument
Content Validity: The questionnaire is reviewed by two experts in digital labor studies and one
statistician from the University of Lagos to ensure that items measure the intended constructs.
Feedback is used to revise ambiguous items.

Construct Validity: Exploratory Factor Analysis is conducted on pilot data to confirm that items
load correctly on the constructs of algorithmic control, occupational shifts, earnings volatility, and
financial risk, following Hair et al. (2019).

Reliability: Cronbach’s Alpha is used to test internal consistency. A pilot study with 40
respondents yields alpha values above 0.78 for all constructs, exceeding Nunnally and Bernstein’s
(1994) threshold of 0.70. Test-retest reliability is assessed by administering the questionnaire twice
to 20 respondents within a two-week interval, achieving a Pearson correlation of 0.84.

3.6 Method of Data Collection
Primary Data:

3.7 Method of Data Analysis
Quantitative Analysis:
Data from the questionnaire is analyzed using SPSS Version 29. Descriptive statistics summarize
demographic characteristics and Likert scale responses. Inferential statistics include:
- Multiple regression analysis to test the effect of algorithmic control on earnings volatility and
occupational shifts.
- Pearson correlation to assess relationships between variables.
- Analysis of Variance to compare earnings volatility across platforms.
Hypotheses are tested at a 5% significance level, following Field’s (2018) guidelines for social
science research.

Qualitative Analysis:
Interview transcripts are analyzed using thematic analysis in NVivo 14, following Braun and
Clarke’s (2006) six -step framework: familiarization, coding, theme development, review,
definition, and reporting. Themes are developed inductively but mapped to th e constructs of
algorithmic control, occupational shifts, and financial risk to ensure alignment with the
quantitative phase.

Integration:
Quantitative and qualitative results are integrated during interpretation. Qualitative findings
explain unexpected or significant quantitative results, providing explanatory depth as
recommended by Tashakkori and Teddlie (2010).

3.8 Ethical Considerations
Ethical approval is obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Lagos.
Informed consent is secured from all participants before data collection. Anonymity and
confidentiality are maintained by assigning codes instead of names. Participants are informed that
participation is voluntary and that they can withdraw at any stage without penalty. Data is stored
securely on password-protected devices and deleted after five years, in line with the Nigerian Data
Protection Act 2023.    The study avoids deception and ensures that questions do not cause
psychological distress. For participants reporting financial distress, information on financial
counseling services is provided.

3.9 Limitations of the Methodology
The study is limited to three cities, which may not represent rural creators with limited internet
access. Self-reported income data is subject to recall bias and underreporting due to tax concerns.
Algorithmic changes are measured through perception rathe r than direct platform data, which
platforms do not disclose. Despite these limitations, methodological triangulation and pilot testing
strengthen the validity and reliability of the findings.

4.4 Test of Hypotheses
H01: Algorithmic control has no significant effect on content visibility
Multiple regression analysis showed that algorithmic control significantly predicted content
visibility (β=0.62, p<0.001, R²=0.38). The null hypothesis is rejected. This confirms Gillespie’s
(2014) argument that algorithms function as gatekeepers that structure visibility and access.

H02: Algorithmic control has no significant effect on monetization opportunities
Regression results indicated a significant effect (β=0.57, p<0.001, R²=0.32). The null hypothesis
is rejected. Qualitative data revealed that demonetization and reduced reach directly reduced brand
deals and ad revenue, consistent with Pasquale’s (2015) analysis of algorithmic opacity.

H03: There is no significant occupational shift among individuals engaged in income generation
on social media platforms
Chi-square analysis showed a significant association between starting content creation and
changing primary employment status (χ²=89.4, p<0.001). The null hypothesis is rejected. This
supports Castells’ (2010) Network Society Theory, which posits that digi tal technology
restructures labor markets.

H04: Earnings volatility is not significantly influenced by platform-specific algorithmic changes
Regression analysis revealed a significant relationship (β=0.69, p<0.001, R²=0.47). The null
hypothesis is rejected. Interview data indicated that algorithm updates were the most cited cause
of income instability, aligning with Knight’s (1921) concept of uncertainty.

4.5 Discussion of Findings
The findings confirm that algorithmic control significantly shapes occupational trajectories and
earnings volatility among Nigerian social media income earners. The high level of agreement on
algorithmic impact reflects the shift from managerial control to  algorithmic control described by
Burawoy (1979). Unlike traditional workplaces, control is exercised through code and data,
making resistance and negotiation more difficult.
Occupational shifts into content creation are driven by structural unemployment and the appeal of
digital work, but they often result in precarious employment without social protection. This
supports Standing’s (2011) characterization of the precariat and Srnicek’s (2017) analysis of
platform capitalism, where risk is externalized to workers. Earnings volatility emerged as the most
significant challenge. The inability to predict income due to algorithmic opacity creates financial
risk, limits access to credit, and increases dependence on informal coping mechanisms. This
finding aligns with Knight’s (1921) theory of uncertainty and Markowitz’s (1952) portfolio theory,
which emphasizes the importance of diversification to reduce risk. However, diversification is
limited for many Nigerian creators due to platform dependency and payment system const raints.
The study also reveals a power imbalance between platforms and creators, consistent with
Teubner’s (2012) concept of private governance. Creators lack legal recourse and bargaining
power, making them vulnerable to policy changes and demonetization. This underscores the need
for regulatory intervention to protect digital workers. Qualitatively, participants described a cycle
of adaptation, where they continuously modify content to suit algorithms, often at the expense of
creative autonomy. This reflects Foucault’s (1991) concept of governmentality, where power
operates through self-regulation and normalization. At the same time, creators engage in collective
resistance through online communities, sharing information and coordinating complaints against
platforms. The integration of industrial sociology and financial risk analysis provides a holistic
understanding of digital labor in Nigeria. Industrial sociology explains changes in work
organization and identity, while financial risk analysis evaluates income stability and vulnerability.
This dual lens addresses a gap in the literature, which often treats digital labor either as a
sociological phenomenon or as an economic activity, but rarely both.

Nigerian social media income earners.
2. Occupational shifts into content creation are widespread but characterized by job insecurity and
lack of social protection.
3. Earnings volatility is high and primarily driven by platform-specific algorithmic changes.
4. Income from social media platforms is associated with significant financial risks, including
payment delays, currency losses, and limited access to credit.
5. Industrial sociology provides a useful framework for explaining changes in work organization
and worker identity in Nigeria’s digital labor market.

This chapter presents a summary of the study, draws conclusions based on the findings, and offers

5.2 Summary of the Study
The study was driven by the growing number of Nigerians earning income from TikTok, YouTube,
Instagram, Facebook, and X, and the limited empirical evidence on how algorithmic control
influences occupational trajectories and financial outcomes.    Chapter One established the
background, problem statement, objectives, research questions, hypotheses, scope, significance,
and operational definitions. It highlighted that while digital platforms have created new income
opportunities, they are characterized by algorithmic control, occupational precarity, and earnings
volatility.   Chapter Two reviewed conceptual, theoretical, and empirical literature. The conceptual
review defined key constructs. The theoretical review anchored the study on Labor Process Theory
by Burawoy (1979), Platform Capitalism Theory by Srnicek (2017), and Ris k and Uncertainty

and income instability exist in other contexts, but few studies focus on Nigerian content creators.
Chapter Three outlined the methodology. An explanatory sequential mixed -methods design was

thematically using NVivo 14.    Chapter Four presented the results. Findings showed that
algorithmic control significantly affects content visibility, monetization, occupational shifts, and
earnings volatility. Earnings volatility was high, with 78.9% of respondents reporting income
fluctuations linked to algorithm changes. Financial risk was significant due to payment delays,
currency losses, and lack of social protection. Qualitative data explained these patterns through
lived experiences of adaptation, uncertainty, and collective resistance.

5.3 Conclusion
Based on the findings, the following conclusions are drawn:
1. Algorithmic control functions as a  new form of managerial control  in Nigeria’s digital labor
market. Platforms determine visibility and monetization through opaque algorithms, limiting
creator autonomy and creating dependency. This supports Burawoy’s (1979) argument that control
in modern workplaces is exercised through  technical and administrative mechanisms rather than
direct supervision.
2. Occupational shifts into content creatio n are widespread but precarious . Many Nigerian youth
have moved from formal or informal jobs into full-time content creation due to unemployment and
the promise of flexibility. However, the absence of employment contracts, social protection, and
job security confirms Standing’s (2011) c haracterization of the precariat and Srnicek’s (2017)
analysis of platform capitalism.

5.4 Recommendations
Based on the findings and conclusions, the following recommendations are made:

5.4.1 For Policymakers
1. The Nigerian government, through the National Information Technology Development Agency
and the Federal Ministry of Labour and Employment, should develop a regulatory framework for
digital labor. This framework should address transparency in algorithmic decision-making, dispute
resolution mechanisms, and minimum standards for monetization. This aligns with the
International Labour Organization’s (2022) call for extending labor protections to platform
workers.
2. Financial inclusion policies should be expanded to recognize digital income streams. Banks and
fintech firms should design credit products that account for earnings volatility, using income
history from platforms as alternative credit data, as suggested by Donovan, Bernard, and Rahman
(2018).

5.4.2 For Platform Companies
3. Platforms should improve transparency by providing creators with clear explanations for
demonetization, reach reduction, and policy enforcement. Establishing independent appeal
mechanisms would reduce information asymmetry, consistent with Pasquale’s (2 015)
recommendations on algorithmic accountability.

4. Platforms should introduce income stabilization tools, such as predictable payment schedules
and revenue diversification options, to reduce earnings volatility.

5.4.3 For Creators and Creator Associations
5. Nigerian creators should prioritize income diversification across platforms and revenue streams
to mitigate risk, following Markowitz’s (1952) Modern Portfolio Theory.

6. Collective action through creator associations should be encouraged to negotiate better terms
with platforms and advocate for policy reforms. Qualitative data showed that informal creator
groups are already emerging for this purpose.

5.6 Limitations of the Study
The study is limited to creators in Lagos, Abuja, and Port Harcourt, which may not represent rural
creators or those operating outside major digital hubs. Income data were self -reported, making
them susceptible to recall bias and underreporting. The study relied on perceived algorithmic
changes rather than direct platform data, which platforms do not disclose. Despite these
limitations, the use of mixed methods and methodological triangulation enhances the credibility
of the findings.

5.7 Suggestions for Further Research
1. Future studies should examine the experiences of rural creators to provide a more
comprehensive picture of Nigeria’s digital labor market.
2. Longitudinal studies are needed to track how earnings volatility and occupational trajectories
evolve over time in response to platform and policy changes.
3. Comparative studies between Nigeria and other African countries would help identify regional
patterns and best practices for regulating digital labor.
4. Research on the gendered dimensions of algorithmic control and earnings volatility would
deepen understanding of inequality in the creator economy.

5.8 Concluding Remark
The rise of social media platforms has transformed work and income generation in Nigeria,
creating opportunities but also new forms of control, precarity, and financial risk. This study shows
that without transparency, regulation, and social protection, al gorithmic control will continue to
produce occupational instability and earnings volatility. Addressing these challenges requires
coordinated action by policymakers, platforms, financial institutions, and creators themselves. By
doing so, Nigeria can harness the potential of the digital economy while protecting the rights and
livelihoods of its digital workers.
