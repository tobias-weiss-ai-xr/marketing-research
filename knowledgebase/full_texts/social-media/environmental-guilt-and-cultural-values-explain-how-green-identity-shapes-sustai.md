# Environmental guilt and cultural values explain how green identity shapes sustainable skincare purchase intention among Arab Generation Z

Dina S. AlGhamdi 1,4 , Tahir M. Nisar2 & Ahmad Shaheen 3
This study explains why Generation Z consumers in Arab markets form intentions to purchase
sustainable skincare products and clarifies the psychological mechanism through which green self-
identities are converted into intention. Extending the Theory of Planned Behavior with constructs
derived from value-belief-norm theorizing (green self-identity and eco-guilt), a Green Influencer
Marketing (GIM) construct measured with a validated eight-item scale and modelled as a single
reflective composite, appearance-related social media consciousness, and the horizontal-vertical
individualism-collectivism framework, the study positions eco-guilt as the mediating mechanism and
appearance-related social media consciousness and vertical collectivism as moderating boundary
conditions. Data were collected in January 2026 through an online survey of Generation Z consumers in
Egypt, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Qatar; after screening and the exclusion of fifteen
underage respondents, 369 adult responses (ages 18 to 29) were analyzed using partial least squares
structural equation modeling in SmartPLS 4 with 10,000 percentile bootstrap subsamples. The findings
show that the model explains 78.3% of the variance in purchase intention with strong out-of-sample
predictive relevance (Q²predict = 0.734). Attitude, eco-guilt, GIM, vertical collectivism, horizontal
individualism, and vertical individualism exert significant positive effects, whereas subjective norms
exert a significant negative effect and perceived behavioral control and horizontal collectivism are
non-significant. Green self-identity influences intention entirely indirectly through eco-guilt (indirect
effect = 0.159, 95% CI [0.100, 0.219]), appearance-related social media consciousness attenuates the
GIM effect, and vertical collectivism strengthens the identity-intention link. The pattern is consistent
with identity-based sustainability dispositions requiring moral-emotional activation and with cultural
hierarchy licensing their behavioural expression, although the cross-sectional design does not
permit causal inference. Theoretically, the study clarifies the mechanism connecting self-identities to
intention and identifies two boundary conditions of digital and cultural origin; practically, it directs
sustainable beauty brands toward moral-emotional framing, relational rather than appearance-centric
green influencer strategies, and family- and duty-anchored positioning in Arab markets.

influencer marketing, Individualism-collectivism, PLS-SEM, Arab consumers

willingness to pay for ethical products compared to previous generations has been documented, for example, by
Becerra et al. 1. However, this expressed concern does not consistently translate into corresponding purchases,
reflecting the well-documented gap between sustainability attitudes and actual green-purchase behavior1,2. The
persistence of the paradox is especially troublesome for the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) 3 as it accounts
for positive attitudes, supportive environmental norms of the community, and a perception of the ability to reach
the goal, yet still produces no intention or behavior to do so.
This study is motivated by three gaps that will be described at different levels of analysis. The first is a
theoretical gap. Literature that combines identity and moral constructs into the Theory of Planned Behavior
(TPB) is extensive, and the cognitive pathway from these constructs to intention is still unclear. One of the
opposing research streams considers green self-identity as a direct predictor of intention and claims that this
adds explanatory power to the TPB 1,4. The other research stream claims that identity acts indirectly through
moral obligation or moral emotions that it may instigate 5,6. The two research streams produce contradictory
hypotheses: the first research stream claims that the strengthening of identity is sufficient to increase intention,
and the second research stream claims that identity, in the absence of moral emotions, is inactive in the realm
of behavior. Theoretically, the question is under which circumstances the first or second research streams is/are
valid, and it has not been studied in the skincares industry, which is predominantly based on hedonic values and
where the identity and moral values may be in conflict.
The second gap is contextual and empirical. The majority of existing research on identity, moral emotion, and
green purchasing intention is from Western Europe, North America, and East Asia2,6 leaving under-researched
the Arab consumer markets with their growing young population and increasing beauty market 2. The four
countries included in this research, Egypt, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Qatar, were selected as
they have the common Arabic language and Islamic culture, are the among the highest countries in the world in
Generation Z social media use, and comprise the high-income, retail-saturated Gulf Arab countries as well as
the populous and low-income North African countries.The gap in research is of theoretical as well as real world
significance. The social and cultural structures of Arab countries combine an intensive social media presence
with a strong and rigid family order. This may have a substantive impact on the operational aspects of the
TPB. Arab family order and social media integration may modify the TPB in Arab contexts and may especially
apply to Arab Gen Z social media users7. TPB and value-belief-norm-sustainability studies may reveal boundary
conditions to what extent core TPB relationships may apply in collectivist Arab countries, particularly for Gen
Z2,8. Additionally, consumer behavior of Generation Z in this area is developed in a digital ecosystem where
green influencer marketing9 is more effective than corporate communication10,11. However, the influence of this
ecosystem combined with the pressure of self-presentation12 remains unclear.
The third gap is methodological. In green consumer research, the extensions of the theory of planned
behavior (TPB) have added a plethora of constructs, the empirical differentiation of which remains a
challenge, as they refer to the same constructs of value, belief, identity, and concern. Numerous reviewers and
methodologists have noted that a lack of discriminant validity severely restricts the meaningfulness of these
models13,14. This study addresses this issue fundamentally. It provides definitional clarity for constructs and
identifies discriminant validity issues, attributable to a lack of clarity for several constructs, removes these via
the well-established purging method, and illustrates discriminant validity utilizing all contemporary methods,
including bootstrapped HTMT confidence intervals. The study focuses on construct validation as a meaningful
contribution, as opposed to a form of reporting.
In order to fill identified gaps in the literature, the present study has defined the following research question:
What digital and cultural contexts allow for the transformation of green self-identities into intentions to purchase
sustainable skincare among Arab Gen Z consumers? To answer this question, three main objectives have been
defined. First, the study explores the mediating role of eco-guilt within the green self-identity construct, and thus
confirms the direct-effect or the mediation position, as described above. Second, the study explores whether the
use of social media for self-presentation and for meeting social needs (the GIM model) acts as a moderating
barrier to the formation of purchase intentions, as posited in the present study. Finally, the study explores
whether vertical collectivism (a culturally-defined group) has an effect on the formation of a purchase intention,
which is a much-needed effect of culturally defining a group8.
The contributions are intentionally modest and claimed only to the extent that the design allows them
to be. Theoretically, the study clarifies the mechanism that self-identities, in order to become relevant to the
formation of intention, necessitate the activation of moral emotions, thereby extending the value-belief-norm
framework15 to the TPB within a context in which the two frameworks have never been merged. Additionally, it
provides two boundary conditions, one digital and one cultural, that indicate when the established relationships
will strengthen, weaken, or reverse. Furthermore, the study provides a theoretically based construct for the
consumer-side Green Influencer Marketing (GIM), drawing upon Kılıç and Gürlek9 validated two-dimensional
scale and illustrating its predictive power in the context of sustainable skincare. The study refrains from claiming
it is the first absolute integration of these theoretical families; instead, it claims to be the first to test this mediated
and doubly moderated framework in sustainable skincare in the Arab markets.
Theoretical background and hypothesis development
The theory of planned behavior as the foundational architecture
The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) suggests that the primary determinant of behavior is intention and that
attitude toward the behavior, perceived social pressure, and perceived behavioral control contribute to formation
of intention3. The most prominent predictor of intention in the existing green consumer behavior literature
is attitude, and this is largely due to the TPB’s simplicity 16,17. Nonetheless, the TPB has a rationalist approach
and there are two critiques that motivate the current study. The first is motivational incompleteness: the TPB
accounts for the role of deliberative evaluation but not the identity commitments and moral feelings that

normative and control paths vary markedly across cultures and cohorts, with meta-analytic evidence attributing
this instability to cultural moderators7 and studies in young samples reporting weak, null, or conditional norm
effects17. These criticisms motivate the extension developed below; they do not, however, invalidate the baseline
paths, which remain the necessary benchmark against which extensions must demonstrate incremental value.

Green self-identity and eco-guilt: an identity-to-emotion mechanism
Precise operational definitions are required before the mechanism can be specified, because conceptual
looseness in this construct family is precisely what has undermined prior TPB extensions. Green self-identity
(GSI) is defined as the consumer’s belief that holding and enacting green values is a self-defining commitment,
operationalized through perceived green value-identity items that capture the self-relevant valuation of
sustainable skincare choices. GSI is a cognitive self-concept construct rooted in value-belief-norm (VBN)
theorizing, in which personal values give rise to self-implicating beliefs15.
Eco-guilt (EG) is defined precisely from its source literature rather than as a generic moral construct. Unger-
Plasek et al.18, p. 1] characterize eco-guilt as “a psychological construct reflecting the guilt felt by individuals about
the environmental consequences of their choices, ” and further specify that eco-guilt is a specific manifestation of
guilt in which consumers feel that they are not complying with environmental standards. Consistent with this,
the ecological-guilt literature defines the construct as a specific form of guilt that may arise when consumers
reflect on the environmental consequences of their consumption behaviour19,20. Critically, eco-guilt is not mere
awareness: as Nielsen et al.21 argue, eco-guilt is more specific than guilt, because it is not just a matter of consumers
being aware of environmental norms but also of accepting them. Building on these direct definitions, the present
study operationalizes EG through eco-guilt items adapted to the skincare domain from the validated Food Eco-
Guilt Scale18, retaining the construct’s defining feature: a self-evaluative negative affect triggered specifically by
the gap between one’s accepted environmental standards and one’s consumption conduct. EG is therefore an
affective, self-conscious moral emotion22, distinct from the cognitive self-definition captured by GSI. The two
constructs occupy adjacent but distinct stages of the VBN causal chain: self-identities are dispositional self-
definitions, whereas eco-guilt is the activated, episodic state those dispositions generate when conduct and self-
definition diverge. This stage adjacency, anchored in norm-activation reasoning23,24, is theoretically necessary for
the mediation argued below and explains why the constructs correlate strongly without being interchangeable.
H4. Green self-identity positively affects sustainable skincare purchase intention.
H5. Green self-identity positively affects eco-guilt.
H6. Eco-guilt positively affects sustainable skincare purchase intention.
H7. Eco-guilt mediates the effect of green self-identity on sustainable skincare purchase intention.
Green influencer marketing and its appearance-related boundary condition
Green Influencer Marketing (GIM) pertains to the relational constructs of the consumer where marketing
practices use social media influencers and are directed towards social constructs of sustainability and eco-
friendly consumer products. Using the two-dimensional scale of Kılıç and Gürlek 9, GIM incorporates two
dimensions: Environmental Sustainability-Oriented Influence (ESI) and Product-Oriented Influence (POI).
ESI recognizes the influencer’s contribution to efforts in raising sustainability, promoting a zero-waste lifestyle,
and encouraging positive changes towards environmentally friendly behaviors. POI reflects the influencer’s
recommendation of eco-friendly products and the framing of eco-friendly businesses. Both dimensions describe
the same underlying consumer experience of green influencer communication, differing in the object of that
communication rather than in its psychological character. GIM is therefore modelled here as a single reflective
composite, with ESI and POI items treated as interchangeable indicators of one relational-influence construct.
This specification follows the principle of parsimony and is supported by the strong internal coherence reported
across the two dimensions in the source validation9. The disaggregated approach is referenced in the limitations
as an opportunity for further testing the concept.
The predictive role of GIM on sustainable purchase intention is grounded in persuasion theory and the growing
literature on green influencer effects. Influencers who communicate both product-level and sustainability-level
green content provide consumers with convergent environmental cues that are more compelling than either
alone9. Consistent evidence shows that influencer credibility and parasocial bonds raise purchase intention
among Generation Z, including for green and beauty products25–27, that green influencer communication shapes
pro-environmental consumer responses through trust and perceived authenticity28, and that regional evidence
from Middle Eastern young consumers confirms affective-relational cues as dominant persuasive drivers 10. A
positive effect of GIM on sustainable skincare purchase intention is therefore predicted.
H8. Green Influencer Marketing positively affects sustainable skincare purchase intention.

Appearance-related social media consciousness (ASMC) is defined as “the extent to which individuals’ thoughts
and behaviors reflect ongoing awareness of whether they might look attractive to a social media audience”12, p. 164],
a construct rooted in self-objectification and self-presentation theory that captures the tendency to view oneself
from the vantage point of an imagined online observer and to manage one’s appearance accordingly 12. ASMC
is consequential in the present setting for three converging reasons. First, the focal category is skincare, the
consumption domain most directly implicated in appearance management, so appearance-related self-
monitoring is not a peripheral trait here but a defining feature of the decision frame. Second, the cohort is
Generation Z, whose purchases are formed inside photo-based platforms where appearance evaluation is
continuous and socially consequential. Third, the persuasive channel under study is the green influencer, whose
content is itself appearance-saturated, so ASMC governs how that content is read. Conceptually, ASMC is
distinct from the model’s focal predictors: it is neither an environmental disposition such as green self-identity
nor an evaluation of the behavior such as attitude, but a self-directed attentional orientation that conditions
information processing, and it is this status that qualifies it as a boundary condition rather than a competing
antecedent.
Theoretical research by ASMC actually helps to determine when (instead of whether) green influencer
marketing persuades. The displacement logic helps to explain the nature of ASMC’s moderation. The content of
influencers is concerned with the presentation of the self, and thus, consumers who are appearance conscious
prioritize content that is concerned with the presentation of the self, leaving little, if any, attention for a message
focused on sustainability with delayed, socially invisible outcomes 29,30. For consumers who demonstrate low
ASMC, the green influencer model operates as a channel of trusted advice, and thus, sustainability messages are
effectively converted to intention; conversely, for consumers who demonstrate high ASMC, the sustainability
messages are diluted by the appearance lens through which they are viewed, and thus, the messages foster
skepticism toward the promotional content28. Therefore, in the context of green influencer marketing, ASMC
acts as an antagonistic moderator and, indirectly, as a barrier to purchase intention by restraining a positive
pathway. This logic helps to explain the overly optimistic perspective adopted by many scholars in the influencer
marketing domain who assume that the exposure to influencers is persuasive. It suggests that the consumers who
are most engaged with beauty-related social media are, in fact, the consumers who are least responsive to green
marketing, and that the relationship is likely to be negative.
H9. Appearance-related social media consciousness negatively moderates the effect of Green Influencer Mar -
keting on sustainable skincare purchase intention.
Cultural orientations: horizontal and vertical individualism-collectivism
Looking only at national-level cultural scores glosses over vital elements of within-country heterogeneity that
are critical for individual-level consumer behavior prediction. The unidimensional individualism-collectivism
continuum only exacerbates the problem of averaging diverse orientations that have different behavioral
implications. The horizontal-vertical individualism-collectivism (HVIC) model decomposes culture into four
individual-level orientations for which the implications for consumer behavior are well-established 31,32. The
horizontal-vertical differentiation, which focuses on orientations based on the values of equality and hierarchy,
has predicted a number of consumer phenomena that the individualism-collectivism differentiations have not
been able to include, such as self-presentation, status consumption, and patterns of persuasion31. This model is
crucial in this context: Arab Generation Z is positioned between a tight family hierarchy and a global, digital, and
self-expressive orientation. Therefore, the vertical (hierarchical) and horizontal (egalitarian) domains of both
collectivism and individualism will operate likely in opposition, and thus, will need to be modeled separately.
Treating these consumers as uniformly collectivist, as is the default in the majority of regional studies, would
miss the critical variation that is necessary to understand and predict their behavior.
Horizontal individualism HI emphasizes the appreciation of uniqueness and self-sufficiency without the need
to differentiate oneself from others. An HI consumer views sustainable skincare as a means to showcase a unique
personal ethic and a true self that is independent of the opinion of others. HI is associated with environmentally
conscious purchasing that is driven by a desire to express oneself rather than by a desire to elevate one’s social
standing33,34. In contrast, VI appreciates the attainment of a competitive higher status and the differentiation that
comes along with achievement. For a VI consumer, sustainable skincare is a product that, in their view, indicates
the possession of a more advanced and progressive status, and, hence, reflects an environmentally conscious
purchasing decision made with the intention of illustrating status to others. VI is known to stimulate a higher
consumer status concern31, and recently, positive individualist impacts, including vertical individualism, were
reported in the area of sustainable purchasing when the concept of environmental self-identity was considered34.
Therefore, it is assumed that both of these perspectives will increase intention to act, but using fundamentally
different psychological reasoning, self-expression for HI and status for VI, which is exactly why it is assumed that
these effects act independently rather than a single individualism effect.
Horizontal collectivism (HC) denotes valuing egalitarian in-group bonding and sociability among equals;
an HC consumer may adopt sustainable skincare as an expression of solidarity with peers and shared concern
for collective wellbeing, and HC has been associated with eco-friendly consumption in cross-cultural work 33.
Vertical collectivism (VC), by contrast, denotes valuing deference to in-group hierarchy and the discharge of
duty to family and community. For a VC consumer, sustainable consumption is constructed as an obligation,
to family health, to communal welfare, and to future generations, rather than as a peer affiliation, and this
duty framing supplies a stronger and more reliable motivational basis in hierarchical Arab family structures
than egalitarian belonging does8,34. Distinguishing HC from VC is consequential rather than pedantic, because
the two are predicted to diverge: duty-anchored VC should predict intention robustly, whereas egalitarian HC,
lacking an authorizing obligation, may not. VC is also assigned a second, conditioning role. Because hierarchical

Methods
Research design
The study adopted a quantitative, cross-sectional, deductive design in which hypotheses derived from the
integrated theoretical framework were tested on primary survey data using partial least squares structural
equation modeling (PLS-SEM). A survey design is appropriate because all focal constructs are individual-level
perceptions, dispositions, and intentions for which validated self-report instruments exist, and PLS-SEM is
appropriate for a prediction-oriented model that integrates several theoretical families and includes interaction
terms35,36.
Population, sampling, and data collection
The targeted sample was Gen Z consumers in Egypt, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Qatar, who buy
and/or think about buying skin care products. The focus on these four markets is intentional. They not only have
the Arabic language and an Islamic culture in common, but also highlight the contrasting structures among the
Gulf countries and North Africa. These markets provide the high-income Gulf markets and the North African
consumers who are more price sensitive. This focus enables the sample to provide the culturally congruent and
Arabic-speaking Gen Z consumers and also allows for consideration of the more salient differences between the
Gulf and North Africa markets (Table 1).

Measures, translation, and pilot testing
All constructs were measured reflectively with multi-item scales adapted from validated instruments and
anchored on five-point Likert scales from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Attitude, subjective norms, and
perceived behavioral control were adapted from the canonical TPB operationalizations 3; purchase intention
from Spears and Singh 37; green self-identity through perceived green value-identity items developed from
value-identity theorizing6,15; eco-guilt through eco-guilt items adapted from the Food Eco-Guilt Scale18; Green
Influencer Marketing (GIM) through the validated eight-item, two-dimensional scale of Kılıç and Gürlek9, which
was developed through a rigorous sequential exploratory mixed-methods design combining content analysis of
green influencer posts with multiple waves of quantitative validation. The scale comprises four Environmental
Sustainability-Oriented Influence (ESI) items capturing awareness-raising and lifestyle-change content, and
four Product-Oriented Influence (POI) items capturing environmentally friendly product endorsement. The
scale’s psychometric properties were confirmed in tourism and environmental contexts; the validated items were
retained in their original wording and administered within the study’s sustainable-skincare survey context, with
their suitability for this domain confirmed through expert panel review and pilot testing. Appearance-related
social media consciousness was adapted from Choukas-Bradley et al. 12 and the four cultural orientations from
Triandis and Gelfand32.
The English source instrument underwent full back-translation following Brislin 38 into Modern Standard
Arabic. A pilot study with 30 Generation Z respondents confirmed pilot reliabilities above 0.70 for all constructs.
Data analysis strategy
Hypotheses were tested with PLS-SEM in SmartPLS 4 39, using path weighting, 3000 maximum iterations, and
a stop criterion of 10 ⁻⁷. Inference relied on 10,000 bootstrap subsamples with percentile confidence intervals
and two-tailed tests at the 5% level. Both moderating effects were estimated with the two-stage approach on
standardized product indicators. Out-of-sample predictive power was assessed with PLSpredict (10 folds × 10
repetitions) and CVPAT40,41.
Common method bias
Common method variance was addressed procedurally and statistically. Harman’s single-factor test on the final
40-item set showed the first unrotated factor extracting 34.98% of variance, well below the 50% threshold. The
full-collinearity approach42 was additionally applied: eleven of the thirteen inner variance inflation factors fall at
or below the conservative 3.3 benchmark, and the two exceptions (GSI: 3.80; SN: 3.44) remain below the critical
threshold of 5 and reflect genuine theoretical adjacency rather than a pervasive method factor.

Predictive relevance, model fit, and robustness
Q²predict is positive and significant for both endogenous constructs (intention: 0.734; EG: 0.467) and all
endogenous indicators. CVPAT indicates that the model significantly outperforms the indicator-average
benchmark. Compared to the linear model, the composite model shows greater predictive accuracy, and as
suggested by Shmueli et al.41, the prediction error serves as a bound of predictive advantage, which is expected in
a theory-based model. Approximate fit was also inspected. SRMR is 0.099 for the saturated model and 0.104 for
the estimated model, and NFI is 0.483. The estimated-model SRMR exceeds the 0.08 value conventionally cited
for covariance-based models, and NFI is low. Both values are reported here without adjustment. Incremental fit
indices of this kind were developed for common-factor estimation and are not established as decision criteria
for composite models, particularly where two single-indicator interaction terms are included 44,45. Evaluation
therefore rests primarily on the predictive evidence reported above, and the fit values are carried forward as an
acknowledged limitation (Table 9).
Discussion
The main result is establishing GSI’s indirect-only mediation on purchase intention via EG. Green self-identity
has no significant direct effect on intention (β = 0.071, p = 0.245). Y et, Green self-identity has a significant
positive effect on eco-guilt (β = 0.687, f² = 0.892). Eco-guilt, in turn, positively affects intention (β = 0.232). The
combined effect of Green self-identity and eco-guilt, with the mediation of eco-guilt, has a significant positive
effect on intention (0.159). This pattern addresses the mechanism question set out in the introduction. This
result is not in accordance with the direct effect theory, which states that Green self-identity has predictive
power on its own 1,4. On the other hand, the result is in accordance with the theory, which states that Green
self-identity, in order to have a behavioral effect, must first be transformed to eco-guilt5,6. The skincare category
offers a plausible setting for this account. Since the industry is hedonistic and appearance-based, a positive

Green self-Identity is in conflict with the hedonistic mindset. The discomfort caused by eco-guilt, as stated in the cited
meta-analysis46, is the most relevant to this case. The most relevant theory in this case is the Norm Activation Model24.
According to this theory, personal norms compel action only when one feels a moral obligation to act. Onwezen et al.23
show that both anticipated guilt and pride act as mediating variables on the personal norms and pro-environmental
behavior continuum, the same as in this case, where eco-guilt is the mediating variable.
The present result extends norm-activation logic within the TPB by demonstrating that the self-identity-to-
intention pathway is obstructed by a self-reflective moral emotion, rather than taking this pathway directly. This
has significant implications. To the extent that the moral-emotional side of green identity is not addressed, the
measurement or promotion of green identity will lead to a systematic overestimation of the propensity to purchase
sustainably.
Subjective norms may reflect the presence of statistical suppression. They are intrinsically linked to green self-
identity and attitude. When accounting for variance along with those constructs, the remaining variance in subjective
norms may be of a negative sign. Therefore, the negative coefficient observed may indicate a partial association, and
the positive correlation (r = 0.61) between subjective norms and intention may capture the relationship best. A second
explanation is psychological reactance, where participants interpret strong green messaging as a signal to change their
behavior. This is coupled with a change in sustainable purchasing intentions which is of particular interest. Strong
green self-identity (the focus of this study) may reinforce the intention to react against perceived social pressures to
comply47. A flexible, green signaling market may reinforce normative ambiguity. To separate authentic reactance from
a suppression artifact requires experiments or longitudinal studies, which the current cross-sectional data do not allow;
however, this finding does establish that the normative component of the TPB behaves in a non-classical manner in
this subgroup, confirming a meta-analytic trend in which normative influences depend on cultural variability7.
Two precise nulls must be explained. Perceived behavioral control has no effect on intention (f² < 0.001). The most
plausible post-hoc explanation is limited opportunity: with sustainable skincare plausibly experienced by this engaged,
sustainability-oriented sample as a niche, premium segment, perceptions of control are pushed to a high, unyielding
limit, and people in this group are unable to differentiate between intention and lack of intention 16. Horizontal
collectivism is also null, while vertical collectivism is the second strongest predictor in the model, which justifies
the distinction of horizontal and vertical collectivism within the framework of Arab consumer behavior, considering
the treatment of Arab consumers as generally collectivist is also justified. This is an extension of the individual-
level evidence of the existence of different cultural pathways to environmentally friendly behavior in collectivist
societies8,32 and justifies the separation of these constructs.

and the sustainability stakes lost persuasive purchase in this processing29,30. This result also indicates that among
the consumers who are immersed the most into beauty social media, the most appearance focused are also the least
influenced by green advertising, while the remaining consumers, who are appearance focused but less so, are quite
influenced.

vertical collectivism, the intention to engage in sustainable consumption is constructed as a duty to the family’s health,
the welfare of the community, and the future generations, thereby authorizing the expression of a green identity.

Within the extensions, the effects of attitude (β = 0.420, f² = 0.287) highlight a strong predictor and replicate a well-

that the TPB does not fail, rather it demonstrates asymmetrical robustness. In this sample the attitudinal pathway held,
the normative pathway inverted, and the control pathway disappeared, while the identity-based extension operated
only through moral emotion and only where cultural conditions permitted it. Whether this asymmetry recurs in other
cohorts and categories is an empirical question this design cannot settle. The asymmetry that this study is dedicated
to explore is concealed in models that report only aggregate support for extended TPB frameworks, and this study’s
disaggregating the pattern, as opposed to citing an overall R² of 0.783 as a success, illustrates the type of analytical
precision that the field of research demands.
Theoretical implications
In accordance with the evidence, three theoretical implications are presented. First, this study adds to the mechanism
clarification literature GSI that is behaviorally inert until transformed into EG, an indirect-only pattern as supports
the activation accounts6 over the direct-effect accounts. Second, this study adds two boundary conditions related
to ASMC that diminish GIM persuasion, importing a self-presentation construct from body-image research into
sustainable consumption, and to VC that permits the direct behavioral manifestation of green identity. Third, the
inverted norm coefficient and the no control effect add boundary evidence on the portability of the TPB across
cohorts and countries. The study also provides initial evidence that the Kılıç and Gürlek9 GIM scale functions in a
sustainable skincare context, offering a consumer-side GIM operationalisation that warrants further validation in
other green consumption settings.
Practical implications
Given that intention is the dependent variable, the implications detailed here are levers for intention formation, the
translation of which into actual purchase behavior remains to be seen. For sustainable beauty brands, the indirect-only
approach suggests that the use of identity-affirming messaging alone is inadequate. Rather, messaging must elicit and
activate moral emotions by making the environmental impacts of using unsustainable skincare products personally
relevant to the consumers, while avoiding the use of guilt messaging, which can produce a backlash49. When it comes
to the Green Influencer Marketing (GIM) strategy, the primary focus should be on the placement of sustainability and
product-oriented creators, while avoiding the use of the GIM strategy with the most appearance-saturated audiences,
as the moderation evidence shows that green appeals are ineffective. In Arab markets, the positioning of sustainable
skincare as a commitment to family health and intergenerational care aligns with the vertical collectivism pathway
identified as active, while generic community-belonging appeals are not supported.
Limitations and future research
Several limitations bound the interpretation of these results. First, the design is cross-sectional and all mediation
and moderation inferences are associational, identifying the emotion mechanism from identity requires longitudinal
studies or experimentation. Second, all data are self-reported from a singular instrument; single-source bias cannot
be eliminated, and the dependent variable is intention, not actual purchase behavior. Third, the sampling strategy
was purposive with snowball elements from communities concerned with sustainability, over-sampling was from the
involved consumers; there is no estimate of the response rate. Fourth, the four Arab markets were pooled at the cohort
level as the research question focuses on Arab Generation Z as a culturally coherent digital group, not as disparate
within-country comparisons; the results should be read as cohort-level regularities and should not be generalized to
any single national market. Fifth, some constructs were adapted within and across domains; independent purification
of the GSI and EG scales is a prerequisite for subsequent use. Sixth, the GIM construct was defined as unidimensional
to maintain parsimony; a disaggregated two-dimensional or higher specification is a desirable avenue for extending
rigor. Future studies should test longitudinally and experimentally the mechanisms of moral activation and behavioral
outcomes, including actual purchase behavior.
Since the four markets were consolidated, future studies ought to obtain country-balanced samples and utilize the
measurement invariance of composite models (MICOM) and multi-group analysis (MGA) to examine whether the
structural paths vary in Egypt, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Qatar. Such studies would assess the cross-
national limits of the cohort-level findings of the current study.
Conclusion
With evidence from 369 adult respondents and data analysis using PLS-SEM and 10,000 bootstrap subsamples, this
study provided an answer to the question of what mechanisms exist, and under what conditions the intention to
engage in sustainable skincare purchasing may exist, as a consequence of adopting a green self-identity, among Arab
Generation Z. The answer is as follows: the self-identity is activated through eco-guilt, which has an expression (of a
sustainable skincare purchasing intention) only when vertical collectivism is high; the intention is influenced by Green
Influencer Marketing, with an exception given to the most appearance-oriented customers; attitude is dominant, and
within the components of the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), the norm is inverted, and the control is lost. The model
has an intention variance of 78.3%, and the constructs show an out-of-sample relevance test. Discriminant validity was
supported by the Fornell-Larcker criterion, the cross-loadings, and bootstrapped HTMT intervals that exclude unity,
although the proximity of the green self-identity and eco-guilt estimate to that boundary is acknowledged as a
limitation. Taken together, the results indicate that sustainable skincare intention in this cohort is driven less by
social pressure or perceived control than by a green self-identity that becomes behaviourally relevant only once
it is translated into moral emotion, and only within cultural and digital conditions that permit its expression.
