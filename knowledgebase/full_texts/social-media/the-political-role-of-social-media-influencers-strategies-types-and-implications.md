Abstract
Social media influencers (SMIs) have become influential communicators of political
and socially relevant content, especially among younger audiences increasingly
disengaged from traditional news. While originally focused on commercial marketing,
many SMIs now address topics such as elections, policy debates, and social justice
issues. These messages are often shared strategically—to expand reach, maintain
authenticity, or express personal convictions. We argue that SMIs should be seen as
strategic providers of political content, raising important questions about their role
in shaping public discourse and democratic engagement. In this introduction to the
special issue, we present a conceptual framework based on three key dimensions:
the Density of Political Content, the Style of Presenting Political Content, and the
Proximity of Political Content to political actors, ideologies, or institutions. Based on
these dimensions, we outline six influencer prototypes to support a more systematic
understanding of their political communication. The articles in this issue explore
these types and their democratic implications.

Introduction
In recent years, social media influencers (SMIs) have emerged as pivotal and persua-
sive figures in the dissemination of political and socially relevant information, particu-
larly among younger audiences (e.g., Dekoninck & Schmuck, 2022; Merz & von
Sikorski, 2025; Riedl et al., 2023; Schmuck et al., 2022; Suuronen et al., 2022;
Zimmermann et al., 2022). SMIs disseminate self-produced content (e.g., text, images,
videos, and audio) with high frequency and—over time—have cultivated substantial
followings by sharing personal experiences, opinions, and various aspects of their
personal lives (Abidin, 2015; Khamis et al., 2017). SMIs often aim to increase their
reach and visibility through the content they disseminate, while simultaneously estab-
lishing a distinct identity that sets them apart (Khamis et al., 2017). Put differently,
“the very idea of being an influencer is largely based on the intensified commodifica-
tion of the self, in and through social media, in order to influence the audience to make
certain choices – choices that might involve consumption practices but also ethics,
values, and politics” (Arnesson & Reinikainen, 2024, p. 1).
Initially, the role of social media influencers (SMIs) was primarily focused on
commercial marketing (i.e., influencer marketing), with influencers partnering with
businesses to promote products (e.g., De Veirman et al., 2017). However, as the land-
scape of social media has evolved, so too has the scope of their content. Today, SMIs
are actively involved in communicating political messages, including those related to
elections, current affairs, political policies, and social issues (Borchers, 2025; Fischer
et al., 2022; Harff & Schmuck, 2024; Riedl et al., 2023). Through social media plat-
forms, SMIs reach large numbers of young people, including those who may not
regularly engage with traditional news media (TV , newspapers, etc.) or actively avoid
it (Newman et al., 2024). Moreover, even so-called lifestyle SMIs—who typically
covered topics like fitness, travel, and beauty—are increasingly addressing political
matters such as climate change, health, gender, and social justice, often integrating
these topics with their broader lifestyle themes (Harff & Schmuck, 2024; Maares &
Hanusch, 2020).
For some time, numerous studies have examined the phenomenon of social media
influencers disseminating political information (including a special issue by Riedl
et al., 2023). Until recently, the focus was primarily on whether social media influenc-
ers share or engage with political content. However, SMIs are becoming increasingly
diverse, and the political influence exerted by different types of SMIs—through vari-
ous strategies—has been understudied, particularly regarding its implications for
democracy and how influencers convey political information.
In our introduction to this special issue, we first explore why politics can be a chal-
lenging topic for SMIs. We then describe three key dimensions—the Density of

Politics as a Challenging Topic for Social Media
Influencers
While influencers can gain visibility, engagement, and even credibility by discussing
political topics, they also risk alienating audiences, facing backlash, and losing com-
mercial partnerships (Suuronen et al., 2022). Following Suuronen et al. (2022), the
thematization of politics by SMIs can be divided into the thematization of formal and
lifestyle-related political topics. Formal topics refer to aspects concerning political
processes, institutions, or actors, such as politicians, parties, or politics in general.
Based on the postulated “rise of lifestyle politics” (Bennett, 1998, p. 741), lifestyle-
related topics are understood as topics that are taken up by an SMI and, from their
point of view, represent a collective concern that affects society as a whole, for exam-
ple, climate change, health, gender, or social justice (Engel et al., 2024; Suuronen
et al., 2022).
On the one hand, content related to issues such as climate change, health, and poli-
tics provides SMIs with the opportunity to distinguish themselves from other content
creators through these “serious” topics and to generate attention on social media (Harff
& Schmuck, 2024). For example, lifestyle topics can be charged with meaning through
socially relevant information and, at the same time, provide followers with an infor -
mative added value. A lifestyle SMI, for instance, that focuses on vegan diets can add
political meaning to content by emphasizing the health aspects of a plant-based diet
for society or highlighting the social and environmental benefits of a reduced carbon
footprint. By adding this political layer, lifestyle SMIs may reach audiences who may
not have previously engaged with political messages (Naderer, 2023). SMIs can also
address complex political topics in a simplified manner (Schmuck et al., 2022) and
present them in innovative ways—both linguistically and visually—targeted at spe-
cific audiences (Harff & Schmuck, 2024).
SMIs consistently demonstrate their ability to skillfully blend “serious” and meaning-
ful topics with the realities of their everyday lives and personal perspectives (Bauer,
2024). Also, some SMIs tend to strategically mix political content with lifestyle topics
and commercial messages, creating a hybrid form of communication that both engages
their audience and monetizes political discourse, often blurring the boundaries between
political advocacy, personal branding, and advertising (De Gregorio & Goanta, 2022).
In general, SMIs manage to communicate in a particularly authentic way—being
“true to oneself” and to their audience (Wellman et al., 2020)—and at the same time

reduce the complexity of topics, make content easier to understand, offer orientation,
and arouse political interest (Schmuck et al., 2022; Stehr et al., 2015). This way, they
position themselves as trustworthy opinion leaders on selected (political) topics. These
functions of SMIs discussed in the literature appear to be particularly important in
times of crises, conflicts, wars, and dynamically changing media usage habits, as well
as dwindling trust in traditional news media and an increase in social media use for
news (Fletcher et al., 2025). For followers, these communicative qualities of SMIs
open up the possibility of building intensive, one-sided (parasocial) relationships with
SMIs over time (Breves & Liebers, 2022), turning SMIs into persuasive parasocial
opinion leaders (Stehr et al., 2015).
On the other hand, politics is also a sensitive topic that SMIs avoid in part or even
completely (Suuronen et al. 2022). Political content can have undesirable effects for
very different reasons, which can lead to various conflicts of interest for SMIs.
Generally speaking, from an SMI perspective, there is always a risk that the dissemi-
nation of certain (political) content, personal statements, or positioning on a (political)
topic will deter followers or potential future followers or even annoy them to such an
extent that followers will “unfollow” an SMI (Suuronen et al., 2022). Some lifestyle
SMIs, in particular, may try to avoid content that could provoke strong resentment
among followers, hindering the “commercialization of the self” (Arnesson &
Reinikainen, 2024, p. 1) and leading to possible damage to one’s own social media
identity (Khamis et al., 2017). These risks may explain why SMIs are more likely to
post political content in ephemeral formats, like stories, which disappear after a certain
time period, rather than in permanent posts on their profiles (Gonzalez et al., 2024).
In addition, when SMIs that have not previously focused on politics “go political,”
this can easily be met with more negative and polarized reactions due to the controver-
sial nature of political issues or a lack of interest in these issues among their followers.
This has at least been observed for corporate political advocacy by brands (Weber
et al., 2023). A perceived lack of authenticity or expertise on these political issues
could harm the credibility of SMIs in general.
Conceptual Boundaries of Social Media Influencers
While SMIs can stimulate political interest and engagement (Dekoninck & Schmuck,
2022; Merz & von Sikorski, 2024; Naderer, 2023), they need to be differentiated from
other actors who engage in political communication in the digital sphere (De Gregorio
& Goanta, 2022; see Riedl et al., 2023). In our article and this special issue, we focus
on SMIs who act as opinion leaders (De Gregorio & Goanta, 2022), have no institu-
tional background (like journalists and politicians), and “whose notoriety and fame is
platform-built” (Riedl et al., 2023, p. 2).
First, we need to conceptually distinguish between SMIs and traditional media,
including journalists. Unlike traditional media and journalists, SMIs’ political engage-
ment or discussions are not bound to professional norms and ethical standards, nor is
their content subject to editorial oversight or fact-checking. Many influencers also
lack formal expertise in the topics they discuss (Huber et al., 2022, but see Zhang

et al., 2023), increasing the risk of spreading political misinformation. Additionally,
they often hold personal biases, such as partisan preferences, and may use their plat-
forms to persuade rather than inform their audiences (Flamino et al., 2023)—after all,
persuasion or influence is their core competence. Another key difference lies in finan-
cial incentives. Traditional media outlets typically have more diverse revenue streams,
such as subscriptions, advertising, and public funding, which can create a degree of
separation between financial interests and editorial decisions. In contrast, influencers
heavily rely on audience engagement and advertising income, making them more
dependent on content that maximizes reach (Fischer et al., 2022). As such, their politi-
cal content can become part of the information diet of digital media users due to stra-
tegic motives, rather than the journalistic goal of informing followers based on ethical
codes of journalism (Thorson & Wells, 2016).
Second, we need to distinguish SMIs from politicians. While politicians have a
formal political role and are tied to political institutions, SMIs—in line with the origi-
nal definition of opinion leaders (Lazarsfeld et al., 1948; Weimann, 1994)—do not
have such a formal status. As such, their strategic motives are more covert than those
of politicians who openly advertise for electoral votes and party supporters in digital
spheres. Due to the (parasocial) relationships, SMIs build with followers (Breves &
Liebers, 2022), they can become part of the social flow of information curation in digi-
tal environments (Thorson & Wells, 2016)—being seemingly part of their followers’
social circle. As a result, followers may be more inclined to “let their guard down,” and
a shared interest with an SMI could make even those typically uninterested in politics
more receptive to political persuasion (Naderer, 2023).
Towards a Typology of Influencers and Their Role in
Disseminating Political Messages
Based on these considerations, we therefore argue that we need to take a more nuanced
look at SMIs’ handling of political messages to better understand their role in com-
municating “serious” political information. Specifically, we argue that we should
understand influencers as strategic providers of political content. What does this
mean? We assume that influencers generally have a great interest in producing and
distributing content that is in demand among followers and potential future followers
and that contributes to the successful further development of their account and per -
sonal brand. This view is in line with the strategic flow postulated by Thorson and
Wells (2016), who argue that digital environments like social media allow strategic
curators like politicians—but also SMIs—to convey information to social media users
out of strategic motives like maximizing followers or financial gain. At the same time,
some SMIs may act out of altruistic motives or feel a responsibility to openly address
topics that are close to their hearts and that they consider important to them, for exam-
ple, to draw attention to social grievances and motivate their followers to take appro-
priate (political) action (Maares & Hanusch, 2020). Overall, it can be assumed that
SMIs “enjoy and carefully maintain—often, though not always, in a professional

capacity—credibility and authenticity through how and which content they choose to
present” (Riedl et al., 2023, p. 1) and generally act strategically (see also Enke &
Borchers, 2019).
However, these strategic considerations may differ significantly in relation to polit-
ical content. We argue that influencers can generally be distinguished along three key
dimensions: First, Density of Political Content refers to the intensity with which influ-
encers discuss political topics and the extent to which they disseminate political infor-
mation. Second, the Style of Presenting Political Content differentiates whether
influencers primarily share opinion-based or ostensibly fact-based political informa-
tion. Third, the Degree of Political Content Proximity describes the closeness of an
influencer’s political content, for example, to political parties, politicians, and
(extreme) political ideologies. In other words, this dimension describes how closely
the political content of an SMI is linked to certain political ideas and goals.
For instance, an SMI may aim to present topics such as democracy and political
education frequently (i.e., high density of political content) in an ostensibly fact-ori-
ented and non-opinionated manner (i.e., style of presenting political content), while
remaining entirely politically independent, with the content having no connection to
political systems, parties, or political actors (i.e., degree of political content proxim-
ity). In contrast, another SMI may seek to influence followers ideologically by posting
a combination of political and non-political lifestyle content (i.e., moderate density of
political content) in a primarily opinionated manner (i.e., style of presenting political
content), while maintaining a close relationship with a specific political party or even
being financially dependent on it (i.e., high degree of political content proximity).
In the following section, we utilize these three dimensions to propose six SMI
prototypes that vary in their communication of political information (Table 1). We
aim to enhance and systematize future research on SMIs and their role in dissemi-
nating political content. As these types are presented as ideal types, overlaps may
exist between them, and different subtypes may emerge. Furthermore, an SMI’s
categorization could change or fluctuate over time. For instance, an SMI may ini-
tially avoid political content but choose to engage with political issues during a
significant election campaign.
The Non-political Influencer
SMIs may consciously or unconsciously avoid posting political content for several
reasons. First, it may simply be that an SMI is not interested in politics, and, from their
perspective, politics does not align with the topics they typically share (e.g., fitness,
beauty).
SMIs may fear negative reactions from followers (Suuronen et al., 2022), particu-
larly when political opinions may cause division. This type of SMI may also avoid
political topics to prevent alienating their target demographic, as political content
can potentially polarize followers. Furthermore, commercial interests may discour -
age political statements, as brands prefer to maintain a neutral stance to reach a

broad audience (Weber et al., 2023). Thus, maintaining a personal brand free from
controversy may be a key concern for this influencer type, as political statements
can potentially damage an influencer’s image (Bishop, 2021). Some influencers may
feel uncertain or lack the expertise necessary to discuss political issues responsibly.
The desire to preserve independence could also play a role, with many influencers
opting for neutrality to avoid being aligned with any particular political stance.
Lastly, the potential for stress and conflict associated with politically charged con-
tent may lead SMIs to steer clear of potentially divisive topics to maintain harmony
in their online presence. Additionally, various contextual factors, such as the politi-
cal system in which an influencer operates (e.g., in non-democratic systems), may
influence the decision to avoid making political statements.

The Lifestyle-Based Sporadic Political Influencer
The Lifestyle-Based Sporadic Political Influencer refers to SMIs who primarily focus
on lifestyle-related content, but occasionally incorporate political themes (i.e., low
density of political content), both in an entertaining, opinionated manner, and in an
ostensibly fact-based presentation style (i.e., style of presenting political content; see
Harff & Schmuck, 2024). The degree of political content proximity is non-existent or
low for this type of influencer (Table 1).
These SMIs frequently use personalization or politicize their private lives (Bauer,
2023), sharing their own experiences and perspectives to engage their audience. The
occasional inclusion of political topics, such as political events, elections, or specific
incidents, is strategically used to attract attention for a political cause, position the
influencer morally, or enhance their reputation, and, in some cases, engage in practices
like greenwashing or rainbow-washing (e.g., Breves & Liebers, 2022). Notably, politi-
cal content is more likely to receive a higher number of likes and views than its non-
political counterpart (Harff & Schmuck, 2024).
By aligning with socially relevant causes like climate protection (Dekoninck &
Schmuck, 2022; Huber et al., 2022; Merz & von Sikorski, 2024) or women’s rights
(Barbala, 2024), these influencers may also aim to “speak their mind” and, at the same
time, boost their likability, credibility, and authenticity, for example, presenting them-
selves as advocates for positive change while maintaining their lifestyle-oriented
brand.
The Lifestyle-Based Politically Engaged Influencer
The Lifestyle-Based Politically Engaged Influencer refers to SMIs who focus on life-
style content while consistently integrating political themes (i.e., moderate density of
political content), presenting them both in an entertaining, opinionated manner and in
an ostensibly fact-based style (i.e., style of presenting political content). The degree of
political content proximity is generally low, with occasional moderate levels (see
Table 1). Thus, the Lifestyle-Based Sporadic Political Influencer and the Lifestyle-
Based Politically Engaged Influencer share some similarities, but the latter has a
higher density of political content and tends to have a more pronounced political con-
tent proximity.
The recurring thematization of politics serves multiple strategic purposes: It gener-
ates attention for a political cause, differentiates the influencer from other SMIs, and
allows for moral positioning. At the same time, this type of SMIs regularly “speaks
their mind” and oftentimes feels a responsibility to openly address (political) topics
that they consider important (e.g., climate change, health, and social justice) and moti-
vate their followers to take appropriate (political) action. Political content is often
presented in a simplified way for followers interested in occasional political issues,
providing them with a digestible version of complex issues (Schmuck et al., 2022).
Additionally, by addressing political aspects, the influencer demonstrates their compe-
tence and engagement with societal matters. Lifestyle topics, such as fitness, food,

nutrition, travel, or fashion, are skillfully intertwined with selected political themes
(Maares & Hanusch, 2020), enriching otherwise non-political and commercial content
(De Gregorio & Goanta, 2022). This approach not only enhances the content’s politi-
cal meaningfulness but also attracts new audiences who resonate with the influencer’s
advocacy for specific causes, such as climate protection (Breves & Liebers, 2022) or
anti-feminism (e.g., Bauer, 2023). By strategically incorporating political topics, the
influencer is able to develop both their personal brand and their relationship with a
broader and more diverse follower base.
The Political Mediator and Educational Influencer
Political Mediators and Educational SMIs primarily share information on political topics
(i.e., high density of political content) in an ostensibly fact-based presentation style pri-
marily with the aim of neutral education. They typically do so through a combination of
strategic and normative intentions (Maares & Hanusch, 2020; see also Henn, 2023). The
degree of political content proximity is nonexistent, with occasional low levels (e.g.,
when cooperating with NGOs or governments for a “good cause”; Table 1).
One key motivation is to position themselves as competent mediators of complex
political content. By reducing the complexity of formal political topics, these influenc-
ers make current political issues more accessible to their audience, offering them
added value in the form of clear and concise information (Schmuck et al., 2022). The
central goal of these influencers is to educate followers, particularly younger audi-
ences, about politics (e.g., current events, democracy, and democratic processes) in an
engaging and digestible way, fostering a more informed public (Henn, 2023; Sehl &
Schützeneder, 2023). In doing so, they also aim to attract new followers who seek to
understand politics and stay updated on political developments. This approach not
only serves to increase their followership but also aligns with broader monetization
goals, such as advertising and brand partnerships. By positioning themselves as
authoritative sources on political matters, influencers are able to build their personal
brand while promoting normative positive objectives like civic education.
Moreover, this type of SMI may incorporate their opinions and viewpoints into
their content. However, unlike other types of SMIs, they typically make an effort to
substantiate their perspectives with factual reasoning, clearly distinguishing personal
opinions from objective information (Sehl & Schützeneder, 2023). This approach
enhances their credibility while ensuring their audience is aware of the subjective
nature of certain statements. In some ways, this influencer type mirrors the work of
traditional journalists. Their content frequently acts as a form of “journalistic exten-
sion” (Fischer et al., 2022, p. 260), where classic news stories are repackaged and
“recycled” in a manner tailored to a specific audience. This often involves the use of
“entertainment packaging” (Fischer et al., 2022, p. 263) and simplified representations
of complex issues (Schmuck et al., 2022), making political information more appeal-
ing and accessible to a broader, often younger, demographic. Ultimately, these SMIs
strategically balance the dual objectives of providing educational content and making
political discourse more accessible while enhancing their online presence.

The Ideological Political Influencer
The Ideological Political Influencer shapes content strategically to promote a particu-
lar political ideology (e.g., liberal or conservative), both directly and, at times, in sub-
tle and indirect ways. These influencers employ both explicitly political and seemingly
non-political content to advance their ideological goals (i.e., moderate-to-high density
of political content). The content is primarily presented in an opinionated manner,
occasionally with an entertaining style, and selectively (ostensibly) factual informa-
tion is shared when it aligns with their political ideology. The political content proxim-
ity is moderate to high (see Table 1). Although some influencers in this category may
have strong ideological ties to political actors and parties and may disseminate extreme
political content, they primarily receive no direct funding from political institutions,
parties, and actors.
In general, the political content presented is usually one-sided and politically
charged, often selected and framed to (subtly) convince followers of certain opin-
ions or political positions or to reinforce existing beliefs. This content can include
mis- and disinformation, which is strategically disseminated to support a clear,
sometimes extreme, ideological line (Rothut et al., 2023). In addition to political
topics, these influencers frequently integrate non-political content—such as “day in
the life” posts, lifestyle snapshots, or humorous incidents (e.g., dance perfor -
mances)—to create a mix of entertainment and political messaging. The strategic
purpose behind this combination is to normalize extreme political views by present-
ing them alongside more neutral or light-hearted content, which may reduce the
perceived extremity of the political positions. Non-political content is also employed
as a tool to attract a broader, especially younger, audience who may not yet be politi-
cized. In this way, SMIs can subtly promote political ideologies and values within
seemingly innocent posts. For instance, when presenting themselves in “a day in the
life” posts as traditional figures, such as caring housewives, they indirectly convey
certain ideological values, reinforcing gender roles or other political ideals without
explicitly stating them (Bauer, 2023).
Some influencers of this type also use “partisan mockery” (Fischer et al., 2022, p.
265) by expressing strong opinions on a topic, attacking political opponents, often in
an emotional and polarizing way (Fischer et al., 2022). These statements are frequently
directed not only against marginalized political groups, but also against traditional
journalism, further polarizing the discourse on social media (Kubin & von Sikorski,
2021). Here too, “entertainment packaging” (Fischer et al., 2022, p. 260) or infotain-
ment (Harff & Schmuck, 2024) is regularly used, with content such as short, emotion-
ally charged videos that are particularly likely to attract viewers. This strategy is
regularly employed by SMIs who sympathize with extreme political movements or,
for instance, populist parties, leveraging both political and non-political content to
further their agenda. This blend of content serves to engage a wider audience, increase
followership, and create an environment where political beliefs are woven into every-
day narratives, subtly shaping the audience’s perceptions and values.

The Political AstroTurf Influencer
The Political AstroTurf Influencer shares some similarities with the Ideological
Political Influencer, as both disseminate political and seemingly non-political content
(i.e., moderate-to-high density of political content), typically presented in an opinion-
ated manner, often enhanced with seemingly factual information, and occasionally
delivered in an entertaining style. However, key differences include a higher degree of
political content proximity, as well as the fact that while this type of SMI claims to be
independent from political institutions, parties, and actors in terms of organization and
funding, it is, in reality, not (see Table 1). The primary strategic aim of this influencer
type is to influence public opinion by disseminating (extreme) partisan and ideological
views in support of specific politicians, parties, or political movements, often during
critical periods like election campaigns (Bene & Juhásza, 2025). These influencers
strategically select topics and frame them to benefit their favored political side while
actively undermining and attacking opposing politicians and parties. Although their
ideological bias is sometimes openly acknowledged, these influencers frequently feign
independence from the political figures they support, masking any direct connections
or financial dependencies. By presenting themselves as grassroots voices, political
AstroTurf Influencers aim to give the illusion of widespread support for particular
political positions, even though they are, in fact, strategically advancing a political
agenda (Bene & Juhásza, 2025). This tactic often involves the use of misinformation
or disinformation to sway public opinion, providing campaign support for “their” side
while attempting to damage the reputation and credibility of the opposing side. The
ultimate goal is to shape the political landscape by influencing election outcomes in
favor of their preferred candidates or parties, while undermining the legitimacy of the
opposition. This type of influencer is, for instance, used by populist and anti-demo-
cratic movements, which employ such strategies to rally support, mobilize voters, and
discredit political adversaries, traditional news media, democratic institutions, or the
democratic process as a whole.
The Articles in this Special Issue
The articles in this special issue can be analyzed through the lens of the proposed SMI
typology, offering empirical insights into how different types of influencers engage
with political content.
Sophia Rothut’s contribution to this special issue presents a systematic review of
110 studies, demonstrating a significant surge in research on political social media
influencers since 2022. The article synthesizes existing knowledge on who political
influencers are, the messages they convey, and the audiences they reach. It also under-
scores both the democratic potential of influencers and the risk of influencers being
exploited by political or extremist actors, or of extremist actors taking the role of SMIs
to recruit followers for their ideologies.
Ariel Hasell and Sedona Chinn’s article explores the role of SMIs as political infor-
mation sources. While influencers are often viewed as credible experts, little is known

about trust in them for political information. Using data from the 2020 U.S. presiden-
tial election, the study identifies demographic and political factors linked to this trust,
such as gender, education, partisan affiliation, and political knowledge. The findings
show that trust in influencers is correlated with higher perceived credibility of main-
stream news but has minimal impact on future news consumption and may reduce
election-related belief accuracy.
Irina Andreeva and colleagues’ article examines how extremist political SMIs use
TikTok and frame extreme ideals as morally justified. Through content analysis of
videos from both right-wing and left-wing SMIs, the researchers found that left-wing
SMIs emphasize fairness, while right-wing SMIs focus on loyalty, authority, and
purity, with calls for violence often linked to appeals to authority and purity.
Additionally, while political content boosts engagement, only about half of extremist
influencers’ videos feature political messaging, highlighting the complex nature of
extremism on social media.
Márton Bene and Vanessza Juhász’s contribution deals with the role of AstroTurf
Influencers in the 2022 Hungarian parliamentary election. The study examines
Megafon, an agency established to train and support pro-government influencers who
became central to the campaign. A content analysis shows that AstroTurf Influencers
on Facebook primarily engaged in attack-oriented, fear- and anger-driven messaging,
while electoral actors focused on positive, policy-driven communication. These find-
ings highlight the strategic outsourcing of campaign messaging and the growing influ-
ence of AstroTurf Influencers in elections.
Conclusion and Future Research
What are the implications of SMI for democracy? While some influencers can educate
and raise political awareness, others may promote political agendas without adhering
to journalistic standards, potentially spreading misinformation, biased narratives, or
extremist content, particularly to young people still shaping their political views. This
may undermine informed decision-making, posing a challenge to the democratic pro-
cess by distorting public discourse and potentially weakening trust in credible
information.
The typology of six different SMI types presented in this article provides an initial
framework for understanding the complex and varied ways SMIs communicate politi-
cal information. The proposed types—ranging from non-political SMIs to political
AstroTurf Influencers—highlight the diverse goals, strategies, and consequences that
different influencers may pursue in the political domain.
Future research on political SMIs can benefit from a deeper investigation into the
evolving nature of influencer categories and the dynamic shifts in their political com-
munication strategies over time. As our typology indicates, influencers may transition
between types or adopt hybrid strategies, particularly during key political events such
as elections. Understanding these transitions will be critical to assessing the long-term
impact of SMIs on political engagement and public opinion.

Future studies should examine the ethical implications of influencer-driven politi-
cal content, especially regarding misinformation, political polarization, and the manip-
ulation of younger, less informed audiences. The rise of AI-generated virtual
influencers also challenges traditional notions of trust and authenticity in political dis-
course, raising new ethical concerns. Finally, comparative studies that examine the
role of SMIs across different cultural and political contexts are necessary to further
clarify how these SMIs shape political communication globally.
