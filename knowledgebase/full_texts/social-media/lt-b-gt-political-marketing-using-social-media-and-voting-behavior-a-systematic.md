Abstract
The present research synthesises existing literature to review the impact of political marketing using social media
on voting behaviour. For this, a systematic literature review is conducted. The purpose of the review is to
comprehend the present state of research on this topic, identify the themes that prevail in the literature, identify
gaps, and suggest a future research agenda. The review systematically analyses scholarly articles published
between 2010-2025, focusing on Political marketing using social media and its impact on voting behaviour.
Although the overall literature review considered studies published before 2010 to provide historical context, the
final analysis and findings are based on 48 papers selected from the period 2010–2025. It uses rigorous screening
and inclusion criteria to identify themes that prevail in the present literature and provide a holistic view of the
topic. The findings show that the Political marketing field is still growing. However, the literature is disjointed or
fragmented and mostly based in the US (United States) setting. Though still in the initial stage, political marketing
has a lot of exciting avenues for future research, which we have examined in this paper.
Keywords: political marketing, social media, voting behavior, elections, political branding
1. Introduction
Political marketing has become a significant global
phenomenon as political parties increasingly apply
marketing principles to develop persuasive
communication strategies, position their political
brands, and influence public opinion (Marshment,
2019). Over the last decade, the rapid growth of social
media has altered the political communication
environment by enabling two-way communication,
real-time messaging, and highly targeted outreach to
voters across diverse geographic contexts (Stier et al.,
2020).
Social media has revolutionised how we interact,
communicate, and consume information in the
contemporary digital era. In addition to transforming
interpersonal connections, marketing and commercial
promotion have also undergone significant changes.
Worldwide social media usage has risen from 970
million in 2010 to over 3.6 billion by 2020, profoundly
changing how political information is created,
disseminated, and consumed (Statista, 2021; Kemp,
2020). It shows that Political marketing and
democracy are experiencing a huge transition. This
rapid expansion has allowed political actors to avoid
traditional media filters and engage directly with
voters, making social media the primary platform for
electoral campaigns in many democracies (Stier et al.,
2020). The expanding impact of social media on
election decision-making has piqued academic and
scholarly interest in understanding how digital
platforms change voter attitudes, their perceptions,
and behaviour (Miller &Vaccari, 2020). There is a lot
of scholarly debate and research on the relationship
between social media and political life (Anstead,
2015). As technology advances and more people use
the internet, digital democracy has emerged
(Priambodo, 2023). Saffiullah et al. (2017) stressed
that political parties are now considering using social
media for marketing objectives in the present
technological revolution, where social media has
evolved into the primary channel and purpose of all
communication. As per Binshad (2020), Politics in
India and throughout the world have changed as a
result of social media. In the general election of India,
social media was a popular word (Metkar & Aade,
2020). According to a report by the Centre for Media
Studies, around $8 billion was spent during the Lok
Sabha elections 2019, which was more than the U.S.
presidential election 2016. Large-scale resource
allocations favour a more in-depth grasp of politics
from a marketing perspective (Marshment, 2019).
Social media marketing has added a new dimension to
online political marketing, making it simple for
political parties, institutions, and leaders to stay in
touch with the public and comprehend their demands,
desires, and behaviours. The rise of social media has
heightened scholarly interest in exploring the
conceptual foundations of this subject (Scammell,
2014). As many researchers investigate its theoretical
foundations, understanding political marketing
through a single disciplinary lens is not possible. So,
Political marketing is complicated as it has many
different antecedents in marketing and political
science, two academic disciplines with separate sets
of theories, guiding principles, and worldviews (Abid
et al., 2023). The academic field of political marketing
works on two levels: first, it comprises explanatory
frameworks for political marketing management
activities used by political actors in practice; second, it

2. Conceptual Boundaries
This section of the paper defines the concepts and
background of terms Political Marketing, social media,
Political Marketing using social media, and Voting
Behavior.
2.1 Political Marketing
Many people have different definitions of political
marketing (Scammell, 1999). Several scholars have
defined Political Marketing differently. Shama (1976)
indicates that political marketing and the marketing
of products and services are the same because both
use similar techniques like market research and other
statistical and computer technologies for exploring
the market. Marshment (2014), defined Political
Marketing as "the strategically employed use of
communication to promote a political party or
candidate to their target audiences to achieve their
goals." Her explanation highlights political
marketing's strategic component and its emphasis on
accomplishing specific political goals. Newman (1999)
explains Political Marketing as the usage of market
segmentation and targeting tactics, as well as the
marketing mix (product, price, place, promotion, and
packaging), to effectively and efficiently accomplish
political goals. Political marketing refers to the
techniques employed by candidates and political
parties to increase voter support. This encompasses
political advertising in the media, social media
campaigns, and political branding techniques (Jahidin
& Muskin, 2024). The relevance of marketing
concepts to politics is emphasised in Newman's term.
The modern political leader must rely on marketing
not only to win elections but also to be successful in
government (Newman, 2002). Many words from
mainstream marketing have been used and adapted
by political marketers, including image management
(segmentation, targeting, and positioning) and
consumer (voter). Political marketing is a
combination of marketing and politics (Marshment,
2001). The sub-discipline of political marketing
encompasses various research streams, including
critical political marketing, political marketing
research, political marketing environment, etc (Pich &
Armannsdottir, 2025).

2.3 Political Marketing Using Social Media
Social media has significantly changed the art of
political marketing by changing how politicians
communicate, persuade their followers, and affect
voter behaviour. In addition to being used by
businesses to manage their public relations, social
media has developed into a crucial instrument for
election advertising and has grown to be a major
global forum for opinion expression (Safiullah et al.,
2017), for example, U.S. presidential elections of 2008,
New Zealand general election of 2011, Korean
elections,2010 and the Swedish election 2010.Early
research focused on social media's interactive
features, noting how platforms like Facebook and
Twitter(X) allow for two-way contact between
political players and citizens (Chadwick, 2017). Unlike
traditional media, social media enables political
parties to have individualised, real-time conversations,
resulting in increased transparency, authenticity, and
relational proximity (Enli, 2017). These traits make
social media especially useful for political branding,
and targeted persuasion. It shows that one of the
marketing strategies employed by political parties to
reach voters is the power of social media (Okan et al.,
2014). Today, Facebook, X, and Instagram can be used
to boost political campaigns, creating new channels of
communication between candidates and voters
(Williams, 2017). A whole new paradigm for
promoting political involvement and engagement has
emerged as a result of the use of social media for
political marketing, campaigns, and engagement
(Ayankoya et al., 2015).
2.4 Voting Behaviour
In politics, various individual factors, including
political awareness, evaluation of voter needs, and
value creation, significantly influence voting
behaviour (Marandi et al., 2025). Voter satisfaction
with a party's or candidate's performance has the
potential to impact loyalty. Satisfied voters are more
likely to remain loyal to their favoured candidate or
party (Nwikinaka & Ofori, 2024). Voters' voting
behaviour reflects their content consumption, making
them prospective customers for the "electoral
marketplace."(Sophocleous et al., 2024). To secure
critical votes and seats, political parties invest a
significant amount of money. Understanding the
decision-making process consequently has a practical
advantage since it is crucial to comprehend voter
requirements and wants for elections to be successful
(O'Cass, 2002). Voting and support for the party that
is most active on social media and in the digital arena
are more likely to occur. It has also been
demonstrated that people who use social media
frequently could be swayed by what they read about a
certain politician when it comes to voting (Biswas et
al., 2014). Various studies show social media has a
significant impact on changing voting behavior
(Biswas et al., 2014; Sharma & Parma, 2016; Kumar et
al., 2021).
3. Research Methodology
Systematic Literature Review is a methodological
approach to compiling, evaluating, synthesising, and
presenting data from multiple research papers on a
topic of interest or research issue (Pati & Lorusso,
2018). SLRs are suitable for establishing themes,
creating conceptual models or integrative frameworks,
synthesising or providing an overview of an academic
topic, and suggesting future research directions (Tian
et al., 2018; Paul & Criado, 2020; Abid et al., 2023).
For the current study, the time selected for the
reviews is between 2010-2025. Although the overall
literature review considered studies published before
2010 to provide historical context, the final analysis
and findings are based on 48 papers selected from the
period 2010–2025. In SLRs, it is usual practice to
extract literature using predefined keywords (Abid et
al., 2023). Keywords that were used during the search:
“Political Marketing”, “social media”, “Voting Behavior”,
“Political Marketing and Social Media”, “Political
Marketing and Voting Behavior”, and “Social media
and Voting Behavior”. To capture the multidisciplinary
view, four databases were used for downloading the
papers for review. The databases are Web of Science,
Google Scholar, Scopus, and Science Direct. Forty-
eight (48) papers were considered after the inclusion
and exclusion criteria (as seen in Table 1):

4.1.4 Voter Segments
In the conducted literature, mainly young voters are
covered in most of the studies (Biswas et al., 2014;
Dabula, 2017; Balo & Sawon, 2019; Dankwah &
Mensah, 2021; Kumar et al., 2021; Abid et al., 2021).

target for political marketers and campaign
strategists. But this heavy emphasis on youth also

flaw. Despite their tremendous voting power in many
democracies, older voters, rural people, and less

An excessive focus on young voters restricts the
generalizability of results and may ignore the impact
of digital political messaging on other age groups,

disparities in political communication and social

4.2 Thematic Analysis
This section covers the explored themes identified
from the literature:
Objective 2: - To explore the current themes that
dominate the literature.
An inductive thematic analysis method was used to
determine the prevailing themes. Following the
completion of the 48 research papers, each article
underwent open coding in order to identify recurrent
themes pertaining to voter behaviour, social media
usage, and political marketing. A three-step coding
procedure was used to determine the themes. Initially,
each article's main ideas, concepts, and recurrent
patterns were extracted using open coding. In order
to find connections and cluster related ideas, the first
codes were grouped using axial coding in the second
step. Ultimately, the categories were narrowed down
into higher-order themes that encapsulated the
essential elements of social media political marketing
by selective coding.
After that, these original codes were classified into
more general axial groups, which were then combined
to create themes. This procedure made sure that the
thematic framework didn't come from presumptions
but rather from the literature itself. To ensure
reliability, the coding was used by two independent
coders. Each coder reviewed the full texts separately
and developed preliminary codes. Coding
disagreements were discussed and resolved through
iterative refinement. Inter-coder reliability was
assessed using Cohen’s Kappa. This procedure made
sure that the themes weren't subjective or driven by
the researcher, but rather were derived systematically.

4.2.1 Marketing Concepts and Digital Political Branding
This theme captures how marketing principles can be
applied in the political field and help in creating
strong positioning. Several studies demonstrate the
relationship and application of marketing concepts to
political marketing. It shows that marketing
techniques can be used in the political field. Many
words from mainstream marketing have been
appropriated and used in political marketing, such as
customer (voter) and image management
(segmentation, targeting, and positioning)
(Winchester et al.,2016). According to Shama (1976),
Political marketing and product and service
marketing share a lot of ideas and techniques. To
succeed both in the short and long terms, parties
must comprehend the fundamentals of marketing
(Niffenegger, 1989; Reid, 1988). Political marketing,
however, is still a relatively recent concept compared
to marketing or political science. Previously a small
topic, it is now an important area of research (Harris
& Lock, 2010; Newman, 2012). As per the literature,
branding is essential to contemporary political
communication because it allows political actors to
differentiate themselves from the competition and
keep a consistent identity across digital platforms.
The branding strategy of Donald Trump has gained
attention (Billard & Moran, 2020). Studies highlighted
that social media plays a very important role in
transforming Political branding (Billard & Moran,
2020; Lucarelli et al., 2021). Brand communities play
a crucial role in co-branding (Billard & Moran, 2020),
whereas some studies show that behaviour towards
political brands is different from commercial brands.
Positioning is the way of building and maintaining
voters' impressions, linkages, and pictures of a
political brand based on its related values and beliefs
through clear, consistent, and coherent marketing
communication (Shahid et al., 2024).
4.2.2 Social Media as a Strategic Tool for Political
Communication
This theme emphasises studies exploring how
political parties use social media strategically for
campaigning, agenda-setting, and political
communication. The usage of social media has
expanded beyond the corporate sector. It has become
an important tool for marketing in the political field
as well (Safiullah et al., 2017), for example, in the US
Presidential election 2008, Korean elections 2010, the
Swedish election 2010, and the New Zealand general
election 2011. Social media serves as a valuable
information source, informing users about politicians
and influencing their decision to support or oppose a
candidate (Sharma & Parma, 2016). Through online
campaigns that seek to foster relationships between
politicians and the public, social media helps to
increase public confidence in elected officials (Balo &
Shawon, 2019). Several studies show the impact of
social media on political marketing (Ghosal, 2018;

5. Conclusion
Systematic review synthesis and presented an
overview of the Political Marketing field. The main
aim of the literature was to describe the current
status of political marketing and to find out the
prevalent themes. To achieve these objectives, the
findings of the papers are separated into two parts: 1)
Descriptive analysis of diverse scenarios of Political
marketing, where we have discussed it in the context
of geographical distributions, elections, voter
segments, social media platforms, data sources, and
research methods. The findings show that political
marketing through social media is no longer limited to
information and content dissemination but has
developed into a multifaceted strategy that includes
digital political branding, data-driven campaigning,
behavioural impact, and help in predicting results. It
is found that the corpus of literature is disjoint and
mostly focused on the United States (U.S.) setting.
Second, the review identifies systematic biases that
restrict the generalizability of current findings by
recording platform preferences, electoral focus, voter
demographics, and methodological trends. These
biases include an excessive reliance on youth samples,
national elections, quantitative surveys, and X-centric
analyses. Overall, the findings of the article suggest
that the field of political marketing is still at a growing
stage and there are more future opportunities for the
researcher to explore this area. 2) Thematic analysis,
which revealed four themes that dominate the
literature. The themes are highly concentrated on the
words Political marketing, social media impact in
predicting election results, determinants of voters'
behavior, etc. Because the area of Political Marketing
is new, it offers several future directions for further
investigation. The field of Political marketing provides
enormous prospects for future researchers to explore
the growing dynamics in response to technological
improvements, growing social media reach, and
changing voter behavior. This review helps marketing
as well as political science scholars for their further
studies. Despite using a careful and organised
approach, the review contains certain flaws. Social
media is not the only relevant search phrase. In a
similar vein, we exclude the word "social network”.
The number of studies is also not sufficient for a
comprehensive review. The number of databases is
also limited. The evaluation is also limited by a
marketing-focused perspective, which is shown in the
themes discovered.
6. Recommendation and Future Research Agenda
The review found several conceptual and
methodological flaws as well as academic gaps in the
literature. Because of these shortcomings and recent
Political marketing literature, we suggest a future
agenda for further study.
From the themes identified, first, more research is
required to investigate digital political branding in
various political cultures, with a focus on how
algorithmic visibility, visual narrative, and
authenticity affect voter trust and the development of
political identities. Second, by examining ethical
issues, voters' opinions of data use, and the
behavioural efficacy of tailored political messages
across platforms, academics should broaden their
studies on microtargeting and strategic campaigning.
Third, elderly voters, rural communities, and local or
regional electoral contexts should be included in
studies on social media's impact on voter attitudes
and behavior in addition to youth-centric samples and
national elections. Establishing causal links between
social media exposure and voting behavior would be
aided by longitudinal and experimental methods.
Lastly, in order to properly evaluate forecast accuracy
and mitigate misinformation risks, studies on election
prediction and disinformation should take cross-
platform and interdisciplinary approaches, fusing
political communication theory with data science and
artificial intelligence.
The descriptive analysis of the paper shows that most
of the studies in the literature focus on young voters
as a voter segment. In future studies, researchers can
focus on First-time voters, women voters, and
minority voters as a segment that will provide a
better insight into the political field. The mainstream
marketing model of Segmentation, Targeting, and
Positioning can be applied in the Political marketing
field. Most studies focus only on a few platforms of
social media like X and Facebook. Further studies can
consider other platforms as well like WhatsApp,
Instagram, LinkedIn, etc. In review, the main
geographical focus was the US and UK. Furthermore,
