ÖZ
Kapitalizm, ortaya çıktığı dönemden itibaren ekonomik olduğu kadar politik ve ideolojik bir düzen
olarak da işlemiştir. Günümüzde farklı olan ise bu etkinin dijital ortamlarda veri temelli, daha görünür
ve bireylerin gündelik pratiklerine nüfuz eden bir biçim almış olmasıdır. Tüketim toplumunun
dinamikleri, bireylerin tüketici olmanın yanı sıra birer pazarlama nesnesi ve gö rünürlük odaklı dijital
özne olarak konumlandırılmasını sağlamaktadır. Bu bağlamda sinema, kapitalist sistemin bireyler
üzerindeki etkilerini görünür kılan eleştirel bir araç olarak öne çıkmaktadır. Bu çalışma, Peter Weir’in
The Truman Show  (1998) filmi üzerinden kapitalist sistemin bireyler üzerindeki etkisini ve tüketim
toplumunun işleyiş mekanizmalarını Peirce’ın üçlü gösterge modeli ve Barthes’ın göstergebilimsel
çerçevesi ışığında ele almaktadır. Çalışmada, filmin göstergebilimsel kodları analiz edilerek kapitalist
düzenin birey üzerindeki kontrol mekanizmaları incelenmiştir. Filmde Truman’ın yaşadığı yapay dünya,
modern tüketim toplumunun simgesel bir modeli olarak kurgulanmış ve bireyin sistem iç indeki
konumuna dair eleştirel bir anlatı sunulmuştur. Truman’ın farkındalık süreci, bireyin özgürlük ve
gerçeklik kavramlarını sorgulamasına olanak sağlayarak, günümüz tüketim toplumunun görünmez
sınırlarına dair bir eleştiri sunmaktadır. Çalışmanın temel  araştırma problemi, günümüz bireylerinin
gözetim ve tüketim mekanizmalarına gönüllü katılımının filmde nasıl temsil edildiğini ortaya koymaktır.
Bulgular, günümüz bireylerinin Truman gibi bir sistem içinde yaşadığını; ancak günümüz toplumunda
bireylerin kendi rızalarıyla birer pazarlama nesnesine dönüştüklerini göstermektedir. Günümüz dijital
içerik üreticileri, sosyal medya ve dijital platformlar aracılığıyla kendi yaşamlarını birer meta haline
getirerek, sistemin aktif katılımcıları olmaktadır. Bu bağlam da The Truman Show  bir sinema eseri
olmanın ötesinde kapitalist düzenin birey üzerindeki etkisini ve modern tüketim kültürünün görünmez
sınırlarını tartışmaya açan eleştirel bir anlatıdır.

ABSTRACT
Since its emergence, capitalism has functioned not only as an economic formation but also as a political
and ideological order. What is distinctive today is that these effects have become more visible and
data-driven within digital environments. The dynamics of the consumer society ensure that individuals
are positioned not only as consumers but also as marketing objects. In this context, cinema stands out
as a critical tool that makes visible the effects of the capitalist system on individuals.  This study
semiotically analyzes the effects of the capitalist system on individuals and the functioning
mechanisms of the consumer society through Peter Weir’s The Truman Show (1998) within the
framework of Peirce’s triple sign model and Barthes’s semiotic framework . The study analyzes the
semiotic codes of the film and examines the control mechanisms of the capitalist order on the
individual. In the film, the artificial world in which Truman lives is constructed as a symbolic model of
the modern consumer society and a critical narrative is presented about the position of the individual
within the system. Truman’s process of awareness allows the individual to question the concepts of
freedom and reality and offers a criticism of the invisible limits of today’s consumer society. The main
research problem of the study is to reveal how the voluntary participation of today’s individuals in
surveillance and consumption mechanisms is represented in the movie. The findings show that today’s
individuals live in a system like Tr uman; however, today, individuals voluntarily turn into marketing
objects. Today’s digital content producers are active participants in the system by turning their own
lives into commodities through social media and digital platforms. In this context, The Truman Show is
not only a work of cinema, but also a critical narrative that discusses the impact of the capitalist order
on the individual and the invisible boundaries of modern consumer culture.
Keywords: Consumer society, Semiotics, Surveillance, Neoliberal subjectivity, Media criticism, The
Truman Show

INTRODUCTION
Today, capitalism is widely recognised not merely as an economic model but as a political,
cultural, and social formation. Although it historically emerged through market expansion and profit-
oriented organisation, from its inception it has also incorporated ethical norms, political power relations,
and techniques of governing conduct. Over time, these mechanisms have penetrated everyday life,
shaping social relations, routines, and value systems. The continuity of the system depends not only on
production but also on the sustained stimulation of desire and the normalisation of consumption as a
mode of existence. Thus, capitalism has evolved into a social order in which individuals are encouraged
to shape their identities, aspirations, and sense of belonging through market-mediated choices. Within
this context, the individual is no longer merely a producer but becomes an object and agent of
circulation, increasingly consuming symbols, experiences, and meanings. What initially appeared as
need-satisfaction has become tied to identity construction, social recognition, and emotional fulfilment,
forming the basis of consumer-oriented lifestyles in contemporary societies.
Particularly within the context of surveillance society discussions, The Truman Show has
frequently been examined with reference to Foucault’s panopticon model (1977). For example, Wise
(2002) reads the film through Gilles Deleuze’s concept of the “society of control”, which he distinguishes
from Foucault’s model of disciplinary surveillance, and argues that the film reveals a culture of
continuous monitoring, branding, and individualism that structures everyday life. Wong (2021) analyzes
the film within the context of Shoshana Zuboff’s concept of “surveillance capitalism”, exploring the
commodification of individual privacy through a culture of profit-driven surveillance. The literature also
examines the film through its exploration of identity formation and spatial manipulation. Fitch (2019)
argues that the artificial space in which Truman lives is constructed as a space of surveillance, and that
this space is instrumentalized in shaping the individual’s identity. In this respect, the film visualizes the
process by which an individual adapts to social roles through internalized surveillance.
The film stands out for its critical reflection of the effects of the capitalist system on individuals
and the mechanisms of the consumer society. The film, which serves as a critique of the system, utilizes
the depth provided by semiotic analysis to examine the layers of meaning within the film. Given the film’s
abundance of semiotic codes, the analysis focused on indicators of the system and the consumer society
it shapes. These indicators are central to the film’s critique of the system on a visual/narrative level.

The Truman Show, through various indicators, suggests to its audience that they are Trumans
trapped within the capitalist world. By prompting individuals to question their own freedom and will, the
film also points to the possibility that another, yet unexperienced, world might exist, independent of this
one. In contrast to this artificial reality that Truman unknowingly experienced, today’s individuals
showcase their individual/private lives in the digital world and often willingly accept to be part of a
consumer society. As Zuboff’s (2019) concept of “surveillance capitalism” suggests, digital content
production relies on the voluntary participation of individuals, even as their data is extracted and
monitored largely without their awareness or consent. Today’s consumer society has moved from being
a system that controls individuals to a structure where individuals produce their own content and serve
the system voluntarily. In this respect, the film is more than just a human story; it is a metaphor that
reflects the effects of the capitalist order on the individual and the possibility of overcoming the limits of
this order from a critical perspective.
Grounded in the broader premise that all aspects of culture can be studied as products of
semiotic activity (Eco, 1976, p. 22), the study employed semiotic analysis within the scope of qualitative
research, structured within the framework of Peirce’s (1931) tripartite model of signs and Saussure’s
(1916/1959, pp. 66-67) foundational distinction between signifier and signified. Furthermore, second-
level meanings were examined within the context of everyday objects and media representations in the
film using Roland Barthes’s (1972) semiotic framework. The study interprets the film’s uses of space,
visual symbols, dialogue, and narrative structure on Barthes’s semiotic plane. The analysis is primarily
based on Barthes’s (1972) two-level sign system (denotation-connotation). While the study is a
descriptive film analysis, the relationship between the meanings developed through the film and the
conceptual framework is considered. The data is based on the film analysis and the theoretical
arguments developed around the film. Five scenes from the film were selected as thematic focal points:
the set space and artificial sky, product placement scenes, the fall of the Sirius lamp, the Truman Bar
scenes, and the final scene. These scenes were analyzed within the framework of Peirce’s tripartite
model of signs and Barthes’s semiotic concepts, with each sign systematically examined. In addition to
semiotics, the analysis mobilises media political-economy and surveillance-studies insights to account for
organisational control, labour, and datafication beyond consumption.
The study’s fundamental problem is how individuals voluntarily participate in the system’s
surveillance and consumption mechanisms in today’s digital age and how this is represented in the film
The Truman Show. The film visualizes the process by which individuals voluntarily turn themselves into

ANATOMY OF THE CONSUMER SOCIETY: FROM NEED TO IDENTITY
CONSTRUCTION
While consumption is fundamentally defined as an action that living beings engage in to survive
and meet their needs, this definition is currently inadequate to define consumption. The current form of
the consumer society, especially after World War II, developed along with the rising welfare economies
in the post-World War II period and brought about a form of social organization based on consumption
rather than production (Baudrillard, 2008; Featherstone, 1991). According to Featherstone, in modern
societies, consumption has gone beyond meeting basic needs and has become integrated with the
processes of individuals constructing identities, generating social belonging, and generating cultural
meanings (Featherstone, 1991). Broadly speaking, consumption, which continues throughout an
individual’s life, is a necessary action to meet the needs necessary for survival. However, unlike other
living beings, humans can also engage in consumption to meet their social and psychological needs (Bakır
& Çelik, 2013, p. 47). For example, drawing on Veblen’s concept of “conspicuous consumption” Bocock
(1993, pp. 15-16) argues that patterns of consumption can serve to mark individuals off from other social
status groups, providing them with a sense of social identity, rather than simply meeting a need. This
difference, which distinguishes humans from other living creatures, causes the phenomenon of
consumption to be given different meanings throughout the process and prevents the concept from
being defined by a fixed and universally accepted definition.

The market has evolved into an era of global competition driven by a significant increase in
product variety, completely reshaping consumer purchasing processes. In this context, advancements in
production processes have led to a gradual increase in the variety of products available to consumers,
ushering in a new era of competition among companies. This process was not limited to intra-market
competition; it also led to the integration of cultural products into the realm of competition and
consumption, as emphasized by Adorno and Horkheimer in the concept of the culture industry (Adorno
& Horkheimer, 2014, p. 166). According to Baudrillard, during this process, manufacturers focused not
only on the use value of products but also on their symbolic meanings in an effort to persuade consumers
(2008, pp. 44-45). In this competitive era, to stay ahead in challenging market conditions, companies
found themselves competing for consumer attention. Paradoxically, Bauman argues that, in this process,
consumers themselves are compelled to transform into commodities capable of continuously capturing
attention (2023). In addition to functionality, emotional and psychological aspects have also become a
part of marketing strategies for products to appeal to consumers. According to Featherstone, products
have become symbols representing consumer values such as status, identity, and social belonging (1991,
pp. 62-63). Intense competition has led to the use of tools such as marketing, advertising, and public
relations to capture consumer attention. Advertisements go beyond simply describing the benefits of a
product and instead emphasize the lifestyle individuals will achieve by owning it, encouraging consumers
to act on emotional impulses rather than rational decisions. According to Bocock (1993, pp. 3, 48), at this
point consumption has acquired a cultural and ideological dimension.
According to Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, individuals pursue self-actualization only after their
more basic needs have been met; however, within consumer culture, possessing a product perceived as
a status symbol can serve as a shortcut through which individuals seek to signal esteem and self-worth,
even when their more basic needs remain unmet (Maslow, 1943; Featherstone, 1991). Accordingly,
consumption, in addition to meeting physical needs, has also become an important tool in establishing
social relationships, determining social status, and constructing individual identities (Bauman, 2023). The
capitalist system has transformed consumption into an effort to satisfy individuals’ endless desires.
Baudrillard argues that the concept of need is linked to the natural inclination toward happiness, and
that this is one of the fundamental elements driving the consumer society (2008, p. 51). While the
concept of equality was important as a political and sociological force in the past, today this concept has
been replaced by the pursuit of happiness. According to Baudrillard, the idea that individuals can achieve
happiness through consumption has become an ideology in modern societies and forms the basis of the

FROM SIMULATION TO REALITY: THE SPATIAL TRAPS OF ORDER
The artificial world Truman inhabits is the setting of a television program, but it simultaneously
shapes his perception of reality as controllable and manipulable. Because it was designed as a television
program, every detail within the set, from the architectural elements to the furnishings used, was
deliberately chosen. These details were designed to present the target audience with a depiction of an
ideal life while minimizing Truman’s awareness. Therefore, while this world is created within a certain
order and harmony, everything it contains is artificial, and this artificial world can be associated with
Baudrillard’s concepts. According to Baudrillard, this artificial world constitutes a hyperreality rather than
a mere representation of reality (2003, pp. 13-14). This constructed world produces its own reality,
aligning with Baudrillard’s concept of “simulacra”. The film provides a metaphorical framework for
understanding Baudrillard’s theory of simulation.
The island where Truman lives is an idealized settlement. The image is portrayed as a small,
quiet, orderly, and peaceful seaside settlement. This image can be interpreted as a reflection of the
“perfect life” offered to individuals in modern consumer society. The styles, layouts, and colors of the
buildings evoke a sense of order in the viewer, providing a sense of security and stability. This flawless
order, which conceals the manipulative nature of the system, creates the impression that the world
Truman lives in is a “perfect simulation” and offers the viewer a critique of the sterile and idealized
lifestyle offered to individuals by the capitalist system. The spatial arrangements reveal that The Truman
Show is not merely a stage but serves as a metaphor for the deceptive realities presented to individuals
by consumer society. The set functions as a prison that physically and mentally restricts Truman’s life,
maintaining the illusion of an ideal world.
The props used in the fictional setting are not comprised of luxury goods; on the contrary, the
vast majority of the furniture, cars, and clothing are affordable, understated items. This deliberate choice
is intended to ensure that the program’s target audience, the broader audience, feels part of the world
they see. Behind the ordinary appearance of the setting lies the product placement strategy, a
cornerstone of the program’s economic operation. Any product could be placed within the set, which
creates a livable world, but even elements that appear as ordinary decor in Truman’s daily life are actually

agent, profiting from ratings generated through Truman’s commodified daily life.
There’s no real sun on set; artificial daylight is generated through artificial light sources.
Therefore, Truman is forced to supplement the vitamin D he should receive from the sun. This world,
where even the basic elements of nature are reproduced as imitations, functions as an artificial order
where every aspect of Truman’s life, from his physical health to his mental perceptions, is manipulated.
Similarly, the moon and stars are also created through artificial light sources. This artificial sky is designed
to completely sever Truman’s connection to the real world and suppress his awareness. This artificial
nature fiction demonstrates that individuals can be directed by manipulating reality with different tools,
and can be metaphorically associated with Baudrillard’s discussions of hyperreality (2003).
The fall of a set projector, one of the artificial light sources, creates a perfectly ordered breaking
point and is a metaphor that gives Truman his first clue about his virtual world. The fallen lamp bears the
word “Sirius”. This detail is a critical turning point in the film’s symbolic narrative because, according to
Kamrin, Sirius (known to the ancient Egyptians as Sopdet) was closely associated with the annual Nile
flood and the marking of cyclical time (Kamrin, 2017). According to Holberg, Sirius is one of the brightest
stars in the night sky and has been used by various civilizations throughout history for orientation,
timekeeping, and religious rituals (Holberg, 2007). The word Sirius, written on the projector in the film,
carries a layer of symbolic and mythological meaning and can be interpreted as “guide” and “herald of

truth” in modern narratives. Therefore, the fall of the Sirius projector represents Truman’s moment of
enlightenment. Sirius, which has guided different civilizations throughout history, offers Truman a cue to
discover his own path. According to Baudrillard’s (2003) theory of hyperreality, such an event can be
interpreted as a blurring of the boundary between reality and simulation, and as the beginning of an
individual’s awareness of the simulation they are in, through errors and/or discontinuities. The fall of the
Sirius projector is the first rupture that reveals the artificiality of the world Truman lives in. Such details
demonstrate the blurring of the boundaries between reality and representation. In Baudrillard’s words,
hyperreality reveals itself precisely in such boundary violations, and this example is a symbolic
representation.
Every detail that disrupts the perfect order attracts more attention and creates breaking points.
Errors resulting from technical glitches, such as rain falling in a single spot, are increasingly visible within
the order and become signs. The controllability of rain, a natural phenomenon difficult to control by
human hands, reveals the visibility of illusions. Order can be linked to Foucault’s concept of the
“panopticon” (1977), because Truman’s world functions as an orderly, controllable, and monitored
prison. However, the film’s surveillance mechanism differs from the classic panopticon model. Because
Truman is unaware that he is being watched, he doesn’t develop self-control. In this respect, the
structure is similar to Mathiesen’s concept of the “synopticon” (Mathiesen, 1997, p. 219), where a large
number of people watch a small number (or a single individual), rather than a small number watching
over a large population. This panoptic-synoptic duality in The Truman Show-where the director functions
as the panoptic overseer while millions of viewers simultaneously watch Truman-has also been identified
in Turkish scholarship on the film (Sucu, 2020, p. 4). The similarity of each day creates monotony and
stagnation for Truman, while also facilitating the detection of deviations from the established order.
According to Bauman, the constant presence of order and repetition can be considered a metaphor
representing capitalist societies’ efforts to keep individuals in a constant cycle (Bauman, 2023). Life in the
real world has a chaotic, unexpected, and constantly changing flow. However, Truman’s world, unlike
this dynamic nature, remains artificially stagnant. This stagnation hints that the individual is disconnected
from their natural environment and living within a controlled simulation. The process of questioning
begins with the environment and progresses toward the individual’s identity, driving Truman to seek out
the truth. All these details are powerful metaphors that shake Truman’s perception of reality and initiate
his journey of breaking away from the simulation. Truman’s questioning reveals the inevitable crumbling
of an order based on an artificial reality and the individual’s struggle to reach the truth.

A NEW SHOW: WILLING PRISONERS OF THE SURVEILLANCE SOCIETY
The Truman Show is a television program watched with great interest worldwide. Truman is a
global star, even if he doesn’t realize it. Broadcasting Truman’s every moment live from his birth onward,
the program has built a loyal audience over the years, transforming him into a media phenomenon. The
program’s popularity stems from the emotional bond viewers form with Truman, leading to his
transformation into a consumer object. In the film, a family of Asian descent is seen wearing Truman
clothing and badges while watching the program. Similarly, Truman pillows, photographs of Truman in
the background, and clothing associated with Truman are prominent in the home of one family watching
the program. These details clearly demonstrate that the program has evolved from a tool for filling time
to a passion and a form of identity expression for viewers.
From a semiotic perspective, Truman’s transformation into a “product” is a powerful metaphor
for understanding how modern consumer society impacts on individuals. In the context of Baudrillard’s
analysis of consumer society, objects are consumed not only for their use value but also for the symbolic
meanings they carry (2008, pp. 44-45). Truman has been stripped of his individual identity and
transformed into a symbol of consumer culture. Everything Truman wears, the badges he wears, and
the products he uses reveal how his global fame has been commodified and transformed into economic
gain.
One of the key venues reflecting the popularity and influence of The Truman Show is the Truman
Bar. This bar serves as an event space and a hub for collective viewing. People of all ages gather at the
bar, watching the program throughout the day. Posters hanging on the bar walls, the aprons worn by
bar staff, and even a photo of Truman on an extra’s tie demonstrate the profound connection the venue
has built through Truman. Truman’s image is ubiquitous, not just an element of the program but also a
brand, a consumer object. The program is followed with the same enthusiasm as a sporting event.
Audience reactions reflect the emotional bond the audience has formed with Truman. This emotional
connection functions as a tool that increases the program’s economic value by prompting viewers to
react strongly to the events Truman experiences. A man who regularly watches the program in the
shower and two security guards at work demonstrate that The Truman Show has become not just
entertainment but an integral part of viewers’ daily lives. In scenes featuring these characters, the
Truman calendar and Truman mug hanging in the security guards’ office demonstrate that the viewer
strengthens their connection with the show through objects. Similarly, the presence of the word
“Truman Show” on the man’s clothing while watching it in the shower clearly demonstrates that the

The Truman Show, a typical example of the commodified cultural products produced by what
Adorno and Horkheimer (2014) call the “culture industry,” not only serves as a means of entertainment

searching for another program, asking, “What’s on the other channel?” This scene clearly critiques the
nature of popular culture and its offerings, demonstrating that the value of consumer objects in popular

audiences, distracted by temporary entertainment and false pleasures in front of their screens, represent
the cultural superficiality of modern societies and the entrapment of individuals in a perpetual cycle of
consumption, while also revealing the capitalist system’s practices of transforming “leisure” spaces into
acts of consumption. This scene overlaps with Adorno and Horkheimer’s concept of culture industry,
making visible the function of popular culture products in accelerating the cycle of consumption.
Viewers’ rapid disengagement from Truman’s emotionally charged life offers a powerful systemic
critique of the impermanent nature of popular culture and its tendency to create fleeting products that
are quickly forgotten after consumption.
Truman’s quest for freedom also creates a powerful tool for critiquing the consumption habits
of the masses. While Truman’s liberation from the artificial world he inhabits is seen as a struggle for
freedom, its viewership represents one of the most distinctive examples of this transition back into a
sphere of endless consumption. While Truman is freed from his own prison, the audience watching the
program continues to live within the invisible prisons created by consumer culture. The desire to be
visible is an important phenomenon that has existed throughout human history and is the source of the
social status of the individual. While visibility in traditional societies was limited by time and space, the
advancement of today’s communication technologies and mass media has eliminated these limitations,
and visibility has evolved into a universal phenomenon. Thanks to the opportunities afforded by
technological advancements, individuals can create their own visibility and reach a wider audience.

Visibility is no longer merely a way of being; it is also one of the crucial dynamics supporting the
sustainability of digital capitalism. This dynamic structure, which determines economic, social, and
cultural values, has imposed new restrictions on individuals’ freedoms, trapping them in a cycle fueled
by a constant anxiety of being monitored and evaluated. When we connect this process to the film The
Truman Show, we can understand the disproportionate nature of the ways in which Truman’s visibility
contradicts his freedom in the artificial world presented by the set. The media’s ownership of the
commodity has completely eliminated Truman’s control over his life. Truman’s lack of control can be
interpreted as a symbolic consequence of his visibility. This situation refers to examples of today’s
individuals sacrificing their privacy and individual space, becoming willing objects of a consumer society
that markets their lives. The Truman Show, while offering a media critique through these readings, offers
a significant narrative for analyzing the increasing “obligation of visibility” on digital platforms today and
its effects on individuals.
When we examine the film in terms of surveillance models, it is possible to see a counterpart to
each model. Foucault’s (1995) panopticon model, which forms the basis of surveillance models, draws
on Bentham’s prison model, and describes a surveillance system based on individuals developing self-
control mechanisms because they believe they are constantly being observed. In this model, individuals
are conscious of being watched, and this awareness shapes their behavior. In the context of The Truman
Show, the situation is closer to Mathiesen’s (1997, p. 219) concept of “synopticon” than to the classical
panopticon, because not a small number of authority figures but a large number of viewers are watching
a single person (Truman). All actors/figures in the program act with the awareness that they have to act
in accordance with the script and instructions set by Christof, the director of the fictional program. Each
actor is aware of being part of the order and of the need to play their role correctly for the continuity of
the structure, and that he/she is constantly monitored by an invisible authority. Truman is the only
exception to this constructed order. Unaware that he is part of the surveillance, Truman begins to realize
that the world he lives in is an illusion by following the clues presented to him as a result of technical
glitches and inconsistencies. This awareness differentiates the film from the classical panoptic model and
points to the possibility that the individual can escape surveillance through consciousness. Therefore, the
concept of synopticon in the film and the viewing culture it creates offer a more functional conceptual
framework. Truman’s efforts transform him from a passively observed object into an active subject
trying to construct his own reality. This can be read as a metaphorical expression of the tension between
surveillance and freedom in the film. Actors/figures voluntarily participate in an artificial reality and

By establishing strong bonds with their followers, influencers can collaborate with brands

lives. Because algorithms reward constant sharing and visibility, users present themselves in an idealized
way to attract more attention, receive likes, and increase engagement. Today, social media has become
a hub for consumption, advertising, and commerce, as well as a space where individual identities are
staged. From this perspective, while Truman unwillingly became a part of surveillance, today’s social
media users voluntarily choose to be monitored and turn it into a sustainable career. In the digital age,
individuals view the sacrifice of privacy not as a cost but as a means of gain.
With social media, visibility and surveillance have evolved into a horizontal model, unlike classical
panoptic relationships. Numerous individuals monitor one another, creating a practice consistent with
Andrejevic’s concept of “lateral surveillance” (2005, p. 488). At the same time, the data-driven operation
of platforms is similar to Poster’s (1990, p. 97) “superpanopticon” model, as surveillance no longer
operates solely through institutions of confinement, but through databases and algorithms. While The
Truman Show reveals the burdens of visibility on individuals, today’s social media users and digital
content creators willingly bear this burden. While Truman is surveilled without his consent, social media
users choose to submit to surveillance willingly. In this context, the film demonstrates a surveillance
system to which individuals are subjected without their consent, while also illuminating the multilayered
surveillance practices in which users voluntarily participate in the modern digital world. This contrast
opens up an important area of discussion regarding the digitalized surveillance mechanisms of modern
society and the changing consciousness of individuals.

CONCLUSION
This study aims to examine the effects of the capitalist system and consumer society on
individuals through The Truman Show, using a semiotic analysis based on Peirce and Barthes. The study’s
fundamental research problem is to reveal how the film represents today’s individuals’ voluntary
participation in consumption and surveillance mechanisms. The signs examined in the study reveal how
individuals’ lives are shaped by consumer culture, how media and mass communication function as
manipulative tools, and how perceptions of freedom are constructed. The film explores the artificial
reality an ordinary individual is exposed to throughout their lives and the process of questioning this
reality, while also offering a powerful critique that questions the boundaries of modern society. At the
same time, this critique remains ambivalent: the film stages both the rationalised enclosure of
bureaucratic control and the flexible coordination of a networked media economy. Accordingly, its

In a consumer society, individuals construct their identities through objects owned, the norms
presented in the media, and the behavioral patterns the system expects of individuals. Truman’s
transformation into a consumer object sold to “audiences” throughout his life highlights how consumer
society reduces individuals to spectacles and commodities. One of the film’s central arguments is that
individuals unknowingly become part of the consumer society they live in and avoid questioning the
reality they are presented with. When Truman begins to question this system-imposed reality, the film
offers the audience the opportunity to question their own current system. The capitalist order is not
merely an economic structure; it is a political-economic system that shapes individuals’ lives through
historical, political, and ideological dimensions, framing individuals with invisible boundaries. Within this
order, the neoliberal subject increasingly appears not as a passive consumer but as an ‘entrepreneur of
himself’ (Foucault, 2008, p. 226), one who manages his own visibility, reputation, and self-presentation
as a form of capital. The artificial world in The Truman Show reveals how individuals are manipulated by
media, advertising, and the consumption habits that permeate society. While the town of Seahaven,
where Truman lives, represents the ideal world capitalism offers individuals, it also legitimizes a living
space that is orderly, controlled, and isolated from the outside world. Such spatial constructs, as Adorno
and Horkheimer’s (2014) critique of the culture industry points out, offer individuals a false illusion of
freedom. Similarly, the modern living spaces offered by the consumer society imprison individuals within
the illusion of freedom. Cities surrounded by skyscrapers, giant shopping malls, and luxurious living

simultaneously limiting their freedom. Like Truman’s city, modern metropolises restrict individuals’
freedom of movement and encourage consumption within the framework of rules imposed by the
system.

approach to individuals. In the modern world, individuals transform their personal lives, identities, and
experiences into spectacles through social media and other platforms, defining their existence through
the objects of consumption offered by the system. The recording of Truman’s every move, transforming
it into a global spectacle, parallels the increasing positioning of today’s individuals within visibility and
surveillance practices, transforming them into objects of consumption. This parallels Zuboff’s (2019)

desire for voluntary surveillance and visibility. As individuals in the digital age redefine the boundaries
