Abstract
Existing studies on loyalty to social media influencers have mainly focused on cognitive dissonance,
parasocial bonding, the halo effect, social identity, and just -world belief, each in isolation. This paper
advances an argument: that these five mechanisms occur not independently but in a sequence, each
reducing psychological resistance to the next and producing lo yalties more resistant than any single
mechanism alone. We call this the compounding loyalty model. Cognitive dissonance is the initial
motivation that causes a follower to realize something is wrong; parasocial bonding makes
rationalization a requirement, not an option; the halo effect provides a surface-level basis for maintaining
trust despite misbehavior; social identity makes loyalty costly to lose; and just -world belief provides a
motivated argument that deception did not happen at all. We draw this model from cases including James
Charles(2021)and Ramon Abbas, convicted of fraud in 2022. We discuss media literacy, ethical
marketing, and platform policy.

Abstract
Existing studies on loyalty to social media influencers have mainly focused on cognitive dissonance, parasocial
bonding, the halo effect, social identity, and just -world belief, each in isolation. This paper advances an
argument: that these five mechanisms occur not independently but in a sequence, each reducing psychological
resistance to the next and producing loyalties more resistant than any single mechanism alone. We call this the
compounding loyalty model. Cognitive dissonance is the initial motivation that causes a follower to realize
something is wrong; parasocial bonding makes rationalization a requirement,  not an option; the halo effect
provides a surface-level basis for maintaining trust despite misbehavior; social identity makes loyalty costly to
lose; and just -world belief provides a motivated argument that deception did not happen at all. We draw this
model from cases including James Charles(2021)and Ramon Abbas, convicted of fraud in 2022. We discuss
media literacy, ethical marketing, and platform policy.
Keywords: Influencers, Social Media, Cognitive Dissonance, Para -Social Relationships, Halo  Effect,
Social Identity Theory, Just World Hypothesis, Compounding Loyalty Model.

hese social media sites have created a whole new type of “public face,” the influencers who  have
become popular for their seeming authenticity, relatability, and success. The numbers  market
products and lifestyles, from skincare to monetary possibilities, and sometimes with  hidden business
incentives and inaccurate results. The author's case in point is that some years  ago after being convinced
that a certain fitness influencer's promotional material about smoothies  was correct, the author bought a
£50 smoothie packet that was found to be just another smoothie  made from a repackaged match powder,

stick with influencers even when they  discover t hat this person is being deceptive, sponsored, is making
false claims, or is outright  lying. This paper investigates the psychological processes underlying that
loyalty.
The existing literature provides a good amount of insight into why each of these p rocesses may
maintain influencer loyalty, but they are presented as standalone explanations. Studies of  parasocial
interaction (Sokolova & Kefi, 2020; Sokolova & Perez, 2021) test that mecha nism  largely on its own;

identity accounts of fan defense (Jenkins, 1992) are  rarely connected back to the dissonance -reduction
literature that explains why the initial  admiration was resistant to revision in the first place. What is missing
is an account of how these  mechanisms relate to one another, whether they are simply redundant alternative
explanations for  the same outcome, or whether they interact in a way that makes the resulting loyalty more
resistant to correction than any one mechanism could produce alone.

The Compounding Loyalty Model
This paper argues for the second possibility. We propose that cognitive dissonance, parasocial   bonding,
the halo effect, social identity, and just-world reasoning do not operate as parallel,  redundant explanations
but as a sequence, in which each mechanism lowers the resistance the  next mechanism subsequently
encounters. We term this the compoun ding loyalty model. In  short: Cognitive dissonance is the first
mechanism activated, usually in response to encountering  disconfirming evidence, and creates a motivated
need to rationalize, not revise. The  rationalization is not enough to make it seem necessary as opposed to
convenient, however,  because there is a relational cost to giving up on the admiration of someone felt as a
quasi-field,  which would not apply to giving up on the admiration of a stranger.
The halo effect then provides an "excuse" for the rationalization that is already going on;  attractiveness
or polish is interpreted as proof that the disconfirming evidence must be "unreal"  or "excessive. Social
identity is about turning something private and individual into something  public and social, when a follower
criticizes the influencer, he or she is not only confronting their  beliefs, he or she is confronting him or her
in a group. Lastly, any lingering doubts are swept away  by providing a general purpose explanation for
why the deception, if it exists, will be 'rewarded'  sooner or later, allowing the follower to avoid specific
action. This type of sequencing creates  predictions that simple listing of independent mechanisms doesn’t.
However, if the mechanisms  are truly sequential , as opposed to simply cooccurring, then an intervention
that only targets the  mechanism activated first in the sequence (e.g., a fact-check aiming at correcting the
dissonance  inducing claim) should have measurably weaker effects than an intervention th at targets both
a  mechanism earlier in the sequence, and another later in the sequence (e.g., a fact -check aimed at
correcting the dissonance inducing claim while also targeting a later stage mechanism like social  identity).
A prediction of this sort is used directly in section 10.1, and is discussed experimentally  in section 11. The
rest of the paper elaborates on each mechanism in turn, pointing to the cases  and evidence that have justified
its inclusion in the series, and then in Section 9 and 10 re visits  the compounding strategy, as opposed to
the “mechanism by mechanism” approach that has
dominated the literature so far.
The central research question for this analysis is thus double: first, what psychological  mechanisms
help followers stick with a deceptive influencer once they've come to know it's  deceptive and second, are
these mechanisms independent and redundant or are they part of a  sequential process through which earlier
mechanisms can be more effective? Together with  empirical stud ies by Chou, Gaysynsky and Cappella
(2020) and Sokolova and Perez (2021), and  by relying on documented cases such as that of James Charles
and the fraud case of Ramon  Abbas (Hushpuppi), we claim the second answer, and uncover its implications
for media literacy  and consumer behaviour.

Cognitive Dissonance
Cognitive dissonance is the feeling of discomfort that occurs when a person has two conflicting  thoughts,
such as liking a particular influenc er and knowing that the influencer may be lying  (Festinger, 1957).
People often develop rationalizations to alleviate this discomfort, instead of  changing their feelings of
admiration. After all, many of the followers of beauty blogger, James  Charles, who has been criticized by
the public for promoting scientifically unproven skin care  products in 2021, downplayed the controversy
and considered exaggeration a normal part of  business promotion. Aronson (1992) reports that this type of
rationalization is typical when the  figure being defended is a figure who has strong emotional investment
for the individual, so that  the follower is able to maintain his or her initial evaluation even though the
evidence is more  likely to be a different evaluation. This rationalization is the first in the chain of events
that make  up the compounding model developed here; by itself it is not definitive and is quickly
undermined by other mechanisms that are described in the following paragraphs.

The Halo Effect
The halo effect is a phenomenon in which people tend to assume that positive attributes such as
trustworthiness and competence are attributed to those who are attractive, despite a lack of  evidence for
such attributes (Thorndike, 1920). For example, Kim Kardashian's SKIMS brand  did not face the same
problems in terms of commercial performance that other similar lines did  in that it was seen as genuine
and consumers rated it highly for its performance. (Ohanian, 1990).  This bias is also a factor in the
comparatively small amount of damage done to the influencer  Tana Mongeau after the 2019 TanaCon
fiasco, to the extent that a smooth façade can mask  problems with handling or even lying to their audience.

Social Identity
Belonging to a particular group or class may be seen as an affiliation to an influencer, and  criticism of an
influencer may be interpreted as criticism of the follower. The pattern can be  explained with the social
identity theory (Tajfel & Turner, 1979) that was the result of an online  controversy in 2018 for YouTuber
Logan Paul, who was criticised for his actions, prompting  many of his followers to mobilize to protect his
identity, claiming that the situation had been  misunderstood. When it comes to defending the criticized
person, it appears that group loyalty  within fan groups is at the forefront of followers' minds, as  Jenkins
(1992) demonstrates.

The Just-World Hypothesis
The just-world hypothesis is that people tend to think that people get what they deserve in life,  that success
is due to merit, and that evil is ultimately retribution (Lerner, 1980). Before being  convicted of fraud in
2022, Ramon Abbas ("Hushpuppi") shared a life of great wealth on
Instagram, with many followers seemingly believing that this was his rightfully earned wealth.  To say
so would have meant admitting that the social world is not alway s reliable, that is, that  hard work is not
always rewarded. As Furnham ( 2003) suggests this bias is especially strong  when it comes to rich
celebrities and can obscure evidence of wrong-doing until it is too obvious to ignore.

Mechanisms of Deception
Whether knowingly or not, influencers engage in the biases outlined above. There are three  common trends
in the literature:
Exaggerated health claims. The false health based claims about vaccines, treatments, and other  relevant
topics are rampant on social media and have been found to spread more quickly and  farther than the truth;
they have been reported to lead to increased vaccine controversy and  promotion of untested treatments
(Chou, Gaysynsky, & Cappella, 2020). This is a big driver of  this trend, especially when it comes to
products like ‘detox' teas and unproven supplements,  which was something that escalated during the
COVID -19 pandemic.

Vulnerable Populations
Susceptibility to these mechanisms is not uniform across the population. A substantial and long running
research tradition, distinct from the process focused account developed in this paper,  addresses this
individual differences question directly: the absorption addiction model of  celebrity worship (McCutcheon,
Lange, & Houran, 2002) proposes that a compromised sense of personal identity leads some individuals to
become psychologically absorbed in a favored  celebrity's life as a substitute source of identity and
fulfilment, with this absorption taking on  addictive qualities that drive escalating attachment. Using the
resulting Celebrity Attitude Scale,  this line of work identifies a trait like disposition toward parasocial over
attachment:  McCutcheon, Ashe, Houran, and Maltby ( 2003) find that individuals scoring highest on the
most  intense, borderline pathological dimension of celebrity worship show measurable deficits in  cognitive
flexibility and associative learning relative to lower scoring individuals, while Maltby,  Houran, a nd
McCutcheon (2003) link this same dimension to poorer psychological wellbeing.  Where the compounding
loyalty model proposed in this paper describes a sequence of  mechanisms that can operate within any
follower confronted with disconfirming evidence, the  absorption addiction literature instead identifies a
stable subset of individuals for whom  parasocial attachment is elevated and more resistant to correction
from the outset, a trait level  vulnerability that would be expected  to accelerate entry into the sequence
described in Section  1.1 and to deepen engagement with its later stages, particularly parasocial bonding
and social  identity, without itself constituting an alternative explanation for the sequence. Individuals
exhibiting patterns consistent with compulsive social networking use, who derive reinforcement  from
engagement metrics such as likes and comments, similarly show reduced critical scrutiny of  online content
(Kuss & Griffiths, 2017). Younger users are of particular concern. Appearance  related social media use is
associated with body image concerns among young women, and this  relationship is mediated by the
tendency to compare one's own appearance with the images  encountered (Fardouly, Diedrichs, Vartanian,
& Halliwell, 2015). In meta-analyses of  experimental and longitudinal evidence, de Valle, Gallego-García,
Williamson, and Wade ( 2021)  confirm that exposure to appearance ideal soci al media imagery has a
detrimental effect on body  image, and that appearance ideal content is more damaging than other
appearance related  content. Younger audiences are therefore doubly exposed: to the idealized standards
influencers  project, and to the commercial claims attached to them.

Synthesis: The Compounding Loyalty Model
The preceding sections established that five mechanisms, cognitive dissonance, parasocial  bonding, the
halo effect, social identity, and just-world reasoning, each independently support  influencer loyalty. Prior
research has largely stopped at this point, establishing that a given  mechanism matters within a single study
designed around that mechanism alone (e.g., Sokolova  & Kefi, 2020, on parasocial interaction; Eisend et
al., 2020, on disclosure and the halo effect).  Treated this way, the five mechanisms function as a list of
alternative explanations, and the  natural next empirical question becomes which one exp lains the most
variance, a question  several of the studies cited above implicitly treat as central.
We believe that this perspective underestimates the reality of what is going on. The mechanisms  are
not alternative explanations of the same result, but  rather consecutive steps in one process,  and are not
additive in their explanatory power. Whereas cognitive dissonance is the starting  point of the sequence and

This account makes a prediction that is specific and falsifiable one that is different from a mere  list of
independent mechanisms: If the compounding model is correct, a particular intervention  that targets an
early stage mechanism, for example, a fact c heck aimed at cognitive dissonance,  should be less effective
the more a given case has already been processed through the sequence  of mechanisms, that is, the stronger
the parasocial bonding and social identity have already been  activated. In contrast, a purely additive model
makes no prediction of such interaction; the  contribution each mechanism can make to loyalty should be
independent and constant  throughout the sequence. The experimental design outlined in Section 11 is
designed to resolve  these two accounts head on.

This paper explores the phenomenon of following and how even the first author, as a follower, is  still

The Compounding Sequence in Practice
In Section 1.1 we proposed that the five mechanisms operate in succession, each reducing the  resistance
that the next encounters this was rearranged and formalised as a falsifiable claim in  Section 10.1, separate
from the list of independent biases. We have documented some of the  cases throughout this paper and these
are intended to be walked through in sequence and not as a  catalogue. The sequence begins with cognitive
dissonance (Festinger, 1957), when the followers  of Ja mes Charles were faced with the 2021 skincare
controversy, the discomfort was solved by  rationalization as the reappraisal of the situation could have
been seen as a loss of personal  identity, but this rationalization, by itself, was not strong enough and it was
just one well  documented case of deception. What prevented it from falling apart was the second stage,
parasocial attachment (Horton & Wohl, 1956). As fitness influencers are not actual people, but  rather props
and props that perform a certain role, it becomes easier for them to substitute actual  personal closeness
with a pattern that can be documented by Sokolova and Perez (2021), where  parasocial closeness towards
fitness influencers facilitates continued engagement regardless of  the accuracy of the fitness influencer's
claims, and the pattern is also more emotionally defended  because followers are willing to forgive the
fitness influencer for replacing actual personal  closeness with patterned. The halo effect (Thorndike 1920)
then provides that defended  rationalization with content and attractiveness is interpreted as evidence that
the disconfirming  claim must have been an aberration, as Kim Kardashian 's ongoing commercial success
despite  controversy (Ohanian 1990) demonstrates. Social identity processes (Tajfel and Turner, 1979)  shift
this rationalization from the private to the public sphere, and embeds followers within  influencers like
Logan Paul, making it harder for them to turn against the influencer than against  their "fans," driving up
the social cost of doing so beyond the follower's private cost benefit  analysis. Finally, the just world
hypothesis (Lerner, 1980) completes the round: the need to  challenge the specifics of the evidence of
Hushpuppi's fraud is not required of followers, just that  to assume, in a general and low effort way, that his
visible wealth must have been earned, and  that anything wrong with it must be discovered and sanctioned
by the systems in place would be  sufficient. These five cases do not represent five different reasons for
loyalty, but instead are five  stages in a process which ma kes the next mechanism that has to work a little
more difficult. The continual and interactive nature of social media further increases this structure: a
traditional  celebrity endorsement would only involve a few direct interactions, whereas influencer
marketing is characterised by ongoing parasocial bonding, which is supposed to increase the  flow of
emotions at the exact moment, which is supposed to be challenging.

Theoretical and Empirical Alignment
These findings extend classical celebrity endorsement research to the distinctive dynamics of  social media,
and clarify why that literature's mechanism by mechanism structure has not  previously produced a unified
account. Erdogan ( 1999) established that celebrity endor sement  leverages perceived credibility and
attractiveness; influencer marketing intensifies this effect  through direct, ongoing personal engagement.
Sokolova and Kefi (2020) found that perceived  authenticity and parasocia l interaction are key drivers of
purchase intention among Instagram  and YouTube followers, even when underlying claims are
unsubstantiated, a finding our model  reframes as evidence for the second stage of the sequence rather than
a standalone effect, as in  the 2019 TanaCon controversy, where the halo effect (stage three) plausibly built
directly on preexisting parasocial trust rather than operating independently of it. Chou, Gaysynsky, and
Cappella ( 2020) further argue that healt h misinformation circulating on social media exploits  both
cognitive dissonance and just-world reasoning to sustain audience engagement, a pairing of  an early stage
and a late stage mechanism that the compounding model predicts should cooccur,  since the  two are not
substitutes for each other but successive links in the same chain. Moran,  Swan, and Agajanian ( 2024)
demonstrate that wellness influencers' parasocial closeness with  followers facilitated the spread of anti-

Implications for Media Literacy, Marketing Ethics, and Policy
These findings carry several practical implications. First, media literacy interventions should  move beyond
generic critical thinking instruction to address the specific psychological  mechanisms identified here.
Given evidence that the appearance of ideal social media content has a  measurable detrimental effect on
body image, particularly among young women (de Valle et al.,  2021), targeted programming, for example,
exercises that help users distinguish parasocial  closeness from genuine relationships, may be more effective
than general literacy instruction  alone. Structured exercises simulating the identification of undisclosed
sponsorships could help  counter cognitive dissonance driven rationalization by normalizing fact-checking
as a routine  practice, and should also aim to strengthen users' confidence in their own independent
judgment.
Second, these dynamics have ramifications for marketing ethics. The use of parasocial trust and  related
biases could provide a quick boost to the bottom line but would come with long -term  reputational and
regulatory risks. While the guidelines for endorsing sponsored content by the  U.S. Federal Trade
Commission (FTC) (2023) clearly state that disclosure is required, there is  still poor adherence to this rule;
brands and platforms should better enforce disclosure  requirements. Third, these findings bear on platform
governance. Policymakers may want to make  a more consistent effort to identify unverified claims, similar
to the “community notes” style  features already available on platforms. Eisend, van Reijmersdal, Boerman
and Tarrahi (2020)  conducted a meta-analysis of sponsorship disclosure studies and reported that disclosure
is a  highly effective way of increasing the recognition of persuasive intent, but the impact on trust  and
subsequent purchase behaviour is less consistent and dep ends upon the timing, modality and  source of
disclosure, meaning that disclosure related policy measures can be effective, but are  not necessarily
enough. On a more social level, unrealistic body standards set by influencers (de  Valle et al., 2021), due to
their parasocial trust, are linked to mental health issues, specifically for  young women; health
misinformation shared by influencers (Moran, Swan, & Agajanian, 2024)  has implications for public health
outcomes more broadly. Cultural change, alongside regulatory,  will likely be necessary to solve these
ramifications, with more critical engagement with  influencer content becoming the norm.

Limitations
This paper has several limitations. It relies on secondary sources and existing empirical literature  rather
than original data collection; while this approach allows the argument to be grounded in  established
findings, it does not provide the granularity  that primary research, for example, a  survey of platform
specific loyalty patterns, would offer. The reflexive account included to  illustrate the analysis reflects a
single individual's experience and cannot be assumed to  generalise; many social media users have not
experienced comparable episodes. The evidence  base is also predominantly drawn from Western contexts
(e.g., James Charles, Hushpuppi); Han  and Balabanis's ( 2024) meta-analysis of social media influencer
research notes that cultural and  platform -specific moderators of influencer effectiveness remain under
examined, so the extent to  which the loyalty dynamics described here generalize to markets such as China
or South Korea is  currently an open question rather than a n established finding. Finally, gender specific
patterns  beyond body image remain under explored; for example, it is unclear whether men are
differentially susceptible to just -world reasoning in the context of financial or cryptocurrency  related
influencers relative to women. These limitations constrain the generalizability of the  present analysis and
point to clear directions for future research.

Future Research
The present analysis is synthetic rather than empirical, and several lines of primar y research  would
substantially strengthen, qualify, or falsify its conclusions. The most direct priority is a  test of the
compounding loyalty model itself; we outline that design first, followed by four  further strands.
Testing the compounding model directly. Section 9 derives a specific prediction: an intervention
targeting an early stage mechanism (cognitive dissonance) should lose effectiveness as later  stage
mechanisms (parasocial bonding, social identity) become more strongly engaged, whereas  a purely additive
account predicts no such interaction. This could be tested with a between  subjects design that first measures
or manipulates participants' existing parasocial attachment and  identity fusion with a given influencer (low
vs. high, using e stablished scales), then exposes all  participants to an identical dissonance inducing
correction (a documented instance of the  influencer's deception), and measures post correction belief
revision as the outcome. The  compounding model predicts that ther e will be a significant interaction,
correction effectiveness  should be significantly less in the high attachment condition, and a strictly additive
model  predicts there will be a main effect for the correction but no interaction. A secondary design  could
test the sequence order itself, by experimentally comparing the effects of disrupting the  latter stage of the
mechanism (e.g., a correction and an in-group identity appeal) to disrupting an  early stage mechanism (e.g.,
a correction) to determine wheth er disrupting a later stage of the  mechanism produces larger gains than
repeating the early stage of the mechanism, as the model  predicts.
Experimental designs for disclosure and warning interventions. Here are the mechanisms  identified,
which genera te further directly testable hypotheses beyond the compounding  prediction above. The
presence of the cognitive dissonance effect should lead to rationalization  of influencer misbehaviour, and
explicit warning labels or regulatory status disclaimers on  sponsored content should measurably lessen that
rationalization, which could be controlled in a  between subjects design with a warning label versus an
unlabelled label. In a pair of pre-registered experiments, Bachmann and Valenzuela ( 2023) demonstrated
that, while fact -checks  significantly reduced misperceptions, they also significantly decreased people's
trust in the news  media and their perceptions of media bias, which has not yet been explored in influencer
contexts. This makes this an interesting testable question about influencer marketing: Do  corrections to
influencer claims come at the cost of a more generalized loss of trust, or does  repeated exposure to them
lead to habituation? Similar designs might include controlling for the  content of the message while varying
its attractiveness or apparent production quality in order to  judge the respective role of the perceived
attractiveness of the message and the parasocial  closeness.
Longitudinal and scandal response designs. The present account cannot comment on change of  loyalty
over time, or in the face of repeated evidence of wrongdoing, as the present account is  based on cross
sectional case examples. Panel studies examining changes in individual followers'  para social attachment
and identity fusion over time before and after a scandal would enable  researchers examine whether
attachment to the celebrity wanes over time, drops precipitously  and then rises back up or remains relatively
unchanged except for a small er portion of the  audience. The design that would be very informative and
informative would be if you followed  the twists and turns of a second, independent scandal for an
influencer, so that you could directly  test the hypothesis that pre rationalization will reduce or increase the
bar set for eventual  “disengagement”.
Cross cultural replication. The sources of the empirical literature summarized here are largely  from
Western and mainly Anglophone locations. Han and Balabanis's ( 2024) meta -analysis of  influencer
research reveals source credibility and parasocial interaction as the two most  prominent explanatory
frameworks in the domain, respectively, along with a range of other  moderators that are significantly under
researched in the field of influencer research, such as  cultural and platform specific factors. A social
identity driven notion of defence and a lack of  responsiveness to individualised fact-checking interventions
is likely to be greater among  followers of influencers from more collectivist cultural contexts (e.g., China,
South Korea, or  Southeast Asian countries) than among followers from more individualist cultural contexts
(e.g.,  the United States or the United Kingdom), which leads to the following compa rative hypothesis:
followers from more collectivist cultural contexts are more likely to exhibit social identity driven  defence

Conclusion
The aim of this paper was to investigate the question of why followers persist in defending  influencers who
have been revealed to be deceitful, and not stop after the evidence has been  brought to light. The
compounding loyalty model is the answer that emerges here: None of the  other models dis cussed would
explain all of the behaviours observed in the study alone, and the  five models analysed are not all alternative
explanations for each other but are rather a series of  stages, with each one reducing the resistance faced by
the next one.
Cognitive dissonance provides the motivation to rationalize; parasocial bonding provides the  emotional
motivation, and the need to rationalize, to continue to trust; the halo effect provides a  superficial basis to
continue to trust; social identity provides a social motivation to accept that  personal loyalty leads to group
loyalty, because the cost of breaking loyalty is higher than it is to  the group; and the just-world hypothesis
provides a motivation to doubt that the deception ever  took place. It is not any one of these biases, but
rather this sequence, each stage constraining the  next mechanism required to meet the need, that yields the
durability seen in cases like James  Charles and Hushpuppi, not any list of contributing biases.
This has a practical corollary, and one of the compounding framings makes sharper than a simple  list
would. Interventions that affect a single mechanism, a single fact-check or a single disclosure  label will be
only partially effective not just because other mechanisms can be left to take up the  belief by the time the
intervention comes, but because these other mechanisms will have  absorbed and re-anchored the belief by
then, which is just the interaction effect that was  predicted in Section 9 and specified for testing in Section
11. The implication for practice is that  effective countermeasures will require multiple and sequential
strategies strategies that include  disclosure, as well as strategies for fact checking and strategies for media
literacy that explicitly  identify Para social attachment and in-group loyalty as psychological phenomena,
not simply act  as generic critical thinking skills that will "transfer" to content from influencers.
More bodily, the persistence of this loyalty is not best understood as a failure of individual  judgement.
The mechanisms reviewed here dissonance reduction, attachment, identity protection,  and belief in a just
world are commonplace mechanisms of cognition with adaptive functions in  other domains of social
interaction, and they are merely provided with a particularly high  frequency and a particularly personalized
array of opportunities in social media environments  that unfold in opposition to the interests of their
