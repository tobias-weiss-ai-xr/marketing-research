ABSTRACT
The stagnation of waqf and zakat collection among Indonesia’s digitally native Generation Z has prompted
Islamic philanthropic institutions to intensify social media marketing . H -owever, the mechanism through
which such campaigns translate into donation in tention remains underexplored. This qualitative study
investigates the mediating role of digital sharia financial literacy in linking social media marketing exposure
to Generation Z’s interest in waqf and zakat. Employing a phenomenological approach, semi -structured
interviews were conducted with 28 Generation Z individuals (aged 17–27) in Greater Jakarta, who actively
engaged with Islamic digital content. Thematic analysis, guided by Hayes mediation logic adapted for
qualitative inquiry, revealed four overarching themes: (1) pervasive but passive social media marketing reach,
(2) fragmented digital sharia financial literacy, (3) evolving philanthropic intention from awareness to action,
and (4) literacy as a cognitive gateway that filters, interprets, and validates marketing messages. The findings
demonstrate that digital Sharia financial literacy fully mediates the relationship when marketing content is
perceived as complex or untrustworthy, yet plays a partial mediating role for simple, affect-driven campaigns.
This study contributes a mediating literacy framework that integrates the Theory of Planned Behavior and
media literacy models, offering actionable insights for digital da’wah strategists and Islamic fintech
developers to design educational content embedded within promotional narratives.

INTRODUCTION
Islamic social finance, epitomized by zakat (obligatory alms) and waqf  (religious endowment), is a
cornerstone of redistributive justice and sustainable development in Muslim -majority societies. Indonesia,
home to the world’s largest Muslim population, holds immense potential for zakat and Waqf mobilization. The
National Amil Zakat Agency (BAZNAS) estimated the national zakat potential at IDR 327.6 trillion in 2023,
yet the actual collection barely surpassed IDR 31 trillion (BAZNAS, 2023). Similarly, the Indonesian Waqf
Board (BWI) projected cash waqf potential at IDR 180 trillion per annum, with a realized accumulation below
5% of that figure. This glaring gap signals not only operational inefficiency but also a profound disconnect
between the philanthropic supply capacity of Muslims and their actual donation behavior, particularly among
the digitally immersed Generation Z cohort (Hoirullah & Adiba, 2026).
Generation Z, defined as those born between 1997 and 2012, constitutes approximately 27.94% of
Indonesia’s population (BPS, 2020). They are characterized by digital nativity, high social media consumption
averaging 3.5 hours daily on platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube (We Are Social, 2024) and
a value-driven orientation toward social causes. Paradoxically, despite their technological fluency and stated
altruism, Generation Z’s participation in formal zakat and waqf channels is marginal. A 2023 survey by the
Indonesian Philanthropy Association revealed that only 18% of Gen Z respondents had ever performed zakat
through official institutions, and less than 7% had engaged in cash waqf, with th e majority citing a lack of
understanding and trust as the primary barriers. This underutilization represents a critical missed opportunity,
not only for resource mobilization but also for cultivating a lifelong habit of structured Islamic giving (Citra
& Fitriah, 2026).
In response, zakat management o rganizations (OPZ) and waqf institutions have aggressively adopted
social media marketing to penetrate the Gen Z segment. Campaigns featuring micro -video storytelling,

One promising explanatory construct is digital Sharia financial literacy. Financial literacy, traditionally
defined as the ability to understand and effectively use financial skills, has been extended to the digital realm
with the inclusion of competencies for navigating digital payment systems, evaluating online financial
information, and safeguarding personal data (OECD, 2018). In the context of Islamic finance, Sharia financial
literacy further encompasses knowledge of fiqh muamalah, the principles of zakat calculation (nisab and haul),
types of waqf (cash, land, productive), and the ability to assess the Sharia compliance and credibility of digital
collection platforms. Therefore, digital sharia financial literacy represents a multidimensional capability set
that empowers individuals to make informed, confident, and religiously grounded philanthropic decisions in
an online environment (Rijadi et al., 2025).
The nexus among social media marketing, digital Sharia financial literacy, and donation intention can be
conceptually framed through dual-process persuasion theories, such as the Elaboration Likelihood Model and
the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB; Ajzen , 1991). Social media marketing often operates via peripheral
cues—attractive visuals, celebrity endorsers, and emotional appeals—that generate initial interest but may be
insufficient to overcome barriers rooted in skepticism or ignorance. Digital sharia financial literacy,
functioning as a central-route cognitive resource, enables deeper elaboration: a literate individual can scrutinize
the authenticity of a waqf campaign, verify the legal standing of a nazhir (waqf manager), and understand how
digital payment gateways comply with sharia contracts. Consequently, literacy may mediate the path from
marketing exposure to behavioral intention by transforming superficial awareness into a trust-based,
knowledge-driven conviction. Despite its theoretical plausibi lity, empirical investigations of this mediating
mechanism, particularly in Islamic social finance and among Indonesian Generation Z, remain absent from the
literature. Previous studies have examined the direct effect of social media on zakat intention  or the influence
of Islamic financial literacy on saving or investment behaviour; however, no study has integrated these
variables into a mediating model within the waqf–zakat domain (Syairi et al., 2025).
Moreover, the epistemological lens adopted in extant research is predominantly quantitative, relying on
variance-based structural equation modeling that captures statistical relationships but elides the lived
experience and meaning -making processes of donors. Generation Z’s philanthropic journey is deeply
contextual and shaped by social media algorithms, peer discourse, fragmented religious learning, and digital
platform affordances. Thus, a qualitative inquiry is imperative to uncover how and why digital Sharia financial
literacy intervenes, the conditions under which it strengthens or weakens, and the nuanced ways in which Gen
Z individuals negotiate marketing messages. This study addresses this gap by posing the following research
questions:
 How do Generation Z individuals in Indonesia experience and interpret social media marketing messages
that promote waqf and zakat?
 What dimensions of digital Sharia financial literacy do they draw upon when evaluating such messages?
 In what ways does digital Sharia financial literacy mediate the relationship between social  media
marketing exposure and the intention to participate in waqf and zakat?
The significance of this study is threefold. Academically, it extends the TPB and media literacy
frameworks to Islamic philanthropic behavior by introducing a qualitative mediation lens, thereby enriching
theoretical discourse on digital Islamic finance. Managerially, the findings offer actionable insights for OPZ,
BWI, and Islamic fintech firms to design integrated marketing -education strategies that embed financial
literacy content within social media campaigns, fostering both immediate donation intention and long -term
empowerment. Socially, enhancing Gen Z’s digital Sharia financial literacy may catalyze a cultural shift
toward sustained, formalized giving, ultimately bridging I ndonesia’s zakat –waqf gap and advancing the
Sustainable Development Goals. The remainder of this article is structured as follows: Section 2 reviews the
literature underpinning social media marketing, digital Sharia financial literacy, and philanthropic intention;
Section 3 delineates the phenomenological methodology; Section 4 presents thematic findings supported by
tables and figures; Section 5 discusses theoretical and practical implications; and Section 6 concludes with
limitations and future research directions.

LITERATURE REVIEW
Social Media Marketing for Islamic Philanthropy
Social media marketing refers to the use of social networking platforms to communicate brand value,
engage consumers, and stimulate desired actions (Tuten  & Solomon, 2017). Its distinctive affordances
interactivity, user-generated content, virality, and algorithmic personalization render it especially potent for
cause-related marketing. In the philanthropic domain, social media reduces information asymmetry, humanizes
beneficiaries through storytelling, and fosters a sense of community around shared values (Ahmad Rizki
Suwardi et al., 2025) . For Islamic philanthropy, platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, and WhatsApp have
become the primary channels through which zakat and waqf institutions disseminate campaign messages, from
emotional videos of mustahik (recipients) to infographics detailing  nisab calculations and interactive live
sessions with ustadz (religious teachers).
The extant literature documents a positive relationship between social media marketing activities and
donation intentions. Social media serves as a hybrid element of the pr omotion mix, enabling consumers to
become brand advocates. Social media marketing significantly enhances brand equity and purchase intention
in luxury fashion, a framework later adapted to nonprofit contexts. Within Islamic giving, social media
marketing, particularly through influencers and emotional content, positively affected zakat payment intention
among Indonesian millennials. Message credibility, perceived usefulness of information, and interactivity on
Instagram predicted waqf participation intention (Kaya & Aydeniz, 2025).
Nevertheless, the direct-effect paradigm has been critiqued for overlooking cognitive processing of media
content. The Elaboration Likelihood Model posits that persuasion occurs via central (high -involvement,
argument-based) and peripheral (low-involvement, cue-based) routes, and attitude change through the central
route tends to be more enduring and predictive of behavior. Social media marketing often relies on peripheral
cues—short video formats , emotional music, endorsements that may generate transient interest but not
sustained behavioral commitment, especially for religious obligations perceived as complex. For Generation
Z, who are adept at scrolling past content and possess a high degree of advertising skepticism, mere exposure
is insufficient. Thus, a mediating cognitive cons truct is required to explain the translation of marketing
touchpoints into donation intention, which digital sharia financial literacy may fulfil (Hidayat, 2025).

Digital Sharia Financial Literacy
Financial literacy has evolved from basic numeracy and money management to a multidimensional
construct encompassing knowledge, attitudes, and behavior (OECD, 2020). Digital financial literacy adds
competencies in the safe use of digital financial products and services, understanding digital risks, and
evaluating online financial information (Prasad et al., 2018). In Islamic finance, Shariah financial literacy
integrates an understanding of maqasid al-shariah, riba-free transactions, zakat obligations, waqf contracts,
and the ethical framework governing wealth distribution (Cahyati et al., 2025). Digital sharia financial literacy,
therefore, merges these domains: it equips a Muslim with the ability to, for example, verify the halal status of
a digital payment gateway, calculate zakat on cryptocurrency holdings using an app, or assess the legitimacy
of a cash waqf platform’s nazhir using its digital footprint.
Empirical studies in conventional finance have consistently demonstrated that financial literacy predicts
positive financial behaviors such as saving, investment diversification, and retirement planning. In the Islamic
context, Islamic financial literacy significantly influenced the adoption of Islamic banking products among
Malaysian youth. For social finance, zakat literacy, defined as knowledge of zakat principles and calculations,
was a strong determinant of compliance behavior in Malaysia. However, these studies treated literacy as an
antecedent or moderator, not as a mediator between marketing stimuli and behavioral intention s. Moreover,
they employed quantitative scales that may not capture the dynamic, situated, and digitally mediated nature of
literacy among Gen Z, who often acquire fragmented religious-financial knowledge from short TikTok videos,
Instagram carousels, and peer -shared links rather than through formal education. This gap necessitates a
qualitative exploration of the form, source, and application of digital Sharia financial literacy in the
philanthropic decision-making process (Afifah Nur Rohmah et al., 2026)s.

Mediating Role of Literacy in Persuasion
The concept of mediation, wherein a third variable transmits the effect of an independent variable on a
dependent variable, has been extensively theorized in psychology and consumer behaviour research. In media
persuasion, the Cognitive Mediation Model posits that the effect of media exposure on knowledge and behavior
is mediated by cognitive processes such as attention, elaboration, and reflection (Eveland, 2001). Similarly,
the Media Literacy Model proposes that individuals with higher media literacy are better able to access,

Conceptual Framework
Synthesizing the above, this study proposes a qualitative mediation framework: social media
marketing exposure (independent variable) shapes Generation Z’s intention to participate in waqf and zakat
(dependent variable), with digital sharia financial literacy acting as a mediator that translates, filters, and
enriches the marketing message. This framework is not intended for statistical hypothe sis testing but as a
sensitizing device to guide data collection and thematic analyses.

RESEARCH METHOD
A qualitative research design anchored in the phenomenological tradition  was adopted to explore
the lived experiences and subjective meaning-making of Generation Z individuals regarding waqf and zakat
social media marketing, their digital sharia financial literacy, and their philanthropic intention formation.
Phenomenology is ideally suited to uncover the essence of a phenomenon as it is consciously experienced
by participants without imposing a priori theoretical structures (Creswell, J. W., & Poth, 2018). Given the
nascent state of the theory linking these constructs, an inductive, discovery -oriented approach was
prioritized.
Participants were recruited using purposive maximum variation sampling to ensure diversity in age,
gender, educational background, occupation, and social media platform preference. The inclusion criteria
were as follows: (1) Indonesian citizen aged 17–27 years (Gen Z cohort); (2) active user of at least two social
media platforms; (3) had been exposed to social media content related to waqf or zakat within the preceding
six months; and (4) had made or considered making a  digital donation. Recruitment occurred through
Instagram and WhatsApp community groups focused on Islamic economics and youth philanthropy,
supplemented by snowball referrals. Thirty-two individuals were screened, and 28 were selected, reaching
theoretical saturation after 24 interviews. Four additional interviews confirmed saturation. Table 1 presents
the participants’ profiles.
Data were gathered through in -depth, semi-structured interviews conducted between January and
March 2024, each lasting 45–80 min utes. An interview guide was developed based on the conceptual
framework, covering (a) general social media habits and exposure to waqf/zakat content; (b) perceptions of
specific campaigns (credibility, emotional appeal, call -to-action); (c) knowledge and sources of sharia
financial information (fiqh of zakat, waqf contracts, digital payment sharia compliance); (d) decision-making
process when encountering a donation appeal; and (e) experiences of actual or intended donation. Interviews
were conducted in Bahasa Indonesia, audio-recorded with consent, and transcribed verbatim. To complement
the interviews, participants were asked to share screenshots of memorable waqf/zakat campaigns, which
served as stimuli for deeper probing. Field notes were maintained to capture non-verbal cues and contextual
observations.
The a nalysis followed Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six -phase thematic analysis: familiarization,
generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing
the report. Two coders independently coded the first six transcripts to develop a codebook, achieving an
inter-coder reliability of Cohen’s kappa = 0.87. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion s. The
remaining transcripts were coded by the primary re searcher using NVivo 14  software. The codes were
iteratively grouped into sub -themes and overarching themes, with constant comparison across cases.
Mediation was analyzed by tracing participants’ narratives of how literacy elements intervened between
marketing exposure and intention, identifying patterns of full mediation (literacy essential), partial mediation
(literacy amplifies but not necessary), and no mediation (direct affect -driven response). Member checking
was conducted with five participants to validate the interpretations.

Discussion
 The ELM postulates that persuasion can occur via central or peripheral routes. Our data demonstrate
that social media marketing for waqf and zakat predominantly operates via peripheral cues emotional videos,
celebrity endorsements, and catchy soundbites. However, the conversion of peripheral persuasion into durable
behavioral intention was heavily contingent upon central-route processing enabled by digital Sharia financial
literacy. Participants such as Dina and Fajar explicitly described a two -phase jour ney: initial attraction
(peripheral), followed by “checking,” “verifying,” and “learning” (central). This suggests that for high -
involvement religious financial decisions such as zakat and cash waqf, peripheral cues alone are insufficient
to overcome cognitive and fiduciary barriers. Thus, digital Sharia financial literacy acts as the missing central-
route capability that completes the persuasion process. This finding enriches the ELM by specifying literacy
as a person-factor that determines whether an individual will engage in central processing when encountering
peripheral appeals, a mechanism previously underexplored in the Islamic marketing literature (Alhassan et al.,
2024).

The thematic analysis revealed that digital Sharia financial literacy is not a monolithic construct but a
composite of foundational fiqh knowledge, digital platform evaluation skills, institutional trust literacy, and
social learning. The mediation operates in four distinct modes: (1) full mediation, where marketing exposure
without literacy yields no intention, evidenced by Dina’s account that verification was the decisive factor; (2)
partial mediation, where a strong affective appeal creates a baseline intention that literacy amplifies, as seen
in Fajar’s appreciation of productive waqf; (3) barrier mediation, where the absence of literacy blocks intention
entirely, as Gilang’s fear of miscalculating zakat paralyzed his action; and (4) socially distributed mediation,
where literacy deficits are compensated by peer networks, as Hana described. These modes resonate with the
Cognitive Mediation Model (Eveland, 2001) which posits that attention and elaboration mediate media effects,
but add a communal dimension inherent to Islamic learning traditions (ta’lim circles adapted to WhatsApp
groups). This four -mode typology constitutes a novel theore tical contribution that can inform quantitative
instrument development (Awwaliah et al., 2025).
The existing literature often portrays Gen Z as either impulsive givers or digitally savvy but institutionally
distrustful. Our findings paint a more intricate picture: Gen Z participants actively seek to be responsible
givers, but their information environment fragmented, algorithmically curated, and rife with scams forces them
into a “verification economy” in which digital Sharia financial literacy becomes a currency. The stark gap
between 100% exposure to waqf/zakat content and the much lower conversion rate (only 12 of 28 participants
made a digital waqf donation) underscores that exposure is necessary but far from sufficient. The mediating
literacy filter explains  this. Witho ut institutional trust literacy knowing how to check BWI/BAZNAS
registrations—marketing content is treated as entertainment rather than a call to action. This finding supports
and extends the TPB: the effect of subjective norms (influencers, peers) and perceived behavioral control (ease
of digital payment) on intention is mediated by actual knowledge structures that define whether the behavior
is seen as feasible and safe. It also highlights a new dimension of perceived behavioral control not captured in
standard TPB scales: sharia-compliance control, the confidence that one’s digital transaction fulfills religious
obligations without innovation (bid’ah) (Hadiyati et al., 2023)s.
This study yields actionable strategies. First, social media marketing campaigns should embed “micro -
learning” moments in their content. Instead of separating educational content from promotional content, OPZ
can integrate bite-sized literacy elements directly into emotional videos: a TikTok Reel showing a beneficiary
can end with a 5-second overlay explaining how the platform’s wakalah contract works or how to verify the
nazhir via a QR code link ed to BWI’s registry. This blends peripheral attraction with central -route triggers,
partially automating mediation. Second, building institutional trust literacy should be a branding priori ty.
Several participants mentioned that they trusted campaigns bearing the BAZNAS or “BWI Verified” badge,
suggesting that a co-branded digital trust seal, prominently displayed, could shortcut the verification process.
Third, leveraging peer co-literacy through micro-influencer programs that train popular Gen Z figures not only
to promote but also to educate by demonstrating nisab calculation using the app live  can harness the social
mediation pathway. Fourth, fintech  developers should enhance UX with built -in literacy features  such as
interactive zakat calculators, explanatory animations on the akad (contract) used, and real-time confirmation
of fund allocation, thereby serving as both a transaction platform and a literacy tutor.
This study contributes a qualitative mediation model that integrates the ELM, TPB, and media literacy
theory within Islamic social finance. This demonstrates that digital Sharia financial literacy is a dynamic,
context-dependent mediator that can be reinforced by social structures. However, several limitations warrant
cautious interpretation of these results. The sample was limited to urban, educated Gen Z in Greater Jakarta,
potentially limiting its transferability to rural or less digitally connected youth. The cross-sectional interview
design captures retrospective sense-making but not real-time decision processes; future research could employ
digital ethnography or eye-tracking to observe literacy-in-action. Moreover, the qualitative nature precludes
statistical generalization, but the thick description provides a robust foundation for developing a survey
instrument to quantitatively test the four mediation modes across a larger, more diverse sample. Cross-cultural
comparisons with Malaysian or Middle Eastern Gen Z could reveal how differing regulatory environments
(e.g., more centralized Waqf authorities) shape the literacy -mediation relationship. Finally, longitudinal
research tracking individuals from initial exposure to repeated donation s could uncover how digital Sharia
financial literacy evolves from a mediator to a moderator as trust is routinized.
