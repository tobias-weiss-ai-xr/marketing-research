# China, Tourism and Technology

China, Tourism
and Technology
China, T ourism and T echnology charts China’s rise to becoming the world’s largest
travel economy and examines how home-grown mobile travel ecosystems, AI
models, Super Apps, mega-brands and government-backed tech strategies continue
to shape tourism worldwide.
Readers will discover how China’s closed-loop travel-tech networks built on
powerful app platforms for booking, payments, social commerce and livestreaming
create unique consumer privileges and formidable entry barriers for global
competitors. Through insider interviews, research and case studies, the book explains
how these innovations drive Chinese travel behaviours at home and abroad, and how
international businesses seeking growth should respond.
This book equips travel industry professionals, policymakers, academics and
international brands to anticipate trends and seize opportunities in a rapidly evolving
market, as well as offering tourism students practical insights into Chinese digital
ecosystems and consumer trends and global market dynamics.
Gary Bowerman is Director of Re:Set Strategies, Founding Partner of High-Yield
T ourism and Asia T ravel Markets Analyst for Phocuswright. Gary is an Asia-based
travel markets, policy and consumer trends analyst, tourism strategy advisor, author,
conference speaker and media commentator. He works with travel organisations,
hotels, airlines, banks, brands, consulting firms and market research agencies
worldwide. Gary lived and worked in Shanghai from 2004 to 2010.

Introduction
From OTAs and the WTO to AI+ and
Deployment Year One
China is a tourism superpower. From almost a standing start at the turn of the
21st century, the nation’s travellers, travel tech brands, airlines and hotel groups
now move global markets, make frequent headlines and shape how destinations and
travel organisations worldwide plan and invest for the future.
In 2000, China was barely a backstage presence in the theatre of world tourism.
Outbound international travel was only recognised by the state in 1997, and China’s
first online travel agency (OTA) website launched in 1999. Until 2003, Chinese cit-
izens were unable to travel independently to Hong Kong. Yet, by 2020, when China
retreated into almost three years of Zero-COVID isolation, countries across Asia
lamented the loss of their largest-spending source of visitors. The growth momen -
tum has returned. In 2025, China counted the world’s largest domestic travel market
(6.5 billion trips), was the largest source of outbound movements (167.9 million)
and had the second-largest air travel market (5.55 million flight take-offs and 770.15
million passengers) after the United States.
Hypersonic expansion of Chinese tourism at home and abroad is widely debated
at business and academic conferences, and analysed by governments and think tanks.
It is embedded into the budget planning of every segment of the travel industry, and
routinely reported across media channels. These discussions are gaining complexity
as more Chinese brands and AI-enabled tech companies leverage the commercial
value of aligning themselves with the nation’s tourists who use their apps, gadgets
and AI models while venturing to all corners of the globe. Chinese brands view

leisure tourism as a funnel to gain visibility for their products and services, and build
penetration and profitability in international markets.
This book was conceived in Autumn 2025, at a time when China’s cloud sec -
tor giants – such as Alibaba, T encent, Baidu, ByteDance, Pinduoduo and JD.com,
plus important layers of the mid-level tech stack, such as DeepSeek, Moonshot,
SenseTime, Zhipu and MiniMax – were testing AI models and interfaces to roll out
for consumers in 2026. In the travel industry, OTAs had introduced AI trip plan -
ning toolkits for customers over the previous couple of years, but it was becoming
clear that these first-mover systems would be challenged by a deep-rooted agentic
revolution fomenting across China’s consumer economy.
The chapters were written in 2026, the year of Apple’s 50th anniversary and
20 years after the launch of its seminal product, the iPhone. That release changed
how the world interacts with content, but in China, it created an entire economic
model shift. Apple’s relocation of its iPhone supply chain to China enabled it to ben-
efit from the rapid scaling of production processes, an abundance of labour, compo-
nents and technical diligence and unmatched entrepreneurial rule-bending. Apple
tapped China’s ability to meet high-volume deadlines. In turn, these same skills
enabled Chinese engineers to deconstruct the smartphone template and reengineer
and redesign it for local requirements. With the delivery surface established, start-up
tech companies in Shenzhen, Guangzhou, Shanghai and Hangzhou grew into
multi-billion-dollar enterprises whose products and services are delivered and con-
sumed daily via smartphones. The era of SuperApps evolved in the late 2010s, and
by the mid-2020s, Chinese OTAs reported that almost 100% of their interactions
with customers are mobile.
The arterial ecosystems that stimulate booking patterns and tourism trends are
filtering into regional and global markets. Meanwhile, the outside world is taking
greater interest in China’s cashless urban economy, “everything in one place” Super-
Apps, its AI-enabled phones and humanoid robots and the ability of short-video
apps, like TikT ok and e-commerce sites, like Shein and T emu, to disrupt global
markets. Chinese electric cars, high-speed trains and jet aircraft embody the govern-
ment’s ambitions to dominate international transport markets in the same way that
its electrovoltaic panels rule the solar power industry.
In 2026, the next “big reveal” began. Chinese electrotech firms released new
models and interfaces integrating generative, agentic and embodied AI to take the
marketing and selling of travel into the cloud. The government calls this AI+, a
framework to instil AI modelling across all consumption-generating areas of the
economy. Also in 2026, China marked the 25th anniversary of its accession to the
World T rade Organization on 11 December 2001. This opened negotiating win-
dows for its leading travel and tech companies to expand their horizons beyond their
homeland. In short, Chinese travel, tourism, e-commerce, cashless payments, OTAs,
Super Apps and, more recently, cloud computing, AI frontier models, underwater
data centres, voice-activated travel booking and the low-altitude economy grew up
together.

Although these travel technologies were engineered for the benefit of Chinese
consumers and travellers, building viable international strategies requires the kind
of collaborations and partnerships that Chinese firms tend to distrust. They must
engage meaningfully in the global markets where a similar distrust exists for Chinese
corporate and financing practices, and where governments are fearful of the price,
volume and quality competition they bring.
At the same time, China’s tourism focus is shifting from the twin pillars of
domestic and outbound tourism. In 2023, the government began rolling out a visa
waiver programme to stimulate a third pillar, inbound tourism. Chinese tech com -
panies are tasked with making Chinese-language apps and travel services accessible
for foreign visitors. Meanwhile, high-tech factories, research centres and the head -
quarters of cloud services giants are opening their doors to global business executives
and leisure tourists as well as Chinese students and families. A new travel segment
known as Industrial or Smart-T ech T ourism is thriving.
As we move closer to 2050 than 2000, this book is my attempt to bring together
the factors behind China’s rising global status in tourism and advanced tech develop-
ment. A theme running through the following pages is that Chinese tech solutions –
whether proprietarily developed by innovation-led private companies, reengineered
from external blueprints or the result of joint ventures – are now enmeshed in global
trade and tourism value chains. Chinese companies invented neither the smartphone
nor e-commerce. They did not originate AI, OTAs, QR-code payments, short-video
apps, high-speed trains, EVs or any of the technologies to be discussed in this book.
Where Chinese firms have proven masterful is by taking original ideas, customising
and redesigning them for Chinese consumer markets, owning the conceptualisation,
building and controlling the supply chains, stamping the branding and utilising
homemade digital networks to market, promote and sell them.
Overarching the whole debate is, of course, political intervention. As with any
sector of the Chinese economy, political leverage is overt and forms an important part
of the story. The growth of travel and tourism, and the ongoing integration with fron-
tier technologies, is overlaid by layers of preferential policies, protective guardrails and
diplomatic power-playing. While Chinese tourism and AI technologies are often dis-
cussed in terms of consumer behaviours and trend patterns, they are firmly embedded
into the state’s trade and diplomacy outlook. In that sense, the AI cloud travel revolu-
tion of 2026 was either a new milestone etched along a 21st-century continuum, or as
one prominent Chinese tech company, Agibot, described it: “Deployment Year One.”
