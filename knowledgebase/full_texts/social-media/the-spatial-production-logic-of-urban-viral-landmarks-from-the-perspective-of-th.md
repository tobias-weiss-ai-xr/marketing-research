Abstract. The rise of social media has fostered the attention economy, where online traffic
becomes a new resource for urban stock space renewal. Viral landmarks revitalize idle
urban spaces via low-cost micro-renovation and check-in marketing to stimulate local
consumption, forming a novel urban renewal mode. Yet they also trigger homogenization,
unbalanced public functions and poor sustainability. Few studies systematically interpret
how the attention economy shapes viral landmarks through Lefebvre's spatial production
theory. This paper adopts Lefebvre's tripartite spatial production framework to unpack the
traffic-driven spatial production mechanism of viral landmarks. The research finds online
traffic can activate underused land cheaply and boost neighborhood vitality. Nevertheless,
traffic-oriented development triggers multiple forms of spatial alienation, including
mismatched space functions, diluted local cultural context, shallow cultural consumption
experience, short-lived traffic bubbles and subsequent waste of idle urban resources. To
tackle these problems, this study proposes optimization strategies coordinating online traffic
value, public services and local culture, guiding viral spaces to shift from fleeting check-in
hotspots to sustainable distinctive urban areas. It expands interdisciplinary research linking
the attention economy and spatial production, and provides a theoretical framework for
urban stock space governance.
Keywords: spatial production logic, urban viral landmarks, attention economy
1. Introduction
Driven by the rapid rise of social media, the attention economy has become a core digital-age
economic model, with online public attention and digital visibility emerging as new resources
reshaping urban spatial production. Urban construction has entered a stock revitalization era amid
tightened new land development limits; activating idle urban spaces and tapping diversified land
value are now central urban priorities. Unlike traditional landmarks built on geography, industry and
history, numerous viral landmarks gain instant fame via short-video platforms. Relying on photo-
check-in traffic, they revive vacant sites and lift surrounding consumption and land value, creating
an innovative urban space value upgrading mode. This traffic-dominated spatial production subverts
conventional urban land development logics, yet brings homogenization, dysfunctional layout and
short-lived popularity. Against such context, this paper addresses three core questions: how the
attention economy reconstructs viral landmarks' spatial production logic, what practical plagues

Current academic research focuses on spatial production, the attention economy, and influencer
spaces. In the field of spatial production, space is not viewed as a static material entity, but rather as
a product of social interaction. The construction of a tripartite analytical framework containing
spatial practices, representations of space, and representational spaces provides a theoretical
paradigm for the study of urban spatial evolution. Urban spatial renewal is the result of the
combined effects of economic and social factors [1]. In the field of attention economy research,
existing studies have shown that social media can reshape the value of physical spaces, and that
traffic and attention can be directly converted into commercial value and spatial premiums [2].
Research on viral landmarks indicates that these spaces can effectively revitalize existing
underutilized urban spaces and contribute to urban micro-renewal [3]. At the same time, excessive
focus on online traffic can lead to issues such as the homogenization of urban spaces and the loss of
cultural significance [4]. A review of existing research reveals that the spatial production and
evolution of cultural and creative tourism districts have been extensively discussed, whereas
academic studies on viral spaces have largely focused on their communication characteristics,
landscape forms, or urban renewal effects [5].
To fill the abovementioned research gaps, this paper takes urban viral landmarks as the research
object. Relying on the attention economy theory and Lefebvre's tripartite spatial production
framework, this paper systematically sorts out the spatial production logic of viral landmarks from
three dimensions, namely physical space construction, spatial narrative construction, and public
experiential perception. The paper then summarizes the current challenges in their development and
proposes targeted optimization strategies. In theory, this paper combines the theory of the attention
economy with the theory of spatial production, thereby enriching the research framework on urban
spatial renewal and the utilization of existing land in the digital age and addressing gaps in current
interdisciplinary research. In practice, the findings of this study can provide feasible insights for
cities to regulate the development of internet-famous spaces, revitalize existing spatial resources,
balance the benefits of visitor traffic with spatial quality, and achieve sustainable urban spatial
development.
2. Analytical framework and theoretical foundation
2.1. Related concepts and fundamental theories
The attention economy is a new economic system that has appeared in the digital age. To generate
economic and social benefits by raising public interest and online traffic. It is a new and important
resource that is currently promoting the development of urban areas. Social media landmarks are a
new type of urban public space that has become popular through social media. They are often
created by using unused or remote areas, and old and inefficient spaces in the city. By providing
unique visual landscapes that meet the public's demand for photo opportunities and social media
sharing, these spaces have attracted considerable attention due to their high traffic, rapid iteration,
and low-cost update model. Thus, they have become popular vehicles for urban space revitalization.
From the perspective of land economics, viral landmarks are a new model of urban renewal that
revitalizes existing land at a low cost and exploits the various values of space.
This paper uses Lefebvre's tripartite framework of spatial production for reference, and the
framework contains three dimensions with mutual effects [6]. The first is spatial practices, that is to
say, the visible behaviour of creating and changing physical space in line with tangible construction

2.2. Analytical framework for spatial production
Traditional urban spatial plans focus on practical functions, cultural heritage, the daily life of
residents and prioritize the long-term development of the city, also the needs of its people. However,
with the emergence of the attention economy, this logic has been completely altered. Online
popularity and communication effects are now also considered in spatial development and planning.
With the spread of social media, many public places have been promoted and added to people's lists
for visiting, thus changing the development of urban spaces, marketing positioning and public usage
patterns.
Based on the above logic, this paper constructs an analytical framework driven by attention
traffic, which influences three aspects of spatial production. First, traffic reshapes the model of
physical space construction, adapting spaces to meet communication needs. Second, traffic
reconstructs the narrative logic of space, creating internet-famous symbolic labels. Third, traffic
redefines the public's spatial experience, forming new patterns of spatial use. At the same time, the
paper examines both the advantages and the issues of alienation arising from the empowerment of
traffic, and ultimately proposes an optimized path toward sustainable development.
3. Triadic analysis of the production of viral landmark spaces
Under the attention economy, physical spatial practices of viral landmarks abandon the traditional
functional-first principle for urban land redevelopment, and social media-friendly photo effects
become core evaluation standards for stock space renewal. Traditional urban stock renewal centres
on the daily needs of local residents, prioritizes infrastructure and public service upgrades to meet
neighbourhood recreation, life requirements, and serves citizens' long-term interests through land
use. On the other hand, viral space micro-renewal fully meets the logic of online content
dissemination by creating eye-catching shapes and colour palettes for check-in spots, but weakens
daily practical functions. A lightweight, low-cost renovation can be applied to the unused urban
fringe area to reduce the cost of land development and improving land-use efficiency [7]. However,
traffic-oriented physical reconstruction still has obvious long-term defects. It excessively pursues the
goal of visual communication and neglects the layout of public services, resulting in a structurally
unbalanced stock of land spaces that only meet tourists' short-term photo requirements and fail to
meet the long-term needs of local residents.
Representations of Space define urban spatial value, guide the orientation of stock land renewal,
and build both exclusive and irreplaceable spatial identities in traditional landmark development
through regional history, folk customs and local heritage. Building upon the rich cultural and
locational resources, the old spatial narrative has shown stable land value appreciation. Driven by
the attention economy, viral landmarks have completely reconstructed such narrative logics and
made online exposure and traffic their main branding goals [8]. To meet the dissemination rules of
short-video algorithms, these spaces have abandoned in-depth local cultural storytelling and adopted
reproducible, shareable generic tags such as "unique atmosphere" to quickly raise regional

Representational Space refers to users' actual spatial experiences and emotional perceptions, and
it is the end result of the value of existing land use. The current Areas in the traditional city are
mainly for local residents, and the space experience is based on immersive daily life scenarios, such
as leisure activities and neighbourhood interactions. These places are the living memories and
emotional homes of the residents, bearing the strong human touch and enduring social values.
Therefore, the land value has been steadily rising due to stable, people-oriented usage [9]. However,
with the rise of the attention economy, both the user group and the concept of "viral" landmarks
have changed significantly [10]. The main users of "internet-famous" places are now non-local
tourists looking for photo opportunities, and the core functions of these places have shifted from
serving daily life to social performance and monetizing online traffic. Users are no longer
experiencing the culture and spirit of the place. They will take pictures, upload them to social media,
and thereby attract the attention of the public online. These fragmented, utilitarian experiences have
replaced a deep, lasting and heartfelt sense of place as part of daily life. Although the short-term
popularity of social media influencers have stimulated the economy of the area significantly, at the
same time, its original charm and local atmosphere have been reduced. As the cultural and
humanistic value of the space continues to decline, these so-called must-see attractions are changing
rapidly and have a short lifespan. Therefore, it is difficult to guarantee the long-term preservation of
value and appreciation for existing land, and there is a high risk of resource idleness and waste after
the hype subsides.
4. Discussion and optimization strategies
4.1. The dilemma of alienation in spatial production
Given the overall changes in the hierarchical structure of the tripartite space, the renewal of
influencer spaces driven by the attention economy offers a new path for revitalising unused urban
land and promoting micro-renewal. It can quickly increase the number of visitors to a place, boost
consumption in culture and tourism, and make better use of idle spaces which is quite feasible.
However, in terms of Lefebvre's dialectical logic of spatial production, the traffic-driven renewal
model has formed an comprehensive but flawed chain of spatial production transmission, leading to
the problem of spatial alienation. The overall transmission mechanism is as follows: the attention
economy first reconfigures the representation of space, and traffic popularity has replaced cultural
heritage and public services as the main goals of existing land renewal. Under the banner of utility,
land developers have increasingly focused on the attractive appeals and check-in opportunities of
new developments for tourism, neglecting other essential functions. The resulting physical spaces
have, in turn, shaped the representational spaces and created an unbalanced use situation where
tourists participate in performative check-ins, and the daily needs of local residents are overlooked.
This chain of traffic flow has disrupted the balanced alignment of value concepts, physical
development and residents' experiences in traditional spatial production, resulting in an imbalance
among the three spatial dimensions and causing systemic development problems, such as
imbalances in land-use structure and a lack of public amenities at the physical level. At the narrative
level, this leads to the homogenization of urban spaces and the erosion of cultural heritage resources.

At the experiential level, it results in the loss of spaces' cultural and humanistic value and

4.2. Optimization strategies
To address the issue of alienation in the creation of viral landmarks, it is first necessary to uphold the
cultural and public standards of urban spaces. In the renovation and transformation of these viral
spaces, efforts should be grounded in local culture, avoiding the blind replication of viral templates
and preventing excessive entertainment-oriented or overly symbolic spatial transformations, thereby
preserving the city's unique cultural context and spatial memory. Then, it must balance the benefits
of traffic flow with the public interest, regulate profit-driven development by capital, safeguard the
public service function of urban spaces, prevent urban public spaces from becoming tools for
monetizing traffic totally, and achieve a balance between leveraging traffic flow and preserving the
public welfare function of these spaces.
The development of urban landmarks popular on the internet should neither disregard the
advantages of online traffic nor rely excessively on it. Moving forward, it is essential to achieve a
deep integration of online popularity with spatial quality, cultural value, and practical functionality.
On the one hand, by using the advantages of social media traffic and combining them with local
culture to create distinctive viral spaces, cities can use this online popularity to promote their culture
and convert online traffic into tangible value. On the one hand, local governments may leverage
social media traffic to create characteristic viral landmarks integrated with local culture; on the other
hand, urban planning shall return to the core attribute of urban space, upgrade infrastructure and
public services of viral landmarks, coordinate residential daily demands and tourist sightseeing
demands, and promote efficient utilization of stock urban land.
5. Conclusion
Based on Lefebvre's tripartite framework of spatial production, this paper analyzes the logic of
spatial production and development issues surrounding urban hotspots from the perspective of the
attention economy. The study demonstrates that the attention economy shapes the spatial production
model of urban viral landmarks through three dimensions containing physical construction, narrative
construction, and public experience. At the level of spatial practice, the focus of urban spatial
transformation has shifted from function to traffic. Although low-cost revitalization of existing
spaces has been achieved, there is a loss of practical functionality. At the level of representations of
place, deep cultural narratives have been replaced by superficial narratives driven by traffic, and a
serious problem of spatial homogeneity has occurred. In terms of representational spaces, immersive
lifestyle experiences have been replaced by performative "check-ins", and as a result, the humanistic
value of these spaces has been continuously eroded. Traffic-led development has opened up new
paths for the rejuvenation of stock urban land and the promotion of spatial vitality, but at the same
time, several problems have emerged, such as unbalanced spatial functions, a loss of local cultural
connotations, and waste of urban space resources. To promote the healthy development of viral
spaces, a standard development model and a balance between diverse values are needed.
This paper integrates the attention economy with a three-dimensional system of spatial
production to explore the development logic of viral landmarks from the perspectives of existing
spatial utilization and the reshaping of spatial value. It addresses the deficiencies in the current
