Introduction: Marketing of foods high in fat, sugar and salt shapes the food pref-
erences and intake of children and adolescents. The primary aim of this study
was to culturally adapt and validate the Scale of Effects of Social Media on Eating
Behavior (SESMEB) for assessing the influence of social media on the eating
habits of adolescents in the Republic of Srpska, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and
to examine whether this influence differs by sex, place of residence, nutritional
status and school attended.
Methods: A cross-sectional study was conducted in six secondary schools in
the city of Doboj in May 2023 and included 362 adolescents attending the third
and fourth grades. The SESMEB scale was cross-culturally adapted into Serbian
through forward and back-translation, expert panel review and pre-testing.
Internal consistency was assessed by Cronbach’s alpha and corrected item-total
correlations, the factor structure by exploratory factor analysis, and test–retest
reproducibility in a subsample of 14 respondents by the intraclass correlation
coefficient (ICC). Group differences were tested by t-test and ANOVA.
Results: The adapted scale showed good internal consistency (Cronbach’s
alpha = 0.865), with corrected item-total correlations from 0.346 to 0.638;
the data were suitable for factor analysis (KMO = 0.857; Bartlett’s test χ2
(153) = 2312.380; p < 0.001). Exploratory factor analysis identified three sub -
scales accounting for 51.5% of the variance, which could not be clearly separated.
Test–retest analysis showed no significant difference between administrations
(t = 0.552; p = 0.591; ICC > 0.7). Mean scores for all 18 items denoted no per -
ceived influence, with 55.0–76.8% of respondents reporting no effect on their
food-related attitudes and practices. Girls scored higher than boys for follow -
ing nutrition-related content (p < 0.05) and for the increase in the desire to eat
(p = 0.048); differences also emerged by place of residence ( p = 0.041) and
school attended (p = 0.008; p = 0.004).
Conclusion: Adopted version of the SESMEB scale is a reliable instrument for
assessing the perceived effects of social media on adolescent eating behavior.

1 Introduction
Unhealthy diets are among the leading global public health risks,
contributing to all forms of malnutrition, including undernutrition,
micronutrient deficiencies, and overweight, obesity and diet-related
non-communicable diseases (NCDs). Unhealthy food and beverage
marketing directed at children and adolescents increases their dietary
intake and negatively affects their preference for foods high in fat,
sugar and salt (HFSS)—that is, foods high in saturated and trans-fatty
acids, free sugars and/or salt, usually highly processed products whose
consumption is associated with adverse health effects (1). In parallel,
the Internet has become widely accessible worldwide, and its influence
on everyday life continues to grow; according to Internet World Stats,
the number of Internet users reached approximately 3.7 billion (49.6%
of the world population) by March 2017, an increase of 93.8% between
2000 and 2017 ( 2). Social networks are today among the most
common online activities of young people. A youth study conducted
in Bosnia and Herzegovina in 2018/2019 found that Facebook and
Instagram were the most frequently used platforms, whereas Snapchat
was not widespread, and concluded that the attitudes of young people
are largely shaped by social networks and online sources of informa-
tion (3). Similar patterns have been reported elsewhere: among 386
adolescents in Türkiye the most frequently used networks were
WhatsApp (88.2%), Instagram (77.5%), Y ouTube (70.9%), TikTok
(43.5%), Twitter (21.5%) and Facebook (18%) ( 4), while among
American adolescents aged 13 to 17 years the most used platforms
were Y ouTube (95%), TikTok (67%), Instagram (62%), Snapchat
(59%), Facebook (32%), Twitter (23%), WhatsApp (17%) and Reddit
(14%) (5).
In this study, exposure to social media is understood as contact
with content published on social platforms and the effect of that con-
tact on the views, attitudes, emotions and behavior of those who see
it. In the context of eating behavior, two pathways are of particular
relevance. The first concerns purchasing and consumption decisions:
advertisements, promotional posts, influencer recommendations and
favorable product reviews increase the likelihood that a particular
food will be bought and consumed, and such content most often pro-
motes highly processed, energy-dense products. The second concerns
the shaping of social norms: repeatedly seeing particular eating behav-
iors among peers, celebrities and influencers, such as eating fast food,
drinking sugar-sweetened beverages or following a particular diet,
may lead adolescents to perceive these behaviors as common, socially
accepted and desirable. Both pathways operate largely without the
deliberate attention of the recipient, which is relevant for the interpre-
tation of self-reported measures of influence.
Adolescence is a period in which young people develop new skills
in their desire to become more independent, while remaining vulner-
able due to lack of experience; during this period, decisions about
food are particularly important because of their long-term health
consequences (6). Because of the exponential growth in social media
use over the last decade, nutrition and health experts, governmental
and non-governmental organizations and health professionals
increasingly try to use social media to strengthen healthy food
choices and nutrition-related behaviors in adults, children and ado-
lescents ( 7). At the same time, advertisements for energy-dense,
nutritionally poor foods specifically target children and young people
and pose a threat to their health. Children continue to be exposed to
powerful marketing of HFSS foods, the consumption of which is
associated with adverse health effects (7, 8). Such marketing is preva-
lent—on packaging, in settings where children gather (e.g., schools
and sports clubs), during children’s viewing times and on children’s
channels, in youth magazines and on social media—and uses many
techniques designed to appeal to young audiences (8). Digital mar-
keting is of particular concern because it facilitates engagement,
which can amplify the marketing message and its overall impact (8).
On the other hand, social media can also be used to encourage
healthy lifestyles through interventions that help individuals adopt
healthy eating habits (9). This constant infiltration of social media
into everyday life makes research into its effects on nutrition-related
behavior increasingly necessary. Considering impact of social media
exposure on children nutritional habits, the primary aim of this study
was to culturally adapt and validate the Scale of Effects of Social
Media on Eating Behavior (SESMEB), previously developed and vali-
dated in Türkiye (10), for assessing the influence of social media on
the eating habits of adolescents, and to determine whether social
norms conveyed through social media influence the eating choices of
adolescents in the Republic of Srpska, Bosnia and Herzegovina.
Accordingly, the study addressed the following research question:
how is the use of social networks related to the eating habits of ado-
lescents, with particular reference to the shaping of dietary prefer-
ences, decisions concerning the consumption of specific products,
and the perception of a healthy diet?
2 Methods
2.1 Study design
A cross-sectional study was conducted in six secondary
schools in the territory of the city of Doboj in May 2023. A vali -
dated and culturally adapted online questionnaire for the purposes
of this research, designed in Google Forms was used. Respondents
received a QR code that allowed them access to the questionnaire
and was limited to one access to avoid duplication of data or
misuse. During 7 days, all respondents received a QR code and
access to the questionnaire. Respondents who in the first phase of
the examination gave their voluntary consent to participate in the
retest received an identification code through which they were

online form of the questionnaire after 7 days. By re-filling the
online form of the questionnaire, data were obtained for examin -
ing the reproducibility of the agreement of repeated measurements
with the same persons, that is, the stability of the questionnaire
over time.
2.2 Participants
This study enrolled adolescents in the third and fourth grades of
secondary schools in the territory of the city of Doboj: (1) Medical
school, (2) Economic school, (3) Gymnasium, (4) Traffic and electrical
school, (5) Technical school, (6) Hospitality and trade school. Of the
562 students attending the final grades of these secondary schools, 402
(71.5%) provided written informed consent, given either by the stu-
dents themselves or by a parent or guardian. Of these, 40 (10.0%) did
not complete the questionnaire and were excluded from further analy-
sis, leaving 362 completed questionnaires (64.4% of all eligible stu-
dents) that constituted the final study sample. The criteria for inclusion
in the research were: (1) written consent to participate in research for
students over 18 years old or written consent of parents/guardians for
students under 18 years of age.
2.3 Questionnaire
The anonymous SESMEB questionnaire ( 10) was used in the
research, which was modified according to the needs of the research
and included two parts.
The first part of the questionnaire covered Sociodemographic data
(gender, date of birth, school type, place of residence (city, village or
suburban settlement)), and self reported Anthropometric data (Body
height, Body weight and Body mass index) Body height and body
weight were self-reported by the participants. Body mass index was
calculated as body weight in kilograms divided by body height in
meters squared, and BMI-for-age z-scores were derived using WHO
AnthroPlus software (WHO growth reference for children and ado-
lescents aged 5 to 19 years) (11). Nutritional status was classified
according to age and sex as thinness (below −2 SD), normal weight
(−2 SD to +1 SD), overweight (above +1 SD to +2 SD) or obesity
(above +2 SD).
The second part of the questionnaire is the Scale of Effects of
Social Media on Eating Behavior (SESMEB), which consists of 18
statements whose answers are measured using a Likert scale from 1 to
5, where one means never and five means always. In this way, test
takers can have a score of a minimum of 18, or a maximum of 90
points. A higher score is associated with a greater influence of social
media on an individual’s eating behaviors.
The scale of the influence of social networks on the eating habits
of high school students was defined based on the average score for the
given question: (i) 1–1.8 has absolutely no effect; (ii) 1.81–2.60 does
not affect; (iii) 2.61–3.40 neutral; (iv) 3.41–4.20 affects; (v) 4.21–5
absolutely affects.
These cut-off bands were defined a priori by the authors for the
purposes of the present study and were not adopted from the original
instrument.
For the SESMEB questionnaire, prior to the beginning of the
research, consent for use was requested and obtained from the author,
via e-mail.
2.3.1 Transcultural adaptation of the SESMEB
questionnaire
Cross-cultural adaptation of the SESMEB questionnaire fol -
lowed a four-stage procedure. In the first stage (forward transla -
tion), the instrument was translated from English into Serbian by
two independent translators who were native speakers of Serbian
with a good command of English; the two versions were subse -
quently reconciled and synthesized into a single Serbian version.
In the second stage (back-translation), this synthesized version
was translated back into English by a third translator, a native
English speaker who had not been given access to the original
English instrument. In the third stage, an expert panel comprising
specialists in primary health care and family medicine together
with qualified translators reviewed all items; no item required fur -
ther modification of the Serbian translation. In the fourth stage,
the pre-final version was pre-tested on a sample of 20 secondary-
school students, all of whom confirmed that the items were clear
and comprehensible. The final Serbian version of the question -
naire was accepted on this basis.
2.4 Statistical methods
Statistical data processing was performed in IBM SPSS Statistics
version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY , United States) and Microsoft
Excel (Microsoft Office 2016, Microsoft Corp., Redmond, W A,
United States). The data collected by the research were analyzed
using the usual methods of descriptive and inferential statistics, i.e.,
methods of univariate and multivariate statistics. The results of the
study of the influence of social networks on the eating habits of high
school students are presented using descriptive and analytical sta -
tistical methods. From the methods of descriptive statistics, the fol -
lowing were used: measures of central tendency (arithmetic mean
and median), measures of variability (standard deviation) for con -
tinuous variables and absolute frequencies and percentages for cat -
egorical variables. The internal consistency of the SESMEB
instrument was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and
corrected item-total correlations. The suitability of the data for
factor analysis was verified using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO)
measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity, after
which the factor structure was examined by exploratory factor
analysis. Test–retest reproducibility was assessed in the subsample
of respondents who completed the questionnaire on two occasions,
using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), the paired-sam -
ples t-test and Pearson’s correlation coefficient. The interval between
the two administrations was set at 7 days, in line with the recom -
mendation that this interval should be long enough for respondents
not to recall their previous answers, yet short enough for their
status to remain unchanged; an interval of one to two weeks is gen -
erally considered adequate for this purpose. Differences in the influ -
ence of social media between two groups (sex) were tested using the
independent-samples t-test, and between more than two groups
(place of residence and nutritional status) using one-way analysis

3 Results
3.1 Characteristics of the sample
The sample consisted of 362 participants, of whom 59.4% were
female. The largest proportion of respondents lived in rural areas
(47.8%) and the smallest in suburban areas (8.8%). The mean age of
the respondents was 18.4 ± 0.59 years ( Table 1). The mean self-
reported body height was 174.1 ± 9.9 cm and the mean self-
reported body weight was 70.3 ± 15.9 kg. According to BMI-for-age
z-scores, 281 respondents (77.6%) were classified as having normal
weight, 49 (13.5%) as overweight and 27 (7.5%) as obese, while 4
(1.1%) were classified as thin; data were missing for one respondent
(0.3%). Overweight and obesity were more frequent among male
respondents (33 and 17 respondents, respectively) than among
female respondents (16 and 10 respondents, respectively).
3.2 Transcultural adaptation and validation
of the SESMEB questionnaire
3.2.1 Factor analysis
In the statistical analysis of the data, Cronbach’s alpha coeffi -
cient, i.e., the average correlation between all values on the scale,
was used to assess the internal consistency of the SESMEB question -
naire. In the first phase of statistical data processing, the research
instrument was validated on a sample of 362 respondents. It was
determined that the total Cronbach alpha coefficient was high
enough (0.865) to conclude that the SESMEB questionnaire is a
reliable research instrument. The suitability of the data for factor
analysis was confirmed by the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of sam -
pling adequacy (KMO = 0.857) and by Bartlett’s test of sphericity
(χ2 (153) = 2312.380; p < 0.001). Using exploratory factor analysis,
three subscales were determined that describe 51.5% of the vari -
ance. The first scale included seven questions, the second six and
the third five questions of the questionnaire ( Figure 1).
3.2.2 Testing the reliability and reproducibility of
the questionnaire
The corrected Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for all items in the
questionnaire was over 0.800, which was considered a satisfactory
value during the reliability testing of the SESMEB questionnaire.
Corrected item-total correlations ranged from 0.346 to 0.638. Two
items, namely “I think that the food that appears on social media is
healthier” (0.346) and “I follow news/blogs/pages about nutrition on
social media” (0.385), fell below the threshold of 0.40 applied by the
developers of the original instrument, while all remaining items
exceeded this value (Table 2).
The reliability of the SESMEB questionnaire was carried out
through the online form of the questionnaire, which the respon -
dents filled out 7 days after the written version of the questionnaire
(test–retest). Fourteen of initial respondents voluntarily agreed to
participate in the retest survey and via SMS received an access link
to fill out the questionnaire. After the identification of the respon -
dents, statistical processing of the obtained data was performed
(Table 3). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) showed high
reliability of our version of the SESMEB research instrument
(>0.7). Using the t-test for paired samples, no statistically signifi -
cant difference was found between the two tests ( t = 0.552;
p = 0.591). A satisfactory positive correlation was established
between the scores of the first and repeated tests (Pearson’s
coefficient = 0.699).

3.3.2 The influence of social networks on the
habits, attitudes and practices of adolescent
nutrition
More than 2/3 of adolescents (76.8%) stated that the number of
likes/shares of a certain food does not affect their consumption of that
food, and 69.9% of them do not consider such food more reliable.
When we asked adolescents whether they consume food posted by
people with a large number of followers, more than 3/5 of adolescents
answered negatively and at the same time more than 2/3 of them
(71.5%) do not consider such food healthier.
It was observed that in more than half of adolescents (55%), the
time spent on social networks does not increase their desire to eat and
does not affect uncritical food intake in almost 3/5 of adolescents
(59.4%). What indicates the need for additional consideration of the
results is the fact that 1/3 of adolescents (34.3%) stated that it is rare,
and 1/5 (21.3%) that sometimes while viewing content on social net-
works, even though they are full, they still eat something, which is not
negligible, and that 2/5 of respondents (42.4%) sometimes believe that
food advertised on social networks is healthier (Table 5).
If we look at the results of the scale of the influence of social net-
works on eating behavior it can be concluded that social networks
have no influence on the eating habits of the adolescents, because the
score values for all the observed statements are found in the group
with absolutely no influence or no influence (Table 6) according to the
proposed classification of influence (Mean range = 1–1.8: absolutely
no effect; Mean range = 1.81–2.60: does not affect).
The three items with the highest mean scores, namely “I think that
the food that appears on social media is healthier” , “I follow news/
blogs/pages about nutrition on social media” and “Food/dishes I see
on social media increase my desire to eat” , were additionally examined
in relation to sex, place of residence, nutritional status and the school
attended. A statistically significant difference by sex was found for
following nutrition-related news, blogs and pages (girls, n = 215,
2.56 ± 1.12; boys, n = 147, 2.07 ± 1.05; p < 0.05) and for the reported
increase in the desire to eat (girls 2.58 ± 1.21; boys 2.33 ± 1.14;
p = 0.048), whereas no difference was observed for the belief that food
appearing on social media is healthier (girls 2.22 ± 0.83; boys
2.22 ± 0.84; p = 0.095) (Table 7).
With respect to place of residence, a statistically significant differ-
ence was found only for following nutrition-related content (city,
n = 157, 2.45 ± 1.19; village, n = 173, 2.23 ± 1.01; suburban settlement,
n = 32, 2.69 ± 1.20; p = 0.041) (Table 8). Post-hoc comparison showed
that this difference lay between respondents living in villages and
those living in suburban settlements, the latter using social networks
for this purpose more frequently.
In relation to nutritional status, none of the three items differed
significantly between groups (normal weight, n = 281; overweight,
n = 49; obesity, n = 27; thinness, n = 4; all p > 0.05). Separate analysis
of two further items showed a higher mean score among adolescents
with obesity (2.70) than among those of normal weight (2.06) for the
statement that posting food on social media influences their opinion
of that type of food, and higher scores among adolescents with obesity
(1.96) and thinness (2.50) than among those of normal weight (1.57)
for eating food seen on social media despite being full. The latter com-
parison rests on only four respondents in the thinness group and
should therefore be interpreted with caution (Table 9).
Comparison according to the school attended showed statistically
significant differences for two of the three items (Table 10). Following
nutrition-related news, blogs and pages differed between schools
(p = 0.008), with the highest mean score recorded in the Gymnasium
(n = 34, 2.71), followed by the Traffic and Electrical School (n = 116,
2.16) and the Technical School (n = 34, 1.97). The reported increase
in the desire to eat also differed between schools (p = 0.004), with the
highest score recorded in the Economics School (n = 49, 2.90), which
differed significantly from the Gymnasium (2.35), the Traffic and

In our research, we used the SESMEB questionnaire to assess the
influence of social networks on the eating habits of adolescents and we
showed that in more than half of the respondents (the range was from
55 to 76.8%), the influence of social networks on attitudes and practices
related to the choice, preparation and consumption of food does not
exist. About 76% of the respondents stated that the number of likes/
shares of a certain food does not influence their consumption of that
food, and 69.9% of them do not consider such food more reliable. More
than 3/5 of respondents (62.4%) said that they do not eat food posted
by people with a large number of followers, nor do they think that food
is healthier (71.5%). It was observed that in more than half of the
respondents (55%), the time spent on social networks does not affect
the desire to eat, and in almost 3/5 of the respondents (59.4%) it was
observed that social networks do not affect uncritical eating (taking
food when full). Alwafi et al. came to a similar conclusion in a study
conducted in Saudi Arabia in 2021, which included 1,124 respondents
aged 18 and older. This study showed that social networks had an influ-
ence on the eating habits of only about one-fifth of the respondents
(15). However, the results of research in Australia showed that there is
a connection between the use of social networks and the number of
profiles on social networks and eating disorders among adolescents
(16). Similarly, a study in the United States of America (USA) con-
ducted on a large cohort of eighth- and eleventh-grade students found
that longer time spent on social media had a greater impact on poor
diet and inadequate sleep in adolescents (17). In our research, 34.3% of
the respondents stated that it is rare and 1/5 (21.3%) that sometimes
while looking at content on social networks, even though they are full,
they snack, which is not negligible, as well as that 2/5 of the respon-
dents (42.4%) sometimes think that the food that appears on social
networks is healthier. The results of a study conducted in Belgium,
which included 1,002 students aged 11 to 19 in 18 secondary schools,
showed that food messages and advertisements on social media are
associated with adolescent diets. Students who reported greater expo-
sure to posts of energy-dense and nutrient-poor foods, such as salty
and sweet snacks, on social media were significantly more likely to
consume higher amounts of these foods (18). The results of Coates et
al. ’s research on children’s exposure to food marketing on social net-
works are similar, where it was determined that children increase their
intake of energy-dense and nutritionally poor foods when they are
exposed to social media messages promoting these foods ( 19). An
online questionnaire survey of 272 medical students in India showed a
significant correlation between increased use of social media and the
development of unhealthy eating habits, namely students who used 4
or more social networks were at greater risk of developing an eating
disorder, as indicated by higher scores on the SESMEB. This was espe-
cially pronounced among first year students compared to final year
students. First-year students were closer to the late adolescent age
group, which is at the highest risk of developing an eating disorder.
Going to medical school is first of all accompanied by leaving home
and family, it implies leaving and living and eating in student canteens
or switching to fast food due to a large number of obligations (20). In
our research, female respondents reported following nutrition-related
news, blogs and pages on social media more often than male respon-
dents, and also reported a greater increase in the desire to eat in
response to food content; both differences were statistically significant.
In their survey of 1,000 students in Türkiye, Durmaz et al. showed that
81.1% of students are interested in the topic of nutrition on social
media, that is, 31.2% of female students and 36.5% of male students are
interested in and follow this type of content on social media (21). Also,
the results of the study by Alwafi et al. indicate the probability that
social networks affect women more (15), and this can be explained by
gender differences in psychology and motives for using social net -
works, which Zhang et al. showed in their study (22). Research con-
ducted in Indonesia among adolescent girls from 14 to 18 years old

watching a video about food, male subjects were more likely to eat what

can really influence body mass, given that social norms encourage the
consumption of certain foods, and that this influence differs depending
on the gender of young people (24). In a study by Temple et al., more

adolescents were exposed to more hours of television viewing, there

energy-dense, nutritionally poor foods (26). This result differed from
the results of a study conducted in the United States of America, which
showed that the consumption of soft drinks while watching television

In contrast to us, Sina et al. concluded in their systematic literature
review that exposure to social media was associated with increased
frequency of consumption of snacks, fast food, sweetened juices and
higher odds of skipping breakfast, with those living in rural and sub-
urban areas being at greater risk ( 28). In relation to the secondary
school attended, our results showed statistically significant differences
for two of the three items examined: adolescents attending the
Gymnasium reported following nutrition-related content most fre-
quently, whereas those attending the Economics School reported the
greatest increase in the desire to eat in response to food content.
Kim and Han showed that adolescents in Korea who use smart-
phones for communication rather than for learning are more likely
to consume fast food (29). Teo et al. showed that adolescents who use
WhatsApp for messaging consume 58% more snacks than adoles -
cents who read an article published online (30). Gascoyne et al. found
that 55% of students reported seeing an advertisement for food or
drink on social media at least once a week, although consuming the
advertised products was less frequent; exposure to such advertise -
ments at least once in the previous week was associated with a high
intake of unhealthy beverages, while liking or sharing food or bever-
age posts at least once in the previous month was associated with a
high intake of both unhealthy beverages and unhealthy foods, and
this association was stronger in boys ( 31). A study conducted in
Indonesia in 2021 among 123 respondents examined the relationship
between eating behavior, frequency and duration of social media use,
and total consumption of energy-dense foods, and found that both
emotional overeating and time spent on social networks were associ-
ated with a higher intake of energy-dense foods ( 32). Social media
use can influence food choices through food advertising and through
the influence of other users, as social networks allow advertising to
spread quickly and reach large numbers of consumers ( 33).
Kucharczuk and colleagues reported two main findings: adolescents
recalled unhealthy foods more often and were influenced by celebri-
ties and influencers, suggesting that food and beverage companies
use social media to target the adolescent population (34). Niškanović
et al. (35) piloted the “L: Landscape of campaigns” step of the WHO
“CLICK” framework in the Republic of Srpska, Bosnia and
Herzegovina, to characterize the digital food and beverage marketing
to which children are potentially exposed through their preferred
digital media. The most frequently advertised food categories on
social media and in Y ouTube influencers’ content were cakes, sweet
biscuits and pastries (31 and 22.7%, respectively) and chocolate and
sugar confectionery (27.5 and 14.7%, respectively). None of the social
media advertisements, and only 2 of 24 Y ouTube influencer market-
ing instances, would have been permitted to be marketed to children,
and most advertisements featured elements potentially appealing to
teenagers (88% on Facebook and 72% on Instagram). The most fre-
quent persuasive appeals on social media were references to holidays,
travel or adventure (23%), taste (12.5%) and premium/contest offers
(11.5%), while Y ouTube influencers predominantly presented
branded foods (62.5%). In line with these observations, our study
showed that adolescent girls followed more food-related content and
that such content increased their desire to eat.
Taken together, these findings should be interpreted in the light
of what the SESMEB scale actually measures. The instrument assesses
the influence of social media on eating behavior as perceived and
reported by respondents themselves, and perceived influence is not
equivalent to actual influence. People systematically judge advertising
to affect others more than themselves, and recognition of persuasive
intent is weakest precisely for influencer and native content, which
does not present itself as advertising; influence that operates through
repeated exposure, affect and perceived social norms is therefore
largely unavailable to introspection. The pronounced floor effect
observed for several items, with between 60 and 77% of respondents
selecting the response “never, ” is consistent with this interpretation.
The contrast with the marketing landscape documented for the same
population is instructive: while the adolescents in our sample reported
almost no influence, an audit of the digital media they prefer showed
intensive marketing of energy-dense, nutritionally poor products,
with 88% of advertisements on Facebook and 72% on Instagram con-
taining elements potentially appealing to teenagers (35). Our results
should therefore not be read as evidence that social media does not
influence the eating behavior of adolescents, but rather that adoles-
cents do not recognize such an influence, which is in itself relevant for
the design of media literacy interventions and for the choice of mea-
surement approaches in future research.
5 Limitations and strengths
This study has several limitations and we can consider it a pilot
study for the area of Bosnia and Herzegovina, but at the same time it

has its advantages since it is a very current topic and this is the first
step in considering this aspect of the influence on the eating habits of
young people. In terms of strengths, this study presents the first efforts
to evaluate the validity and reliability of the SESMEB questionnaires
which is easy to use, cost-effective, and reproducible.
The first limitation is reflected in the fact that it was conducted
only in one geographical area and that the results cannot be applied to
the whole of Bosnia and Herzegovina.
Second, the questionnaire has so far been applied and validated in
a smaller number of countries, and the results cannot be compared
and monitored.
Third, the lack of validated versions of this questionnaire that have
been used in other countries and in other languages, which would
allow for comparison of the results obtained with other countries, is
also considered a limiting factor.
Fourth, the absence of questions that examine the frequency and
duration of social media use, as well as the focus on the content that
respondents use, which may influence their answers, can be also rec-
ognized as a limitation of the study and the instrument used in
the study.
Fifth, body height and body weight were self-reported rather than
measured, which may have led to misclassification of nutritional
status. Sixth, the test–retest subsample comprised only 14 respon -
dents, so the reproducibility estimates should be regarded as prelimi-
nary. Seventh, measurement invariance across sex, place of residence
and nutritional status was not tested, and the comparisons between
these groups should therefore be interpreted with corresponding cau-
tion. Finally, several items showed a pronounced floor effect, with
between 60 and 77% of respondents selecting the response “never, ”
which limits the variability available for detecting differences between
groups. In addition, the design does not allow the two pathways
through which social media may affect eating behavior, namely the
effect on consumption decisions and the shaping of social norms, to
be separated empirically, since the instrument measures perceived
influence and contains no measure of actual exposure, of the fre -
quency or duration of use, or of the type of content followed.
6 Conclusion
As colleagues from Türkiye concluded, there is no comprehensive
tool in the literature for measuring the impact of social media on
eating habits. The SESMEB Questionnaire proposed by them, which
was tested in our research, can be applied as an adequate instrument
for measuring the impact of social media use on eating habits. Our
research also indicated the need to correct certain questions in order
to get more reliable answers to certain questions. Certainly, the use of
the mentioned instrument by a larger number of authors in several
countries would give an opportunity for a better understanding of its
application and its improvement, since it represents an important con-
tribution in the field of considering the effect of social media on eating
habits. The results of our study represent the basis for conducting
future studies on the mentioned topic in our area in order to gain
insight into the influence of social networks on the eating habits of
adolescents in different geographical areas in Bosnia and Herzegovina
and to be able to undertake interventions. Future research may explore
whether the type of food largely advertised varies depending on ado-
lescents’ nutrition knowledge and interests (for example, whether
adolescents who choose to follow an exercise regimen or implement
a particular diet receive more or less fast-food and HFSS foods
advertisements).
Two practical implications follow from these results. First, ado -
lescents reported almost no influence of social media on their
eating behavior, although the same population is intensively
exposed to digital marketing of energy-dense, nutritionally poor
products. Preventive work should therefore be directed toward
media literacy, and specifically toward the recognition of persuasive
intent in influencer and native content, rather than toward nutri -
tional knowledge alone. Second, the differences observed between
schools indicate that school-based programs should not be uni -
form, and that the school context is a relevant unit for the planning
of preventive activities.
