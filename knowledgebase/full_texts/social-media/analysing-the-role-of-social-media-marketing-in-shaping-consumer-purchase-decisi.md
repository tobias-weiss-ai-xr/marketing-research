ABSTRACT:
Generation Z — the first cohort to grow up fully immersed in social media — makes purchase decisions
in ways that look very different from the generations before it. This study looks at how different pieces
of social media marketing, from influencer posts to user -generated reviews, shape what young
consumers actually decide to buy. Using a structured questionnaire completed by 200 respondents aged
16 to 28, the study measure d attitudes toward influencer credibility, content authenticity, platform
habits, and emotional drivers such as Fear of Missing Out (FoMO) and social proof. The resulting model
explained a striking 73 percent of the variation in purchase intention (R² = 0. 730), with engagement
intensity, perceived authenticity, and influencer credibility standing out as the strongest predictors.
FoMO and social proof also played a meaningful supporting role, mediating the path between exposure
and action. Taken together, th e results point to one clear message for brands: genuine, engaging,
platform-appropriate content beats sheer reach every time when it comes to winning over Gen Z
shoppers.

I. INTRODUCTION
Over the last decade, the way people shop has been completely rewritten by social media. Generation
Z — roughly a quarter of the world's population — has grown up never knowing a world without a
smartphone in hand, and this shows up clearly in how they discover products, weigh options, and decide
what to buy. Platforms like Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, and Facebook now sit at the center of
everyday brand discovery, creating layered, fast-moving decision environments that are worth studying
carefully.
What makes social media marketing different from a traditional billboard or TV spot is its two -way
nature. Instead of simply broadcasting a message, brands can now build ongoing, personalized
conversations with their audience, and this dynamic resonates strongly with younger consumers.
Influencer marketing in particular has exploded into a multi-billion-dollar industry, with creators acting

close those gaps wit h a reasonably sized sample, a broad set of measured variables, and appropriate
statistical modeling.
II. LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1 Theoretical Background
Social media marketing spans everything from organic brand presence and user -generated campaigns
to paid ads and influencer deals. Researchers have drawn on a range of theories to explain how it works
— Social Capital Theory, the Theory of Planned Behavior, the Technology Acceptance Model, and
Social Influence Theory all offer useful, complementary lenses. Wh at ties them together is a growing
emphasis on emotional engagement, perceived authenticity, and the parasocial bonds that form between
consumers and the creators they follow. Gen Z, being digital natives, process this content differently:
they're more media-literate than earlier generations, yet oddly still susceptible to persuasion when it's
wrapped in entertainment.
2.2 Influencer Marketing
Influencer marketing is probably the most heavily studied piece of this puzzle. The evidence
consistently shows tha t an influencer's perceived trustworthiness, expertise, and likability drive both
brand perception and purchase intent. Interestingly, micro- and nano-influencers — those with follower
counts in the tens of thousands rather than millions — tend to outperfo rm mega-influencers, likely
because smaller audiences foster tighter, more believable relationships. The persuasive effect seems to
travel through two channels: a direct route built on product information, and an indirect route built on
entertainment, aspiration, and social proof. Content that feels organic rather than obviously promotional
tends to land better, which puts a spotlight on how transparently sponsorships are disclosed.
2.3 Content and Platform Effects
Beyond who delivers the message, what the content looks like matters a great deal. User -generated
content — reviews, testimonials, unpolished demos — is particularly good at lowering perceived risk
and building trust. The rise of short-form video on TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts has
introduced a new kind of persuasion built on entertainment value and rapid trend cycles rather than
detailed argument. Platforms aren't interchangeable, either: Instagram and YouTube, with their higher
production values, tend to work better for higher -involvement or luxury purchases, while TikTok's
algorithmic, entertainment-first feed is better suited to driving impulsive, FoMO-fueled buys.
2.4 Psychological Drivers
Authenticity keeps surfacing as the linchpin of trust — genuine product experience, honest disclosure,
and a believable fit between influencer and brand. Interestingly, Gen Z consumers are quite good at
spotting inauthentic content, yet they still engage with creators they know are being paid, suggesting
that consistency and effort matter more than some abstract notion of pure authenticity. Trust itself has

2.5 What Makes Gen Z Different
Gen Z shoppers care noticeably more about brand values — sustainability, social responsibility,
representation — than previous cohorts, and they lean harder on peer recommendations and user content
over polished advertising. FoMO is especially salient for a generation raised inside always -on social
networks, functioning simultaneously as a reason to stay on the platform and a trigger for impulsive
purchases. Some patterns vary by gender and by how heavily someone uses social media, with moderate
users often showing stronger purchase intent than either light users or the most saturated, possibly
fatigued, heavy users.
2.6 Mediators, Moderators, and the Gap This Study Fills
FoMO and social proof both act as psychological bridges between exposure and behavior, while
variables like product involvement, self -control, and cultural context shape how strong these effects
turn out to be. Still, few studies test several of  these dimensions together, most rely on purchase
intention rather than actual behavior, and probabilistic sampling with an adequate sample size remains
rare. This study tries to address all three shortcomings in one design.
III. RESEARCH GAP AND OBJECTIVES
● Identify the main dimensions of social media marketing that brands currently rely on —
influencer partnerships, user-generated content, platform-specific tactics, and interactive
engagement.
● Measure how strongly each dimension predicts Gen Z purchase intention and reported buying
behavior.
● Test the psychological and social variables — FoMO, social proof, trust — that mediate the
link between exposure and purchase.
● Examine which demographic factors, if any, moderate marketing effectiveness.
● Translate the findings into practical guidance for marketers working with this audience.
IV. HYPOTHESES
● H1: Social media marketing activity positively predicts Gen Z purchase intention.
● H2: Influencer credibility and perceived authenticity mediate the exposure–intention
relationship.
● H3: FoMO mediates the link between marketing activity and impulsive purchasing.
● H4: User-generated content is more persuasive than brand-generated content.
● H5: Platform (Instagram vs. TikTok vs. YouTube) moderates the exposure–intention
relationship.
● H6: Brand authenticity and trust moderate the effect of influencer recommendations on
behavior.
● H7: Engagement intensity has a non-linear relationship with purchase intention, peaking at
moderate levels.

V. METHODOLOGY
5.1 Design and Sample
This was a quantitative, cross-sectional, descriptive study. Two hundred respondents aged 16–28 were
recruited through convenience sampling — a pragmatic but non -probabilistic method common in
exploratory marketing research. Eligibility required active social  media use (at least two hours daily)
and a purchase within the previous three months. The final sample skewed 58% female and 42% male,
spanned a mix of income brackets and education levels, and was active mainly on Instagram (98%),
TikTok (87%), YouTube (82%), and Facebook (72%).
5.2 Instrument and Procedure
A 20-item, 5-point Likert questionnaire was distributed through Google Forms across social platforms,
email, and institutional networks over eight weeks. It covered demographics, marketing exposure and
engagement, perceived authenticity and trust, psychological constructs (FoMO, social proof, brand
affinity), and self-reported purchase behavior. Respondents gave informed consent and were assured of
confidentiality.
5.3 Analysis
Data were analyzed in SPS S 27 and AMOS 28 using descriptive statistics, Cronbach's Alpha for
reliability, Pearson correlations, multiple regression, and path analysis for mediation, with significance
set at p < .05.
VI. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
6.1 Reliability
All multi -item scales cleared the standard 0.70 reliability threshold: social media engagement (α =
0.82), perceived authenticity/trust (α = 0.79), FoMO (α = 0.81), and purchase intention (α = 0.84).
6.2 Correlations and Regression
Engagement intensity showed the strongest biva riate correlation with purchase intention (r = 0.612),
followed by authenticity (r = 0.547) and influencer credibility (r = 0.534); FoMO and social proof were
moderately correlated as well. The full regression model explained 73% of the variance in purchas e
intention (F(8,191) = 42.35, p < .001, R² = 0.730).

6.3 Mediation and Platform Effects
FoMO significantly mediated the path from engagement to impulsive purchasing, accounting for about
38% of that relationship, while  trust mediated roughly 45% of the effect of authenticity on purchase
intention. Platform mattered too: influencer credibility predicted purchase intention more strongly on
Instagram (β = 0.412) than TikTok (β = 0.267), whereas short-form video content was a stronger driver
of impulsive buying on TikTok (β = 0.358) than Instagram (β = 0.214) — a pattern consistent with each
platform's distinct culture and algorithm.
VII. Discussion and Key Findings
● Marketing dimensions together explain 73% of purchase intention variance — a notably
strong effect for a consumer research model.
● Active engagement outpredicts passive exposure, meaning reach and impressions are weaker
performance metrics than genuine interaction.
● Perceived authenticity is the second-strongest predictor, ahead of formal credibility markers
like expertise or brand size.
● Platform choice changes what works: Instagram favors polished influencer credibility, TikTok
favors entertainment and urgency.
● FoMO and social proof are real psychological bridges between marketing exposure and actual
buying, raising ethical questions about how far urgency tactics should be pushed.
● User-generated content and peer reviews outperform brand-produced messaging by a wide
margin.
● Age and gender barely matter — Gen Z looks fairly uniform in how it responds to marketing,
so behavioral and psychographic targeting beats demographic targeting.
VIII. Recommendations for Marketers
● Design for engagement, not just reach — polls, prompts, and community discussion
outperform passive impressions.
● Be transparent about sponsorships; disclosed partnerships with a genuine brand fit often out-
convert hidden ones.
● Tailor strategy by platform rather than repurposing the same content everywhere.
● Invest in customer reviews and UGC campaigns, even at the cost of some control over the
brand narrative.
● Favor micro- and nano-influencers over mega-influencers for better authenticity and cost
efficiency.
● Use urgency and scarcity messaging responsibly — real constraints only, not manufactured
hype.
● Back sustainability or social-responsibility claims with substance; Gen Z is quick to spot
greenwashing.

IX. CONCLUSION
This study set out to understand how social media marketing shapes the way Gen Z decides what to
buy, and the results are fairly clear: it matters a great deal, and it works mainly through psychological
channels rather than straightforward information transfer. Engagement depth beats audience size,
authenticity beats formal credibility, and platform context changes which levers actually mo ve
behavior. Demographics turned out to be almost irrelevant compared with behavioral and psychological
variables, which has real implications for how brands should segment their audiences going forward.
That said, the study has limits worth flagging. Conv enience sampling restricts how far these findings
can be generalized, the cross-sectional design can't establish causality, and self -reported purchasing is
always vulnerable to some social -desirability bias. Future work would benefit from longitudinal
tracking, cross -cultural comparison, objective purchase data from e -commerce platforms, and
experimental designs that manipulate specific marketing elements directly. Even with those caveats,
the picture that emerges here is a useful one: for brands trying to reach Gen Z, authentic, engaging, and
platform-appropriate content will consistently outperform louder, broader, but shallower campaigns.
