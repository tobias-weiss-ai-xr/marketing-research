Introduction
Background And Rationale
As social media platforms become a major channel for brands to communicate with consumers, posts, videos, stories, and influe
 ncer
collaborations are now widely used as a key tool in maintaining a long
 -
term relationship (Curris et al., 2023).
This transformation
has transformed content marketing from a tactical communication strategy to a strategic asset that is thought to influence th
 e
perception of the brand's authenticity and ultimately make consumers more likely to be brand advocates.
 However, with the
increase of digital content volumes, there has been a rise in consumers' scepticism about brands' messages being commercial a
 nd
not authentic (Foroudi
et al., 2020, as cited in Kaushal et al., 2023) which is not yet resolved, as recent research on sustainability
messaging and Instagram content has demonstrated (Bulmer et al., 2024).
The digital consumer economy has seen a sharp growth in Pakistan with increased penetration of social media and mobile use of
internet, especially among young and urban population (Kaushal et al., 2023).
In this area, Punjab's increasing digital integration
provides a relevant but under
 -
researched context for the examination of the link between value of content marketing and
perceptions of brand authenticity and then to consumer advocacy, and the way both
 of these translations are influenced by
consumers' prior familiarity with the brand and their level of digital engagement.
Problem Statement and Research Gap
Brands invest heavily in content marketing on the assumption that valuable content builds trust and drives advocacy, yet the
pathway from content to advocacy is neither direct nor uniform. Curris et al.
(2023) and Kaushal et al.
(2023) provide evidence for
both direct and authenticity
 -
mediated pathways, but their studies are concentrated in specific product categories (beauty products,
smartphones) and in Pakistan’s major metropolitan centres
 . Whether these relationships hold among social media users in Punjab,
and whether brand familiarity and digital engagement jointly condition them, remains empirically untested. Without this evide
 nce,
brand managers in secondary markets lack a validated ba
 sis for calibrating content strategy to their specific consumer base.
Three gaps motivate the study. First, a
 contextual gap
 : existing evidence is drawn from major Pakistani cities and from other
product categories and countries
 —
for example, the nutrition sector in Lyu et al.
(2025) and smartphone brands in Elsotouhy et
al.
(2025)
 —
leaving mid
 -
tier cities of Punjab underrepresen
ted. Second, an
integration gap
: brand familiarity and digital engagement
have each been examined as moderators of isolated pathways (Aljarah
 et al., 2024), but few studies test both simultaneously
alongside authenticity
 -
based mediation in a single structural model. Third, a
 boundary
-
condition gap
 : the literature offers
competing accounts of how brand familiarity moderates the content value
 –
authenticity relationship
 —
a ceiling effect for highly
familiar consumers versus a signalling effect for less familiar ones
 —
that warrants further empirical testi
 ng (Curris et al., 2023).
Research Objectives, Questions, and Significance
The study addresses six research questions and corresponding objectives: (1) does content marketing value significantly predi
 ct
brand authenticity (RQ1/H1); (2) does it significantly predict consumer advocacy directly (RQ2/H2); (3) does brand authentici
 ty
significantly predict consumer advocacy (RQ3/H3); (4) does brand authenticity mediate the content value
 –
advocacy relationship
(RQ4/H4); (5) does brand familiarity moderate the content value
 –
authenticity relationship (RQ5/H5); and (6) does digital
engagemen
t moderate the authenticity
–
advocacy relationship (RQ6/H6)?
Theoretically, the study builds on previous research by Curris et al. (2023) and Kaushal et al. (2023) by incorporating Signa
 lling
Theory, Uses and Gratifications Theory and Social Exchange Theory in a common testable model that accounts for both mediated
and dual
-
moderated pathways from content marketing value to consumer advocacy. In practice, the results can be used by brand
manag
ers and digital marketers in secondary urban markets for an awareness level to adjust the content strategy, not following a
one
-
size
-
fits
-
all approach.
The study is limited to business
 -
to
-
consumer marketing, offline consumers and other cities in Pakistan, as well as to adult social
media users (18 years and above) who are active users of Facebook, Instagram, TikTok or YouTube and who have interacted with
brand
-
generated content, and who have made a purchase or followed a brand account in the last six months of their life. The study
is also conducted as a cross sectional
design so that this study does not reflect causal precedence as discussed in Section 5.4.

Content Marketing Value
Content marketing value refers to the perceived value and usefulness that consumers get from the brand's content on social me
 dia.
It is a multidimensional communication phenomenon that includes informational accuracy and content's ability to entertain, bu
 i
ld
trust and emotional connection, beyond content's informational component; a perception of content being truly useful and not
intrusive in the commercial message. (Curris et al., 2023). These dimensions are not assessed alone, but act in conjunction i
 n
infl
uencing the way in which brand content is processed by consumers and accordingly require an integrative view that places the
value of the content within the context of the consumer
 –
brand relationship, such as consumers' prior familiarity with the brand
and
 perceived brand authenticity (Curris et al., 2023).
 Islam et al. (2026) also explored that
 how social media marketing
translates into consumer purchase intention, the mechanism through which it operates, and the platform conditions that shape
 its
strength.
Brand Authenticity
Brand authenticity is the consumer's belief that a brand is authentic, transparent, consistent, and keeps to its values. Foro
 udi et al.
(2020) operationalized it as the extent to which consumers feel that a brand is sincere, congruent in the values it comm
 unicates
and in the behaviors
it exhibits, and that a brand has a history that supports the credibility in the marketplace. Therefore, brand
authenticity is not a natural property of the brand, but a socially constructed feature that is generated by the interaction
 of brand
communicat
ion, consumer expectation, and the sociocultural environment (Deng et al., 2025). Authenticity, as a psychological
linking factor between brand marketing stimuli and downstream outcomes, is confirmed by Kaushal et al. (2023), where consumer
 s
exposed to aut
hentic brands are likely to experience brand love and brand advocacy, which is similar to the findings of Deng et al.
(2025), where brand attachment and brand trust are psychological linkages that mediate the effects of brand authenticity on b
 rand
loyalty,
in international brand contexts. Authenticity, therefore, relies on the brand's uniformity of communication, the appearance
of sincerity in its actions and the fact that the brand maintains its values in business, which is why it is central to this
 model.
Consumer Advocacy, Brand Familiarity, and Digital Engagement
Consumer advocacy refers to the unprompted voluntary behavior consumers engage in when they recommend, endorse or promote
a brand in their social network based on their positive satisfaction and emotional bonds with the brand. It is not just satis
 faction o
r
repeat purchases
–
it's the higher
 -
order relational outcome in which consumers are brand ambassadors, which Kaushal et al (2023)
describe as a proactive relational interaction that is social
 –
advocates invest personal social capital, reaching the brand
 beyond paid
media. The result of their study corroborates with
 Aljarah et al. (2024), who find that the dimensions of authenticity, especially
brand love and brand loyalty, have a significant influence on brand advocacy of Gen Z smartphone users in Pakistan.
Brand familiarity is the accumulation of direct and indirect experiences consumers have with a brand, forming a cognitive sch
 ema
that shapes how brand information is processed and retained. Curris et al.
(2023) and Aljarah et al.
(2024) treat it as a bound
 ary
condition, not a brand
 -
generated asset, that moderates
 relationships within content marketing and advocacy frameworks by
reflecting the depth of prior knowledge a consumer holds toward a brand. Digital engagement, by contrast, is the behavioural
 ,
cognitive, and emotional investment consumers make in interacting with brand content online
 —
liking, sharing, commenting,
following, and creating user
 -
generated content (Curris et al., 2023)
 —
and functions in the present model as a relational amplifier
gro
unded in Uses and Gratifications Theory, which holds that consumers actively seek content fulfilling specific psychological o
 r
social needs.

Research Design
 a
nd Philosophy
B

(CA)

context (Bryman, 2016). A quantitative paradigm allows for systematic measurement, empirical hypothesis testing and

for five latent constructs and two moderation pathways. A cross sectional

Data were gathered using the structured self
