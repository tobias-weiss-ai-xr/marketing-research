ABSTRACT
This study examines the relationship between entrepreneurial marketing orientation (EMO) and marketing
performance, as mediated by digital marketing capabilities (DMC), among small and medium enterprises
(SMEs) in north-western Nigeria. Grounded in the Resource-Based View and dynamic capabilities framework,
the research adopted a quantitative cross-sectional design. Data were collected from 312 SME owner-managers
in Katsina and Kano states through an online survey and analysed using Partial Least Squares Structural
Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM). Findings reveal that EMO has a significant positive direct effect on marketing
performance and exerts a strong influence on DMC, which in turn drives marketing performance. Digital
marketing capabilities partially mediate the relationship, representing the dominant pathway through which
EMO enhances performance outcomes. The study concludes that superior marketing performance among
Nigerian SMEs results from the synergy between entrepreneurial marketing orientation and digital marketing
capabilities. In resource-constrained emerging markets, EMO functions as a higher-order capability that
primarily generates value by enabling the development and adaptive deployment of digital tools, particularly
social media proficiency. SME owner-managers should cultivate entrepreneurial behaviours, especially
customer intensity, while prioritising digital capabilities, beginning with social media marketing and progressing
to data analytics. Policymakers and support organisations such as SMEDAN should design integrated
interventions that combine entrepreneurial marketing training with practical digital skills development rather
than infrastructure alone. The findings advance understanding of the marketing-entrepreneurship interface in
sub-Saharan Africa and offer actionable insights for improving SME competitiveness in digitally transitioning
economies.

1. Introduction
Small and medium enterprises occupy a central position in Nigeria's economic structure. According to the Small
and Medium Enterprises Development Agency of Nigeria (SMEDAN, 2021), the sector accounts for approximately
96 per cent of all registered businesses, generates roughly 84 per cent of employment, and contributes an estimated
48 per cent of gross domestic product. These are striking figures, yet they coexist with equally striking evidence of
fragility. Survival rates for Nigerian SMEs remain alarmingly low: studies conducted over successive years
consistently report failure rates in excess of 60 per cent within the first three years of operation, a pattern attributed
variously to inadequate financing, weak institutional support, and, critically, chronic marketing underperformance
(Onuoha, 2022).
The inadequacy of marketing practice in Nigerian SMEs is not simply a matter of budget. It reflects a structural
mismatch between classical marketing frameworks, which were developed in the context of large, resource-rich
firms operating in stable, formalised markets, and the conditions that actually characterise most small Nigerian
enterprises. In this environment, market structures are frequently informal, consumer behaviour is highly
heterogeneous, institutional infrastructure is weak, and access to formal market research is prohibitively expensive.
Against this backdrop, entrepreneurial marketing has gained considerable traction as a theoretically coherent and
practically viable alternative. Unlike conventional marketing, which proceeds from structured planning and
dedicated departmental resources, entrepreneurial marketing is driven by opportunity recognition, customer
intensity, resource creativity, and a willingness to accept calculated risk in the pursuit of value creation (Morris et
al., 2002; Hills & Hultman, 2006; Alqahtani & Uslay, 2020).
In parallel with this conceptual shift, Nigeria's digital economy has expanded considerably over the past decade.
The country recorded approximately 103 million active internet users by the end of 2023, representing a penetration
rate of around 48 per cent and placing Nigeria among the largest internet markets on the African continent
(DataReportal, 2024). Mobile telephony and social media platforms have become primary commercial channels for
a growing segment of Nigerian businesses. Instagram, WhatsApp Business, and Facebook now function as
storefronts, customer service channels, and brand-building tools simultaneously, particularly for SMEs that lack
resources for conventional advertising. Despite this observable shift in marketing practice, the academic literature
has not kept pace. Research examining how digital marketing capabilities interact specifically with entrepreneurial
marketing orientation to influence SME marketing performance in Nigeria remains sparse and largely inconclusive
(Afolabi et al., 2024; Ijomah et al., 2024).
This paper addresses that gap. The central argument advanced here is that digital marketing capabilities do not
merely supplement conventional marketing effort; they function as strategic resources that amplify the performance
effects of entrepreneurial marketing behaviour. Drawing on the Resource-Based View (RBV) of the firm
(Wernerfelt, 1984; Barney, 1991) and the dynamic capabilities extension (Teece, Pisano, and Shuen, 1997), the
study constructs and tests a conceptual model in which entrepreneurial marketing orientation predicts both digital
marketing capabilities and marketing performance directly, and indirectly through those capabilities. The model is
tested using PLS-SEM on data from 312 Nigerian SME owner-managers drawn from Katsina and Kano states,
producing findings with theoretical relevance to the entrepreneurial marketing literature and practical relevance to
SME policy and practice in north-western Nigeria and, more broadly, in sub-Saharan Africa.
The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews the relevant literature and develops the
research hypotheses. Section 3 describes the research methodology. Section 4 presents the empirical results. Section
5 discusses the findings in relation to theory and practice. Section 6 addresses limitations and future research
directions, and Section 7 concludes.

disciplines. Its intellectual foundations lie in the work of Hills (1987), who drew systematic attention to the

marketing practices of small, growth-oriented firms, and Stokes (2000), who characterised entrepreneurial
marketing as innovation-led rather than market-led. Morris et al. (2002) subsequently developed the most widely
cited multidimensional model of the construct, encompassing innovativeness, risk-taking, proactiveness,
opportunity focus, customer intensity, resource leveraging, and value creation. The enduring influence of this model
is evidenced by its continued use in empirical studies across diverse contexts, from Jordan (Al-Rahamneh &
Castillo-Palacio, 2024) to Indonesia (Dermonde et al., 2024) to sub-Saharan Africa (Deku et al., 2024).
What distinguishes entrepreneurial marketing from its conventional counterpart is not primarily a matter of scale
but of orientation. Where conventional marketing proceeds from structured market research through segmentation
and positioning to the construction of a marketing mix, entrepreneurial marketing frequently begins with an
opportunity that the entrepreneur has identified, often before a clear market exists for the product or service in
question. The entrepreneur simultaneously shapes markets and responds to them. Collinson and Shaw (2001)
describe this as the dialectic between market orientation and entrepreneurial orientation, and Jones and Rowley
(2011) develop it further into a framework they term "EMICO," emphasising innovation, customer, intelligence,
opportunity, and communication as the constitutive dimensions of entrepreneurial marketing in small firms.
More recent scholarship has extended this framework in productive directions. Alqahtani and Uslay (2020)
conducted a comprehensive synthesis and conceptual development of the entrepreneurial marketing - firm
performance relationship, concluding that the construct requires disaggregation into its component dimensions if
performance effects are to be precisely identified. Alqahtani et al. (2022) subsequently developed and validated a
dedicated scale for entrepreneurial marketing that offers improved psychometric properties over earlier instruments.
In the digital era specifically, the adequacy of classical entrepreneurial marketing principles has also been
questioned. Conventional frameworks were developed under analogue conditions; their applicability to digitally
mediated market environments warrants empirical verification rather than assumption (Jones & Rowley, 2011;
Dermonde et al., 2024).
Within the Nigerian context, the available evidence suggests that entrepreneurial marketing practices are
widespread, if largely unarticulated. Gbadeyan (2010) observed that most small-firm owners in South-West Nigeria
relied heavily on word-of-mouth, personal selling, and trade associations as their primary marketing channels, a
pattern broadly consistent with the opportunity-driven and relationship-intensive logic of entrepreneurial marketing.
More recently, Afolabi et al. (2024) found that SMEs in Lagos and surrounding areas that combined informal
relational marketing with structured digital engagement demonstrated measurably stronger customer acquisition
and retention outcomes than those relying on either approach in isolation. This finding is consistent with the
theoretical proposition that entrepreneurial marketing orientation and digital capability are complementary rather
than competing orientations.
2.2 Concept of Digital Marketing Capabilities
Digital marketing capabilities are defined in this study as a firm's ability to organise and deploy digitally mediated
marketing resources to generate desired market outcomes (Day, 2011; Trainor et al., 2011). This definition, which
draws on Makadok's (2001) characterisation of organisational capabilities as enabling activities rather than mere
inputs, makes clear that the possession of digital tools is necessary but not sufficient for performance gains. What
matters is the firm's capacity to deploy those tools purposefully and adaptively in relation to its competitive context.
The taxonomy of digital marketing capabilities has evolved considerably over the past decade. Lamberton and
Stephen (2016) distinguished between owned, earned, and paid digital media capabilities. Kannan and Li (2017)
proposed a broader categorisation that includes digital customer engagement, data analytics, digital content
creation, and platform management, and their framework has been applied in diverse empirical settings. More
recently, Liang et al. (2025), drawing on data from Chinese listed manufacturing firms, demonstrated that the
performance effect of digital marketing capability is heterogeneous across capability dimensions, with data
analytics capability producing the most durable and least imitable performance advantage. Al Koliby et al. (2024)
similarly found that innovation capability and digital marketing capability jointly predict competitive performance
in emerging market SMEs, with interactive effects that exceed the sum of their individual contributions.

For the purposes of this study, digital marketing capabilities are operationalised along four dimensions considered
most relevant to the Nigerian SME context; social media marketing capability, search engine optimisation (SEO)
and search engine marketing (SEM) capability, digital content marketing capability, and data analytics capability.
Social media marketing capability refers to a firm's proficiency in using social platforms for brand awareness, lead
generation, and customer relationship management. In Nigeria, where Facebook had approximately 33 million
active users and Instagram and WhatsApp had established themselves as primary commercial channels by 2024,
this dimension is arguably the most immediate and practically consequential for SMEs (Nigerian Communications
Commission, 2024; Adeniyi et al., 2024). SEO and SEM capability encompasses the ability to optimise digital
presence for organic and paid search, an increasingly relevant skill as Nigerian consumers use search engines more
systematically for product discovery. Content marketing capability covers the creation, curation, and distribution of
brand-relevant digital content, while data analytics capability involves the collection, interpretation, and
deployment of customer and market data from digital channels for strategic decision-making.
The relatively low adoption of SEO and data analytics among Nigerian SMEs has been documented in recent
empirical work. Adeniyi et al. (2024) found that social media platforms dominated digital marketing adoption in
Lagos, with SEO and analytics tools recording the lowest uptake rates. This pattern is consistent with earlier
observations by Taiminen and Karjaluoto (2015) in Finnish SMEs, suggesting that the capability gap in analytics
and search optimisation is not unique to Nigeria but may be particularly acute in resource-constrained environments
where formal skills training and digital literacy infrastructure are less developed. Deku et al. (2024), in a study of
Ghanaian SMEs, further established that the relationship between digital marketing adoption and SME business
performance is moderated by environmental dynamism, implying that the value of digital marketing capabilities is
contingent on market conditions in ways that purely capability-focused models may understate.
2.3 Concept of Marketing Performance
Marketing performance is defined in this study as the degree to which a firm's marketing activities generate desired
outcomes relative to its competitive context and strategic objectives (Vorhies & Morgan, 2005). The measurement
of marketing performance in SME research is a contested and unresolved problem. Owner-managed firms
frequently conflate marketing outcomes with overall business performance, formal tracking systems are rare, and
financial records in the Nigerian context are inconsistently maintained (Clark, 1999; O'Sullivan & Abela, 2007).
These practical realities counsel a multi-dimensional measurement approach that captures both market-based
outcomes and financial indicators, calibrated to the informational realities of small enterprises.
Following Clark (1999) and O'Sullivan and Abela (2007), this study adopts a composite measure of marketing
performance that includes market-based outcomes (customer acquisition rate, market share perception, brand
awareness) and financial outcomes (revenue growth, customer retention, and return on marketing expenditure). This
dual approach is appropriate given that SME marketing performance is not adequately captured by financial
indicators alone, particularly in environments where informal transactions are common. The use of subjective,
perceptual performance measures is well-established in the SME literature where respondents cannot or will not
provide objective financial data (Wall et al., 2004; Boso et al., 2013), and the validity of such measures has been
empirically supported in a range of African SME contexts (Deku et al., 2024; Issau et al., 2022).
2.4 Resource-Based View and Dynamic Capabilities
The theoretical architecture of this study rests on the Resource-Based View (RBV) of the firm as developed by
Wernerfelt (1984) and refined by Barney (1991). RBV posits that sustained competitive advantage arises from the
deployment of resources that are valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-substitutable. Applied to the marketing domain,
the framework suggests that a firm's marketing capabilities, encompassing its customer knowledge, its relational
assets, and its ability to organise and deploy marketing activities effectively, constitute strategic resources capable
of generating durable performance advantages (Vorhies & Morgan, 2005; Day, 2011).
The dynamic capabilities extension (Teece et al., 1997; Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000) is particularly relevant to the
digital marketing context, where competitive conditions shift rapidly and static resource configurations quickly lose
their advantage-generating potential. Teece (2007) identifies three micro-foundations of dynamic capability:

2.5 Hypothesis Development
A positive relationship between entrepreneurial marketing orientation and marketing performance is among the
most consistently replicated findings in the entrepreneurial marketing literature. Becherer et al. (2012) demonstrated
that SMEs with stronger entrepreneurial marketing orientations achieved significantly higher sales growth and
customer acquisition rates than firms operating within conventional marketing frameworks. Tolossa et al. (2024)
more recently confirmed that competitive advantage mediates the entrepreneurial marketing - firm performance
relationship in SMEs across multiple sectors, suggesting that the performance benefits of entrepreneurial marketing
are partly indirect and operate through strategic positioning. In developing country contexts, Egwuonwu et al.
(2019) and Kibera and Waruingi (2016) documented similar patterns among African firms, and Al-Rahamneh and
Castillo-Palacio (2024) extended comparable evidence to the Jordanian services sector through competitive
aggressiveness as a further mediating mechanism.
H1: Entrepreneurial marketing orientation is positively associated with the marketing performance of SMEs in
Nigeria.
The relationship between digital marketing capabilities and firm performance is supported by a substantial and
growing literature. Taiminen and Karjaluoto (2015) established that SMEs with more developed digital marketing
capabilities reported higher customer engagement and stronger financial outcomes. Deku et al. (2024), drawing on
Ghanaian SME data, found that digital marketing adoption positively affects SME business performance, with a
positive moderating role for environmental dynamism. Liang et al. (2025) demonstrated that digital marketing
capability significantly predicts firm performance in manufacturing, with effects that vary across the capability
dimensions. In the Nigerian context specifically, recent empirical work confirms that SMEs using digital platforms
more intensively, particularly social media and content marketing tools, consistently outperform less digitally
capable counterparts on measures of revenue growth, customer reach, and brand visibility (Afolabi et al., 2024;
Ijomah et al., 2024).
H2: Digital marketing capabilities are positively associated with the marketing performance of SMEs in Nigeria.
It is proposed that the innovative, proactive, and opportunity-seeking characteristics of entrepreneurial marketing
orientation predispose SME operators to adopt and develop digital marketing capabilities more readily than their
conventionally oriented counterparts. Firms inclined toward creative resource leveraging and experimental
approaches to market engagement are more likely to invest time and effort in developing social media proficiency,
content creation skills, and data-driven decision-making practices. This proposition is consistent with the broader
argument in the dynamic capabilities literature that entrepreneurial orientation facilitates the development of
firm-specific capabilities in response to environmental opportunities (Teece, 2007; Dermonde et al., 2024). Al
Koliby et al. (2024) provide direct empirical support in an emerging market context, finding that entrepreneurial
culture significantly predicts digital marketing capability development among manufacturing SMEs.
H3: Entrepreneurial marketing orientation is positively associated with digital marketing capabilities in SMEs.
The most theoretically consequential hypothesis in this study concerns the mediating role of digital marketing
capabilities in the relationship between entrepreneurial marketing orientation and marketing performance. The
argument is that entrepreneurial marketing orientation generates superior performance not only through its direct
effects on marketing behaviour but also, and substantially, through its tendency to stimulate the development and

3. Research Methodology
3.1 Research Design
This study adopts a positivist, quantitative research design consistent with the deductive approach to hypothesis
testing. A cross-sectional survey was employed to test the proposed structural relationships at a single point in time.
While longitudinal designs offer stronger causal inference, the resource constraints common in Nigerian academic
research, combined with the exploratory state of this specific research intersection, justify the cross-sectional
approach. The study follows established conventions in SME marketing research in using self-reported, perceptual
measures from owner-managers, on the grounds that formal performance records are frequently unavailable in
small Nigerian enterprises (Boso et al., 2013; Egwuonwu et al., 2019). Respondent self-reports of this kind have
demonstrated acceptable validity when assessed against objective benchmarks in comparable settings (Wall et al.,
2004; Issau et al., 2022).
3.2 Sample and Data Collection
The target population comprised registered SMEs operating across two commercially active states in north-western
Nigeria: Katsina and Kano. These states were selected for their economic importance within the north-western
geopolitical zone, their relatively active informal and formal commercial sectors, and their representation of the
specific environmental and infrastructural conditions under investigation. Together, they account for a significant
share of registered SME activity in northern Nigeria. SMEs were defined in accordance with the updated SMEDAN
(2021) classification, which covers enterprises with between 10 and 199 employees and annual turnover between
five million and five hundred million naira. It should be noted that this classification differs modestly from the
threshold used in earlier Nigerian SME research, and future studies should ensure definitional consistency as the
regulatory framework continues to evolve.
The required minimum sample size was determined using Dillman's (2014) tailored design method formula for
finite populations: n = Np(1-p) / [(N-1)(d/z)2 + p(1-p)], where N represents the estimated population of registered
SMEs across both states (N = 64,579, derived from SMEDAN, 2021 administrative records), p = 0.50 (maximum
variability), d = 0.05 (margin of error), and z = 1.96 (at the 95 per cent confidence level). Applying these parameters
yielded a minimum required sample of 380, which was rounded to 400 so as to allow for proportionate allocation
across sectors. A stratified random sampling technique was then employed to ensure representation across four
industry sectors (retail and trade, professional services, manufacturing, and hospitality) and two firm-size categories
within each state. Structured questionnaires were administered exclusively to owner-managers or senior marketing
officers via an online survey platform (Google Forms). Links to the survey were distributed through registered SME
business associations, state-level SMEDAN offices, and relevant WhatsApp and Telegram trade group networks in
both states. Data collection took place over an eight-week period. Of 400 survey invitations distributed, 318
responses were received, of which 312 were deemed complete and usable after screening for straight-line response
patterns and missing data, yielding an effective usable response rate of 78 per cent. Non-response bias was assessed
by comparing early and late respondents on key demographic variables (firm age, sector, and employee size),
following the procedure recommended by Armstrong and Overton (1977). No significant differences were found
between the two groups (all p > 0.05), providing reasonable assurance that non-response bias does not materially
threaten the validity of the results. Sample characteristics are presented in Table 1.

3.3 Measurement Instruments
All constructs were measured using multi-item scales adapted from established instruments. Entrepreneurial
marketing orientation was assessed with a 20-item scale adapted from Morris et al. (2002), covering five
subdimensions: innovativeness, proactiveness, opportunity focus, customer intensity, and resource leveraging. This
instrument, while foundational, was supplemented with item refinements informed by the scale development work
of Alqahtani et al. (2022), whose validated instrument provides improved discriminant properties between the
innovativeness and proactiveness subdimensions. Digital marketing capabilities were measured using a 10-item
scale developed from Trainor et al. (2011), Taiminen and Karjaluoto (2015), and Day (2011), covering social media
marketing and data analytics. Marketing performance was assessed using an 8-item composite scale adapted from
Vorhies and Morgan (2005) and O'Sullivan and Abela (2007), spanning both market-based and financial
performance dimensions. All items were measured on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to
5 (strongly agree). The instruments were pre-tested with a convenience sample of 30 SME operators drawn from
both states to assess face validity and readability, and minor wording adjustments were made to six items before the
main data collection.
3.4 Method of Data Analysis
Data analysis proceeded in two stages. First, the measurement model was evaluated within a Partial Least Squares
Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM) framework using SmartPLS 4.0. PLS-SEM was preferred over
covariance-based SEM in view of its suitability for smaller samples, its tolerance of non-normal data distributions,
and its capacity for formative measurement models, all of which are characteristic of SME research in developing
economies (Hair et al., 2017). Convergent validity was assessed through Average Variance Extracted (AVE) and
composite reliability. Discriminant validity was evaluated using the Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratio, which is
now widely preferred over the Fornell-Larcker criterion in PLS research given its superior sensitivity to
discriminant validity failures (Henseler et al., 2015; Hair et al., 2017; 2021). Second, the structural model was
tested to estimate path coefficients and the significance of direct and indirect (mediated) effects using 5,000
bootstrap subsamples, consistent with the recommendations of Preacher and Hayes (2008) for mediation analysis in
survey-based research.
Common method variance (CMV) was assessed and mitigated through procedural and statistical means.
Procedurally, the questionnaire separated predictor and outcome items with an intervening section and used varied
scale formats where feasible, following Podsakoff et al. (2003). Statistically, Harman's single-factor test was

effect of digital marketing capabilities was large (f2 = 0.331), while the direct effect of entrepreneurial marketing

5. Discussion
5.1 Entrepreneurial Marketing Orientation and Marketing Performance
The finding that entrepreneurial marketing orientation (EMO) exerts a significant positive direct effect on
marketing performance (beta = 0.236, p < 0.001) supports Hypothesis 1 and aligns with the broader entrepreneurial
marketing literature (Morris et al., 2002; Becherer et al., 2012; Alqahtani & Uslay, 2020). This result extends
previous evidence from other African contexts, such as South Africa (Egwuonwu et al., 2019), Ghana (Boso et al.,
2013), and Kenya (Kibera & Waruingi, 2016), to the under-researched north-western Nigerian SME setting.
Notably, the magnitude of the direct effect is relatively modest compared with some prior studies. This pattern
becomes theoretically meaningful when viewed alongside the strong mediation result: a substantial proportion of
EMO's performance benefits operates indirectly through digital marketing capabilities. In resource-constrained
environments characterised by informal markets and limited formal infrastructure, entrepreneurial behaviours such
as opportunity recognition, customer intensity, and resource leveraging appear to generate value primarily by
enabling SMEs to identify, adopt, and creatively deploy digital tools. The high loading of the customer intensity
dimension within the EMO construct further underscores the continued relevance of relational, trust-based
marketing practices in Nigeria, where institutional voids make personal relationships a critical source of
competitive advantage (Issau et al., 2022; Nwosu, 2013).
5.2 Digital Marketing Capabilities as Drivers of Marketing Performance
The strong positive relationship between digital marketing capabilities (DMC) and marketing performance (beta =
0.603, p < 0.001) provides robust support for Hypothesis 2 and represents one of the study's most pronounced
findings. This effect size substantially exceeds that of the direct EMO-performance path, highlighting the pivotal
role of actual digital execution capabilities in translating entrepreneurial intent into measurable market outcomes.
This result is consistent with emerging evidence from both developed and developing economies (Taiminen &
Karjaluoto, 2015; Trainor et al., 2011; Deku et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2025). Within the Nigerian context, the
dominance of social media marketing capability (with the highest sub-dimension loadings) reflects the realities of
the local digital ecosystem: high mobile penetration, widespread use of WhatsApp Business, Instagram, and
Facebook, and relatively lower adoption of more technically demanding tools such as SEO and advanced analytics
(Adeniyi et al., 2024; Nigerian Communications Commission, 2024). Social media platforms serve multiple
functions simultaneously, customer service channel, and brand-building tool, making proficiency in this area
particularly impactful for SMEs operating with limited resources.
Conversely, the comparatively lower loading of data analytics capability (0.67) points to a persistent capability gap.
Most sampled SMEs rely on rudimentary, platform-generated metrics rather than systematic data-driven
decision-making. This finding echoes broader concerns about digital literacy and skills deficits in Nigerian SMEs

5.3 The Mediating Role of Digital Marketing Capabilities
The partial mediation established in Hypothesis 4 constitutes the study's most substantive theoretical contribution.
Digital marketing capabilities account for approximately 67.9% of the total effect of entrepreneurial marketing
orientation on marketing performance (indirect effect beta = 0.500, p < 0.001), while the direct path remains
significant. This result clarifies the mechanism through which EMO generates performance benefits in this context
and directly responds to calls for greater attention to mediating processes in entrepreneurial marketing research
(Alqahtani & Uslay, 2020).
From a dynamic capabilities perspective (Teece et al., 1997; Teece, 2007), entrepreneurial marketing orientation
functions as a higher-order capability that facilitates the sensing of market opportunities and the seizing of those
opportunities through the reconfiguration of digital resources. In the north-western Nigerian setting marked by
informal market structures, infrastructural constraints, and rapid digitalization, EMO appears to operate most
powerfully by stimulating the development and adaptive deployment of digital marketing capabilities. The
complementarity between the two constructs is evident: entrepreneurial behaviours provide the strategic intent and
resourcefulness, while digital capabilities supply the operational mechanisms for execution.
Importantly, the persistence of a significant direct effect after mediation suggests that EMO retains unique value
that cannot be fully substituted by digital tools. Elements such as personal relationship-building, contextual market
judgement, and creative resource leveraging remain essential in an environment where formal institutions are weak
and consumer trust is often built through direct, face-to-face interactions (Afolabi et al., 2024; Nwosu, 2013). This
finding supports the view that entrepreneurial marketing and digital capabilities function as complementary rather
than substitutive strategic resources.
5.4 Theoretical and Practical Implications
Theoretically, this study advances the marketing-entrepreneurship interface by demonstrating how RBV and
dynamic capabilities frameworks apply in sub-Saharan African SME contexts. It provides empirical evidence that
digital marketing capabilities serve as a critical bridging mechanism between entrepreneurial orientation and
performance outcomes, thereby enriching the limited body of knowledge on entrepreneurial marketing in digitally
transitioning emerging markets.
Practically, the results offer clear guidance for SME owner-managers operating in similar contexts. Success
increasingly depends on the synergistic integration of entrepreneurial mindsets with digital execution skills.
Owner-managers should therefore treat digital capability development not as a peripheral activity but as a core
strategic priority, beginning with social media proficiency and progressively building toward more advanced
analytics capabilities. At the policy level, interventions that focus solely on providing digital tools or infrastructure
are likely to yield limited returns unless they simultaneously cultivate the entrepreneurial orientation needed to
deploy those tools effectively. Support programmes should therefore adopt an integrated approach that combines
digital skills training with modules on opportunity recognition, customer intensity, and resourceful marketing
practices.
These insights are particularly timely given Nigeria's growing digital economy and the persistent challenge of SME
mortality. By illuminating the dominant mediated pathway, the study suggests that targeted investments in the
EMO-DMC nexus have the potential to significantly improve marketing performance and, by extension, the
survival and growth prospects of SMEs in north-western Nigeria and comparable emerging market settings.

6. Conclusion
This study establishes that entrepreneurial marketing orientation (EMO) significantly enhances marketing
performance among SMEs in north-western Nigeria, both directly and indirectly through digital marketing
capabilities (DMC). Using PLS-SEM on data from 312 SME owner-managers in Katsina and Kano states, the
analysis reveals a strong path from EMO to DMC (beta = 0.828), with DMC emerging as the dominant driver of
marketing performance (beta = 0.603). Digital marketing capabilities partially mediate the relationship, accounting
for 67.9% of the indirect effect.
Theoretically, the findings advance the Resource-Based View and dynamic capabilities perspective by
demonstrating that EMO functions as a higher-order capability that enables SMEs to sense opportunities and
reconfigure digital resources into competitive assets in resource-constrained, informally structured markets. This
addresses a key gap in the entrepreneurial marketing literature regarding the mechanisms linking orientation to
performance, particularly in sub-Saharan African contexts.
Practically, the results highlight that superior marketing performance arises from the synergy between
entrepreneurial mindsets and digital execution capabilities. In Nigeria's rapidly digitalising economy, neither EMO
nor DMC alone suffices; their combination offers the strongest pathway to improved customer acquisition,
retention, and revenue growth.
7. Recommendations
For SME practitioners, owner-managers should cultivate entrepreneurial behaviours especially customer intensity
and opportunity recognition while prioritising digital marketing capabilities. Short-term focus should be on
strengthening social media proficiency (WhatsApp Business, Instagram, Facebook) for customer engagement and
lead generation. Longer-term efforts should target data analytics skills to build more sustainable advantages.
For policymakers and support organisations, interventions must integrate entrepreneurial marketing training with
practical digital capability development rather than focusing solely on infrastructure or tools. Targeted programmes
should address sector-specific needs, reduce barriers such as data costs and digital literacy gaps, and promote
complementary use of relational and digital marketing practices.
By leveraging the EMO-DMC nexus, Nigerian SMEs can better convert digital opportunities into sustained
marketing performance, contributing to improved survival rates and economic resilience in north-western Nigeria
and similar emerging markets. Future research using longitudinal designs, multi-regional samples, and emerging
technologies such as AI will further enrich this understanding.
8. Limitations and Future Research Directions
This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its cross-sectional design limits causal
inference; longitudinal studies are needed to establish temporal relationships among entrepreneurial marketing
orientation, digital marketing capabilities, and marketing performance. Second, reliance on self-reported perceptual
data from owner-managers raises potential common method variance, although procedural and statistical remedies
were applied. Future studies could employ multi-informant designs or objective performance measures where
feasible.
Third, the sample was restricted to SMEs in Katsina and Kano states, limiting generalisability to other regions of
Nigeria with different digital infrastructure and market conditions. Multi-regional comparative studies would help
determine whether the observed relationships hold across diverse contexts. Fourth, digital marketing capabilities
were examined as a composite construct; disaggregating the analysis into specific dimensions (social media,
SEO/SEM, content marketing, and data analytics) could yield more granular insights.
Finally, the study did not explore potential moderating effects of firm age, industry sector, owner digital literacy, or
environmental dynamism. Future research should also investigate the role of emerging technologies such as
artificial intelligence (AI) in shaping digital marketing capabilities and their interaction with entrepreneurial
marketing orientation in Nigerian SMEs.
