Abstract
User-generated content (UGC) has become increasingly popular among tourists, largely due to its
widespread circulation on social media; however, its inﬂuence on user perceptions and behaviors has
received limited academic attention. Drawing on grounded theory and three-level coding, this study
systematically develops a conceptual framework of UGC popularity based on semi-structured interviews
with 17 active social media users on RedNote (Xiaohongshu). The analysis identiﬁed 10 categories,
which were further integrated into three overarching dimensions: attractiveness (data indicator and
content value), relevance (content characteristics, platform operation mechanisms, user interaction,
account attributes, and external environment), and evaluation (destination cognition, travel decision, and
trust perception). These categories are intricately connected and reﬂect the underlying logic of the three
dimensions. By clarifying the meaning and constituent elements of UGC popularity, this study
establishes a theoretical basis for future quantitative research in social media marketing. It offers
practical insights for destination marketing organizations, content creators, and platform operators.
1. Introduction
Driven by the rapid advancement of mobile internet and digital technologies, social media has become
embedded in daily life and has reshaped individual consumption decisions. In this digitally connected
social ecosystem, user-generated content (UGC) has emerged as a core engine of content production
that inﬂuences how users access information, form attitudes, and shape behavioral intentions (Kaplan &
Haenlein, 2010). UGC Popularity is widely used in academic and business contexts to assess content
performance. It is generally understood as the degree of user engagement with voluntarily created and
shared content across digital platforms in diverse formats, including text, images, and videos. Prior
studies commonly measure popularity using observable interaction metrics such as likes, comments,
shares, and saves (Revathi, 2024); however, these indicators may oversimplify popularity by emphasizing
surface behaviors while overlooking the social, psychological, and technological drivers of engagement.
Research increasingly conceptualizes UGC popularity as multidimensional. Content that elicits strong
emotions is more likely to go viral (Berger & Milkman, 2012). In a systematic review, Woods (2023)
summarized the research landscape of social media popularity, spanning consumer behavior effects to
content diffusion mechanisms, and classiﬁed inﬂuencing factors into two categories: behavioral
strategies (e.g., emotional expression, visual design, posting frequency) and social structural conditions
(e.g., follower size, resource support, identity veriﬁcation). Furthermore, Zhuang et al. (2025) emphasized
that UGC popularity is jointly affected by information quality and source credibility, with text length,
emotional intensity, and media richness as critical determinants. Collectively, UGC popularity is a
multidimensional construct shaped by content, emotion, social context, and platform-related factors.
However, UGC popularity is still often treated as a unidimensional, indicator-based construct, and its
conceptual deﬁnition remains underdeveloped. Moreover, existing research tends to emphasize
platform- and algorithm-centered explanations while giving less attention to users’ subjective

perceptions and cognitive interpretations of what constitutes “popular” UGC. This ambiguity limits
theoretical advancement and weakens the practical application of UGC insights in marketing and
platform governance. To address these gaps, this study uses RedNote (Xiaohongshu) as the research
context and conducts semi-structured interviews to conceptualize UGC popularity and its core
dimensions. The study develops a theoretically grounded framework and provides actionable
implications for destination marketing organizations, content creators, and platform operators.
2. Literature review
2.1 User-generated content
UGC is conceptualized as a heterogeneous set of media content (e.g., text, images, videos, and
comments) created, edited, and shared by end users rather than professional content producers or
commercial entities (Martí-Parreño et al., 2015). Compared with brand-generated content (BGC), which is
often guided by commercial objectives, UGC is characterized by grassroots production, spontaneous
dissemination, and a user-centric value orientation. Prior research commonly highlights three core
characteristics that underpin UGC’s inﬂuence: interactivity, authenticity, and perceived expertise.
Interactivity enables two-way communication between content creators and audiences and supports
peer-to-peer interaction among users (Li et al., 2025). Through likes, comments, and shares, interactivity
increases user involvement, strengthens community ties, and supports platform retention. It also
contributes to the iterative development of UGC, as feedback can shape creators’ subsequent content.
Authenticity is widely recognized as UGC’s key advantage over BGC. Since it is grounded in personal
experience and voluntary sharing, UGC is perceived as less commercially manipulated and therefore
more credible (Levallois, 2024). This perceived authenticity directly impacts users’ attitudinal and
behavioral responses, including their trust in information, willingness to adopt recommendations, and
subsequent consumption decisions. However, recent research has highlighted the conditional nature of
this authenticity: when users perceive hidden commercial intent (e.g., disguised promotions), credibility
declines (Werenowska & Jaska, 2025).
Perceived expertise and content quality further extend UGC’s value proposition. When creators possess
domain-speciﬁc knowledge, practical experience, or professional skills, their content can rival that
produced by professional institutions (Li et al., 2025). In turn, users rely on peer-generated content for
practical guidance and specialized insights. Content quality, such as visual design, narrative clarity, and
technical execution, also shapes UGC’s functional, emotional, and social value (Kim et al., 2012).
In addition to these inherent attributes, Hsiao and Wang (2015) argue that the social and psychological
drivers of UGC production reﬂect a socially embedded process shaped by social comparison and social
identity. High engagement with others’ posts can create comparison pressure that motivates more
frequent and reﬁned content creation, thus reinforcing a self-perpetuating cycle within social networks.

2.2 Theoretical frameworks for understanding ugc and its
popularity
Classic communication models provide a foundation for explaining information diffusion in UGCs.
Lasswell’s (1948) model breaks communication into ﬁve components: communicator, message,
medium, audience, and effect, addressing the fundamental question of “who says what, through which
channel, to whom, and with what effect” (Ouirdi et al., 2014). This model provides a framework for
analyzing the roles of UGC creators (communicators), content (messages), social media platforms
(mediums), users (audiences), and behavioral outcomes (effects).
The Shannon-Weaver model (1949) extends this framework by emphasizing information transmission
and introducing “noise” to describe interference factors that may distort messages during dissemination
(Nag, 2024). In social media contexts, algorithmic ﬁltering, information overload, and competing content
can function as noise that shapes UGC reach and impact. Additionally, the two-step ﬂow theory
(Lazarsfeld et al., 1944) highlights the mediating role of opinion leaders, suggesting that UGC often
inﬂuences opinion leaders, who then transmit this information to broader audiences (Gangadharbatla &
Valafar, 2017).
Uses and gratiﬁcations theory (UGT) offers a user-centered explanation of engagement, arguing that
users actively select and interact with media to meet speciﬁc requirements rather than passively
receiving information (Ruggiero, 2000). On social media, these needs include information acquisition
(e.g., travel guides), entertainment (e.g., visually appealing content), and social interaction (e.g., peer
discussion). Du et al. (2024) further demonstrate that algorithmic recommendations aligned with user
interests strengthen these gratiﬁcations and sustain engagement with popular UGC, positioning
popularity as a signal of content relevance to user needs.
Social identity theory (SIT) explains herd behavior and community dynamics in the formation of
popularity. SIT argues that individuals seek social validation by aligning with group norms, particularly in
uncertain situations (Tajfel & Turner, 1979). On social media, UGC popularity indicators such as likes,
comments, and shares can signal perceived consensus and a reinforcing feedback loop: high popularity
attracts more engagement, which in turn enhances popularity (Yang et al., 2019). High-popularity UGC
may reﬂect community norms, enabling users to strengthen group identity through interactive behaviors.
Users may also adjust interaction strategies (e.g., using likes rather than commenting in negative
discussion contexts) to maintain social identity boundaries and avoid conﬂict (Yang et al., 2019).
Information adoption theory (IAT) focuses on how users evaluate and adopt UGC as information. IAT
identiﬁes two determinants: content quality and source credibility (Zhuang et al., 2023). Content quality
is shaped by attributes such as detail, readability, and objectivity, while source credibility is inﬂuenced by
the creator’s expertise, social recognition, and perceived independence from commercial interests. Kim
and Song (2020) ﬁnd that perceived advertising reduces trust, especially for experiential information.
Khobzi and Lau (2017) further note that negative comments may not reduce UGC popularity. Some
Page 4/27

highly endorsed negative comments can create “controversial popularity”; this phenomenon may
suppress likes but increase shares, which reﬂects the complex emotional dynamics of UGC engagement.
2.3 UGC popularity: Conceptualization, antecedents, and
measurement
Despite growing interest in UGC popularity, a uniﬁed conceptual deﬁnition and measurement framework
remain limited. Popularity generally refers to the state of being widely recognized and endorsed by a
large group (Cillessen & Marks, 2011; Shin, 2020). In the UGC context, popularity refers to the extent to
which user-created content attracts attention, generates engagement, and spreads across social media
platforms. Existing research mainly examines what drives UGC popularity and how it is measured, but
important conceptual and methodological gaps persist.
Studies typically group antecedents of UGC popularity into content, creator, and contextual factors. At
the content level, features such as text length, emotional intensity, entertainment value, and practicality
inﬂuence popularity, often depending on content type (Liu-Thompkins & Rogerson, 2012). Emotionally
evocative content is more likely to go viral (Berger & Milkman, 2012), whereas practical information
tends to generate sustained engagement. At the creator level, professional identity, niche positioning,
and alignment with trends can increase visibility and reach (Lu & Zheng, 2025; Zhuang et al., 2025).
Social ties and peer interaction further facilitate sharing and popularity increases (Hsiao & Wang, 2015).
At the contextual level, platform algorithms can amplify popularity and exposure bias by extending the
visibility of already popular content and shaping user perceptions (Bartley et al., 2021). Popularity can
also be event-driven and time-sensitive, with short-term “buzz” and longer discussion cycles (Deusser et
al., 2018), and it may vary with external conditions such as holidays and tourism promotions (Tsiakali,
2018).
Most studies operationalize UGC popularity using unidimensional, quantitative metrics such as likes,
comments, shares, and saves (Revathi, 2024). While these indicators are intuitive and easy to measure,
they may not capture users’ perceptions or the conditions under which popularity translates into
inﬂuence. Evidence of heterogeneous user responses suggests that popularity effects are conditional on
user identity, trust patterns, and involvement levels (Beurer-Zuellig & Klaas, 2020). Recent advances in
popularity measurement have shifted from static cross-sectional indicators to modeling popularity as a
dynamic trajectory, including the detection of emerging topics, classiﬁcation of growth patterns, and
time-series prediction (Garroppo et al., 2018; Reed et al., 2011; Xu et al., 2025). In addition, Woods (2023)
argues that existing measurement approaches often overlook structural constraints (e.g., race, class,
and gender) that affect the distribution of UGC popularity, and these gaps may carry oﬄine inequalities
into online spaces.
In tourism, UGC popularity is closely tied to electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM) because consumers rely
heavily on peer-generated content for experience-based decisions (Litvin et al., 2008). Research
distinguishes “naturally generated” eWOM (organic UGC) from “marketing-generated” eWOM
(commercial UGC) and cautions that disguised promotional content may increase short-term popularity
Page 5/27

but undermine user trust (Tsiakali, 2018). A loss of trust can lead to negative secondary dissemination,
thus highlighting the importance of maintaining UGC’s perceived authenticity in tourism marketing.
3. Methodology
3.1 Methods
This study aims to investigate the conceptual meanings, key dimensions, and primary characteristics of
UGC popularity on social media. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews, and the
ﬁndings were analyzed using grounded theory with thematic analysis. Grounded theory is particularly
well-suited for exploratory research because it supports concept identiﬁcation and theory development
from empirical data when established frameworks are limited (Charmaz & Thornberg, 2021). Consistent
with thematic analysis procedures, the study followed six steps: familiarizing oneself with the data,
generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, deﬁning and naming themes, and
producing the report (Braun & Clark, 2006).
Initially, a comprehensive literature search was conducted on Scopus using keywords such as user-
generated content, social media popularity, destination marketing, and user perception. This literature
review outlined the current research landscape, clariﬁed key concepts, and established a preliminary
analytical framework. This framework provided a theoretical foundation for designing the interview guide
and served as a point of reference for subsequent coding and theme development.
Given its distinctive communication culture and high conversion rate, RedNote was selected as the
research context for examining UGC popularity. A total of 17 active RedNote users participated in in-
depth interviews, including 15 primary interviews and two additional interviews to conﬁrm theoretical
saturation. The interviews centered on users’ perceptions of UGC popularity, the factors contributing to
its formation, and its perceived impacts.
Lastly, interview transcripts were imported into NVivo 15 Plus for data management and coding.
Analysis proceeded through iterative coding and constant comparison, followed by theme development
and interpretive explanation. Drawing on uses and gratiﬁcations theory and social identity theory, an
initial coding framework organized the analysis around three elements: phenomenon recognition, cause
exploration, and inﬂuence and perception. Grounded theory emphasizes the construction of theory from
empirical data (Thornberg & Charmaz, 2014). By integrating grounded theory with thematic analysis, this
study identiﬁed the core dimensions and characteristics of UGC popularity and developed a theoretical
model of the construct.
3.2 Data collection
This study collected data from active users of the RedNote platform, tourism content creators, and
online marketing personnel from travel agencies. It centered on key research topics, including
perceptions of popularity, its causes, and effects. RedNote is a vital hub for tourism-related UGC, with its
Page 6/27

interactive environment and the evolution of content popularity serving as crucial contexts for examining
the dissemination of social media content and the dynamics of destination marketing. By collecting data
on RedNote, the study captured users’ perceptions and evaluative logic concerning popularity. This, in
turn, provided empirical support to develop a theoretical framework with practical relevance.
Furthermore, this study offers targeted insights for destination marketing, content creation, and platform
management.
The main data collection method involved semi-structured interviews conducted both online via audio
calls and face-to-face. A total of 17 participants were included, with 15 participating in primary
interviews to gather essential data and two additional participants (i.e., A12 and A17) interviewed to
conﬁrm theoretical saturation. Respondents were selected based on three criteria: participants used the
platform at least three times a week to demonstrate familiarity with the platform’s ecosystem; they had
published or engaged with tourism-related UGC in the past year, thereby possessing practical experience
in perceiving popularity; and their demographic characteristics spanned the age range of 20 to 40, while
also considering a diverse user group with varying levels of UGC creation experience. This approach
supported sample diversity and representativeness. Table 1 presents an overview of the interview

3.3 Data analysis
This study systematically organized and analyzed the interview data. Coding followed the three-stage
grounded theory procedures: open coding, axial coding, and selective coding.
1. Open coding: The interview transcripts were analyzed line by line to identify initial concepts related
to UGC popularity directly from participants’ interview statements. For example, terms such as
Page 10/27

“number of likes” and “number of saves” were identiﬁed as ﬁrst-level codes, supporting
comprehensive coverage of the raw data.
2. Axial coding: Based on the logical linkages among concepts, the initial codes were compared and
synthesized into second-level codes. For example, “cover visual appeal” and “title relevance” were
grouped under the category of “content characteristics”.
3. Selective coding: Second-level categories were then integrated around the study’s core concerns to
form a third-level coding system.
To enhance analytical rigor and reliability, the coding process incorporated iterative veriﬁcation of the
alignment between interview statements and assigned codes. As for theoretical saturation, analysis of
the ﬁrst 15 interviews revealed no emergence of new concepts, categories, or dimensions, which led to
the establishment of a preliminary framework. To further conﬁrm saturation, two additional interviews
(A12 and A17) were analyzed as validation cases. The analysis indicated that no new categories or
dimensions emerged, suggesting that the existing coding structure adequately captured the essential
information in the additional data. Consequently, the theoretical framework was conﬁrmed, and data
collection was concluded. Figure 1 presents a word cloud visualization of the interview data.

4.4 Conceptual framework of UGC popularity
Based on the grounded theory analysis presented, this study proposes a conceptual framework for
understanding UGC popularity (see Fig. 2). This framework conceptualizes UGC popularity as a dynamic
phenomenon on social media platforms, shaped by the collective actions of various stakeholders,
including users, platforms, creators, and external factors. At the core of the model, UGC popularity is
represented through three interrelated dimensions: attractiveness, evaluation, and relevance. Data
indicators and content value function as key informational bases through which UGC popularity is
manifested and assessed. The framework speciﬁes how UGC popularity affects users' behavior by
inﬂuencing their destination cognition, travel decisions, and trust perception. The framework identiﬁes
antecedent conditions that shape UGC popularity within the platform environment: content
characteristics, platform operation mechanisms, user interaction behaviors, account attribute inﬂuences,
and external environments. Overall, this conceptual framework depicts UGC popularity as a
multidimensional construct within a broader social media ecosystem.
5. Discussion and conclusion
5.1 Discussion
This study employs grounded theory to develop a comprehensive framework for understanding UGC
popularity on social media. The framework integrates three dimensions that explain how users perceive,
evaluate, and respond to UGC popularity: (1) Attractiveness reﬂects users’ dual cognitive approach to
UGC popularity; (2) Relevance reveals the multifaceted mechanisms behind the formation of UGC
popularity; and (3) Evaluation clariﬁes how UGC popularity inﬂuences user cognition and behavior.
First, Attractiveness integrates objective metrics and subjective value. The attractiveness dimension,
which includes data indicators and content value, enhances the current understanding of UGC popularity
Page 17/27

measurement. Prior studies have predominantly relied on overt interaction metrics (likes, comments,
saves) as proxies for popularity (Revathi, 2024). Still, this study demonstrates that users’ assessments
of popularity are dualistic: they ﬁrst rely on quantitative data as a quick heuristic, but ultimately judge
popularity’s “meaningfulness” through content value (practicality, authenticity, emotional resonance).
This aligns with Berger and Milkman’s (2012) argument that content resonance, not just quality, drives
virality, but further speciﬁes that resonance is rooted in both emotional and functional value. For
example, respondents emphasized that “authentic, non-advertising-laden content” (A09) and “substantial
practical help” (A01) are as critical to perceived popularity as high like counts. This ﬁnding enriches uses
and gratiﬁcations theory by showing that users’ engagement with popular UGC is motivated by both
informational and emotional gratiﬁcations (Du et al., 2024), while also highlighting the conditional nature
of authenticity—a factor previously noted to be non-universal (Werenowska & Jaska, 2025).
Second, Relevance from a multi-stakeholder framework of popularity formation. The relevance
dimension identiﬁes ﬁve interrelated categories (content characteristics, platform operation
mechanisms, user interaction, account attributes, external environment) that collectively shape UGC
popularity, addressing the literature’s overemphasis on platform/algorithm-centered explanations
(Bartley et al., 2021; Deusser et al., 2018) and neglect of user and contextual factors. Content
characteristics emerged as the foundational determinant, consistent with Liu-Thompkins and Rogerson’s
(2012) ﬁnding that content value (entertainment, education) drives dissemination; this study further
speciﬁes key attributes: cover visual appeal, title alignment with trends, and information timeliness.
Notably, respondents highlighted “content controversy” as a driver of comment volume, echoing Khobzi
and Lau’s (2017) observation of “controversial popularity” but extending it to tourism UGC contexts.
Platform operation mechanisms, including algorithmic recommendations and secondary ﬂow, conﬁrm
Bartley et al.’s (2021) observation of “popularity bias” but add nuance: users are aware of algorithmic
inﬂuences (e.g., “likes increase exposure,” A08) and even strategically adapt content (e.g., “editing viral
posts for secondary ﬂow,” A15) to sustain popularity. User interaction, meanwhile, is framed as a
“sustaining force” rather than a mere outcome of popularity, aligning with Hsiao and Wang’s (2015)
emphasis on social relational networks in UGC creation. Account attributes (nichedness,
professionalism) and external environments (cultural tourism promotion, holiday demand) further
expand the antecedents of UGC popularity, ﬁlling gaps in prior research that focused on content or
creator factors in isolation (Zhuang et al., 2025; Lu & Zheng, 2025).
Third, the evaluation focuses on the causal pathway from popularity to user outcomes. It clariﬁes how
UGC popularity inﬂuences destination cognition, travel decisions, and trust perception—addressing the
literature’s limited focus on the consequences of popularity (Litvin et al., 2008; Tsiakali, 2018). High
popularity evokes emotional appeal (e.g., “longing for the destination,” A10) and improves information
acquisition eﬃciency, enhancing destination cognition.. This aligns with social identity theory: popularity
signals a “community consensus” (Yang et al., 2019), prompting users to align their perceptions with the
collective. However, the study also identiﬁes a novel “reverse selection” phenomenon: some users avoid
overly popular destinations to escape crowds (A09), suggesting that popularity’s effects are not uniform
Page 18/27

—a ﬁnding supported by Beurer-Zuellig and Klaas’s (2020) observation of heterogeneous user responses
to UGC.
Regarding trust perception, popularity serves as a credibility endorsement (A08: “high-popularity notes
are the result of many people’s choices”), but users also scrutinize comment authenticity (A14: “positive
comments indicate authenticity”). This reconciles conﬂicting ﬁndings in the literature: while Zhuang et al.
(2023) emphasize source credibility as a determinant of information adoption, this study shows that
popularity enhances trust only when paired with perceived authenticity, thereby avoiding the “trust
collapse” risk of disguised promotional content (Tsiakali, 2018).
5.2 Theoretical implications
The primary contribution of this study is clarifying the multifaceted nature of user-generated content
(UGC) popularity, which has often been oversimpliﬁed in previous research as merely a collection of
quantitative metrics (Revathi, 2024; Beurer-Zuellig & Klaas, 2020). The proposed three-dimensional
framework—comprising attractiveness, relevance, and evaluation—demonstrates that UGC popularity is
not merely a static representation of interaction data but a dynamic construct shaped by the interplay
among content attributes, platform mechanisms, user behaviors, and external contexts. This interplay
has signiﬁcant implications for user cognition and decision-making. This ﬁnding effectively addresses a
critical gap in the literature: the absence of a systematic deﬁnition and conceptualization of UGC
popularity that integrates both objective indicators and subjective user perceptions (Woods, 2023;
Zhuang et al., 2025).
This study integrates multiple theoretical frameworks—uses and gratiﬁcations theory, social identity
theory, and information adoption theory—to advance understanding of how UGC popularity inﬂuences
users' cognition and decision-making, addressing the literature’s limited focus on the consequences of
popularity (Litvin et al., 2008; Tsiakali, 2018). The evaluation dimension (destination cognition, travel
decision, trust perception) clariﬁes the causal pathways through which popularity shapes user
outcomes, reconciling conﬂicting ﬁndings in existing research and providing a more comprehensive
theoretical explanation.
From the perspective of uses and gratiﬁcations theory, the study enriches understanding by showing that
users’ engagement with popular UGC is motivated by both informational (e.g., itinerary planning,
information acquisition eﬃciency) and emotional (e.g., emotional appeal activation) gratiﬁcations (Du et
al., 2024). This extends the theory by demonstrating that popularity cues enhance these gratiﬁcations, as
users perceive popular content as more valuable and credible. From the lens of social identity theory, the
study conﬁrms that popularity signals a “community consensus” (Yang et al., 2019), prompting users to
align their perceptions with the collective. However, it also identiﬁes a novel “reverse selection”
phenomenon—some users avoid overly popular destinations—highlighting the heterogeneity of user
responses and challenging the assumption that popularity uniformly inﬂuences behavior (Beurer-Zuellig
& Klaas, 2020).
Page 19/27

Regarding information adoption theory, the study reconciles conﬂicting ﬁndings by showing that
popularity enhances trust only when paired with perceived authenticity (Zhuang et al., 2023). It shows
that users do not blindly trust popular content; instead, they examine the authenticity of comments and
the value of the content, steering clear of the "trust collapse" risk related to disguised promotional
material (Tsiakali, 2018). This integration of theoretical perspectives provides a more nuanced
understanding of the psychological mechanisms underlying popularity’s inﬂuence, bridging gaps
between different streams of UGC research.
Lastly, based on the proposed framework, the study identiﬁes a triangular relationship among the
company, community, and customer engaged in social media marketing (see Fig. 3). Building on the
three-dimensional UGC popularity framework, this study proposes a “UGC popularity triangle marketing”
model that identiﬁes a dynamic triangular relationship among companies, communities, and customers
in social media marketing. This theoretical contribution advances social media marketing theory by
moving beyond traditional dyadic models (company-customer) to a holistic framework that emphasizes
the role of community in mediating the relationship between UGC popularity and marketing outcomes.
The “company” plays an essential role in driving UGC popularity by creating engaging materials and
connecting with its audience. It focuses on establishing a strong brand identity while delivering valuable
content that aligns with its core values. To accomplish this, companies should cultivate a distinctive
UGC presence characterized by a unique voice and persona, maintain consistent messaging across
platforms, and share behind-the-scenes content to humanize the brand and strengthen connections with
the audience.
The “community” involves followers, fans, and advocates who contribute to UGC popularity. It
emphasizes the importance of relationships and ongoing member engagement. To nurture this
community, brands can facilitate discussions and interactions through polls, questions, and comments;
create community-driven content featuring user-generated submissions; and organize events or live
sessions to reinforce connections among members.
The “customer” refers to end users or consumers of a company’s products or services. It underscores
the importance of understanding customer needs, preferences, and behaviors to effectively tailor
marketing strategies that leverage UGC popularity. To accomplish this, companies can monitor UGC
popularity through surveys and social listening tools, provide personalized experiences based on
customer data, and respond promptly to inquiries and feedback. These actions demonstrate that
customer voices are valued and acknowledged.
The triangle framework conceptualizes companies as drivers of UGC popularity (creating valuable
content, building brand identity), communities as facilitators (engaging with content, fostering social
connections), and customers as recipients and co-creators (interpreting popularity cues, making
decisions). This model illustrates how the interaction among these three stakeholders creates a self-
reinforcing engagement loop: content generated by the company resonates with communities, drives
Page 20/27

customer engagement, and boosts the popularity of user-generated content (UGC), while feedback from
customers and communities reﬁnes future content strategies.
This theoretical model ﬁlls a gap in social media marketing research by highlighting the central role of
UGC popularity in connecting companies, communities, and customers. It provides a theoretical basis
for understanding how UGC can be leveraged to build brand loyalty and drive growth, extending prior
research on UGC marketing (Martí-Parreño et al., 2015; Li et al., 2025) by linking popularity dynamics to
stakeholder interactions.
5.3 Practical implications
This study outlines actionable recommendations for social media stakeholders. Destination marketing
organizations (DMOs) should leverage the relevance dimension to design effective UGC marketing
strategies. First, align promotional activities with external conditions (holiday demand, social hotspots)
to enhance content resonance (e.g., creating travel guides for peak seasons). Second, collaborate with
niche professional creators (rather than solely pursuing high follower counts) to ensure content
authenticity and practicality, thus avoiding the risk of “trust collapse” associated with disguised
promotion (Tsiakali, 2018). Third, monitor UGC evaluation dynamics: while high popularity can enhance
destination visibility, DMOs should tackle the issue of "reverse selection" by promoting lesser-known
attractions alongside popular ones. This strategy helps to balance mass appeal with niche interests.
Additionally, DMOs can use the proposed framework to evaluate the effectiveness of UGC by focusing on
both data indicators and users' trust.
Creators should prioritize both data-driven strategies and content value to enhance popularity. First,
optimize data indicators by focusing on high-impact metrics (saves and comments, which respondents
prioritized over likes) and aligning with platform algorithms (e.g., updating content for secondary ﬂow).
Second, enhance content value by emphasizing authenticity (avoiding overt commercialization),
practicality (detailed itineraries, local tips), and emotional resonance (genuine personal experiences).
Additionally, creators should cultivate account nichedness and professionalism—consistent posting of
travel-related content builds audience trust and improves content visibility (A14). Engaging with
commenters (author responses) and encouraging cross-platform sharing also sustain popularity by
reinforcing user interaction.
Platforms should reﬁne algorithmic mechanisms to balance popularity bias and content quality. While
interactive data (likes, saves) are useful for identifying popular content, algorithms should also prioritize
content value (authenticity, practicality) to avoid amplifying low-quality, commercially manipulated
content. Targeted pushes based on user preferences (A15) should be paired with support for niche
content to diversify the distribution of popularity, addressing Woods’s (2023) concern about the
reproduction of online inequality. Additionally, platforms could provide creators with guidance on
optimizing content characteristics (e.g., cover design, title trends) to improve content discoverability, and
transparency about algorithmic rules would help creators make informed decisions.
Page 21/27

In addition, platforms can support capacity building and informed consumption. For content creators,
platforms should provide educational resources that explain the mechanisms behind popularity, such as
content optimization guidelines and algorithmic logic, to help creators standardize and enhance content
quality. For users, platforms should develop tools to assess content credibility, including labels for
commercial collaborations and disclosure of creator veriﬁcation information, to help users critically
evaluate popularity metrics and avoid placing blind trust in high-popularity content.
5.4 Limitations and future research
This study had several limitations. First, this research was conducted only on the RedNote platform,
which has unique algorithmic, cultural, and user characteristics. Factors inﬂuencing UGC popularity may
differ across platforms such as TikTok, Instagram, or Weibo; thus, generalizability beyond RedNote
remains limited. Second, this study employs a qualitative, grounded theory design, which supports
conceptual development but does not allow for statistical quantiﬁcation of effect sizes or causal
inference. The relative importance of each dimension cannot be determined from the present data. Third,
the study reﬂects cross-sectional user perceptions rather than longitudinal dynamics of popularity. UGC
popularity often evolves through growth, saturation, and decay; this study does not capture such lifecycle
patterns. Fourth, data rely on self-reported interview responses, which may be subject to recall bias or
social desirability, potentially inﬂuencing the consistency of interpretive codes. Future research may
address these limitations by developing quantitative scales to measure UGC popularity, conducting
cross-platform comparisons, applying longitudinal designs to model popularity trajectories, and using
experimental methods to verify causal effects of popularity cues on user behavior.
Future research could build on the conceptual framework established in this study by employing
quantitative methods to develop a measurement scale for UGC popularity and empirically validate the
proposed framework. Comparative studies across platforms (e.g., TikTok and Instagram) and content
domains (e.g., food and beauty) could further examine similarities and differences in UGC popularity.
Longitudinal tracking studies could also be undertaken to analyze the lifecycle of UGC popularity, from
growth and peak to decline, and identify factors inﬂuencing these phases. Additionally, experimental
research designs could be employed to manipulate popularity indicators (e.g., likes and comments) to
test their causal effects on user perception and decision-making. Finally, future work could examine
whether cultural background and demographic characteristics (e.g., age and gender) moderate
perceptions of UGC popularity.
