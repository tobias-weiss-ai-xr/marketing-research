ABSTRACT
This study examines how five dimensions of social media marketing (SMM) — paid advertisements, influencer content, peer
recommendations, user -generated reviews, and brand engagement — shape the buying behaviour of college -going youth in
Bagalkote district,  Karnataka. Primary data were collected from 100 college students through a structured Likert -scale
questionnaire. Percentage analysis, mean and standard deviation, Pearson correlation, and Chi -Square tests were employed for
analysis. User -generated conten t emerged as the most influential SMM dimension (weighted mean = 3.20), while influencer
marketing ranked last (mean = 2.73). Brand engagement showed the strong est correlation with buying behaviour (r = 0.751).
Instagram dominated platform usage (78%), with Facebook largely absent from this cohort (10%). A significant gender -based
difference was confirmed (χ² = 18.648, p = 0.0009). All five hypotheses were accepted. The study provides original empirical evidence
from a semi-urban Karnataka context underrepresented in existing Indian SMM literature.
KEYWORDS: Social Media Marketing, Buying Behaviour, Youth Consumer, Instagram, User Generated Content, Peer
Influence, Karnataka

1. INTRODUCTION
Ask a college student in Bagalkote how they found out about the last thing they bought online, and there is a reasonable chance the
answer involves an Instagram reel, a WhatsApp forward, or a comment section. Social media has moved well beyond social
networking — it is now one of the primary channels through which young Indians discover products, form brand opinions, and
make purchase decisions. India crossed 760 million internet users in 2025, with the 18 –35 cohort accounting for the most
commercially acti ve segment (TRAI, 2025; Statista, 2025). Brands have followed the attention. Yet the academic literature
documenting how SMM actually influences buying behaviour in semi-urban India — as opposed to metropolitan settings — remains
thin.

Most existing Indian studies are anchored in cities like Mumbai, Delhi, or Bengaluru, treat social media as a single variable , and
say little about gender as a moderating factor. This study addresses those gaps directly. Drawing on primary data from 100 co llege
students in Bagalkote district, it tests five distinct SMM dimensions individually and examines whether gender significantly shapes
their influence on purchase decisions. The aim is not to confirm what the literature already knows but to see what it looks like when
the same questions are posed to a population existing research has largely ignored.

1.1 Research Gap
The existing literature has a geography problem. Studies by Yadav and Rahman (2017), Shareef et al. (2019), and Godey et al.
(2016) pull their Indian data almost entirely from Delhi, Mumbai, and Bengaluru. A college student in Bagalkote spending less than
₹1,000 a month, following local Kannada content, buying cautiously — simply does not appear in any of these samples. Whether
their findings travel to this context is, at best, an untested assumption. Beyond geography, most studies treat social media marketing
as one thing — a single composite score — when it is actually several structurally different things. An Instagram ad, a WhatsApp
forward from a friend, an influencer review, and a comment section all work through different psychological channels and sh ould
not be lumped together. Gender, similarly, gets mentioned but rarely tested properly in the Indian literature. And the platfo rm
question — whether Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp each pull different commercial levers — goes almost entirely
unexamined. This study is built around all four of those gaps.

1.2 Objectives of the Study
(i) To map social media usage patterns of college students in Bagalkote, Karnataka.
(ii) To measure the influence of social media advertisements on youth buying behaviour.
(iii) To assess how influencer marketing and peer recommendations shape purchase intentions.
(iv) To evaluate the role of user-generated content in buying decisions of young consumers.
(v) To test whether gender significantly moderates the SMM–buying behaviour relationship.

1.3 Research Hypotheses
H₁: Social media advertisements significantly influence the buying behaviour of youth.
H₂: Influencer marketing significantly affects the purchase intention of college students.
H₃: Peer recommendations on social media significantly impact buying decisions of youth.
H₄: User-generated content significantly influences purchase behaviour of young consumers.
H₅: A significant gender-based difference exists in social media marketing's influence on buying behaviour.

2. REVIEW OF LITERATURE
Three theoretical threads run through this study. The AIDA model (Barry, 1987) offers the oldest account: a sponsored Instagram
post grabs attention, holds interest, builds desire, and eventually triggers a purchase. Useful as far as it goes, though it says nothing
about why some content triggers that chain and ot her content does not. Ajzen's (1991) Theory of Planned Behaviour fills part of
that gap — purchase intentions, it argues, are shaped not only by personal attitude but by what peers are doing and approving of.
Social media is unusually good at making peer b ehaviour continuously visible, which is why it activates subjective norms so
efficiently. Festinger's (1954) Social Comparison Theory adds the third piece: people benchmark themselves against relevant others,
and social media raises the frequency and intensity of those comparisons to a level no previous medium came close to matching.

On the empirical side, Vinerean et al. (2013) were among the first to show systematically that visual and interactive social content
shapes consumer attitudes more than static formats. Godey et al. (2016), working across four countries including India, con firmed
that both brand-produced and user-produced social media content lift purchase intention — and that the effect is stronger in younger
consumers. In the Indian context specifically, Yadav and Rahman (2017) studied Delhi students and found Instagram and Facebook
activity predicts impulse buying, though their sample is solidly metropolitan and their findings may not travel far from it. Shareef
et al. (2019) brought in a variable that turns out to be important here: perceived content authenticity. When audiences sense they are
being sold to, the effect weakens; when content feels genuine, it converts. Singh and Kaur (2022) made a related point about
WhatsApp — that its closed -group architecture generates a peer -trust dynamic that open -platform advertising simply cannot
replicate. Dwivedi et al. (2021), reviewing the broader engagement literature, confirmed that social media interaction correlates with
purchase behaviour in both millennials and Gen Z, while also noting that the mechanisms shift considerably by platform and context.

3. RESEARCH METHODOLOGY
A descriptive and analytical design fitted the purpose here — the aim was both to describe what the sample looks like and to draw
conclusions about the relationships among variables. Primary data came from a structured Google Forms questionnaire administered
to 100 college students in Bagalkote district in July 2026. The questionnaire had four parts: demographics, social media usage habits,
a 24 -item Likert scale running from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree), and two open -ended questions on produ ct
preferences and personal experience. Each of the five independent variables — social media advertisements, influencer marketing,
peer influence and social sharing, user-generated content, and brand engagement — was measured through four Likert items. Buying
behaviour, the dependent variable, was captured through four items covering purchase frequency, impulse buying, spending levels,
and brand preference shaped by social media. Respondents were selected by convenience sampling from enrolled students; the
limitations of this approach are acknowledged, but it sits comfortably within accepted norms for exploratory consumer behavio ur
research of this scope (Malhotra, 2010). Analysis drew on percentage breakdowns for demographic description, mean and standard
deviation for Likert responses, weighted average mean for dimension ranking, Pearson correlation to test H₁ through H₄, and C hi-
Square to test H₅ — all at a significance threshold of α = 0.05.

3.1 Scope of the Study
This study is confined to college students in the age group of 18 to 25 years enrolled in undergraduate and postgraduate programmes
in Bagalkote district, Karnataka, India. The study covers academic year 2025–26 and focuses specifically on the influence of social
media platforms — Instagram, WhatsApp, and Facebook — on buying behaviour. It does not extend to other states, other age
groups, or working professionals. The findings are therefore most directly applicable to the semi-urban Karnataka student population
and should be interpreted with that boundary in mind.

5. DISCUSSION
The most striking finding in this dataset is probably the one that gets the least airtime in standard marketing discourse: user-generated
content outranks paid influencer marketing as a driver of youth purchase behaviour in this population. Influencer marketing h as
attracted enormous commercial investment in India — brands across fashion, beauty, food, and technology have poured money into
partnerships with creators at every level from mega -influencers with millions of followers down to nano -influencers with a few
thousand. Yet in this sample, it is t he unsponsored comment, the stranger's product review, the unboxing video by an ordinary
consumer, that carries the most persuasive weight. The mean score for the influencer marketing dimension (2.73) is not just t he
lowest among the five SMM factors — it falls below the neutral midpoint of the scale in a way that none of the others does quite as
clearly.

The most plausible explanation for this lies in the economic context of the sample. These are students spending less than ₹1, 000 a
month — people for whom every purchase involves a genuine trade-off. In that situation, trust matters enormously, and the perceived
authenticity gap between a paid influencer endorsement and an organic peer review is consequential. Shareef et al. (2019) fou nd
that content credibility significantly moderates social media marketing's effect on purchase intention; in a low-income, high-scrutiny
buying context like this one, that moderation effect is probably amplified. An influencer saying a product is good is one data point.
Fifty strangers in a comment section saying the same thing — and twenty saying the opposite — is an information environment.
These students appear to prefer the latter.

The brand engagement finding presents an interesting puzzle. Following brand pages is the lowest -scoring individual item in the
study (Q17: mean = 2.52), suggesting that most respondents do not actively seek out or maintain brand relationships on social media.
Yet brand engagement as a composite dimension correlates most strongly with buying behaviour (r = 0.751) — stronger than any
other SMM factor. The resolution is probably in the composition of the dimension: even if students do not follow brand pages, they
respond to flash sales and coupon codes (mean = 3.00), their opinion of brands is shaped by creative content they encounter passively
(mean = 2.90), and a brand's social media visibility functions as a proxy for its credibility and market presence (me an = 2.97).
Passive brand exposure — encountering brand content without actively following it — may be as commercially potent as active
engagement for this population, a pattern that resonates with the parasocial influence literature (Horton & Wohl, 1956; Yuksel &
Labrecque, 2016).

The gender finding deserves attention beyond the statistical result. Female respondents outnumber males two to one in this sample,
and the Chi -Square test confirms that gender significantly moderates the SMM -buying behaviour relationship (χ² = 18.648, p =
0.0009). Research on gender differences in social media consumption consistently shows that women spend more time on visual
platforms, engage more with lifestyle and fashion content, and place greater weight on peer and social recommendations when
making purchase decisions (Dittmar et al., 2004; Flanagin & Metzger, 2003). In a sample dominated by young women who are also
constrained by tight budgets, the combination of high social media engagement and careful purchase deliberation — mediated by
peer reviews and social validation — creates a pattern that this study captures but cannot fully explain within its design. A more
detailed gender-disaggregated analysis, ideally with a more balanced gender sample, would be a valuable contribution to future
research.

6. LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDY
Every study has its edges, and this one is no exception. The most obvious is sample size — 100 respondents is workable for this
kind of exploratory empirical work, but it is not large enough to make strong claims about the broader Karnataka student population.
Convenience sampling compounds this: respondents were drawn from accessible institutions in Bagalkote district, which means the
sample is not random and may carry biases that are difficult to fully account for. Geographically, the study is limited to a single
district in North Karnataka. Whether its findings hold for students in Bengaluru, Mysuru, or coastal Karnataka — where social
media habits, income levels, and platform preferences may differ — is an open question.

The data are also cross -sectional, captured at a single point in time in July 2026. Social media behaviour shifts quickly — the
platforms that dominate today may look different in two years — and a longitudinal design would track those shifts in ways this
study cannot. Finally, all responses are self-reported. Students may have overstated or understated their social media-driven purchase
behaviour, either because they genuinely cannot recall it accurately or because of social desirability bias. These limita tions do not
undermine the core findings, but they do mark the boundaries within which those findings should be read.

7. CONCLUSION
The central question this study set out to answer — does social media marketing really change what young people buy? — has a
clear answer in this data. Yes, it does. Every SMM dimension tested shows a statistically significant positive relationship w ith
buying behaviour, all five research hypotheses are accepted, and 79% of respondents have purchased something after seeing it on
social media. The commercial influence of these platforms on youth buying is not marginal or conditional; it is substantial a nd
consistent across dimensions.

User-generated content, not paid advertisements or professional influencer endorsements, emerges as the most influential force in
this sample's purchase decisions. For cost-conscious students in semi-urban Karnataka, the authentic voices of ordinary consumers
carry more weight than carefully produced branded content. Influencer marketing remains statistically significant but ranks l ast
among the five dimensions. Instagram dominates the platform landscape; Facebook has effectively exited this demographic's
attention economy.

For marketers, strategies that amplify authentic user reviews and leverage WhatsApp-based peer sharing likely deliver better returns
than premium influencer partnerships for this population. For researchers, questions about influencer tier effects, longitu dinal
income-driven changes in SMM responsiveness, and cross-district comparisons within Karnataka remain open and worth pursuing.
