Social commerce has emerged as a central paradigm within electronic commerce
by seamlessly integrating transactional capabilities with social interaction, user-
generated content, and platform-driven influence mechanisms. This scoping
review systematically maps the body of research on consumer behavior in social
commerce. Unlike previous reviews constrained by narrower temporal scopes
or specific theoretical lenses, the present study synthesizes bibliometric,
geographical, methodological, theoretical, and thematic evidence drawn from a
comprehensive corpus of 472 studies published between 2010 and 2026. The
findings indicate sustained growth in the literature, particularly since 2019,
characterized by a strong predominance of quantitative cross-sectional designs
and structural equation modeling (SEM). Core constructs such as trust, perceived
value, purchase intention, and social influence continue to dominate the field.
Nevertheless, emerging research streams increasingly examine phenomena
including herd behavior, fear of missing out (FoMO), social proof, influencer-
consumer relationships, live commerce, and short-video environments. The
review also highlights a pronounced asymmetry in global knowledge production,
with substantial contributions from Asia, North America, and Europe, contrasted
by comparatively limited representation from Latin America and Africa. By
updating and integrating previously fragmented strands of the literature, this
review provides an evidence-based mapping of the field’s evolution—from early
technology-adoption and generalized trust models toward more psychologically
nuanced and platform-sensitive explanations of consumer decision-making.
Furthermore, it identifies key priorities for future scholarship, including greater
theoretical integration, methodological diversification, longitudinal and
comparative designs, and expanded research in underrepresented regions.

1 Introduction
Social commerce has established itself as an evolution of electronic commerce by
integrating social interaction processes, user-generated content, and transactional
mechanisms within digital platforms, thereby reconfiguring information, persuasion,
and purchase decision processes. In particular, the rise of live streaming shopping has
intensified the experiential dimension of consumption by combining audiovisual
stimuli, social presence, and real-time interpersonal influence. Recent evidence shows

purchase intention, mediated by approach-avoidance

persuasive strategies (Dang-Van and Nguyen, 2025). These
findings confirm that social commerce environments operate not
only as sales channels but also as spaces for the symbolic
construction of value and meaning.
Contemporary literature highlights the central role of trust,
brand–consumer relationship quality, and B2C interaction in
shaping purchase intention and loyalty in social commerce
platforms. Recent studies show that brand relationship quality
has a direct positive effect on brand loyalty, moderated by
cultural values, underscoring the need to incorporate
sociocultural dimensions into the analysis of digital consumer
behavior (Mazaheri et al., 2025). Complementarily, interaction
between companies and consumers strengthens retailer brand
equity, which acts as a mediating mechanism toward purchase
intention on social platforms (John et al., 2025). These results
reinforce the idea that relational value constitutes a strategic
asset in socially mediated digital ecosystems.
The field has also advanced toward the integration of hybrid
theoretical frameworks to explain consumer behavior in social
commerce. The articulation of technology acceptance, signaling
theory, and social contagion has enabled more comprehensive
explanations of repurchase behavior among Generation Z,
showing that digital trust signals, lenient return policies, and
fear of missing out (FoMO) act as key drivers of repurchase
intention (Khoa et al., 2025). Convergingly, the Stimulus–
Organism–Response (S-O-R) model has been widely used to
explain how digital environment stimuli (e.g., social presence,
immersion, perceived enjoyment) activate psychological states
that lead to trust and purchase intention (Hongsuchon et al.,
2025; Yang et al., 2025).
On the other hand, the evolution of electronic word-of-mouth
(eWOM) has expanded formats, channels, and audiences that
influence purchase decisions within social commerce. The
transition from textual reviews to audiovisual content, virtual
influencers, and immersive environments has complicated
credibility and persuasion processes (Filieri et al., 2025). In
specific contexts such as WhatsApp, exposure to promotional
content increases purchase intention through perceived
credibility, with effects moderated by tie strength (Nyazabe
et al., 2025), highlighting the importance of relational structures
in social messaging platforms.
Finally, recent literature shows thematic diversification toward
areas such as sustainable consumption and AI-driven
personalization. In the green real estate sector, social commerce
acts as an influence channel on purchase intention through
social norms, positive eWOM, and pro-environmental identity
(Anand et al., 2025). In mobile commerce, app design, AI-based
personalization, and social commerce integration significantly
influence Generation Z’s purchase intention, mediated by
mobile app marketing (Hasim and Nazri, 2025). Nevertheless,
gaps remain in the integration of these approaches within inter-
regional and longitudinal comparative frameworks.
Although previous reviews have laid valuable foundations,
they have left behind a rapidly evolving and fragmented body of
evidence. Busalim and Hussin (2016) synthesized studies
published between 2010 and 2015, Han et al. (2018) mapped the
literature up to 2017, and Zhao et al. (2023) examined
consumer behavior research from 2017 to 2021 through the lens
of the S-O-R framework. More recent syntheses have tended to
concentrate on specific outcomes—such as customer experience
or engagement—rather than providing a comprehensive
integration of publication dynamics, methodological patterns,
theoretical constructs, international collaboration networks, and
emerging platform-specific psychological processes.
Since 2021, the field has expanded significantly, with growing
attention to live commerce, short-video platforms, influencer
marketing, fear of missing out (FoMO), social proof, herd
behavior, AI-driven personalization, and consumer behavior in
emerging-market contexts. Consequently, there is a clear need
for an updated scoping review that goes beyond merely
enumerating publications. Such a review should critically
examine how the conceptual architecture of the field is shifting,
identify persistent methodological and geographical
concentrations, and highlight mechanisms that remain
insufficiently integrated.
Accordingly, the present study aims to systematically map the
scientific literature on consumer behavior in social commerce,
with particular emphasis on the evolution and current
configuration of the field. Specifically, this scoping review
addresses the following research questions:
• RQ1: How has scientific production and citation impact
evolved over time and across different publication outlets?
• RQ2: Which methodological approaches and theoretical
constructs dominate the literature, and to what extent do
signs of methodological or theoretical concentration persist?
• RQ3: How is research geographically distributed, and how
is it structured through patterns of international
collaboration?
• RQ4: Which thematic clusters and emerging socio-
psychological or platform-specific processes characterize
the current research agenda?
By addressing these questions in an integrated manner, the review
seeks to update and extend prior syntheses, while rendering the
field’s fragmentation, concentrations, and emergent directions
more transparent and actionable for future scholarship.
2 Methodology
2.1 Study design
This study was conducted as a scoping review, following
the methodological framework originally proposed by Arksey and
O’Malley (2005) and further refined by Levac et al. (2010). The
reporting of the review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items
for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping
Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines developed by Tricco et al.
(2018). A scoping review design was deemed most appropriate
given the study’s aim: to comprehensively map the breadth, key
characteristics, conceptual structure, methodological patterns, and
existing research gaps within a rapidly evolving and heterogeneous
body of literature, rather than to synthesize evidence for estimating
pooled effects or testing specific hypotheses.

2.2 Information sources and search
strategy
The systematic literature search was conducted in the Web of
Science (WoS) and Scopus databases in 2026, prior to manuscript
submission. These two databases were selected for their extensive
multidisciplinary coverage across the social sciences, business,
information systems, marketing, and consumer behavior
domains. No lower temporal limit was applied to the search.
This approach was deliberate: rather than imposing an arbitrary
starting date, the review allowed the historical emergence and
development of the field to emerge organically from the
retrieved evidence. The final corpus of included studies spans
the period from 2010 to 2026. Because 2026 was an incomplete
publication year at the time of the search, productivity data for
that year were treated as partial and were not directly compared
with fully completed prior years in the temporal trend analyses.
The search equations were applied to the title, abstract, and
keywords fields, adapted to the syntax of each database, as follows:
Web of Science (TS field): TS = ((“social commerce” OR
“social media commerce” OR “social shopping” OR
“commerce through social media” OR “purchas* through
social media” OR “buy* through social media” OR “shopping
on social media”) AND (“consumer behavior” OR “consumer
behaviour” OR “purchase intention” OR “buying behavior”
OR “online consumer behavior” OR “purchase decision” OR
“consumer decision”))
Scopus (TITLE-ABS-KEY fields): TITLE-ABS-KEY ((“social
commerce” OR “social media commerce” OR “social
shopping” OR “commerce through social media” OR
“purchas* through social media” OR “buy* through social
media” OR “shopping on social media”) AND (“consumer
behavior” OR “consumer behaviour” OR “purchase intention”
OR “buying behavior” OR “online consumer behavior” OR
“purchase decision” OR “consumer decision”))
Eligibility criteria were applied to include only original research
articles and review articles published in English or Spanish. No
lower temporal restriction was imposed. The upper temporal
boundary was determined by the records indexed in the selected
databases at the time of the search in 2026; consequently,
publications from 2026 represent a partial year and were
interpreted accordingly in the temporal analyses.
2.3 Eligibility criteria
Included were original articles and reviews published in
scientific journals that explicitly addressed consumer behavior,
purchase intention, or decision-making processes in social
media environments. Excluded were documents other than
articles and reviews (e.g., conference proceedings, editorials),
studies focused solely on traditional electronic commerce
without a social media component, documents in languages
other than English and Spanish, and duplicate records
across databases.
2.4 Study selection process
Records retrieved from Web of Science were exported in.xlsx
format, while those from Scopus were downloaded in.csv
format. The datasets were subsequently merged and processed
using RStudio. Duplicate records were identified and removed
using the Digital Object Identifier (DOI) as the primary key and
title matching as a secondary criterion.
From an initial yield of 1,434 records (795 from Scopus and
639 from Web of Science), 679 were excluded prior to screening
due to duplicates and the application of document-type and
language filters. This process left 755 records for title and
abstract screening.
A preliminary semi-automated screening of titles and abstracts
was then conducted in R using the grepl() function with keywords
related to social commerce, consumer behavior, purchase
intention, and shopping via social media. This step served solely
to prioritize potentially relevant records and did not substitute
for full eligibility assessment. All preselected records underwent
manual review against the predefined inclusion criteria. Of the
755 records screened, 279 were excluded at the title and abstract
stage, leaving 476 for full-text eligibility assessment. Four
additional records were excluded during this phase because they
did not focus on purchases within social media environments or
did not examine consumer behavior. Consequently, the final
synthesis included 472 studies.
The PRISMA flow diagram visually documents this selection
process, illustrating the progressive reduction of the initial
database yield at each stage and explicitly linking every reported
number to a specific inclusion or exclusion decision, thereby
enhancing the transparency and auditability of the review
(View Figure 1).
2.5 Data extraction
From the included studies, information was extracted
regarding year of publication, country or region of study,
objectives, methodological approach, theoretical frameworks,
variables analyzed, social media platforms considered, and
main findings.
2.6 Analysis strategy
The analysis integrated descriptive bibliometric indicators
with thematic and network mapping techniques. First, annual
publication counts and cumulative citation totals were examined
to delineate the temporal evolution of the field and account for
citation-window effects. Second, journal-level productivity and
accumulated citations were analyzed to identify the most prolific
and influential publication outlets. Third, country-level co-
authorship networks were constructed to map the geographical
structure of knowledge production. In these networks, node size
reflects publication volume (prominence), links represent
collaborative relationships between countries, and total link
strength (TLS) indicates the overall intensity of each country’s
international collaborations. Fourth, keyword co-occurrence

3 Results
3.1 Publication trends and citation impact
Figure 2 shows the temporal evolution of scientific production
and impact — measured through the number of annual citations
— in the field of consumer behavior in social commerce during
the period 2010–2026.
In terms of productivity, an initial phase of incipient growth is
observed between 2010 and 2014, with a reduced number of annual
publications, suggesting that the social commerce phenomenon was
still in an emerging stage with low theoretical consolidation. From
2015 onward, a progressive and sustained increase in the volume of
studies is identified, which intensifies markedly from 2019 onward.
This growth accelerates significantly in the period 2021–2025,
reaching the highest number of annual publications and evidencing
the consolidation of social commerce as a relevant line of research
within studies on consumer behavior, digital marketing, and
electronic commerce.
The pattern of cumulative growth reflects a clearly exponential
trend, indicating not only an increase in the quantity of research
but also an expansion of interdisciplinary interest in the
phenomenon, integrating perspectives from management,
information systems, consumer psychology, and digital
communication studies. This increase temporally coincides with
the global expansion of social media use as commercialization
channels, as well as with changes in digital consumption habits
intensified in the post-pandemic context.
Regarding scientific impact, measured by the number of
citations received per year, a more fluctuating pattern is
observed. The highest citation peaks are concentrated between
2016 and 2020, suggesting the publication of seminal or highly
influential works that structured the field and served as
conceptual foundations for subsequent research. From 2021
onward, a progressive decrease in the number of annual
citations is apparent — a phenomenon that can be explained by
the citation window effect, since more recent studies have not
yet had sufficient time to accumulate impact.

3.3 Geographical distribution of scientific
production and international collaboration
networks
Figure 3 presents the co-authorship network by country in
research on consumer behavior in social commerce.
The results evidence a highly concentrated network structure
around a reduced set of countries that act as central nodes in the
production and circulation of knowledge in the social commerce field.
China positions itself as the main global actor, both in
publication volume (135 documents) and impact (10,804
citations) and relational connectivity (total link strength = 79). Its
central location in the visual network reflects a dominant role as
a collaboration hub, articulating links with countries in East Asia,
North America, and Europe. Complementarily, the United States
(60 documents; 5,662 citations; TLS = 53) and the United
Kingdom (28 documents; 1,984 citations; TLS = 52) stand out as
strategic nodes connecting multiple regional clusters,
consolidating their role as referents in the generation of widely
cited theoretical, methodological, and empirical frameworks.
Within the Asian context, a dynamic cluster is observed
formed by Taiwan (41 documents; 2,410 citations; TLS = 34),
South Korea (27 documents; 2,200 citations; TLS = 8), and
Malaysia (45 documents; 1,177 citations; TLS = 53), evidencing
the relevance of Asia as an empirical and conceptual epicenter
of social commerce, consistent with the high penetration of
social commerce in these markets. Likewise, countries such as
India (39 documents; 561 citations; TLS = 28) and Pakistan (16
documents; 759 citations; TLS = 25) reinforce the expansion of
the field in emerging economies with strong adoption of social
platforms as purchase channels.
In contrast, the network reveals still marginal participation
from Latin America and Africa, with limited presence of
countries such as Brazil, Chile, Mexico, Colombia, Ecuador, and
South Africa, characterized by low production volumes and
reduced relational centrality. This pattern suggests a relevant
geographical gap in the literature, opening opportunities for the
development of contextual research in developing economies,
particularly in regions where the use of social media as an
informal and social commerce channel has high penetration but
scarce academic systematization.
Overall, the results confirm an asymmetrical international
collaboration structure, dominated by production poles in Asia,
North America, and Europe, with significant potential for
expanding South–South networks and inter-regional
comparative studies in future research agendas.
The observed geographical asymmetry should not be interpreted
merely as a bibliometric artifact. Several contextual factors may
plausibly account for this pattern. The prominent representation of
China and other Asian economies aligns with the earlier and more
extensive diffusion of integrated social-shopping platforms, live-
streaming commerce, and sophisticated digital ecosystems in these
markets. Such environments have provided fertile empirical settings
for examining social interaction and commercial transactions at
scale. Cultural dimensions may further contribute to this
concentration, as social influence, trust transfer, relationship quality,
and collective decision-making cues often carry heightened salience
in certain cultural contexts. Supporting this view, cross-regional
studies have demonstrated that consumer responses and purchase
intentions in social commerce vary significantly across cultures (Ng,
2013), while more recent research suggests that cultural values can
moderate the effects of brand relationships (Mazaheri et al., 2025).
In contrast, the comparatively limited research output from
Latin America and parts of Africa likely reflects not only
differing trajectories of technology adoption but also structural
disparities in research infrastructure, funding, international
collaboration networks, database indexing, and the academic
institutionalization of social commerce as a research domain.
It is important to note that these explanations remain interpretive
rather than causal. Future research should test them through rigorous
comparative, cross-cultural, and institutional studies.

3.4 Thematic structure of the field and
keyword co-occurrence
Figure 4 presents the keyword co-occurrence map in social
commerce research.
The central node of the map is occupied by social commerce
(261 occurrences; TLS = 431), confirming its role as the
articulating concept of the study domain. This term is strongly
linked to purchase intention (111; TLS = 216) and trust (68;
TLS = 176), evidencing that purchase intention and trust
constitute the main explanatory mechanisms of consumer
behavior in social commerce environments.
A second relevant thematic axis is structured around consumer
behavior and its terminological variants, reflected in the high
frequency of consumer behavior (31; TLS = 62) and consumer
behaviour (19; TLS = 39), indicating conceptual convergence
between Anglo-Saxon traditions. This cluster connects intensely
with classic electronic commerce constructs, such as e-commerce
(36; TLS = 89), electronic commerce, and e-WOM/ewom (13–9
occurrences; TLS = 29–17), suggesting that the social commerce
literature draws from previous electronic commerce frameworks,
incorporating social dimensions such as interaction, social presence,
and user-generated content (Liang and Turban, 2011; Chen et al.,
2017; Cheung et al., 2014; Wang and Yu, 2017; Yusuf et al., 2018).
Likewise, a cluster centered on platforms and interaction
environments emerges, where social media (43; TLS = 102), social
media marketing (17; TLS = 38), Facebook, and Instagram stand out,
reflecting the empirical centrality of these platforms as contexts for
analyzing purchase behavior (Bai et al., 2015; Molinillo et al., 2020;
Jin and Ryu, 2020; Xiang et al., 2016). In parallel, a recent subfield
associated with consumption practices mediated by live streaming
emerges, with terms such as live streaming (10; TLS = 19) and live
commerce (5; TLS = 7), evidencing the evolution of social commerce
toward more immersive and interactive formats (Guo and Li, 2022;
Sun et al., 2019).
Finally, the consolidation of recurrent theoretical and
methodological frameworks is observed, such as perceived
usefulness, perceived ease of use, perceived risk, and signaling
theory, as well as methodological approaches such as SEM and
fsQCA, indicating analytical diversification in the study of
determinants of consumer behavior (Kim and Park, 2013; Lu
et al., 2016; Leong et al., 2020; Huang, 2016).
Overall, the map reveals a mature thematic structure organized
around purchase intention and trust, with a recent expansion
toward emerging phenomena such as live commerce, social
influence, and the role of influencers, suggesting promising lines
for future research.
4 Discussion
The findings of this scoping review extend and update previous
syntheses by demonstrating not only the substantial growth of social
commerce research, but also a notable shift in its explanatory center
of gravity. While earlier reviews primarily organized the field around
broad platform categories, technology adoption factors, website
features, or stimulus–organism–response (S-O-R) frameworks
(Busalim and Hussin, 2016; Han et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2023), the
present mapping—which integrates literature up to 2026 and
combines bibliometric, collaboration, and keyword analyses—
reveals a more nuanced transition. Foundational constructs such as
trust and purchase intention remain central; however, they are

This interplay of theoretical continuity and progressive
diversification represents the primary contribution of the

In line with prior literature that has highlighted the central role
of trust as a predictor of purchase behavior in social commerce
(Hajli et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2019; Tseng et al.,
2025), recent studies continue to position this construct as a key
mediating mechanism. For example, Al-Omoush and Shuhaiber
(2026) integrate the “wisdom of the crowd” as an antecedent of
trust and perceived value, expanding classical adoption models by
incorporating collective dynamics characteristic of social
platforms. Complementarily, Herzallah and Al-Sharafi (2025)
delve into mechanisms for reducing seller uncertainty through
pre-purchase signals, demonstrating that information quality and
seller reputation remain fundamental pillars for trust formation.
These findings reinforce the idea that, despite the technological
sophistication of platforms, credibility evaluation processes remain
anchored in social and informational quality signals.
In terms of socio-psychological processes, recent literature extends
traditional social influence approaches toward more specific
constructs. While earlier studies documented the impact of eWOM
and consumer-to-consumer interaction on purchase intention
(Charles and Kanani, 2025; John et al., 2025), more recent
investigations introduce variables such as herd behavior (Chen et al.,
2025) and fear of missing out (FoMO) (Khoa et al., 2025) to explain
imitative behaviors and purchase urgency, particularly among young
users of TikTok. These results suggest a transition from general
social influence models toward finer-grained explanations of the
cognitive and emotional mechanisms underlying decision-making in
highly stimulating social environments.
Likewise, there is growing attention to impulse buying
mediated by short-video platforms and live commerce. While
previous works linked impulse purchasing to hedonic value and
flow (Mashilo et al., 2025), recent studies such as Huang et al.
(2025) and Thu et al. (2026) demonstrate that variables such as
empathy, perceived enjoyment, and social proof signals in
audiovisual content intensify the propensity for impulse buying.
This empirical convergence suggests that the interactive
architecture of platforms like TikTok and Douyin amplifies
affective processes that reconfigure the classical determinants of
purchase behavior in e-commerce.
In the realm of influencer marketing and value co-creation,
current results expand approaches focused on engagement and
brand–consumer relationships. Nibras et al. (2025) demonstrate
that brand engagement and relationship quality drive co-creation
processes in social commerce, aligning with the findings of
Migkos et al. (2025), who emphasize the importance of
authenticity and transparency of influencers in sustaining digital
marketing effectiveness. These results suggest that persuasion in
social commerce is not limited to content exposure but depends
on sustained symbolic and emotional relationships over time.
Finally, the discussion reveals an expansion of the field toward
specific sectoral and geographical contexts, such as the trade of
aquaculture products in Bangladesh (Sharmin et al., 2025) or
social commerce in emerging African economies (Charles and
Kanani, 2025; Mashilo et al., 2025). These studies contrast with
the strong historical concentration of the literature in East Asia
and evidence a progressive contextual diversification.
Nevertheless, a persistent gap remains in the representation of
Latin America, reinforcing the need for future research
examining how cultural, institutional, and socioeconomic factors
moderate social commerce mechanisms in understudied regions.
A second contribution lies in the identification of persistent
patterns in the methodological and geographical structure of the
literature. The marked predominance of quantitative cross-
sectional designs and structural equation modeling (SEM) has
yielded a relatively robust understanding of the associations
among trust, perceived value, social influence, and purchase
intention. However, this approach offers limited insight into
temporal dynamics, platform migration processes, repeated
exposure effects, or underlying causal mechanisms. Future
research would therefore benefit from greater methodological
diversification, including longitudinal panel studies, field and
platform-based experiments, digital trace analyses, qualitative
process-oriented inquiries, and mixed-methods designs.
Likewise, the strong geographical concentration of research
collaboration in Asia, North America, and Europe implies that
constructs often presented as universal may in reality be
significantly shaped by cultural values, institutional trust systems,
payment infrastructures, platform regulations, and informal
commercial practices. The relative underrepresentation of studies
from Latin America and Africa thus constitutes not only an
important coverage gap but also a substantial theoretical
opportunity for advancing context-sensitive and culturally
nuanced understandings of consumer behavior in social commerce.
Overall, the comparative evidence shows that the field of social
commerce has transitioned from explanatory models centered on
technological adoption and basic trust toward more integrative
frameworks that incorporate emotions, collective dynamics,
parasocial relationships, and platform architectures. This
evolution poses the challenge of developing more holistic
theoretical models and methodologies capable of capturing the
dynamic complexity of consumer behavior in socially mediated
digital ecosystems.
5 Conclusions
This review confirms that social commerce has evolved from
explanations centered on technology adoption and purchase
intention toward more integrative approaches in which trust,
perceived value, social signals, emotional mechanisms, and
platform dynamics converge. In recent studies, trust remains a
structural axis, but it is explained with greater precision through
antecedents such as the wisdom of the crowds and perceived
value (Al-Omoush and Shuhaiber, 2026), as well as through
seller uncertainty reduction mechanisms based on pre-purchase
signals (Herzallah and Al-Sharafi, 2025).
Concurrently, social influence is shifting from general notions
(eWOM, C2C interactions) toward more specific behavioral
phenomena such as herd behavior (Chen et al., 2025) and fear
of missing out (FoMO) (Khoa et al., 2025), especially in high-

Taken together, the evidence portrays a research field that is

culturally embedded forms of digital commerce.

the continued dominance of cross-sectional quantitative designs

of existing knowledge.
5.1 Theoretical implications
The findings of this review reinforce the central role of trust as
a foundational construct in social commerce while highlighting
increasing differentiation in its antecedents. Trust is now more
frequently explained through a multifaceted array of collective
information, seller signals, reputation mechanisms, perceived
value, social presence, and platform-specific cues, moving
beyond its treatment as a single undifferentiated predictor.
These insights suggest that future models should explicitly
distinguish between the objects and sources of trust and
systematically examine how social and informational signals
interact across diverse platform contexts (Al-Omoush and
Shuhaiber, 2026; Herzallah and Al-Sharafi, 2025).
The review further underscores the need for greater theoretical
granularity regarding social influence. Traditional broad categories
such as electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM) or general social
influence are increasingly supplemented by more specific
mechanisms, including imitation, herd behavior, social proof,
fear of missing out (FoMO), parasocial relationships, and
responsiveness to collective cues (Chen et al., 2025; Khoa et al.,
2025). These developments point toward the value of integrating
cognitive and affective pathways, particularly in short-video and
live-commerce environments, where enjoyment, empathy,
perceived usefulness, urgency, and real-time interaction often
operate concurrently. In this regard, frameworks such as S-O-R
and the Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM) may prove most
productive when employed as integrative architectures rather
than standalone explanatory templates.
Finally, the evidence expands the unit of analysis beyond isolated
individual purchase decisions. Constructs such as engagement,
relationship quality, community interaction, and value co-creation
suggest that social commerce generates value within relational
ecosystems in which consumers simultaneously act as reviewers,
content creators, recommenders, and active participants in brand
communities (Migkos et al., 2025; Nibras et al., 2025). From a
theoretical standpoint, this calls for models that bridge individual
psychological mechanisms with networked and platform-level
processes, while explicitly testing the extent to which these
relationships are moderated by cultural and institutional contexts.
5.2 Practical implications
For platforms and practitioners, the findings underscore that
reducing consumer uncertainty remains a central managerial
imperative. Providing comprehensive product information,
verifiable reviews, strong seller reputation signals, transparent
return policies, traceability features, and prompt responses to
frequently asked questions can significantly enhance credibility
and facilitate trust formation (Herzallah and Al-Sharafi, 2025).
In live streaming and short-video environments, these
informational cues should be thoughtfully integrated with
interactive features that enable consumers to pose questions,
observe peer reactions, and evaluate products without
compromising visibility of essential product details.
Content and marketing strategies should also carefully
differentiate between genuine engagement and manipulative
pressure. While social proof, enjoyment, empathy, and perceived
usefulness can effectively heighten purchase urgency on
platforms such as TikTok and Douyin (Huang et al., 2025; Thu
et al., 2026), the same mechanisms risk becoming coercive when
paired with artificial scarcity or deceptive urgency tactics.
Responsible social commerce practices therefore demand
transparent disclosure, effective moderation of user-generated
content, verifiable testimonials, and judicious application of
FoMO-based appeals—particularly in campaigns targeting
younger audiences (Khoa et al., 2025).
Finally, community and market contexts should guide
strategic implementation. Consumer-to-consumer interactions
can enhance purchase intentions by building trust (John et al.,
2025), supporting the development of Q&A functionalities,
community reputation systems, and high-quality reviews.
Nevertheless, strategies should not be applied uniformly across
regions. Substantial differences in cultural values, security
expectations, payment infrastructures, platform adoption
patterns, and informal commercial practices necessitate localized
adaptation of segmentation and messaging approaches. This is
especially critical in emerging markets and underrepresented
regions, where social commerce is often widespread in practice
yet remains significantly understudied in indexed
academic literature.
