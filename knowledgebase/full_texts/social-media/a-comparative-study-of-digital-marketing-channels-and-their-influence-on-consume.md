Abstract
Consumers now meet brands on social media, search engines, video platforms, e-mail and mobile alerts, but many firms
still choose where to spend their advertising money without clear evidence. This study looks at how ad engagement,
trust, brand awareness and offer attractiveness relate to consumers’ intention to buy after seeing digital ads, and whether
the likelihood of buying differs across four digital channels. A structured online questionnaire was filled in by 49
respondents. After screening, 46 comple te responses were analysed using descriptive statistics, Cronbach’s alpha,
correlation, the Friedman test, multiple linear regression and the Kruskal –Wallis test. Average scores for all five
measures were close to the neutral mid-point of 3 on a 5-point scale. The likelihood of buying did not differ significantly
across the four channels, χ²(3, N = 46) = 5.65, p = .130, although e -mail / SMS / app alerts had the highest average
(3.00). The regression model was significant and explained 77.3% of the variatio n in purchase intention, F(4, 41) =
34.94, p < .001. Offer attractiveness (β = .61) and ad engagement (β = .62) were the strongest predictors. Purchase
intention did not differ by income group. Because the sample is small and the predictors overlap strongl y, the findings
should be treated as exploratory. They suggest that attractive offers and engaging content matter more than the choice
of channel alone.
Keywords: digital marketing channels, consumer buying behaviour, purchase intention, ad engagement, trust, offer
attractiveness, regression
1. Introduction
Digital media have changed how people find, compare and buy products. A shopper may see a product in a social media
post, search for reviews on a search engine, watch a video advertisement and finally buy after getting a discount message
by e-mail or on a phone app. Each of these touchpoints is a different digital marketing channel, and each works in its
own way. Social media builds attention and conversation, search captures people who are already looking, video adds
visual impact, and e-mail or app alerts keep a direct link with the customer.
Because digital spending can be measured, managers expect to know which channel works best. In practice the answer
is not simple. Channels reach different people at different stages of the buying journey. Much of the earlier research
studies one channel at a time, most often social media, so it is hard to compare channels using the same respondents and
the same outcome. This study places several channels in one framework and also asks which consumer attitudes are
linked most strongly with the intention to buy.
1.1 Statement of the problem
Firms invest in several digital channels at the same time, but there is limited comparative evidence on how these channels
differ in their influence on buying behaviour, and on which consumer attitudes explain that influence. Without such
evidence, budgets are often set by habit or by platform-reported numbers instead of by consumer data.

1.2 Objectives of the study
• To measure consumers’ levels of ad engagement, trust, brand awareness, offer attractiveness and purchase
intention.
• To test whether the likelihood of buying differs across four digital channels: social media / influencers, search
engines, YouTube / video, and e-mail / SMS / app notifications.
• To find how far ad engagement, trust, brand awareness and offer attractiveness predict purchase intention.
• To examine whether purchase intention differs across income groups.
• To give recommendations on how to use digital marketing effort.
1.3 Scope and significance
The study covers people who answered an online questionnaire about how digital advertisements affect their buying. It
is useful for marketers and small businesses deciding where to place limited budgets, and for students who want a simple
and repeatable analysis. Its main value is that it compares channels and also explains the attitudes that sit behind purchase
intention.
2. Review of Literature
This section reviews twelve earlier studies and works. They are grouped by theme: digital channels, social and influencer
media, advertising value and trust, brand awareness and offers, and the buying journey.
2.1 Digital marketing channels
1. Kannan and Li (2017) describe digital marketing as a system in which firms, consumers and platforms meet through
data-rich touchpoints. They note that these touchpoints differ in how much control the firm has over the message and in
how they can be measured.
2. Chaffey and Ellis -Chadwick (2019) group the main channels into search marketing, social media, e -mail, display
advertising and affiliate or influencer partnerships. Each channel has its own cost and its own role in the customer’s
journey, so channels should not be expected to give the same results.
2.2 Social media and influencer marketing
3. Kaplan and Haenlein (2010) define social media as internet applications that let users create and share content. They
explain the opportunities and challenges these platforms bring to firms.
4. Mangold and Faulds (2009)  argue that social media are a hybrid part of the promotional mix. Firms can speak to
customers, but customers also speak to each other, so the firm has less control over the message. This peer -to-peer
quality is one reason social content is often seen as more believable than ordinary advertising.
5. De Veirman et al. (2017) studied marketing through Instagram influencers. They found that an influencer’s number
of followers affects how popular and likeable the influencer seems, and that the fit between the influencer and the brand
also matters.
6. Dwivedi et al. (2021)  review the fast -growing research on digital and social media marketing. They point to
engagement, trust and content quality as common themes and call for more studies that compare channels.
2.3 Advertising value and trust
7. Ducoffe (1996) studied advertising on the web and showed that people judge ads by their value to them. Ads seen as
informative and entertaining were valued more, while ads seen as irritating were valued less. This supports the idea that
engaging ads work better.
8. Gefen, Karahanna and Straub (2003) studied online shopping and found that trust in the online seller, together with
how useful and easy the website seems, influences the intention to buy. Trust is therefore a key factor when people buy
online.

2.4 Brand awareness and offers
9. Keller (1993) explained customer-based brand equity and showed that brand awareness, meaning how easily people
recognise and recall a brand, is a basic part of a strong brand. Advertising is one way to build it.
10. Dodds, Monroe and Grewal (1991)  showed that price discounts raise the value buyers see in a product and their
willingness to buy. This supports the idea that attractive offers help purchase intention.
2.5 Consumer buying behaviour and the decision journey
11. Court et al. (2009) suggested that the consumer decision journey is a loop, in which consideration, evaluation and
loyalty are shaped again and again by brand touchpoints. Buying is not a straight line from one ad to one purchase.
12. Lemon and Verhoef (2016)  extended this into a customer -experience view that covers many touchpoints before,
during and after the purchase. Kotler and Keller (2016) describe the basic buying process in a similar way: recognising
a need, searching for information, comparing options, buying and judging the purchase. Ajzen’s (1991) theory of planned
behaviour supports the use of purchase intention as a close predictor of actual behaviour, because intention reflects the
motivation to act.
2.6 Research gap and conceptual framework
The literature describes the growth of each channel and the value of engagement, trust, awareness and offers, but fewer
studies compare several channels in one sample and test these attitudes together. This study addresses that gap. The
framework treats ad engagement, trust, brand awareness and offer attractiveness as the drivers of purchase intention. It
also compares the stated likelihood of buying across four channels.
2.7 Hypotheses
H0₁ (null): There is no significant difference in the likelihood of buying across the four digital channels.
H1 (alternative): The likelihood of buying differs significantly across the four digital channels.
H0₂ (null):  Ad engagement, trust, brand awareness and offer attractiveness do not significantly predict purchase
intention.
H2 (alternative):  Ad engagement, trust, brand awareness and offer attractiveness significantly predict purchase
intention.
H0₃ (null): There is no significant difference in purchase intention across income groups.
H3 (alternative): Purchase intention differs significantly across income groups.
3. Research Methodology
3.1 Research design
The study uses a descriptive and cross-sectional survey design with a quantitative approach. A structured questionnaire
collected data from respondents at one point in time.
3.2 Sample and data screening
The questionnaire was shared online as a form [state your sampling method, for example convenience sampling through
messaging groups, and your city or region]. In total, 49 respondents submitted answers. Three of them left all the rating
questions blank, so they were removed. This left 46 usable responses. A few respondents skipped one or two individual
statements (three skipped Question 26 and three skipped Question 35). Their scores were averaged using the statements
they did answer.
The original data file also contained 42 extra rows that were clearly marked as computer -generated (synthetic) and not
from real participants. These rows were left out of every analysis and are not part of the sample.

3.4 Statistical tools
The data were analysed with Python (pandas and SciPy). The same results can be reproduced in Microsoft Excel. The
following methods were used at the 5% significance level (α = .05).
• Cronbach’s alpha to check whether the statements in each variable measure the same idea (Cronbach, 1951).
A value of .70 or more is usually accepted (Nunnally, 1978).
• Descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation to describe the scores and the links between variables.
• Friedman test to compare the four channel-wise likelihood scores. This test is used when the same people rate
several items (Friedman, 1937). Pairs were compared with Wilcoxon signed -rank tests and a Bonferroni
correction. Kendall’s W was used as the effect size.
• Multiple linear regression  (least squares) to test how far the four attitudes predict purchase intention (H2).
Variance inflation factors (VIF) were checked for overlap between predictors (Hair et al., 2019).
• Kruskal–Wallis test to compare purchase intention across income groups (H3), because some groups were very
small.

Interpretation. The model is significant and explains 77.3% of the variation in purchase intention (adjusted R² = .751),
which is a large effect by Cohen’s (1988) benchmarks. Looking at each predictor:
• Offer attractiveness (β = .61, p < .001) and ad engagement (β = .62, p = .009) are positive and significant.
People who find offers attractive and who engage with ads report a higher intention to buy.
• Brand awareness is not significant (p = .283) once the other three variables are in the model.
• Trust has a significant but negative coefficient (β = −.52, p = .037). This should not be read as “trust lowers
intention”. On its own, trust is positively linked with intention (r = .74). The negative sign appears because trust
overlaps almost completely with ad engagement (r = .93).
The overlap between predictors is a real concern. The VIF values (4.8 to 10.6) are high, and a VIF above 10 is usually
taken as a warning (Hair et al., 2019). This means the model as a whole is more reliable than the size of any single
coefficient. The residuals were slightly non-normal (Shapiro–Wilk p = .032) but there were no extreme outliers (largest
standardised residual 2.13). The ratio of about 11 cases per predictor is above the minimum of 5 but below the 15 –20
that is preferred (Hair et al., 2019). H2 is partly supported : the model is significant, but only engagement and offer
attractiveness are clear individual predictors.

7. Discussion
The results do not show a clear winner among the four channels. This agrees with the idea that channels do different
jobs at different points in the buying journey (Court et al., 2009; Lemon and Verhoef, 2016), and that no single channel
suits every consumer. The slightly higher score for e-mail / SMS / app alerts fits the view that direct messages work well
because they come from a brand the person already knows. This is only a hint, and it needs a larger sample to confirm.
The regression result is the most useful. Respondents who found offers attractive and who engaged with ads were more
likely to say they plan to buy. This agrees with earlier work that stresses engagement as a key part of digital marketing
success (Dwivedi et al., 2021), and with the link between intention and behaviour in the theory of planned behaviour
(Ajzen, 1991). It is interesting that the offer statements had the lowest average score (2.80), and yet offer attractiveness
was one of the strongest predictors. A possible reading is that offers do not appeal to everyone, but they matter a great
deal to the people who do respond to ads.
The very high correlations between variables should be read with care. Respondents who rated one group of statements
high tended to rate every group high. This can happen when people are positive or negative about digital ads in general,
and it may be made stronger by the straight-lined answers. It is also the reason why trust and awareness did not show a
clear separate effect. The study therefore cannot say that trust is unimportant. It can only say that, in this sample, trust
could not be separated from engagement.
8. Implications and Recommendations
8.1 Managerial implications
• Put offers and engagement first. Clear, attractive offers and content that people want to click, watch or share
were linked most strongly with the intention to buy. Plan the message before choosing the channel.
• Do not rely on one channel.  No channel was significantly better than the others. Test two or three channels
with small budgets and compare the real results.
• Try e-mail, SMS and app alerts.  They had the highest average likelihood of buying and are usually low in
cost. Test this with your own customers before making a large shift in budget.
• Keep building trust. Trust did not show a separate effect here, but it moved together with engagement. Reviews,
honest claims and safe checkout remain good practice.
• Measure intention and actual sales together, not only clicks and views.
8.2 Theoretical implications
The study supports the view that attitudes such as engagement and offer appeal are shared across channels and help
explain purchase intention. It also shows that these attitudes are closely related to each other, so future models should
consider combining them or testing how they influence each other, for example with structural equation modelling.
9. Limitations and Scope for Future Research
Several limitations should be kept in mind.
• Small sample. Only 46 responses were analysed. Small samples give wide confidence intervals and can miss
real differences, especially in the channel and income tests.

• Sampling. [Confirm your method.] If convenience sampling was used, the results cannot be generalised. The
sample is also mostly low-income (71.7% below ₹10,000 a month), so it may not represent all online shoppers.
• Response quality. About 22% of respondents gave the same rating to every statement, and the variables were
very strongly correlated.
• Overlapping predictors. High VIF values make individual regression coefficients unstable, and the negative
sign for trust should not be taken at face value.
• Data fields not analysed. The questionnaire also asked about the channel used most, the latest purchase channel,
purchase in the last six months and other habits, but these answers were not available in the dataset. The planned
tests of channel against actual purchase and of age group against channel preference could therefore not be done.
Channels were compared only through stated likelihood to buy.
• Self-reported and cross-sectional data. The results show association, not cause, and may be affected by recall
and social desirability bias.
Future studies could use a larger probability sample, include actual purchase data and age, and use structural equation
modelling to test how engagement, trust and offers work together.
10. Conclusion
This study looked at how engagement, trust, awareness and offers relate to the intention to buy after seeing digital ads,
and whether the likelihood of buying differs across four digital channels. Using 46 usable survey responses, it found that
respondents were neutral on average, that the four channels did not differ significantly, and that income did not matter.
The four attitudes together explained 77% of the variation in purchase intention, and offer attractiveness and ad
engagement stood out as the strongest individual predictors. For businesses, the message is to focus on strong offers and
engaging content, and to test channels instead of assuming one is best. Because of the small sample and overlapping
predictors, these findings should be confirmed with a larger and more varied sample.
