Abstract
Social media, electronic word-of-mouth, and in ﬂuencers are essential parts of our daily lives, all of which have an
impact on our shopping habits, consumer decision-making, and health consciousness. The aim of the study on the
impact of social media in ﬂuencers (SMIs) on consumer behavior was to explore the mechanisms of in ﬂuence among
Hungarian consumers, with a special emphasis on health-conscious consumer behavior. The research was based on
primary data collection, on a sample of 506 people, representative of gender, age, and type of settlement in Hungary.
Conﬁrmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was used to analyze the data within the framework of a second-order
hierarchical model. The results showed that both in ﬂuencer and social media use have a signi ﬁcant positive impact on
shopping behavior, and that in ﬂuencer credibility is the strongest in ﬂuencing factor. It is important to note that
younger, lower-educated groups with more informal schedules are more receptive to digital recommendations. The
research highlighted that Instagram and eWOM (electronic word-of-mouth) have a stronger in ﬂuence among
Hungarian consumers than TikTok. Based on the results, the credibility of an in ﬂuencer signiﬁcantly increases trust,
which drives emotional attachment and the purchase intention of consumers. The results also highlight the growing
importance of authentic interactions and perceived social connections, which are now the driving forces of digital
trust. The study provides practical recommendations for the development of targeted marketing strategies.
Introduction
The rise of social media has fundamentally transformed 21st-century communication and decision-making, evolving
from simple networking tools into in ﬂuential marketplaces. Platforms like Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok now
host social media in ﬂuencers (SMIs) who dominate this digital ecosystem. These in ﬂuencers shape public opinion
and consumer behavior through their signi ﬁcant followings, a phenomenon particularly relevant in the health-
conscious market where personal recommendations differ from traditional advertising. While idealized content offers
inspiration, it carries risks regarding misinformation. This study analyzes the mechanisms through which SMIs
inﬂuence health decisions, body image, and purchase intentions in Hungary. It seeks to determine how well inter-
national trends prevail domestically, how effects vary across demographics, and the role of trust in accepting digital
recommendations. The paper reviews literature on eWOM and in ﬂuencer marketing, de ﬁnes objectives and
hypotheses, details the methodology and results, and discusses ﬁndings to better understand Hungarian in ﬂuencer
marketing dynamics.

Literature review
Social Media and eWOM
Social media has fundamentally transformed how individuals connect and consume information (Gupta et al. 2022),
driving adoption of marketing technologies (Eze et al. 2021). Platforms such as Facebook, YouTube, Instagram,
Twitter, and TikTok are increasingly popular; Statista ( 2025) data indicates Facebook was the most used platform in
2025 with nearly 3.07 billion monthly users, followed by Instagram and TikTok. While continuous connectivity
serves as a constant stimulus (Lupinacci 2021), research highlights both bene ﬁts, like community building (Zhang
2023), and risks, including anxiety and family con ﬂict (Beyari 2023; Wilmer et al. 2017). Despite facilitating
connection, social media is linked to mental health challenges and the proliferation of idealized content (Limone and
Toto 2021). Social support is crucial, as shoppers often view information from friends as more credible than business
sources (Hu et al. 2016; Kelly et al. 2010). However, the lack of non-verbal cues in user-generated content com-
plicates trust-building (Leong et al. 2020), necessitating further studies on social support and trust (Sheikh et al. 2019;
Khaw et al. 2022). Technological advances have revolutionized communication (Saura et al. 2021; Behrad 2025),
allowing users to share opinions globally and evolving traditional word-of-mouth into electronic word-of-mouth
(eWOM) (Tran and Strutton 2020; De Boeck et al. 2022). eWOM extends beyond local circles (Sun et al. 2021),
positively in ﬂuencing social trust (Pang et al. 2024) and purchase intention (Kumar et al. 2023).
The mechanisms and in ﬂuencing effect of in ﬂuencer marketing
Today, inﬂuencer marketing is an emerging trend (Tao et al. 2019; Abdul Wahab et al. 2024) referring to an eWOM
strategy where brands collaborate with in ﬂuencers to promote businesses (Sokolova and Ke ﬁ 2020), a strategy now
central to the commercialization of social media stars (Hudders et al. 2021). Inﬂuencers act as content producers who
shape the attitudes of their target audience to generate pro ﬁt (Campbell and Grimm 2019; De Veirman et al. 2017;
Gräve 2017). The rise of these platforms has created key information sources that in ﬂuence purchasing through social
interactions (Lin et al. 2018; Sheikh et al. 2019), a phenomenon studied by several researchers (Casalo et al. 2018;
Jimenez-Castillo and Sanchez-Fernandez 2019; Ki and Kim 2019; De Veirman et al. 2017; Xiao et al. 2018).
A central mechanism of this in ﬂuence is the credibility and trust. Abdul Wahab et al. ( 2024) used a PLS-SEM
model to demonstrate that in ﬂuencer credibility signi ﬁcantly impacts followers ’ parasocial interactions and pur-
chasing behavior. This is supported by Abdul Aziz et al. ( 2023), who found that credibility in ﬂuences trust in
multichannel marketing and emotional brand engagement. Similarly, Perdana et al. ( 2023) identi ﬁed consumer trust
as a key mediator between content quality and purchase intentions, where source credibility and disclosure remain
paramount (Weismueller et al. 2020). Alwuqaysi ( 2023) further noted that this social connection fosters emotional
support, particularly regarding health and lifestyle choices.
The impact of in ﬂuencers also extends to ef ﬁciency and brand awareness. Al-Zubaidie and Al-Balaghee ( 2024)
observed that in ﬂuencers effectively guide individuals with busy schedules who avoid extensive information
searching. Several studies that have shown the positive impact of social media in ﬂuencers on purchase intent (Gautam
and Sharma 2017; Chun et al. 2018; Loureiro and Sarmento 2019; Saima and Khan 2020). Finally, the landscape is
evolving with virtual in ﬂuencers. Biason et al. ( 2024) showed that virtual in ﬂuencer marketing signi ﬁcantly in ﬂu-
ences product involvement and brand familiarity. These virtual entities are effective in building brand trust and
connecting with customers through emotional bonds, demonstrating effectiveness even in speci ﬁc sectors like the
sporting goods industry.

Research objectives and hypotheses
Literature highlights the global importance of social media, in ﬂuencer marketing, and eWOM in shaping consumer
behavior, yet gaps remain regarding empirical examinations within the Hungarian context. Consequently, the primary
objective of this study is to explore in ﬂuencer marketing mechanisms among Hungarian consumers, emphasizing
online credibility, consumer trust, and purchase intentions. The research seeks to understand the factors determining
the success of in ﬂuencer content and the role of trust. Secondary objectives include examining the mediating role of
consumer trust, analyzing the relationship between eWOM and in ﬂuencer marketing, and formulating practical
recommendations for Hungarian professionals to develop effective, trust-based collaborations.
Based on these literature and research goals, several hypotheses were formulated. H1 posits that in ﬂuencers and
social media use positively impact buying behavior; speci ﬁcally, inﬂuence is exerted indirectly through social media
use (a), though in ﬂuencers have a more signi ﬁcant impact on purchase intent than usage frequency (b), with
credibility acting as a dominant latent factor in the model (c). H2 suggests that Facebook and TikTok are the most
popular platforms. H3 proposes that consumer trust plays a mediating role between in ﬂuencer marketing exposure
and actual buying decisions among Hungarian consumers. H4 assumes signi ﬁcant demographic differences: younger
consumers (18-30) are expected to be more affected by visual platforms and in ﬂuencer attractiveness (a); lower
education and income groups may be more susceptible to in ﬂuence and perceive higher credibility (b); and those with
ﬂexible schedules, such as students, are likely more active in in ﬂuencer-driven shopping than full-time workers (c).
Finally, H5 posits that health awareness drives signi ﬁcant behavioral differences, where engagement with in ﬂuencer
products is higher among health-conscious individuals compared to those with lower awareness.
Theoretical Framework
To understand the mechanism of in ﬂuencer impact, this study utilizes the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R)
model as its primary framework. In the digital commerce environment, in ﬂuencer content —characterized by infor-
mativeness, entertainment, and social presence —serves as a “Stimulus” that triggers internal psychological changes
in the “Organism” (the consumer). These changes, such as positive affect, a state of ﬂow, and emotional attachment,
ultimately drive the “Response,” which manifests as purchase intention or impulsive buying behavior (Hongsuchon
et al. 2025). This process is heavily mediated by Source Credibility Theory, which posits that the effectiveness of a
communication depends on the perceived expertise and trustworthiness of the sender (Hovland and Weiss 1951).
Inﬂuencer marketing has evolved into a structural element of the modern advertising system, fundamentally altering
how agencies manage strategic planning and media creativity (Jiménez Marín and Fernandez-Osso Fuentes 2024).
This shift is particularly visible in the wellness and “place marketing ” sectors, where strategies focus on generating
high emotional impact. By aligning product features with consumer happiness, brands create a continuous cycle of
consumption where satisfaction is sought through innovative digital engagement (Fernández-Ossó Fuentes and
Sánchez-Gey Valenzuela 2024). Furthermore, in ﬂuencers act as digital leaders who facilitate electronic word-of-
mouth (eWOM), providing a more personal and persuasive alternative to traditional corporate messaging (Byrne et al.
2017).

In the speci ﬁc context of health communication, in ﬂuencers can promote healthy behaviors but also present
signiﬁcant risks. While some “onco-inﬂuencers” provide vital social support and boost self-esteem for patients in
niche communities (Cuenca-Octavio and Llorente-Barroso 2023), many others share nutritional advice without
professional quali ﬁcations. The promotion of “fad diets ” by non-experts can lead to nutritional de ﬁciencies and the
spread of scienti ﬁc misinformation (Byrne et al. 2017). This is especially concerning for “digital natives ” (children
and adolescents), whose interactions with social media are often driven by the Fear of Missing Out (FoMO) and the
pursuit of social standing (Elías-Zambrano et al. 2025). Scoping reviews indicate that while in ﬂuencers build strong
trust with adolescents, they frequently promote unhealthy products or unrealistic body images, requiring a focus on
media literacy and stricter regulation (Engel et al. 2024). Systematic evidence con ﬁrms that exposure to idealized
inﬂuencer imagery consistently results in body dissatisfaction and anxiety (Powell and Pring 2024). Finally, the labor
performed by in ﬂuencers occupies a complex space between private life and commercial entity, posing unique
challenges for labor law and consumer protection (Mangan 2020).
Methodology
Sample of the research and data collection
The research is based on primary data collection, the aim of which was to empirically examine the relationships
between the theoretical variables identi ﬁed in the literature (in ﬂuencer credibility, parasocial interaction, eWOM,
purchase intent) among Hungarian consumers. The data collection was carried out using an online questionnaire at
the beginning of 2025. The target group was the Hungarian population aged 18 to 65. The sample size is 506, which
is representative of the Hungarian population based on the demographic characteristics of gender, age, and type of
settlement. The age group between 18 and 65 years is covered by the widest commercial target group typical of
market research. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the
Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Economics and Business at the University of Debrecen (Ref. No. GTK-
KB 001-3/2025). Prior to accessing the survey, all participants were informed about the study ’s purpose and provided
written electronic informed consent ensuring anonimity and voluntary participation. The demographic characteristics
of the sample are summarized in Table 1.

Measurement tools and questionnaire structure
The data collection tool for the research was a structured online questionnaire. The ﬁrst part of the questionnaire
assessed the social media usage habits of the respondents. In the second part, respondents were asked to rate different
statements adapted from the literature on a 5-point Likert scale (where 1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). The
statements in the questionnaire were based on previous research by Abdul Wahab et al. ( 2024), Al-Zubaidie & Al-
Balaghee (2024), and Biason et al. ( 2024). The constructs used (latent variables) and the number of items measuring
them are shown in Table 2.
Methods of data processing
The relationships between items and blocks were explored, and measurement tools were validated using Con-
ﬁrmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). CFA is a special case of Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) (Bollen 1989;
Jöreskog et al. 2016) focused on the measurement model (Vandenberg and Lance 2000), which examines rela-
tionships between latent factors and observed indicators (Byrne 2013). Unlike Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA),
CFA tests a preliminary theoretical hypothesis rather than deriving structure from data (Brown 2015), offering more
reliable results when the theory is established. The method is particularly useful in psychometrics, questionnaire
validation, and measuring complex constructs (Kline 2015), allowing for measurement invariance studies across
groups (Vandenberg and Lance 2000). Model speci ﬁcation determines the number of factors, variable loadings, and
error correlations (Byrne 2013), often visualized via path diagrams.
CFA requires continuous, normally distributed data and careful handling of missing values (Tabachnick and Fidell
2013). Estimating the model involves calculating parameters; Maximum Likelihood (ML) is standard, but robust
methods (e.g., MLR) are recommended if normality is unmet (Hair et al. 2018). Model ﬁt is evaluated using indices
comparing the model-implied covariance matrix to the sample matrix. The most commonly used ﬁt indices are the
economy index , calculated as the quotient of Chi-squared ( χ2) and degrees of freedom (df), where a value below 3
indicates an adequate ﬁt; the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) , where a value below 0.06
indicates a good ﬁt and below 0.08 an acceptable ﬁt (Hu and Bentler 1999); and the Standardized Root Mean Squared
Residual (SRMR) , for which the acceptable value is below 0.08. Furthermore, the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and
the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) are employed to compare the ﬁt of the model to a null model, with values above 0.95
indicating a good ﬁt and those above 0.90 indicating an acceptable ﬁt (Hu and Bentler 1999). Finally, the analysis
incorporates the C90, which is the 90% con ﬁdence interval for the RMSEA value, and pClose/pFar values , which

Results
To analyze the data, Con ﬁrmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted within the framework of Structural
Equation Modeling (SEM), using the lavaan package in R. The aim of the research was to construct a complex,
second-order hierarchical model. This model comprises two levels: at the ﬁrst level, the individual social media
platforms (Facebook, Instagram, TikTok) and eWOM are modeled separately as latent variables, which are captured
by a single higher construct, “Social Media Use ”, at the second level. A similar hierarchical structure was applied to
the main constructs “Inﬂuencer Effect” (PI, AT, TR subgroups) and “Purchasing Habits and behaviors ” (SMIC, PB,
PIN, BD, INT subgroups). The prerequisite for specifying a second-order model is that the number of ﬁrst-order
factors must be at least three, a condition met in all cases in the present study. Prior to specifying the ﬁnal model, a
ﬁrst-order CFA was performed separately for each main construct in order to validate the substructures. The nor-
mality of the variables was examined using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the condition of multivariate normality was
examined with the Energy Test (using the energy package). Due to the violation of the assumption of multivariate
normality, the more robust Unweighted Least Squares (ULS) method was used to estimate model parameters instead
of Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation. To determine standard errors and signi ﬁcance levels, 500 bootstrap
samples were used. The survey included a total of 63 items; the rate of missing data ranged from 0.4% to 6.7%.
Because this rate is considered low, we handled missing data via mean imputation. The ﬁt of the models was
evaluated using the following indices: χ2/df (Chi-squared/degrees of freedom), RMSEA (Root Mean Square Error of

Discussion
The results of the CFA models suggest that social media in ﬂuencers (SMIs) exert a signi ﬁcant in ﬂuence on the
purchasing behavior of Hungarian consumers. The research validated theories of digital consumer behavior positing
that social interactions and visual stimuli are strong purchasing incentives (Samala and Rawas 2024; An and Ngo
2025). Our ﬁnding that in ﬂuencer credibility is the most decisive factor aligns with the structural importance of
inﬂuencers in modern digital strategy identi ﬁed by Jiménez Marín and Fernandez-Osso Fuentes ( 2024). By applying
the S-O-R model, we can see that in ﬂuencer content acts as a stimulus that triggers an emotional “organism” state—
speciﬁcally emotional attachment —which then leads to the purchase response (Hongsuchon et al. 2025). The high
level of engagement observed in the Hungarian sample mirrors the “happiness-driven” consumption cycles described
by Fernández-Ossó Fuentes and Sánchez-Gey Valenzuela ( 2024), where emotional resonance often supersedes
rational product evaluation.
The study also corroborates the research showing that in ﬂuencers impact consumer decision-making (Shaheen
et al. 2025; Balakrishnan et al. 2025). Results demonstrated that both the in ﬂuencer effect and social media use have a
signiﬁcant positive impact on purchasing behavior, with the former being much more powerful than the mere
intensity of media use (Djurica and Mendling 2020; Ramadanty et al. 2020). This suggests consumers make decisions
based on personal, credible information rather than solely on the intensity of media consumption. The primacy of
credibility in our model supports the classic Source Credibility Theory (Hovland & Weiss 1951), con ﬁrming that
trustworthiness is the bedrock of digital persuasion. The in ﬂuencer effect was the strongest explanatory factor in
developing purchase intentions, exceeding the direct effect of social media use, consistent with ﬁndings by (Sun et al.
2025) and further supported by the insights from Garg and Bakshi ( 2024), who highlighted the importance of
interactional elements used by beauty vloggers in in ﬂuencing consumer behavior. Notably, the in ﬂuencer effect
impacts purchases not only directly but also through the intensity of media use as a mediator. This means the presence
of in ﬂuencers encourages more intensive platform use, which itself generates purchase impulses; thus, social media
functions as both a communication space and a behavior-shaping channel. This observation underpins the integrated
functioning of the digital ecosystem highlighted by (Afzal et al. 2024; Nguyen and Nguyen 2025). The research
demonstrated that in ﬂuencer credibility is the most important factor in the in ﬂuence process; the loading on the
Trustworthiness factor was extremely strong. This con ﬁrms previous results of other researchers (Khurana et al. 2025;
Byrne et al. 2017) and underscores the risks associated with non-quali ﬁed in ﬂuencers sharing unscienti ﬁc or mis-
leading nutritional advice, which can lead to unnecessary dietary restrictions and potential nutritional de ﬁciencies
among the general public.
Regarding platform preferences, eWOM and Instagram dominated in ﬂuencing factors in the Hungarian sample,
while the role of TikTok and Facebook was relatively smaller, differing from international trends (Cruz-Cardenas
et al. 2025; Mehta et al. 2025; Reyes-Menendez et al. 2020). Demographic analysis identi ﬁed additional segments.
The younger age group and women constitute the most active target group, especially on Instagram and TikTok (An
and Ngo 2025; Ates et al. 2025; Hung et al. 2025). This supports the observations of Elías-Zambrano et al. ( 2025),
who argue that younger “native” consumers use these platforms as a primary tool for identity construction. At the
same time, the impact on purchase intentions is weaker in the older age group. Educational attainment is also an
inﬂuencing factor: those with lower education are more susceptible to in ﬂuencers (Papadakis et al. 2020; Bellacicco
and Pavone 2020). These lower-educated groups ’ higher trust in digital recommendations suggests a speci ﬁc vul-
nerability to the “unhealthy product promotion” often found in inﬂuencer content (Powell and Pring 2024; Engel et al.
2024). Differences in employment status were also signi ﬁcant, with groups having ﬂexible schedules (students, those

on maternity leave) being more active in in ﬂuencer-driven processes. Finally, marked differences were observed
based on health awareness. Among those claiming to be health-conscious, there was a higher level of commitment to

Facebook and TikTok, highlighting the importance of media literacy and misinformation risks.
Overall, this research con ﬁrms that inﬂuencer marketing is a universal phenomenon, yet local cultural and platform
usage differences modify its intensity. For Hungarian consumers, the role of credibility, social support, and eWOM is
particularly strong, suggesting that trust and emotional attachment remain the most important drivers of digital
inﬂuence. The research highlighted that in Hungary, the co-presence of visual content and existing social networks
(Instagram, Facebook) continues to have a stronger impact than newer, dynamic platforms. A speci ﬁc feature of the
Hungarian model is that the role of consumer trust is even more pronounced than in international research, indicating
a local market characteristic contrasting with global trends. This difference is likely explained by the higher degree of
distrust among Hungarian consumers towards advertising, which is partially compensated by the personal commu-
nication style of in ﬂuencers.
Conclusions
The Con ﬁrmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) method was employed to explore the complex structure behind social
media use, the in ﬂuencer effect, and consumer purchasing decisions. The data indicate that engagement with both
inﬂuencers and social media has a signi ﬁcant positive effect on the willingness to buy. This is consistent with digital
consumer behavior theories emphasizing the role of personal in ﬂuence and social proof in the online environment. A
key ﬁnding is that the credibility of an in ﬂuencer is a more decisive factor than the mere frequency of platform use.
The ﬁndings suggest that consumers are more likely to accept recommendations from individuals they perceive as
trustworthy and whose communication remains consistent. Therefore, brands should work with in ﬂuencers who
reﬂect credibility derived not merely from reach but from consumer perception. It is important not only to ask for
product endorsements but to allow in ﬂuencers to present the product in their own voice, building on their stories to
strengthen the credibility identi ﬁed as a high-impact factor.
The CFA results also show that the in ﬂuencer effect works indirectly through the intensity of media use. Con-
sequently, in ﬂuencers should be encouraged to engage followers as actively as possible —for example, through live
videos, Q&A sessions, or interactive stories. The higher the activity within the platform, the stronger the impact on
purchasing. Platform preferences also provided important lessons. In the Hungarian sample, eWOM and Instagram
proved to be the strongest in ﬂuencing channels, while TikTok and Facebook played a relatively smaller role. This
suggests classic social media platforms remain dominant, especially among users who are older or possess higher
education. Consequently, brands should use different communication styles per platform: on Instagram, visually
strong, lifestyle-oriented content works, whereas on Facebook, detailed, information-rich posts are preferred. For
TikTok, especially among lower-educated and less health-conscious groups, fast, entertaining content proves
effective.
Demographic segments provide further clues. Women and the younger age group are the most responsive target
groups, reacting strongly to Instagram and TikTok. For them, short, visually appealing, trend-driven communication
is ideal. Conversely, interest in in ﬂuencers diminishes among older users, so traditional, rational approaches are more
effective. For them, Facebook is the right platform, where credibility is strengthened by detailed information and
reviews. Educational attainment is also relevant: groups with lower education are more susceptible to in ﬂuencer-
driven incentives and use Facebook and TikTok more often. For them, simple, easy-to-understand messages
emphasizing everyday bene ﬁts are best. However, for users with higher education, eWOM and Instagram dominate,
so content offering professional credibility or quality visuals works best.
Differences based on health consciousness allow for further ﬁne-tuning. Research shows a greater willingness to
buy recommended products among health-conscious groups, who prefer Instagram and eWOM. For them, involving
expert opinions, detailing ingredients, or presenting lifestyle examples is effective. Less health-conscious groups are

more active on Facebook and TikTok, where emotional or humorous content works well. Overall, the results suggest
that a segmented, platform-speci ﬁc, and credibility-based strategy is essential for effective digital in ﬂuence.
Based on these ﬁndings, several managerial and policy recommendations can be formulated. For marketing
professionals in Hungary, the results suggest that “reach” is a secondary metric to “credibility.” Because Hungarian
consumers show high distrust toward traditional advertising, brands should prioritize long-term partnerships with
inﬂuencers who possess genuine professional expertise, especially in the health and wellness sectors. Managers
should focus on Instagram and eWOM strategies, leveraging “social presence ” and “informativeness” as stimuli to
trigger the necessary emotional attachment for purchase. Content should focus on self-esteem and well-being rather
than just product utility, as this drives the happiness-based consumption cycles identi ﬁed in recent literature. From a
policy perspective, the ﬁndings highlight an urgent need for enhanced regulation of digital health communication.
Policy makers should implement stricter disclosure requirements for in ﬂuencers providing health or nutrition advice,
ensuring that those without scienti ﬁc quali ﬁcations are clearly identi ﬁed. Improving “social media literacy ” is
essential to protect younger “digital natives ” and lower-educated demographics from the negative impacts of idea-
lized body imagery and misinformation. Furthermore, the legal distinction between an in ﬂuencer’s private life and
commercial activity must be clari ﬁed to ensure consumer protection laws remain enforceable. The primary limitation
of the research is the geographical restriction to a Hungarian sample; future research directions should include studies
in other countries to draw widely generalizable conclusions about in ﬂuencer marketing.
