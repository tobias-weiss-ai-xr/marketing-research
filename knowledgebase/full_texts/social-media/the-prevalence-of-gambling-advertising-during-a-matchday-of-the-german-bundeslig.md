Abstract
Gambling advertising is prevalent in sports, especially football. Sponsoring teams,
leagues, and national and international competitions is an effective marketing strategy
for gambling providers to reach a wide audience. This ‘gamblification’ of football poses
risks to public health, particularly for vulnerable groups. To protect the population from
gambling-related harm, the State Treaty on Gambling stipulates that gambling advertising
must not be excessive and must not target at-risk groups. Therefore, this study conducted
an initial investigation of gambling advertising during a German Bundesliga matchday.
Nine matches from the 31st matchday of the 2024/2025 Bundesliga season were anal -
ysed for the frequency and content of gambling advertising. The analysis covered live
television broadcasts, pre- and post-match coverage, half-time, and commercial breaks.
Two sports highlight programmes were examined, and social media activity was evalu -
ated by reviewing the accounts of ten sports betting providers and using the Meta-Ad
Library. The prevalence of gambling advertising was substantial, with 18,708 references
identified. Most references occurred during live broadcasts on pitchside hoardings with
minimal implementation of social responsibility measures. Providers predominantly use
paid-for advertising on social media to target a young male demographic. This study uses
a Bundesliga matchday to illustrate the role of gambling advertising in German football
events. The scope, content, and strategies of such advertising raise significant concerns,
particularly regarding the potential risks posed to children and adolescents. These findings
can assist policymakers in evaluating whether the current regulations designed to protect
the public from gambling-related harm are sufficiently robust.

Introduction
The global availability and accessibility of gambling have contributed to the widespread
presence of gambling advertising across many countries (Wardle et al., 2024). The mar -
keting of gambling is particularly prominent in sports (Newall et al., 2019), reflecting the
increasing integration of gambling into sports contexts through marketing, sponsorship, and
media coverage. This process is referred to as the ‘gamblification of sports’ (McMullan &
Miller, 2008), which extends beyond advertising (Macey & Hamari, 2024). It is evident
in sports such as rugby (Columb et al., 2023) and boxing (Purves et al., 2020), as well as
basketball and ice hockey (Rossi et al., 2025; Wheaton et al., 2024), but most notably in
football (Lopez-Gonzalez & Griffiths, 2018; McGee, 2020; Purves et al., 2020). Gambling
providers use a variety of marketing strategies to raise brand awareness. Sponsoring teams,
leagues, and competitions serves as an effective strategy for reaching a broad audience
(Gainsbury et al., 2015; Hsiao et al., 2021) and aligning with the emotional connections that
sports fans have with teams and events (Purves et al., 2017, 2018).
Gambling marketing is used in various forms and media channels (Guillou-Landreat
et al., 2021; Newall et al., 2019; Rossi & Nairn, 2022; Singer et al., 2024; Torrance et al.,
2021), for example, as logos on team shirts (Bunn et al., 2019), in matchday programmes
(Sharman et al., 2020, 2023), or on pitchside hoardings (Purves et al., 2020). It also appears
in the form of commercial ad breaks (Newall et al., 2019) or as part of TV highlight shows
(Cassidy & Ovenden, 2017). In digital media, gambling marketing can be found in the
form of direct marketing (Jones et al., 2020) and smartphone applications (Syvertsen et al.,
2022). Finally, gambling marketing is present on social media (Singer et al., 2024), affiliates
(Houghton et al., 2019), and via the accounts of gambling providers (Rossi & Nairn, 2022).
Gambling marketing is prevalent in football, evident in international competitions such
as the UEFA European Championship (Newall et al., 2022) and the FIFA World Cup (Shar-
man et al., 2024), as well as in national football leagues such as the English Premier League
(Rossi et al., 2023, 2024; Rossi et al., 2025; Torrance et al., 2023). The Bundesliga also
presents attractive marketing opportunities for gambling providers. It is the highest division
in German professional football and consists of 18 clubs that compete against each other
for 34 matchdays. In the 2024/25 season, it achieved its highest-ever revenue of over 5.1
billion euros, with more than half derived from advertising and media rights sales (DFL
Deutsche Fußball Liga GmbH, 2026). It is also among the most popular football leagues
in Europe in terms of spectator attendance (DFL Deutsche Fußball Liga GmbH, 2025b). In
the 2024/25 season, over 11.5 million tickets were sold, averaging over 38,000 tickets per
match (DFL Deutsche Fußball Liga GmbH, 2025a). The most-watched live television game
attracted 2.38 million viewers (DFL Deutsche Fußball Liga GmbH, 2025a). Moreover, all
18 Bundesliga clubs had at least one gambling provider as an official sponsor in the pre -
ceding season. Furthermore, both the Bundesliga and the German Football Association are
sponsored by a sports betting provider.1
The State Treaty on Gambling, enacted in 2021, enabled the link between German pro -
fessional football and gambling (Staatsvertrag zur Neuregulierung des Glücksspielwesens in
Deutschland: Glücksspielstaatsvertrag 2021—GlüStV 2021, 2021). The State Treaty on Gam-
bling facilitated the liberalisation of the German gambling market, resulting in the legalisation

of previously prohibited forms of gambling, such as virtual slot machines and online sports
betting. However, this is not without problems, as they are considered two of the riskiest forms
of gambling in Germany (Buth et al., 2026). Conversely, all licenced gambling providers are
now authorised to advertise their products, including sponsorships. The purpose of gambling
advertising under the State Treaty on Gambling is to serve a channelling function by guiding
individuals interested in gambling towards licensed providers, thereby reducing their exposure
to illegal and unregulated gambling offers (Staatsvertrag zur Neuregulierung des Glücksspiel-
wesens in Deutschland: Glücksspielstaatsvertrag 2021—GlüStV 2021, 2021). Furthermore,
these providers are supervised by the German Gambling Supervisory Authority (GGL) and
are monitored to ensure compliance with legal regulations.
However, both gambling and gambling advertising pose risks to public health (Wardle et
al., 2024). A growing body of research provides evidence of a positive association between
exposure to gambling advertising and the intention to try gambling, as well as increased
gambling activity (McGrane et al., 2023). A high volume of gambling advertising can lead
to the normalisation of gambling (Gainsbury et al., 2015), whereby gambling is perceived as
a harmless leisure activity (Deans et al., 2017; Nyemcsok et al., 2021), fostering unrealistic
expectations of winning and positive impulses toward gambling (Rossi & Nairn, 2022).
This is of particular concern for vulnerable groups, including children, adolescents, young
adults (McGrane et al., 2023), and those who already suffer from gambling-related disorders
(Syvertsen et al., 2022).
In Germany, the prevalence of gambling participation over the past 12 months was
36.4% of the total population (Buth et al., 2026). Of the total population, 5.5% exhibited
risky gambling behaviour, while 2.2% experienced gambling-related disorders. It is particu-
larly concerning that young adults are the most affected demographic group, with 4.6% of
individuals aged 18–25 years impacted. Furthermore, 1.2% of those aged 16–17 years have
already experienced the consequences of gambling-related disorders (Buth et al., 2026). The
primary objective of the State Treaty on Gambling is to safeguard the population from gam-
bling-related harm, with a particular focus on implementing effective safeguards for indi -
viduals participating in gambling activities and for youth (Staatsvertrag zur Neuregulierung
des Glücksspielwesens in Deutschland: Glücksspielstaatsvertrag 2021—GlüStV 2021,
2021).2 It seeks to prevent the development of gambling and betting addictions by laying
the foundations for effective addiction prevention. In this context, regulations on gambling
advertising have been established. 3 Such advertising must align with the core objective of
the State Treaty on Gambling; specifically, it must not be excessive and must not target
minors or other similarly vulnerable groups. Additionally, efforts should be made to exclude
minors from gambling advertising exposure. Misleading advertising is strictly prohibited;
advertisements must not be presented as editorial content, and gambling outcomes must
not be depicted as being influenced by the player. Furthermore, gambling should not be
portrayed as a solution to financial problems.

These regulations also apply to social media, which has established itself as an attrac -
tive advertising platform (Gainsbury et al., 2016; Singer et al., 2024; Torrance et al., 2021).
Social media allows gambling providers to promote their brands within a global network
and directly engage with users (Gainsbury et al., 2015). The aim is to attract potential cus -
tomers and retain existing ones (Gainsbury et al., 2015), thereby enhancing brand awareness
and brand loyalty (Barreda et al., 2015). Existing studies indicate a high volume of gam -
bling advertising on social media (Guillou-Landreat et al., 2021; Newall et al., 2019; Rossi
& Nairn, 2022; Torrance et al., 2021), with posts from gambling providers containing posi-
tive sentiments (Bradley & James, 2019) and humourous elements (Houghton et al., 2019).
However, informative elements are also combined with less neutral content (Singer et al.,
2022), blurring the line between neutral content and advertising (Singer et al., 2024). Fur-
thermore, content marketing is increasingly being adopted by providers as a primary adver-
tising strategy (Rossi et al., 2021). Unlike conventional advertisements, content marketing
rarely includes explicit references to gambling products. Instead, it focuses on fostering
user engagement and enhancing shareability to extend the reach of content on social media
(Rossi & Nairn, 2022). The accounts and content of sports betting providers are particularly
popular among young people (Rossi et al., 2021). Given the risks associated with gambling
advertising, especially for children, adolescents, and young adults (McGrane et al., 2023), it
is concerning that the most active social media users in Germany are aged 14–29 (Tippelt,
2025). While just over half (63%) of the general population uses social media weekly, this
figure increases to 91% among young people, with 66% of those aged 14–29 years using
social media daily (Tippelt, 2025).
This study investigates the connection between gambling and sports in German profes -
sional football based on the conceptual framework of the ‘gamblification of sports’ (McMul-
lan & Miller, 2008). Drawing on McCracken’s ( 1986, 1988) ‘Meaning Transfer Model’
(MTM), the attributes or characteristics of cultural goods can be transferred to other objects
through contact and, in a further step, to individual consumers. Consequently, consumer-
good producers invest resources to ensure that potential consumers perceive their products
as carriers of positive attributes (Jain & Roy, 2016). When MTM is applied to the ‘gambli-
fication of sports’ (McMullan & Miller, 2008), it results in a symbolic association between
sports and gambling, whereby positive attributes and characteristics of sports are transferred
onto gambling products, such as sports betting (Lopez-Gonzalez et al., 2021). For example,
attributes such as skill, training, and performance, which are central to professional sport,
may be transferred to sports betting, creating the perception that successful betting is based
on knowledge and expertise rather than chance (Lopez-Gonzalez et al., 2021). Advertis-
ing is a central indicator of the symbolic construction of sports betting (McCracken, 1986,
1988) and a useful starting point for examining the symbolic connection between sports
and gambling (Lopez-Gonzalez et al., 2021). In this regard, while the content and attributes
conveyed in gambling advertisements serve as the mechanism through which positive sport-
ing attributes are transferred to gambling products, it is primarily the cumulative presence
and pervasive extent of gambling marketing that drives the normalisation process (Lopez-
Gonzalez & Griffiths, 2018). This is also consistent with cultivation theory (Gerbner &
Gross, 1976; Gerbner et al., 1980), according to which it is not the content of an individual
message, but rather the cumulative and repeated exposure to media content that shapes
viewers’ perception of social reality over time. Frequent exposure to gambling advertising
during football broadcasts can therefore lead to an overestimation of the normality and an

underestimation of the risks associated with sports betting. The pervasive and widespread
prevalence of gambling marketing and sponsorship has led to the normalisation of gam -
bling in sports (McGee, 2020), portraying it as a harmless aspect of sports and reducing
risk awareness (Lopez-Gonzalez et al., 2021). Research conducted by Pitt et al., ( 2016)
and Djohari et al., (2019) demonstrates that even minors view gambling as a typical aspect
of sports events. Consequently, the symbolic association between sports and gambling is
particularly problematic for vulnerable groups, such as children, adolescents, and young
adults, as well as individuals suffering from gambling-related disorders (Lopez-Gonzalez
& Griffiths, 2018).
Gambling marketing is prevalent in German-licensed football and presents potential pub-
lic health risks, particularly for vulnerable populations. The State Treaty on Gambling aims
to protect the public from gambling-related harm by establishing regulations on advertising.
This study on gambling marketing within the German Football Bundesliga seeks to inform
policymakers and regulators about the extent of gambling advertising and assess compli -
ance with legal standards. Additionally, there is a need to expand the international literature
on gambling marketing. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate
gambling marketing in German professional football clubs.
Therefore, this study addresses the following research questions:
RQ1: What is the frequency and content of gambling advertisements during matchday
31 of the 2024/25 Bundesliga season?
RQ2: How do marketing and advertising strategies contribute to the normalisation of
gambling, particularly among children, adolescents and young adults?
RQ3: To what extent do gambling advertisements adhere to the regulatory requirements
set out in the State Treaty on Gambling, particularly regarding the protection of minors
and other vulnerable groups?
Methodology
Study Design
This study involved a frequency and content analysis of gambling advertisements during a
German Bundesliga football matchday. Drawing on comparable studies, the design included
an examination of live TV broadcasts of football matches and sports highlight programmes,
as well as additional live content and an analysis of social media.
Selection of Broadcasts, Sports Highlight Programmes and Social Media
All nine matches on the 31 st matchday of the Bundesliga, held from April 25 to April 27,
were recorded (s. Table 1). Each recording included the entire match, encompassing pre-
and post-match coverage as well as half-time and commercial breaks. Owing to shared
broadcasting rights, the matches were recorded on two private television channels. The six
Saturday matches were recorded on Sky Sport, whereas the Friday and two Sunday matches
were recorded on DAZN.

Coding
The codebook employed to identify and analyse exposure to gambling advertising during
broadcasts, additional live content, and sports highlight programmes was adopted from pre-
vious studies (Rossi et al., 2023, 2024; Rossi et al., 2025) to enable direct comparisons and
is primarily based on the work of Purves et al., ( 2020), which draws on previous research
on sports betting, alcohol and tobacco advertising in sports (Adams et al., 2014; Graham
& Adams, 2014; Lindsay et al., 2013; Purves et al., 2017; Thomas et al., 2012). Across
this body of research, similar variables and coding categories have been used to identify
and classify advertising exposure in different sports and sporting events, including national
leagues and international tournaments, indicating methodological continuity over time.
Table 5 presents the variables used in the codebook.
Following the methodology outlined by Purves et al., ( 2020), each verbal or visual ref -
erence to a gambling activity, brand, or logo that lasted at least one second was identified
as a gambling reference. Whenever a gambling reference appeared within a camera shot,
the duration of that scene was defined as an episode, lasting until all gambling references
were no longer visible. Within each episode, all relevant variables were recorded, and the
maximum number of gambling references visible simultaneously was noted. An episode
ended with each camera cut, at which point a new episode commenced if the gambling
reference remained identifiable in the subsequent shot. Accordingly, if the same gambling
reference reappeared after a camera change, it was recorded as a new episode. If multiple
gambling references from different brands or formats were visible simultaneously, each was
recorded as a unique observation. If multiple identical references were visible within the
same episode, for example, the same brand logo appearing on several pitchside hoardings,
the maximum number of visible references was noted as a separate variable.

Results
During the 31 st matchday of the German football Bundesliga, held from April 25 to 27,
a total of 18,708 gambling references were identified (s. Table 9). The following section
examines gambling advertising by considering three dimensions: duration, scope, and type.
The analysis began with an examination of the broadcasts of individual matches, encom -
passing pre- and post-match coverage, as well as halftime and commercial breaks. This was
followed by an analysis of sports highlight programmes and additional live content, and
concluded with an examination of gambling advertising on social media.
Gambling Advertising During Television Broadcast
Duration
The total recording time for the nine Bundesliga matches was 1,526.4 min (s. Table 10).
During this period, gambling references were visible for 498.4 min, accounting for just
under a third (32.7%) of the total recording time. The duration of individual match record -
ings varied, ranging from 140.4 min ( FC Bayern München versus FSV Mainz 05) to 217.8
min (Eintracht Frankfurt versus RB Leipzig). The total duration of gambling episodes per
match ranged from 6.9 min (FC Bayern München versus FSV Mainz 05) to 139.9 min (VfB
Stuttgart versus 1. FC Heidenheim ), indicating that during this match broadcast of 199.5
min, gambling references were visible for 139.9 min. On average, gambling references
appeared for 55.4 min per match. The share of gambling episodes per match ranged from
4.9% (FC Bayern München versus FSV Mainz 05) to 77.4% (Holstein Kiel versus Borussia
Mönchengladbach).

Paid-for Advertising
In total, 130 paid-for advertisements were identified, constituting 58.3% of all gambling
advertisements on social media. Of these, 119 (91.5%) were classified as conventional
advertisements, whereas 11 (8.5%) were categorised as content marketing (s. Table 12).
In paid-for advertising, the differences in marketing design are minimal (s. Table 13).
Neither content marketing nor conventional advertisements included references to pop cul-
ture or mentions of well-known sports personalities or active athletes. Humour is sparsely
used in conventional advertisements (4.2%), whereas references to the current Bundesliga
matchday are significantly more common in content marketing (36.4%). Emojis were found
in all content marketing (100.0%) and were predominantly used in conventional advertising
(79.8%).
There are major differences in the information about gambling (s. Table 13). Of the con-
tent marketing, 27.3% included a signpost to the method of gambling and offered a bonus
or inducement but did not provide any odds for sports betting. In contrast, all conventional
advertisements included a signpost to the method of gambling (100.0%), and nearly all
offered bonuses or inducements (88.5%). Additionally, 15.4% presented betting odds.
Social responsibility measures were fully implemented, with one exception (s. Table
13). All paid-for advertisements included age restrictions and harm reduction information.
Furthermore, while all conventional advertisements were clearly identifiable as advertising,
this was the case for only slightly more than half (54.5%) of the content marketing.
In total, paid-for advertising reached over 10.3 million users on Facebook and Insta -
gram (s. Table 14). The primary demographic targets were males (90.8%) and younger age
groups, specifically individuals aged 18–24 years (30.1%) and 25–34 years (36.0%). Most
of these paid-for advertisements were commissioned by two sports betting companies:
Betano (62.6%) and Tipico (21.5%).
Discussion
This study is the first to examine the frequency and content of gambling advertising on a
matchday of the German Bundesliga during the 2024/25 season, including TV broadcasts,
sports highlight programmes, additional live content, and social media. In total, 18,708
gambling references were identified. Of these, 15,089 occurred during live TV broadcasts
of individual matches, 1,478 in sports news, and 1,918 in additional live content. A total of
223 gambling references were observed on the social media platforms Facebook and Insta-
gram. These results are consistent with existing research and highlight the high volume of

Using the example of the selected Bundesliga match day, the ‘gamblification of sports’
(McMullan & Miller, 2008), in this case, football, becomes apparent, as viewers are continuously
exposed to high levels of gambling advertising, especially during live TV broadcasts. Accord-
ing to Lopez-Gonzalez and Griffiths (2018), this exposure can create a symbolic association
between sports and gambling, leading to normalisation, where gambling is seen as a harmless
aspect of sports, thereby reducing risk perception. Per match, the duration for which gambling
messages are visible exceeds one half of the match itself at 55.4 min. Even when excluding pre-
and post-game coverage, as well as halftime and commercial breaks, the average duration of
advertising messages per game is 40.9 min—nearly as long as one half-time of the match itself.
This adds up to a total of 498.4 min, which corresponds to 32.7% of the total recording time.
On average, a new scene containing at least one gambling reference appears every 20.9 s, with
an average of 9.9 gambling references per minute. Compared to three studies from the United
Kingdom, this rate falls within the middle range (s. Table 15); during the opening weekend of
the Premier League in the 23/24 season, an average of 4.8 gambling references per minute was
identified (Rossi et al., 2023), 16.7 in the 24/25 season (Rossi et al., 2024), and 12.6 in the 25/26
season (Rossi, Moradipour, et al., 2025). Although Germany has introduced a comparatively
more restrictive gambling advertising framework under the State Treaty on Gambling, which
establishes legally binding restrictions on gambling advertising in contrast to the largely self-
regulatory approach of the United Kingdom (Rossi et al., 2024), exposure rates remain broadly
comparable to those observed in the UK, a market widely regarded as having a permissive
regulatory stance (Sharman et al., 2024). This near-constant exposure is particularly relevant
given evidence that gambling has become a normalised aspect of sports fandom (McGee, 2020)
and that greater advertising exposure is linked to more positive attitudes towards gambling and
increased gambling activity (McGrane et al., 2023). This suggests that regulatory intent does
not directly lead to reduced advertising exposure, and that broadcast-specific restrictions may be
necessary to achieve significant reductions in exposure.

The high volume of gambling advertising is problematic because it can lead to the nor -
malisation of gambling (Gainsbury, King, Abarbanel, et al., 2015). This can create the
impression that gambling is a harmless leisure activity, similar to any other activity (Deans
et al., 2017; Nyemcsok et al., 2021). This is especially evident among football fans, who
often establish a connection between sports and gambling (Hing et al., 2023; Jones et al.,
2020; McGee, 2020; Nyemcsok et al., 2021), as well as among children and adolescents
(Djohari et al., 2019; Pitt et al., 2016). In addition, exposure to gambling advertising can
have a positive influence on the intention to gamble and gambling behaviour (McGrane et
al., 2023). This is particularly true for children and adolescents (McGrane et al., 2023) as
well as for people who suffer from gambling-related disorders (Syvertsen et al., 2022). The
widespread distribution of gambling advertisements across various media channels makes it
possible to reach the largest possible audience (Gainsbury et al., 2015; Hsiao et al., 2021),
as well as vulnerable groups who are particularly in need of protection.
According to Rossi et al., ( 2021), accounts of sports betting providers are particularly
popular among children and young audiences on social media. It can thus be assumed that
young people in Germany are also exposed to gambling advertising on social media, as
14–29-year-olds are the most active group of social media users (Tippelt, 2025). Regard-
less, paid-for advertising on Facebook and Instagram has reached over ten million users (s.
Table 13). Here, a young audience is specifically targeted, with nearly a third (30.1%) of all
paid-for advertisements aimed at 18–24-year-olds. This is concerning, as young adults in
Germany are most vulnerable to gambling-related harm, with 4.6% of 18- to 25-year-olds
affected (Buth et al., 2026). The fact that nearly a third of all paid advertisements specifi -
cally target the age group most vulnerable to gambling-related harm in Germany highlights
a significant intersection between commercial advertising practices and public health risks.
This targeted exposure may further reinforce the symbolic association between gambling
and sport, contributing to the normalisation of gambling, whereby it is increasingly per -
ceived as a harmless part of sports consumption and risk awareness may decline (Lopez-
Gonzalez & Griffiths, 2018). Thus, specific regulatory attention to social media advertising
practices in Germany may be warranted.
More than one third (37.6%) of all organic advertisements consisted of content marketing
(s. Table 12), which has become a central advertising strategy for sports betting providers
(Rossi & Nairn, 2022; Rossi et al., 2021). Unlike traditional advertising, the aim of content
advertising is not to directly promote a product or brand. Instead, the focus is on encourag-
ing user engagement and enhancing content shareability to maximise reach. As shown in
Table 13, content marketing offers minimal information on odds, bonuses and incentives.
To encourage users to share content within their personal networks, content marketing often
features entertaining elements such as information or statistics about the current Bundesliga
matchday. Similarly, showcasing active athletes or well-known sports personalities helps to
convey a positive image and may contribute to a symbolic association between gambling
and sport, as the positive attributes associated with athletes are transferred, for example,
to sports betting products (Lopez-Gonzalez et al., 2021). The prevalent use of emojis in
content marketing suggests that gambling providers prioritise visual appeal and emotional
engagement (s. Table 13).
The fact that content marketing has established itself as a central advertising strategy is
problematic. It often appears harmless and neutral or positive, for example, in the form of
information or statistics, to attract as many users as possible on social networks. However,

this blurs the boundaries between advertising and neutral content and poses a particular risk
to children and adolescents, who often struggle to identify content marketing as advertising
(Rossi & Nairn, 2025), yet find this content appealing, which can foster positive attitudes
and impulses toward gambling (Rossi & Nairn, 2024).
Finally, the implementation of social responsibility measures should be improved. During
live TV broadcasts, only 3.3% of all gambling advertisements included age restriction and
harm reduction messages (s. Table 15). This rate is considerably lower than those observed
in the United Kingdom across all three comparable seasons (Rossi et al., 2023, 2024; Rossi
et al., 2025), even though the UK is known for its liberal policy towards gambling advertis-
ing (Sharman et al., 2024). This discrepancy can be attributed to the absence of mandatory
labelling requirements under Germany's regulatory framework, as German law does not
require the inclusion of harm reduction or age restriction messages on pitchside hoardings
or players' shirts, highlighting a specific gap in the State Treaty on Gambling legislation.
In contrast, social responsibility measures on social media are almost fully implemented
(s. Table 13), as they are mandatory requirements set by the gambling regulatory authori -
ty.9 Consequently, the warning notices from gambling providers are typically structured
similarly. They generally include an age restriction, a caution about the addictive nature of
gambling, a statement confirming the provider's licensing and legality, and a URL direct -
ing to a website that serves as an initial contact point for family members and individuals
affected by gambling issues. However, the positive effects of warning notices are currently
under discussion (Newall et al., 2022a, b; Newall et al., 2025a, b), particularly with regard
to vulnerable individuals (Newall et al., 2025a, b).
Overall, the findings of this study indicate a high volume of gambling advertising, lim -
ited social responsibility measures, and targeted social media marketing strategies that are
associated with risks. The results reveal that the volume and reach of gambling advertising
in Germany, despite the relatively recent implementation of a regulatory framework, are
largely comparable to those in markets generally considered liberally regulated (Sharman et
al., 2024). Drawing on the conceptual framework of the ‘gamblification of sports ‘ (McMul-
lan & Miller, 2008), this pattern is of concern, as the widespread prevalence of gambling
marketing and sponsorship may contribute to the normalisation of gambling, whereby it
is perceived as a harmless part of sports consumption and the risks associated with it are
underestimated. In this regard, it is the extent and pervasive presence of gambling market -
ing and sponsorship, rather than the specific content of individual messages, that is most
consequential. This symbolic association between sports and gambling is of particular rel -
evance to vulnerable groups (Lopez-Gonzalez & Griffiths, 2018). Therefore, the findings
suggest that the current regulatory framework does not sufficiently limit exposure to gam -
bling advertising, particularly among children, adolescents, and young adults.
Limitations and Future Directions
This study has several limitations. First, it focuses solely on a single matchday in the Ger -
man Bundesliga. Future research could include multiple matchdays to facilitate compari -
sons and offer a more extensive dataset. Similarly, as demonstrated by Rossi et al., ( 2023),

Rossi et al., ( 2024) and Rossi et al., ( 2025) comparing matchdays from different seasons
could ensure the long-term comparability of results and help create a longitudinal dataset.
Second, the visibility of gambling advertisements varies across venues. Football
teams alternate between playing at home in their own stadium and away at another club
venue. Because each club has different sponsors, the advertisements displayed in each
stadium differ. To assess the visibility of gambling advertising during a club’s game, at
least two match days should be analysed to account for each club’s home game in its
own stadium.
Third, this study only examined football matches on one matchday in the national
football league. The extent and nature of gambling advertising may differ at other foot -
ball events, such as international competitions like the Champions League and major
tournaments like the European and World Championships. These events are subject to
different advertising regulations and costs, which can influence the presence of gam -
bling advertising.
Fourth, although this study quantifies gambling advertising and analyses its content, it
does not draw conclusions about the effects of exposure to gambling marketing. Future
research should explore perceptions of gambling advertising and the effects of exposure,
particularly among vulnerable individuals. For instance, the influence of gambling advertis-
ing during televised sporting events on gambling behaviour can be assessed using a quasi-
experimental approach, as demonstrated by McGrane et al., (2026).
Fifth, no sociodemographic data were collected regarding viewers of TV broadcasts
or social media users, except for paid-for advertising on social media. Gathering such
data could enhance our understanding of the demographic groups targeted by gambling
advertising.
Sixth, the coding scheme lacks differentiation regarding the size of the references. It is
reasonable to assume that a full-screen logo attracts more attention than a smaller image on
a billboard does. Future research should also consider logo size.
Seventh, only the presence of social responsibility measures in gambling advertising was
recorded. As the positive effects of warning notices are under discussion, future research
should examine whether social responsibility messages have a positive effect in the German
speaking context. Conversely, consideration should be given to how messages on social
responsibility can be framed so that they have a positive impact and help to reduce the
stigma associated with gambling.
Finally, the analysis of gambling marketing was conducted manually by the authors and
research assistants. Future research should explore the use of machine learning to automate
these processes. For instance, gambling advertising on broadcasts and social media can be
automatically captured through logo detection, and gambling marketing content can also be
analysed.10

Conclusion
The findings allow for comparisons with international studies and can inform policymakers,
regulators, and supervisory authorities in assessing whether advertising regulations are being
adhered to, thereby contributing to enhanced player protection. The study reveals a significant
amount of gambling advertising during a specific matchday of the German Bundesliga in the
2024/25 season, appearing in live TV broadcasts, sports highlight programmes, additional live
content, and social media. The scope, content, and advertising strategies of gambling advertis-
ing observed raise concerns about the risks for children and young people. Overall, the results
demonstrate that the 'gamblification of sports ‘ (McMullan & Miller, 2008) is evident in the
German market to an extent broadly comparable to that observed in the United Kingdom,
despite the introduction of a more restrictive regulatory framework. The extent and pervasive
presence of gambling advertising may contribute to the normalisation of gambling through
the symbolic association between gambling and sport, whereby, gambling may increasingly
be perceived as a harmless part of sports consumption. Consequently, risk awareness may
decline, posing particular risks for vulnerable groups (Lopez-Gonzalez & Griffiths, 2018). The
findings suggest that regulatory authorities should consider the overall volume and pervasive
presence of gambling advertising when assessing compliance with the objectives of player and
youth protection under the State Treaty on Gambling.
Recommendations for safeguarding gamblers and youth include implementing manda -
tory labelling of gambling advertisements with social responsibility measures. Messages
regarding age restrictions and harm reduction can highlight the risks associated with gam -
bling, provided they do not contain stigmatising language (Palmer et al., 2025). On social
media platforms, mandatory labelling of content marketing can help clearly identify it as
advertising. Given that social media is predominantly used by younger people, an addi -
tional measure could involve concealing gambling advertisements on these platforms. For
instance, gambling advertisements can be marked as unsuitable for children and made vis -
ible only after a manual click of the user.
Further measures could include restricting the scope and duration of gambling advertise-
ments during live sports broadcasts, such as prohibiting them during halftime and commer-
cial breaks. A more stringent approach would involve a comprehensive ban on sports betting
advertising on television, radio, and social media, as implemented in Belgium (Schütze et
al., 2023), or a complete prohibition of gambling sponsorship in sports, as introduced in
the Netherlands, Italy, and Spain (Wilson et al., 2024). Evidence from Spain suggests that
restrictions on gambling advertising can reduce the number of new customers and overall
stakes in online gambling, although their impact on regular gamblers has so far been limited
(Aonso-Diego et al., 2025). In Germany, young people are the most affected by gambling-
related disorders. Consequently, expanding the definition of minors to include individuals
aged 18–24 years, as suggested by Rossi and Nairn, ( 2025), appears to be a promising
approach, given that this age group finds content marketing particularly appealing and is
specifically targeted through paid-for advertising on social media. Future research should
extend the analysis to multiple matchdays and seasons to enable longitudinal comparisons,
examine the effects of exposure to gambling advertising on gambling behaviour among
different population groups, and investigate the application of automated approaches, such
as machine learning, to facilitate more comprehensive and efficient analyses of gambling
advertising in sports broadcasts.
