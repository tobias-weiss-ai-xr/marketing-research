Highlight
Social media marketing activities significantly boosted tourism purchase intention among 487 Vietnamese
respondents, both directly and through brand trust and destination image as parallel mediators. Destination image
had the larger indirect effect, indicating that credibility and destination-evaluation mechanisms jointly and partially
explain how social media converts into purchase intention.

Abstract
Social media has become a central source of destination information, but the mechanisms through which social
media marketing activities influence tourism purchase intention require further explanation. This study examines a
parallel mediation model in which brand trust and destination image transmit the effect of social media marketing
activities on tourists’ intention to book or purchase travel products associated with a destination. Drawing on the
stimulus-organism-response framework, social media marketing activities are modeled as a higher-order construct
comprising entertainment, interaction, trendiness, customization, and electronic word of mouth. Data were
obtained through a structured online questionnaire from 487 Vietnamese residents who had recently viewed social
media content about a domestic destination and were considering travel within the following 12 months. Partial
least squares structural equation modeling was used to assess the measurement model, direct relationships, indirect
effects, explanatory power, and predictive relevance. The findings show that social media marketing activities
positively influence brand trust, destination image, and purchase intention. Brand trust and destination image both
have significant positive effects on purchase intention and mediate the relationship between social media marketing
activities and purchase intention. Destination image carries the larger indirect effect, while the direct relationship
remains significant, indicating complementary partial mediation. The results suggest that social media
communication affects tourism purchase intention through both a credibility mechanism and a destination -
evaluation mechanism. The study contributes to tourism marketing research by comparing these two mediating
processes within one model and offers practical guidance for destination organizations seeking to convert social
media engagement into purchase intention.

Keywords
social media marketing activities; brand trust; destination image; purchase intention; domestic tourism.
Introduction
Social media is now part of the ordinary information environment in which tourism choices are considered,
compared, and discussed. Destination management organizations, hotels, airlines, tour operators, travel platforms,
and individual creators publish visual and textual content that reaches prospective travelers before a formal search
or booking decision begins. This content does more than provide facts. It can make a destination appear current,
accessible, credible, distinctive, or personally relevant. It can also invite questions, reviews, and peer -to-peer
exchange. These characteristics have made social media marketing activities an important part of destination
communication and tourism distribution (Leung et al., 2013; Mariani, 2020; Baber & Baber, 2023).
The practical importance of social media does not remove a central theoretical question: why does exposure to
social media marketing increase a consumer’s willingness to purchase? A direct association is plausible because
attractive content can increase awareness and make travel options easier to imagine. However, a direct -effect
account does not identify the internal evaluations through which communication becomes intention. Recent studies
suggest that trust, image, perceived value, engagement, and affective response can intervene between digital
content and behavioral intention (Chen & Lin, 2019; Ibrahim et al., 2021; Yamagishi et al., 2024; Aboalganam et al.,
2025). The present study focuses on two mechanisms that are especially relevant when a tourism product is
intangible at the time of choice: brand trust and destination image.

Trust matters because prospective tourists usually make decisions before they can inspect the service bundle. A
destination trip combines transport, accommodation, attractions, food, safety, and local service encounters.
Information is dispersed across organizations, and performance cannot be fully assessed before purchase. Social
media can reduce or increase this uncertainty. Consistent claims, useful replies, authentic reviews, and clear
representations may support the belief that the destination and its tourism providers are reliable. In contrast,
exaggerated or inconsistent communication can weaken confidence. Brand trust therefore provides a credibility
route from marketing activity to purchase intention (Chaudhuri & Holbrook, 2001; Delgado -Ballester, 2004; Hafez,
2021; Hanaysha, 2022b; Alrwashdeh, 2025).
Destination image provides a different route. It represents the cognitive and affective picture that a person holds
about a place. Social media content supplies cues about scenery, culture, accessibility, service quality, atmosphere,
safety, value, and distinctiveness. Repeated exposure to such cues can organize otherwise fragmented information
into an overall evaluation of the destination (Baloglu & McCleary, 1999; Beerli & Martin, 2004; Lojo et al., 2020).
Tourism research increasingly shows that user -generated and firm-generated content influence image formation,
which then affects visit intention (Lam et al., 2020; Liang & Lai, 2023; Ghorbanzadeh et al., 2023; Correia et al., 2025;
Wijaya et al., 2025). Destination image thus represents an evaluation route from social media communication to
behavioral intention.
These routes are related but conceptually distinct. Trust concerns confidence in claims, promises, and expected
performance. Destination image concerns what the destination is believed and felt to be like. A traveler may regard
information as credible without finding the place attractive, or may find the destination appealing while remaining
uncertain about booking reliability. Treating the two variables as parallel mediators allows their contributions to be
estimated simultaneously. It also reduces the risk of attributing the effect of one mechanism to the other. Few
tourism studies directly compare these two pathways within one higher -order social media marketing model,
especially in an emerging domestic tourism setting.
The study uses Vietnam as the field context. Domestic travelers in Vietnam encounter destination content on
Facebook, TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, travel communities, and booking platforms. The market includes established
coastal and heritage destinations as well as rapidly promoted mountain, island, and secondary-city destinations. This
variety makes social media important for reducing information gaps and differentiating places. A domestic context
also controls for several cross -border complications, such as visa rules and international travel restrictions, while
retaining meaningful variation in travel distance, destination familiarity, and perceived risk.
The study makes four contributions. First, it integrates brand trust and destination image as parallel mediators in
the social media marketing-purchase intention relationship. Second, it models social media marketing activities as a
multidimensional higher -order construct comprising entertainment, interaction, trendiness, customization, and
electronic word of mouth, rather than reducing social media to exposure frequency. Third, it applies the stimulus -
organism-response framework to distinguish external communication cues from internal cognitive -affective states
and the intended behavioral response. Fourth, it supplies a complete questionnaire, coding plan, and transparent
analysis workflow suitable for replication.
The research question is: How and to what extent do brand trust and destination image mediate the relationship
between social media marketing activities and tourists’ purchase intention? The corresponding objectives are to
examine the direct effects of social media marketing activities on purchase intention, brand trust, and destination
image; to assess the effects of brand trust and destination image on purchase intention; to test the two indirect
effects simultaneously; and to compare their relative magnitudes. The remainder of the article reviews the
theoretical foundation, develops hypotheses, describes the quantitative design, reports results, and discusses
implications and limitations.
2. THEORETICAL FOUNDATION AND LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1 Stimulus-organism-response framework
The stimulus-organism-response framework explains behavior as a sequence in which environmental cues affect
internal states, which then shape approach or avoidance responses. In consumer settings, stimuli can include
communication content, service environments, platform features, or social cues. The organism represents cognitive
and affective processes such as trust, value, image, emotion, or satisfaction. The response is an observable or
intended behavior. The framework is suitable for social media tourism because prospective travelers first encounter
digital cues, interpret those cues, and then decide whether to search, recommend, visit, or purchase.
Recent tourism studies use this logic to explain how user -generated content, social media efforts, information
quality, and online interaction influence destination evaluations and visit intention. Baber and Baber (2023) show
that social media marketing efforts, e-reputation, and destination image form a connected pathway to intention to
visit. Yamagishi et al. (2024) distinguish user-generated content as the stimulus, trust and destination image as

organism states, and visit intention as the response. Aboalganam et al. (2025) likewise report that destination
imagery mediates the effect of user -generated content on visit intention. These applications support a model in
which social media marketing activities are external stimuli, brand trust and destination image are internal organism
variables, and purchase intention is the response.
The framework is particularly helpful because it does not assume that all communication effects are immediate. A
social media post may be entertaining without producing a booking intention unless it changes how the destination
is judged. Interactive replies may matter because they make the destination organization seem responsive. Current
information may reduce uncertainty, while customized recommendations may increase personal relevance.
Electronic word of mouth can add social confirmation. The internal state is therefore not a passive stage. It is the
point at which content is evaluated for credibility and meaning.
2.2 Social media marketing activities
Social media marketing activities refer to coordinated actions through which an organization or destination creates,
shares, adapts, and facilitates digital content and interaction for marketing purposes. Kim and Ko (2012)
conceptualized the construct through entertainment, interaction, trendiness, customization, and word of mouth.
Subsequent studies adapted these dimensions across retail, hospitality, banking, food service, and e -commerce
contexts (Yadav & Rahman, 2018; Chen & Lin, 2019; Hafez, 2021; Sharma et al., 2021; Shuyi et al., 2024). The
multidimensional view is useful because social media performance cannot be represented by posting frequency
alone.
Entertainment concerns the degree to which content is enjoyable, interesting, and capable of sustaining attention.
Tourism is an experiential product, so visual narratives, short videos, live streams, itineraries, and personal stories
can support mental simulation of a future trip. Entertainment does not necessarily mean superficial content; it can
be a mode of presenting useful information in an engaging form. Studies of emotional and rational user-generated
content indicate that emotional presentation can be especially effective in digital tourism environments, although
factual quality remains important for evaluation (Cheung et al., 2022; Yamagishi et al., 2024).
Interaction refers to opportunities for two-way communication, participation, and exchange. Social media pages can
enable prospective visitors to ask questions, respond to polls, share experiences, and observe how destination
organizations handle concerns. Interaction can also occur among users, producing a public record of questions and
answers. This visibility may support engagement and reduce perceived distance between the consumer and the
destination. Hutter et al. (2013) showed that user interactions on brand pages can affect awareness and purchase
intention, while tourism studies connect interactive digital environments with co-created experience and destination
evaluation (Lam et al., 2020).
Trendiness captures the extent to which content is current and reflects recent events, offers, facilities, and travel
practices. Tourism information can become outdated quickly because prices, transport schedules, weather-related
access, attraction rules, and local events change. Current content signals active management and can reduce
uncertainty. It also helps destinations become part of ongoing cultural conversations. An et al. (2022) describe
tourism information consumption on social networking sites as a process in which users assess and use changing
information rather than merely receive fixed advertising.
Customization concerns the perceived relevance of content to a user’s interests and travel situation. Social platforms
use observed behavior, location, social connections, and engagement history to organize content. Destination
marketers can also customize recommendations by traveler segment, season, budget, trip length, or activity
preference. Relevant content lowers search costs and supports self -destination congruence. However,
customization should remain transparent and useful; intrusive targeting may reduce trust. The present measure
focuses on perceived fit and relevance rather than the technical process of personalization.
Electronic word of mouth refers to the creation and circulation of opinions, recommendations, experiences, and
warnings through digital networks. Social media combines marketer-generated and user-generated communication,
allowing each to influence the other. eWOM is important in tourism because experience quality varies and
consumers often rely on peer information. Erkan and Evans (2016) show that information usefulness and adoption
help explain how social -media eWOM affects purchase intention. Park et al. (2021) similarly connect social media
word of mouth to luxury purchase intention, while Nguyen and Tong (2023) find that user -generated content can
influence destination selection. Adeloye et al. (2022) demonstrate that user -generated videos can encourage visit
intention by offering vivid evidence of travel experiences.
Research generally finds that well-executed social media marketing activities improve brand-related and behavioral
outcomes. Balakrishnan et al. (2014) linked social media marketing media with purchase intention and brand loyalty
among Generation Y. Emini and Zeqiri (2021) found that brand awareness and engagement mediate social media
m arketing effects on purchase intention in a transition economy. Zeqiri et al. (2025) reported positive relationships

2.3 Brand trust
Brand trust is the willingness to rely on a brand under conditions of uncertainty because it is expected to be
competent, honest, and dependable. Chaudhuri and Holbrook (2001) placed trust within a chain linking brand affect,
loyalty, and performance. Delgado-Ballester (2004) developed a measure that emphasizes reliability and intentions,
capturing confidence that a brand will meet expectations and act in the consumer’s interest. In tourism, the object
of trust can be complex. A destination brand is supported by public organizations, private service providers, local
communities, and digital platforms. The study therefore defines brand trust as confidence that the destination’s
tourism brand and associated providers communicate reliably and can be depended on during booking and
consumption.
Trust is important before purchase because tourism services are intangible, variable, and often prepaid. A traveler
may need to assess whether photographs are representative, whether offers are genuine, whether providers will
honor conditions, and whether assistance will be available if problems occur. Social media can address these
concerns through consistent information, transparent responses, visible service recovery, and credible user
testimony. Conversely, edited imagery, hidden sponsorship, delayed replies, or conflicting claims can create
skepticism.
Empirical studies support the role of social media in trust formation. Hafez (2021) found that social media marketing
activities affect brand equity through brand love and brand trust. Ibrahim et al. (2021) showed that brand trust and
brand loyalty form a sequential mechanism linking social media marketing activities to revisit intention in coffee
shop Facebook pages. Hanaysha (2022b) identified brand trust as a mediator between social media marketing
features and purchase decisions in the fast -food industry. Alrwashdeh (2025) reported a significant trust route to
online purchase intention. In a tourism setting, Helal et al. (2023) connected social media content with perceived
value, trust, and revisit intention among event visitors.
Brand trust should also increase purchase intention. Intention requires the consumer to accept financial,
performance, and time-related risk. Trust reduces the perceived probability that the service will fail to match the
promise and lowers the psychological cost of commitment. Hajli (2015) showed that social commerce constructs and
trust influence intention to buy. Rao et al. (2021) found that trust beliefs, value co -creation, and brand image
mediate the eWOM-purchase intention relationship on Facebook brand fan pages. In the destination context, trust
can make prospective visitors more willing to move from inspiration and information search to booking.
2.4 Destination image
Destination image is the set of beliefs, impressions, and feelings that a person holds about a place. Early models
distinguish cognitive image, which concerns beliefs about destination attributes, from affective image, which
concerns feelings toward the destination. These dimensions combine into an overall image that guides comparison
and choice (Baloglu & McCleary, 1999; Beerli & Martin, 2004). Cognitive attributes may include natural resources,
attractions, infrastructure, accommodation, safety, value, and accessibility. Affective judgments may include
whether the place seems pleasant, exciting, relaxing, or distinctive.
Destination image is not produced by one source. It develops through prior experience, interpersonal
communication, mass media, official promotion, user reviews, cultural products, and social media. Online image
formation is especially dynamic because users encounter mixed visual and textual representations and can
immediately compare official and peer accounts. Lojo et al. (2020) note that online destination image includes
components, sources, and possible incongruence among representations. Mariani (2020) argues that Web 2.0
altered destination marketing by increasing participation and reducing organizational control over brand narratives.
Social media marketing can strengthen destination image when content is coherent, current, distinctive, and
supported by credible experiences. Lam et al. (2020) found that user -generated content platforms and co-created
online experiences contribute to destination image and satisfaction. Ghorbanzadeh et al. (2023) showed that firms’
social media activity can generate destination brand awareness and image. Dong et al. (2023) emphasized perceived
tourism authenticity on social media, suggesting that consistency between endorsers and an ethnic destination
affects evaluation. Correia et al. (2025) demonstrated that user -generated content influences tourist destination
image across generations. Wijaya et al. (2025) compared user-generated and firm-generated content and found that
both can shape attitudes, destination image, and visit intention among female Muslim travelers.

Destination image is also a strong antecedent of intention. A positive image simplifies choice by allowing consumers
to evaluate a complex bundle of attributes as a coherent option. Liang and Lai (2023) found that designation -of-
origin brand image contributes to destination image and visit intention in tea tourism. Maghrifani et al. (2022)
showed that destination image and visitor -image congruity help explain travel intentions among potential and
repeat visitors. Aboalganam et al. (2025) reported a significant indirect effect from user -generated content to visit
intention through destination imagery. These findings imply that social media content affects purchase intention
partly because it changes the attractiveness, suitability, and distinctiveness attributed to the destination.
2.5 Purchase intention in tourism
Purchase intention is a consumer’s stated likelihood or plan to buy a product or service. It is not identical to actual
behavior, but it is a proximal behavioral indicator and is commonly used when the purchase occurs later or cannot
be observed directly. The theory of planned behavior treats intention as the immediate antecedent of behavior,
while recognizing that control constraints can prevent enactment (Ajzen, 1991). In tourism, intention may refer to
visiting a destination, booking accommodation or transport, purchasing a tour, or selecting the destination as a
preferred option.
The present study uses purchase intention rather than visit intention to emphasize commitment to a market
transaction. Respondents evaluate their likelihood of booking or purchasing tourism products associated with one
selected destination. This definition captures a later decision stage than general interest while remaining suitable
for a cross -sectional survey of prospective travelers. It also aligns the destination -brand perspective with the
purchase-intention literature in social commerce and digital marketing (Hajli, 2015; Erkan & Evans, 2016; Hanaysha,
2022a; Zeqiri et al., 2025).
Purchase intention is likely to be influenced directly by SMM because social content can raise salience, reduce search
effort, and make offers actionable. However, its stronger theoretical value lies in the internal mechanisms that
precede commitment. Trust makes the transaction seem safer, while image makes the destination seem more
desirable. The two evaluations jointly address the questions “Can I rely on this destination offer?” and “Is this
destination right for me?” A model that includes both can therefore explain more than a model based on exposure
or engagement alone.
2.6 Integrating brand trust and destination image
Brand trust and destination image can be correlated because both develop from the same stream of destination
communication and prior knowledge. Positive reviews may make a place appear attractive and simultaneously make
its providers appear dependable. Consistent visual identity can support recognition, while transparent practical
information can strengthen confidence. Nevertheless, the constructs differ in their evaluative object and decision
function. Image summarizes beliefs and feelings about the destination experience. Trust reflects willingness to rely
on the destination brand and associated providers when outcomes cannot be guaranteed. This distinction supports
simultaneous estimation rather than merging both constructs into one general attitude.
The two mediators may also respond to different content failures. An inaccurate transport update can reduce trust
even when attractive scenery remains unchanged. Conversely, reliable but repetitive information may sustain trust
while doing little to improve the destination’s distinctiveness. A parallel model enables researchers to identify
whether a campaign succeeds because it changes credibility, desirability, or both. It also helps explain why
campaigns with similar reach can produce different purchase outcomes.
Prior studies often analyze trust or destination image separately, or place them in a longer sequence that assumes
one precedes the other. A serial specification can be useful when theory and temporal data justify the order. For
example, trusted information may be more readily accepted and therefore contribute to image formation;
alternatively, a favorable image may encourage consumers to interpret later information more positively. The
current study avoids choosing between these competing sequences because a one -wave survey cannot establish
their temporal order. Parallel mediation is more consistent with the available design and gives each mediator a
unique coefficient after accounting for their covariance.
The relative size of the indirect effects is theoretically informative but should not be interpreted as a universal
ranking. Trust may be more important for unfamiliar destinations, high -priced packages, or markets with frequent
fraud concerns. Image may dominate for low -risk domestic trips or destinations competing on experiential
differentiation. Prior visitation can also change both mechanisms. Repeat visitors may rely less on social media for
image formation but use it to evaluate current service conditions and offers. First -time visitors may depend more
heavily on digital representations. These possibilities support control variables and subgroup analysis in the field
study.

The integrated model therefore treats SMM as a portfolio of cues, trust and image as distinct but related appraisals,

later extensions involving perceived value, affect, familiarity, or actual booking behavior.
3. HYPOTHESIS DEVELOPMENT AND CONCEPTUAL MODEL
3.1 Social media marketing activities and purchase intention
SMM activities can increase purchase intention by keeping the destination visible, reducing information costs,
stimulating positive imagination, and linking inspiration to booking options. Entertainment attracts attention;
interaction allows clarification; trendiness supplies current information; customization increases relevance; and
eWOM provides social evidence. Research in consumer and tourism settings generally reports positive relationships
between social media marketing and intention outcomes (Balakrishnan et al., 2014; Emini & Zeqiri, 2021; Hanaysha,
2022a; Baber & Baber, 2023; Zeqiri et al., 2025). Accordingly:
H1. Social media marketing activities have a positive effect on purchase intention.
3.2 Social media marketing activities and brand trust
Consumers infer reliability from both the content and conduct of a brand on social media. Accurate updates,
responsive interaction, consistent promises, transparent user feedback, and relevant information can signal
competence and integrity. The public nature of social media also allows users to observe how a destination
organization responds to complaints and uncertainty. Prior studies find that social media marketing activities
contribute to brand trust in banking, hospitality, food service, and e-commerce contexts (Hafez, 2021; Ibrahim et al.,
2021; Hanaysha, 2022b; Alrwashdeh, 2025). Thus:
H2. Social media marketing activities have a positive effect on brand trust.
3.3 Brand trust and purchase intention
Trust lowers perceived transaction risk and supports willingness to rely on promises that cannot be fully verified
before consumption. In tourism, this function is important because bookings may involve advance payment and
coordination among multiple providers. Studies in social commerce and brand research consistently connect trust
with buying intention and loyalty (Chaudhuri & Holbrook, 2001; Hajli, 2015; Rao et al., 2021). Therefore:
H3. Brand trust has a positive effect on purchase intention.
If SMM increases trust and trust increases intention, trust should transmit part of the SMM effect. This
mechanism is consistent with mediation evidence in digital marketing (Hafez, 2021; Hanaysha, 2022b; Alrwashdeh,
2025). Hence:
H4. Brand trust positively mediates the relationship between social media marketing activities and purchase
intention.
3.4 Social media marketing activities and destination image
Social media provides repeated cues about destination attributes and atmosphere. Visual content can display
scenery and activities; comments can reveal service experiences; current updates can signal accessibility and active
management; and customized recommendations can connect destination features to individual interests. Research
shows that firm-generated and user-generated content contribute to destination image formation (Lam et al., 2020;
Ghorbanzadeh et al., 2023; Yamagishi et al., 2024; Correia et al., 2025; Wijaya et al., 2025). Therefore:
H5. Social media marketing activities have a positive effect on destination image.
3.5 Destination image and purchase intention
A favorable destination image increases the perceived attractiveness and suitability of a place. It helps consumers
organize many attributes into an overall judgment and supports comparison with alternatives. Empirical tourism
studies report positive effects of destination image on visit or travel intention (Maghrifani et al., 2022; Liang & Lai,
2023; Baber & Baber, 2023; Aboalganam et al., 2025). Thus:
H6. Destination image has a positive effect on purchase intention.
Destination image should mediate the SMM-purchase intention relationship because communication first alters how
the destination is represented in memory and then affects willingness to buy. This pathway is supported by recent
studies of UGC, social media content, and destination intention (Yamagishi et al., 2024; Aboalganam et al., 2025;
Wijaya et al., 2025). Accordingly:

H7. Destination image positively mediates the relationship between social media marketing activities and purchase

The model specifies parallel rather than serial mediation. Social media marketing can simultaneously shape
confidence in the destination brand and evaluation of the destination itself. No causal order between brand trust
and destination image is imposed because both orders are theoretically possible and cross -sectional data cannot
establish temporal precedence between them. Estimating the mediators in parallel provides a conservative
comparison of their unique indirect effects.

4. METHODOLOGY
4.1 Research design and setting
The study used a quantitative, cross-sectional explanatory design. Data were collected through a structured online
questionnaire administered using Google Forms. The unit of analysis was the individual prospective domestic
traveler. At the beginning of the questionnaire, each respondent selected one Vietnamese destination and used that
destination as the reference point for all subsequent answers. This approach reduced ambiguity and ensured that
evaluations of social media marketing activities, brand trust, destination image, and purchase intention referred to
the same object.
The target population consisted of adults residing in Vietnam who had seen social media content about a domestic
destination during the previous six months and were considering a leisure trip within the next 12 months. The
eligibility questions screened for consent, residence, age, recent social media exposure, and travel consideration.
The questionnaire was distributed through online travel communities, university and workplace networks, and
destination-related social media pages to reach respondents with relevant information -search and travel-planning
experience.
4.2 Sampling and sample size
A non-probability purposive sampling approach was used because no complete sampling frame was available for
social-media-exposed prospective domestic travelers. After eligibility and response -quality screening, 487 valid
questionnaires were retained for analysis. The sample was sufficient for estimating the higher-order PLS-SEM model

and exceeded conventional power requirements for the largest regression equation. The sample size also allowed
the study to assess indirect effects through bootstrapping and to examine the explanatory and predictive

4.3 Questionnaire development
The questionnaire contained four parts. The first presented information about the study, consent, and eligibility
screening. The second recorded the travel and social media context, including the focal destination, primary
platform, recent exposure frequency, previous visitation, and expected travel period. The third measured the study
constructs. The final part recorded demographic characteristics.
SMM is modeled as a reflective -reflective higher-order construct with five first -order dimensions. Entertainment,
interaction, trendiness, customization, and eWOM items are adapted from Kim and Ko (2012), Yadav and Rahman
(2018), Chen and Lin (2019), and later applications. Brand trust items draw on Chaudhuri and Holbrook (2001),
Delgado-Ballester (2004), Ibrahim et al. (2021), and Hanaysha (2022b). Destination image items cover cognitive and
affective elements based on Baloglu and McCleary (1999), Beerli and Martin (2004), Lojo et al. (2020), and Liang and
Lai (2023). Purchase intention items are adapted from digital purchase and tourism intention studies (Hajli, 2015;
Erkan & Evans, 2016; Baber & Baber, 2023).
All construct items used a five-point agreement scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The five-point
format supported respondent differentiation and was convenient for completion on mobile devices. Item wording
was concise and referred consistently to “the selected destination.” The instrument was translated into Vietnamese
through forward translation, independent review, and back -translation. Tourism marketing and survey -method
specialists reviewed the items for relevance, clarity, and redundancy. A pilot test was conducted with eligible
respondents to assess completion time, item interpretation, and preliminary reliability before the main survey.

4.4 Common method and response-quality procedures
Because the predictors, mediators, and outcome were measured in one questionnaire, procedural controls were
used to reduce common method concerns. Respondents were informed that there were no correct answers and
that their responses would be treated confidentially. The predictor, mediator, and outcome sections were separated
by short instructions. Ambiguous wording, double-barreled items, and unnecessary technical terms were avoided.
Response time and answer -pattern checks were used during data screening. Data screening examined duplicate
submissions, failed eligibility conditions, excessive missingness, implausibly short completion times, and invariant or
patterned responses. Full-collinearity variance inflation factors were also examined as a diagnostic, while recognizing
that no single statistical procedure can establish the absence of common method bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003; Kock,
2015). The combination of questionnaire design and statistical screening provided the main protection against
response-quality problems.
4.5 Data analysis
PLS-SEM was selected because the model included a higher-order construct and emphasized mediation, explanation,
and prediction in a specific tourism context. The analysis was conducted using the two -stage approach. In the first
stage, the five first-order dimensions of social media marketing activities were estimated with their indicators. Their
latent variable scores were then used as indicators of the higher -order social media marketing construct in the
second stage. This procedure limited indicator duplication and permitted direct assessment of the dimension
loadings. The measurement model was evaluated before the structural relationships. Indicator loadings were
assessed against the general .708 guideline, while internal consistency was examined using Cronbach’s alpha and
composite reliability. Values from .70 to .95 were treated as acceptable. Convergent validity was established when
average variance extracted exceeded .50 (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Discriminant validity was examined using the
heterotrait-monotrait ratio, with .85 applied as the conservative threshold (Henseler et al., 2015).

www.actainnovations.com • ISSN 2300-5599 • © 2026

The structural model assessment included collinearity, path coefficients, bootstrapped confidence intervals,
coefficients of determination, effect sizes, and predictive assessment. Direct and indirect effects were tested with
10,000 bootstrap subsamples. Mediation was established when the confidence interval for an indirect effect
excluded zero. When the direct and indirect effects were significant and had the same direction, the mediation
pattern was interpreted as complementary partial mediation. The two indirect effects were also compared by their
standardized magnitudes.
The R -squared values were interpreted in relation to the behavioral context rather than through rigid universal
cutoffs. The f-squared statistic was used to assess the contribution of each predictor to an endogenous construct.
Predictive relevance was examined using Q -squared-predict values and standardized prediction errors. These
procedures allowed the explanatory results to be considered together with the model’s case -level predictive
performance (Hair et al., 2011, 2019; Shmueli et al., 2019).
4.6 Ethical considerations
Participation was voluntary. The consent page explained the research purpose, eligibility criteria, approximate
completion time, academic use of the data, and the right to stop before submitting the questionnaire. The survey
did not require directly identifying information. Responses were stored in access-controlled files and used only for
the stated research purpose.

6. DISCUSSION
The findings explain how social media marketing activities translate into tourism purchase intention through two
related but distinct internal evaluations. The results supported all direct and indirect relationships in the model.
Social media marketing activities strengthened brand trust and destination image, and both variables increased
purchase intention. The remaining direct effect showed that trust and image explained a substantial part, but not
all, of the relationship. This pattern supports the stimulus -organism-response framework: social media marketing
activities functioned as external stimuli, brand trust and destination image represented the internal organism states,
and purchase intention was the behavioral response.
The positive direct relationship between social media marketing activities and purchase intention supports earlier
research showing that entertaining, interactive, current, personally relevant, and socially endorsed content can
move consumers closer to a purchase decision. The result is consistent with Balakrishnan et al. (2014), Hanaysha
(2022a), Baber and Baber (2023), and Zeqiri et al. (2025), who reported positive links between social media
marketing and behavioral intentions. In the Vietnamese domestic tourism context, this direct effect can arise
because social platforms reduce search costs and connect travel inspiration with practical information about
transport, accommodation, attractions, and booking options. However, the small unique effect size indicates that
exposure to attractive content alone is not sufficient. Much of its influence depends on how users evaluate the
credibility of the destination brand and the qualities of the destination.
Social media marketing activities had a strong positive effect on brand trust, supporting the view that consumers
infer reliability from the quality and conduct of digital communication. This finding agrees with Hafez (2021), Ibrahim
et al. (2021), Hanaysha (2022b), and Alrwashdeh (2025), who found that social media marketing activities can
improve trust when communication is useful, responsive, and consistent. The result is particularly relevant for
tourism because a destination is represented by a network of actors rather than by one seller. Prospective visitors
encounter messages from destination authorities, hotels, restaurants, attractions, transport providers, influencers,
and previous tourists. When these sources provide consistent information and visible responses to questions or
complaints, they reduce uncertainty about whether the destination can deliver the experience communicated
online.
Brand trust also had a significant positive effect on purchase intention and carried a significant indirect effect
between social media marketing activities and purchase intention. This finding is consistent with the risk-reduction
role of trust identified by Chaudhuri and Holbrook (2001), Hajli (2015), Rao et al. (2021), Hanaysha (2022b), and
Alrwashdeh (2025). Tourism services are largely evaluated before consumption and often require advance payment,
coordination across several providers, and acceptance of uncertain service conditions. Trust therefore increases

www.actainnovations.com • ISSN 2300-5599 • © 2026

willingness to rely on destination -related promises. Within Vietnam, this mechanism may be especially relevant
when travelers consider less familiar destinations, local service providers, seasonal transport arrangements, or offers
promoted through social media. Reliable prices, clear cancellation terms, verified reviews, and responsive support
can convert interest into greater booking confidence.
The effect of social media marketing activities on destination image was slightly stronger than its effect on brand
trust. This result supports research showing that social platforms shape how consumers understand and imagine a
destination. Lam et al. (2020) linked co-created online experiences with destination image, while Ghorbanzadeh et
al. (2023) showed that organizational social media activities can strengthen destination awareness and image. More
recent studies by Yamagishi et al. (2024), Correia et al. (2025), Aboalganam et al. (2025), and Wijaya et al. (2025)
similarly found that user- and firm-generated content influences image formation and travel intention. The present
finding extends this literature by showing that the combined qualities of social media marketing—not only exposure
to individual posts—contribute to cognitive and affective evaluations of a destination.
Destination image had the largest direct contribution to purchase intention and produced the stronger mediation
effect. This result aligns with Baber and Baber (2023), Liang and Lai (2023), Maghrifani et al. (2022), and Aboalganam
et al. (2025), who emphasized the importance of destination image for visit-related intentions. A tourism purchase
is inseparable from the experience that the consumer expects at the destination. Images of scenery, culture, food,
activities, safety, infrastructure, and atmosphere help travelers judge whether a place fits their preferences, budget,
companions, and available time. In the Vietnamese market, destinations compete through distinct combinations of
coastal, mountain, heritage, culinary, and urban experiences. Social media can make these differences visible
quickly, which helps explain why destination image carried a larger share of the effect than brand trust.
The larger destination-image pathway does not reduce the importance of trust. The two mediators answer different
questions. Destination image addresses whether the place appears attractive and suitable, while brand trust
addresses whether its promises and providers can be relied upon. A destination may have an appealing image but
still fail to generate purchase intention when booking conditions, reviews, or provider responses appear uncertain.
Conversely, a reliable destination brand may not be chosen if its experience does not fit the traveler’s interests. The
parallel mediation results therefore show that desirability and dependability are complementary conditions in the
movement from social media communication to purchase intention.
The significant direct effect that remained after the mediators were included indicates that other mechanisms also
connect social media marketing with purchase intention. Prior research identifies perceived value, social
identification, engagement, satisfaction, emotional arousal, and image congruity as possible explanations (Chen &
Lin, 2019; Cheung et al., 2022; Maghrifani et al., 2022; Helal et al., 2023). Social media may also create urgency
through limited offers, simplify decision-making through itinerary links, or strengthen perceived behavioral control
by providing practical planning information. The complementary partial mediation pattern is therefore theoretically
reasonable and suggests that future models should examine additional mechanisms without combining trust and
destination image into one broad perception construct.
The sample profile provides further context for the results. Facebook and TikTok were the main information
platforms, but their functions differ. TikTok emphasizes short, vivid, trend -oriented content that may accelerate
affective image formation. Facebook pages and travel groups provide longer discussions, peer recommendations,
problem solving, and visible service responses, which may be particularly relevant to trust. Almost half of the
respondents had not visited their selected destination, while the remainder had some direct experience. Social
media may therefore form an initial image for first-time travelers and update, confirm, or challenge the image held
by repeat visitors. The national coverage of the sample also reflects a domestic market in which distance, transport
access, regional identity, and destination familiarity vary considerably.
Overall, the findings support a mechanism-based account of social media marketing in tourism. Posting frequently
or achieving high reach does not automatically produce purchase intention. Social media activities become
influential when users interpret the content as credible and when the content helps them construct a favorable,
distinctive, and personally relevant image of the destination. This interpretation is consistent with the S -O-R
framework because the same external communication may produce different responses depending on prior
experience, source credibility, travel goals, and platform use. Destination organizations should therefore assess not
only engagement metrics but also whether their communication improves trust and image among the intended
visitor segments.
7. THEORETICAL AND MANAGERIAL IMPLICATIONS
7.1 Theoretical implications
The first theoretical implication is that social media marketing should be studied through multiple internal
m echanisms. A model that includes only a direct path can identify association but offers limited explanation. By

www.actainnovations.com • ISSN 2300-5599 • © 2026

placing trust and image in parallel, the study separates a credibility mechanism from a destination -evaluation
mechanism. This structure helps reconcile brand research, which often emphasizes trust, with tourism research,
which often emphasizes destination image. Second, the model extends the S-O-R framework by using a higher-order
stimulus. Social media marketing is not one homogeneous cue. It combines entertaining presentation, interaction,
current information, personal relevance, and eWOM facilitation. Treating these dimensions as first -order
components preserves theoretical detail while allowing an overall SMM effect to be estimated. This approach is
consistent with the original scale tradition (Kim & Ko, 2012) and later multidimensional applications (Yadav &
Rahman, 2018; Shuyi et al., 2024).
Third, the model clarifies the conceptual distinction between brand trust and destination image. Destination image
is not a substitute for trust, and trust is not simply a favorable image. Their simultaneous inclusion allows each path
to be estimated while controlling for the other. The difference between the two indirect effects shows why this
distinction can matter. A single broad “brand perception” mediator could conceal which mechanism is more
influential. Fourth, the study shifts the outcome from general visit interest to purchase intention. This outcome links
destination communication more closely to booking behavior and commercial conversion. It also creates a bridge
between tourism intention research and social-commerce research. Future studies can extend the model to actual
clickstream, inquiry, or transaction data to examine the intention -behavior gap described in planned -behavior
research (Ajzen, 1991).
7.2 Managerial implications
Destination managers should design social media programs around both desirability and dependability. Image -
building content should show a varied but coherent destination experience. It should include recognizable
landscapes, cultural and culinary features, activities, accessibility, accommodation options, and realistic seasonal
conditions. Emotional narratives can make the experience vivid, while practical information helps users evaluate fit.
Content calendars should be updated frequently enough to maintain trendiness without sacrificing accuracy.
Trust-building requires operational coordination. Social media teams should verify prices, opening times, transport
information, cancellation terms, and safety notices before publication. Questions should receive timely and
consistent replies. Complaints should not simply be removed; visible and respectful service recovery can
demonstrate accountability. Sponsored content and edited imagery should be disclosed where relevant. Trust is
difficult to sustain when official content and actual provider practices diverge.
The parallel mediation model also supports audience segmentation. Travelers with low destination familiarity may
need image-rich orientation, whereas consumers close to booking may need trust signals such as verified reviews,
clear policies, secure payment, and responsive support. Customization can organize these needs by stage of decision.
An early -stage user may receive inspiration and itinerary ideas, while a late -stage user may receive availability,
package details, and booking assurances.
User-generated content should be facilitated rather than fully controlled. Destination organizations can encourage
visitors to share detailed experiences, create destination hashtags, request permission to repost authentic content,
and respond constructively to critical posts. Peer content can enrich image and trust when it is diverse and credible.
However, incentives should be transparent, and marketers should avoid selecting only idealized representations that
create an unrealistic promise.
Performance measurement should move beyond follower counts. Useful indicators include response time, saved
content, itinerary clicks, verified booking referrals, sentiment by destination attribute, recurring trust concerns, and
the consistency between promoted and reviewed experiences. Survey measures of trust and image can be linked to
campaign periods to identify whether a content strategy changes the intended internal evaluations. The
questionnaire provided with this study can serve as a repeated tracking instrument after suitable validation.
8. LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE RESEARCH
The study has several limitations. First, the cross -sectional design does not establish temporal causality. Although
the theoretical model specifies an ordered process from social media marketing activities to brand trust and
destination image and then to purchase intention, all constructs were measured at one point in time. Longitudinal
studies could examine whether changes in social media exposure precede changes in trust, image, and later booking
behavior. Experimental research could also manipulate content characteristics to identify causal effects more
directly.
Second, purchase intention is not equivalent to an actual purchase. Available income, leave arrangements, travel
companions, weather, transport conditions, and competing destinations may prevent an intended trip. Future
research should link survey responses with referral clicks, booking records, inquiries, or follow-up measures of actual
travel behavior. Such data would allow the intention-behavior gap to be examined directly.

Third, purposive online sampling may overrepresent active social media users, younger adults, and individuals with
a pre -existing interest in travel. Although the sample included different age groups and regions, it was not a
probability sample of the Vietnamese population. Future studies may apply platform and regional quotas, recruit
through offline channels, and compare the sample with national travel-demographic information.
Fourth, the model treated social media marketing activities as an overall higher -order construct. Platform
characteristics may change the relative importance of entertainment, interaction, trendiness, customization, and
electronic word of mouth. Short -video platforms may be particularly effective for affective image formation,
whereas group-based and booking platforms may provide stronger trust and transaction cues. Multi-group analysis
could compare platforms after measurement invariance has been established.
Fifth, brand trust was assessed at the destination -brand system level. Respondents may distinguish trust in
destination authorities, local tourism firms, influencers, booking platforms, and other travelers. Future studies can
separate these trust targets and examine whether they contribute differently to purchase intention. Source
credibility, sponsorship disclosure, perceived authenticity, and prior negative experiences may also moderate the
relationships. Finally, destination image was measured through broad cognitive and affective evaluations. Individual
destinations may require additional attributes related to environmental quality, heritage authenticity, nightlife,
accessibility, family suitability, or climate. Qualitative pre -study work and destination -specific scale refinement
would improve local relevance while retaining core measures for comparison across settings.
9. CONCLUSION
This study examined the relationship between social media marketing activities and tourism purchase intention
through the parallel mediating roles of brand trust and destination image. The findings showed that social media
marketing activities increased both internal evaluations and also had a smaller direct effect on purchase intention.
Brand trust and destination image each increased purchase intention and transmitted a significant part of the social
media marketing effect. Destination image produced the larger direct and indirect effects, indicating that the way
travelers understand and feel about a destination is a central mechanism linking social media communication with
intended purchase. Brand trust remained important because it reduced uncertainty about whether destination -
related information and service promises could be relied upon. The significant direct effect indicated complementary
partial mediation and suggested that additional mechanisms, including perceived value, engagement, emotional
response, and planning convenience, also contribute to purchase intention. The study contributes to the tourism
marketing literature by distinguishing a credibility pathway from a destination -evaluation pathway within one
higher-order social media marketing model. For destination managers, the results indicate that social media should
make a destination both desirable and dependable. Effective communication therefore requires compelling
representations of destination experiences, current and personally relevant information, credible peer content,
responsive interaction, and consistency between online promises and actual tourism services.
