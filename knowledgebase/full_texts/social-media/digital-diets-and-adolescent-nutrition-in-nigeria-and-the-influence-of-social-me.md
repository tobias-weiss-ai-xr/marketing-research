Abstract
Background The rapid digitalisation of youth culture has transformed the food
environment in Nigeria, where social media platforms such as TikTok, Instagram, and
YouTube increasingly shape adolescents’ dietary preferences and lifestyle aspirations.
These exposures may reinforce unhealthy food preferences, body-image pressures,
and nutrition misinformation. This narrative review synthesises current evidence on
the influence of social media on dietary behaviours and NR-NCD risk among Nigerian
adolescents, emphasising behavioural, psychosocial, and policy implications.
Methods This structured narrative review searched PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar,
and African Journals Online, supplemented by a separate ResearchGate search for grey
literature, for literature published between 2015 and 2025 on adolescents aged 10–19
years, social media exposure, dietary behaviour, digital food marketing, body image,
nutrition misinformation, and NR-NCD risk in Nigeria or comparable Sub-Saharan
African settings.
Results Available evidence suggests that social media may function as a digital food
environment in which influencer marketing, peer validation, and algorithmic exposure
are associated with greater consumption of ultra-processed, calorie-dense foods.
Repeated exposure appears to reinforce unhealthy dietary norms, while body-image
ideals and misinformation are associated with disordered eating risk. Nonetheless,
social media also offers potential for nutrition education and digital health promotion
through culturally sensitive and youth-centred approaches.
Conclusion Social media simultaneously poses risks and opportunities for adolescent
nutrition in Nigeria. Effective interventions require regulatory oversight of online
food marketing, integration of media literacy into school curricula, and cross-sectoral
collaboration to harness digital platforms for nutrition promotion.

  1 Introduction
Adolescence is a critical developmental stage characterised by rapid physical, cognitive,
and psychosocial growth. Adequate nutrition during this period is essential for achiev -
ing optimal growth and establishing lifelong dietary and health behaviours. In Nigeria,
adolescents aged 10–19 years constitute a significant proportion of the population, mak-
ing their nutritional well-being central to national health and economic productivity
[1]. Recent Nigerian evidence shows that adolescents are increasingly affected by both
undernutrition and overweight/obesity, with micronutrient inadequacies also remaining
common across different settings. These findings suggest a widespread nutritional imbal-
ance driven by diets high in starchy staples, snacks, and sugar-sweetened beverages,
and low in fruits, vegetables, and animal-source foods [ 1]. Consequently, adolescents
in Nigeria face growing vulnerability to nutrition-related noncommunicable diseases
(NR-NCDs), including obesity, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes, which are increasingly
manifesting during youth. However, evidence directly linking social media exposure
to measured adolescent nutrition outcomes in Nigeria remains limited. Therefore, this
review distinguishes between Nigeria-specific evidence, broader African evidence, and
global evidence used to explain plausible mechanisms. This distinction is important to
avoid overgeneralizing findings from non-Nigerian contexts while still situating Nigeria
within wider global debates on digital food environments and adolescent health.
1.1 Dietary transitions and the global rise of NR-NCDs
Globally, NR-NCDs such as obesity, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and dyslipidemia
have surged, largely due to unhealthy dietary patterns and sedentary lifestyles established
during adolescence [ 2]. Between 1990 and 2022, the prevalence of adolescent obesity
surpassed that of thinness in nearly two-thirds of countries [ 3]. Global evidence shows
increasing adolescent exposure to ultra-processed foods and sugar-sweetened bever -
ages, but the extent and pathways of this transition vary across countries and should be
interpreted within local food systems, income patterns, and cultural dietary practices.
Within Nigeria, rapid urbanisation and westernisation have accelerated changes in food
environments. Adolescents are increasingly replacing traditional diets based on legumes,
cereals, and vegetables with refined grains, fried snacks, and sugary drinks [ 4]. Omisore
et al. [ 5] reported a 10.2% prevalence of overweight and obesity among adolescents in
Ile-Ife, alongside elevated blood pressure readings, and Ene-Obong et al. [ 6] found that
11.4% of urban adolescents in southern Nigeria were overweight and 2.8% obese. Diets
dominated by ultra-processed and energy-dense foods may increase the risk of excessive
energy intake and metabolic disturbances associated with NR-NCD development [ 2]. In
contrast, traditional Nigerian diets rich in plant-based fibres and micronutrients confer
protective metabolic effects [5].
1.1.1 Digitalisation, social media, and adolescent nutrition
Beyond dietary transitions, Nigeria’s rapid digitalisation has introduced a new layer of
complexity to adolescent nutrition. Social media platforms now operate not only as
spaces for communication but also as commercial and algorithmically curated environ -
ments where food preferences, body ideals, peer norms, and consumption aspirations
are repeatedly produced and reinforced. With widespread smartphone penetration, digi-
tal media serves as both a social and commercial environment where fast-food brands

and influencers actively engage adolescents. Bankole et al. [ 7] reported that half of Nige-
rian adolescents aged 14–16 regularly use social media, while Kayode et al. [ 8] found
high engagement among university students, with WhatsApp (96.2%), YouTube (84.8%),
Facebook (78.5%), and Instagram (72.8%) as the most used platforms. Social media thus
acts as a conduit for digital food marketing, defined as the promotion of high-fat, high-
sugar, and ultra-processed foods through targeted advertisements, sponsored posts, and
influencer endorsements [ 8, 9]. These strategies often link fast-food consumption with
social aspiration, convenience, and westernised lifestyles [ 7]. This digital food environ -
ment amplifies adolescents’ exposure to unhealthy dietary cues while reinforcing psy -
chological and social pressures tied to idealised body images [ 9]. Such exposure may
contribute to unhealthy food preferences, increased interest in ultra-processed foods,
and body-image-related dietary practices such as meal skipping or fad dieting, although
direct causal evidence among Nigerian adolescents remains limited [ 9, 10]. Algorithmic
curation further complicates this digital food environment. Platforms such as TikTok,
Instagram, and YouTube recommend content based on predicted engagement, mean -
ing that adolescents who interact with fast-food posts, dieting content, or body-trans -
formation videos may be repeatedly exposed to similar material. This creates a potential
feedback loop in which calorie-dense foods, appearance-focused ideals, and unverified
dietary advice become more visible in adolescents’ online spaces. Although this mecha -
nism has not been sufficiently studied in Nigeria, it is important for understanding how
social media may intensify dietary and body-image risks.
1.2 Aim and scope of the review
This narrative review explores the influence of social media on dietary behaviours
among Nigerian adolescents and how these digital exposures contribute to the risk of
nutrition-related noncommunicable diseases. Specifically, it aims to:
i. Describe the relationship between social media use and adolescents’ food choices in
Nigeria;
ii. Identify pathways linking social media influence to diet-related health risks; and
iii. Highlight implications for NR-NCD prevention, health education, and policy
interventions.
The review adopts an integrated conceptual framework combining Social Cognitive
Theory, the Health Belief Model, and Cultivation Theory to explain how digital exposure
shapes dietary decisions and nutritional outcomes. Within this framework, social media
influence refers to user exposure to online interactions, influencer content, and peer-
generated messages that shape dietary attitudes [ 11]; digital food marketing encom -
passes strategic promotion of ultra-processed foods via social media platforms [ 10]; and
dietary behaviour reflects habitual food choices and consumption patterns [ 12]. Framed
within Nigeria’s ongoing nutrition transition [ 13], this review integrates theoretical and
empirical evidence to elucidate the digital determinants of adolescent nutrition and their
public health implications.
1.3 Adolescent health and nutrition-related noncommunicable diseases (NR-NCDs)
Adolescence represents a pivotal developmental phase during which physiological, cog -
nitive, and behavioural patterns are established, laying the foundation for lifelong health

outcomes. Nutritional behaviours adopted in this stage have long-term implications,
influencing the risk of developing nutrition-related noncommunicable diseases (NR-
NCDs) such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia [14, 15]. In Nige-
ria, adolescent NR-NCD risk is shaped by the combined influence of nutrition transition,
urbanisation, changing food availability, sedentary lifestyles, and digital media exposure.
Importantly, social media should not be treated as an isolated determinant of NR-NCD
risk. Its influence interacts with broader food systems and social determinants, includ -

differences, and cultural perceptions of modern and traditional diets. Recognising these
interactions strengthens the public health relevance of the review and supports a more
balanced interpretation of the evidence.
1.4 Theoretical framework
This review is grounded in three complementary theoretical perspectives: Social Cogni -
tive Theory (SCT), the Health Belief Model (HBM), and Cultivation Theory, which col -
lectively elucidate the psychosocial pathways linking social media exposure to adolescent
dietary behaviours and NR-NCD risk. These theories were selected because they explain
different but complementary pathways through which social media may influence ado -
lescent dietary behaviour. Social Cognitive Theory explains how adolescents may model
food-related behaviours observed among influencers and peers. The Health Belief Model
explains how digital cues may affect perceptions of risk, benefits, barriers, and moti -
vation to act. Cultivation Theory explains how repeated exposure to food-related and
body-image content may gradually normalise particular dietary practices and body ide -
als. Together, these theories provide a useful framework for interpreting digital dietary
influence, while recognising that adolescent food behaviour is also shaped by structural
and environmental conditions.
1.4.1 Social cognitive theory (SCT)
Bandura [16] emphasises that human behaviour is learned through observation, imita -
tion, and reinforcement within social contexts. Adolescents frequently observe influ -
encers, celebrities, and peers modelling eating behaviours on social media platforms,
such as frequent fast-food consumption or endorsement of sugar-sweetened beverages.
These behaviours are rewarded through visible markers of approval, likes, shares, and
positive comments, which serve as vicarious reinforcements that strengthen the likeli -
hood of imitation [17]. Within Nigeria, such exposure may be particularly influential, as
social validation and digital participation are commonly associated with constructions
of modern identity among adolescents. However, this link has not been directly tested in
the Nigerian studies reviewed. Available evidence suggests that influencer-driven food
content is associated with normalised fast-food consumption among Nigerian youth,
as well as with perceptions of desirability, convenience, and social prestige [ 18]. SCT
thus explains how digital modelling and reinforcement may shape dietary preferences,
although the ability to imitate online food practices also depends on affordability, access,
peer norms, and parental control.

1.4.2 Health belief model (HBM)
Rosenstock [ 19] and Carpenter [ 20] posit that health-related behaviours are influ -
enced by individuals’ perceptions of susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, and cues
to action. Within social media contexts, digital food marketing and influencer content
act as powerful cues that modify these perceptions. Furthermore, glamorous portray -
als of fast foods, desserts, and sugary beverages often downplay perceived susceptibility
to chronic diseases while highlighting immediate gratifications such as taste, affordabil -
ity, and social inclusion [ 21]. Among Nigerian adolescents, these cues are particularly
potent given limited health literacy and a cultural emphasis on social belonging. Empiri -
cal evidence supports the applicability of HBM constructs to this population: Ademi -
luyi et al. [ 22] found that adolescents’ beliefs about personal vulnerability and perceived
benefits significantly shaped their dietary and lifestyle choices. In the context of social
media, perceived benefits may be shaped by taste, appearance, popularity, convenience,
and social belonging, while perceived barriers to healthy eating may include cost, avail -
ability, time, and limited access to credible nutrition information.
1.4.3 Cultivation theory
Gerbner et al. [ 23] extend the discussion by explaining how sustained media exposure
cultivates perceptions of social norms and reality over time. In the context of adolescent
nutrition, repeated exposure to social media content that depicts fast food, snacks, and
sugary drinks as integral to modern lifestyles fosters a “mainstreaming” effect, where
such foods become internalised as normative or aspirational [ 24]. Among Nigerian ado-
lescents, this process may contribute to a gradual erosion of traditional food values, with
home-cooked meals increasingly perceived as less socially desirable; although direct
empirical application of Cultivation Theory within Nigerian nutrition research remains
limited, the observed normalisation of Western dietary patterns is consistent with its
premise. Cultivation effects are nonetheless likely to vary with platform use, digital lit -
eracy, socioeconomic position, gender, and exposure to alternative nutrition education.
It is important to acknowledge that SCT, HBM, and Cultivation Theory are individual-
level psychosocial models, none developed in Sub-Saharan African digital contexts, and
their application to Nigerian adolescents therefore requires interpretive caution. These
frameworks do not fully account for structural conditions, poverty, food prices, limited
access to healthy foods, school food environments, and regulatory gaps that constrain
adolescent food choices independently of social media exposure. This review applies
them as explanatory lenses for psychosocial pathways while recognising that a fuller
account of adolescent dietary behaviour in Nigeria requires a socio-ecological framing
spanning individual, interpersonal, school, community, and policy levels.

social media exposure within Nigeria’s broader nutrition transition and food environ -
ment, linking psychosocial mediators to dietary outcomes while accounting for struc -
tural moderators, detailed below.

2 Methodology
2.1 Review design
This study adopted a structured narrative review design to synthesise evidence on the
influence of social media on adolescent dietary behaviour and nutrition-related non -
communicable disease risks in Nigeria. The review was not intended to generate pooled
effect estimates or establish causal relationships. Instead, it aimed to identify key themes,
plausible mechanisms, contextual patterns, evidence limitations, and policy implica -
tions. The reporting of the search and study selection process was informed by PRISMA
principles to improve transparency, while retaining the interpretive flexibility appropri -
ate for a narrative review.
2.2 Search strategy and information sources
A structured literature search was conducted across four bibliographic databases,
PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, and African Journals Online (AJOL), covering records
published between 1 January 2015 and 31 October 2025. All database searches were exe-
cuted on 15 October 2025. Searches were restricted to English-language publications; no
other language limits were applied. Search terms were combined using Boolean opera -
tors and adapted to the indexing conventions of each database. Records were managed
and de-duplicated using Mendeley reference management software; no specialised sys -
tematic-review screening software was used, and title/abstract screening was performed
manually by two reviewers. Because ResearchGate is a researcher-networking platform
rather than a bibliographic database, it was not included in the formal Boolean search
strategy; instead, it was searched separately and used only as a supplementary source to
retrieve grey literature and theses not indexed elsewhere, with all records independently
cross-checked against PubMed, Scopus, or AJOL before inclusion to avoid duplication
and to verify peer-review status.
The following structured Boolean search strings were used:
PubMed / Scopus Core String: (adolescent* OR youth) AND Nigeria AND (“social
media” OR “digital marketing” OR “digital food environment” OR “influencer market -
ing”) AND (diet* OR nutrition OR “food choice” OR “dietary behaviour” OR obesity OR
“noncommunicable disease”).
Google Scholar / AJOL Supplemental String: (TikTok OR Instagram OR WhatsApp
OR YouTube) AND “dietary behaviour” AND Nigeria AND (“noncommunicable dis -
eases” OR NR-NCDs OR obesity OR hypertension).

2.3 Eligibility criteria
Studies were selected based on relevance to adolescent nutrition, social media exposure,
digital food environments, dietary behaviour, body image, nutrition misinformation, and
nutrition-related noncommunicable disease risk. The inclusion and exclusion criteria
are summarised below.
2.4 Study selection process
Retrieved records were first screened by title and abstract to assess relevance to the
review objectives. Full texts were then assessed for eligibility based on the inclusion and
exclusion criteria (Table 1). Studies were excluded if they did not focus on adolescents or
youth, did not address nutrition-related outcomes, focused only on non-dietary aspects
of social media use, were not relevant to Nigeria or comparable contexts, or lacked suf -
ficient methodological detail. Where Nigeria-specific evidence was limited, African and
global studies were retained only to support contextual interpretation or to explain plau-
sible mechanisms. Although this article is a narrative rather than a systematic review, a
simplified study-selection flow diagram is presented (Fig.  2) to improve methodological
transparency, alongside a tabulated summary of the selection process.

2.5 Data extraction
Data were extracted using a structured template. Extracted information included author,
year of publication, country or setting, study design, sample characteristics, population
age group, exposure type, dietary or health outcome, key findings, study limitations, and
relevance to adolescent nutrition or NR-NCD risk in Nigeria.
2.6 Evidence appraisal
Because this was a narrative review involving methodologically heterogeneous evidence,
formal meta-analytic risk-of-bias scoring was not conducted. Evidence appraisal fol -
lowed an adapted narrative appraisal approach informed by Mixed Methods Appraisal
Tool (MMAT) criteria, applied descriptively. Each retained source was evaluated against
five dimensions:
  • Clarity and appropriateness of study design and methods;
  • Relevance of the study population to the review objective (adolescents aged 10–19
years; Nigerian or Sub-Saharan African settings);
  • Directness and contextual transferability of findings to Nigeria;
  • Methodological risk, including self-report bias, cross-sectional design limitations,
and non-probability sampling; and.
  • Consistency of findings with related evidence in the broader literature.
Greater interpretive weight was assigned to peer-reviewed Nigerian and African studies
with clear sampling strategies, validated dietary assessment instruments (where appli -
cable), and direct measurement of relevant nutritional or behavioural outcomes. Afri -
can studies from comparable LMIC settings were used for regional comparison. Global
studies were applied cautiously and exclusively to explain mechanistic pathways not yet
empirically investigated in Nigeria. All evidence-tier attributions are signalled explicitly
in the main text using the designations: Nigeria-specific evidence; Regional African evi -
dence; and Global mechanistic evidence.
2.7 Data synthesis
Findings were synthesised thematically rather than statistically. The synthesis focused on
five analytical themes: digital food marketing and influencer exposure; peer validation
and online food trends; nutrition misinformation, body image, and fad diets; algorith -
mic reinforcement and predictive targeting; and public health and policy implications.
Within each theme, Nigerian evidence was prioritised, African evidence was used for
regional comparison, and global evidence was used cautiously to explain mechanisms
not yet well studied in Nigeria.
The synthesis also considered structural determinants such as household income,
poverty, food affordability, school food environments, urban–rural differences, cultural
dietary systems, and regulatory gaps.
2.8 Methodological limitations
First, because it was designed as a narrative review, it does not provide pooled effect
estimates or formal causal inference. Second, the available Nigerian evidence directly
linking social media exposure to measured adolescent dietary or metabolic outcomes
remains limited. Third, some mechanisms discussed in the review, such as algorithmic

3 Main text
3.1 Social media exposure and adolescent dietary behaviour in Nigeria
Social media has become a defining force in shaping the dietary behaviours of Nigerian
adolescents, exposing them to continuous food marketing, celebrity endorsements, and
peer-driven consumption norms [ 25]. The rapid expansion of smartphone access and
internet penetration has enabled food and beverage companies to extend their mar -
keting reach through digital platforms, employing interactive and personalised strate -
gies that target adolescents’ social aspirations and emotional vulnerabilities [ 26]. These
trends mirror global patterns yet unfold within a uniquely Nigerian sociocultural land -
scape characterised by rapid digital adoption, urbanisation, and shifting youth identities
[26]. Bankole et al. [ 7] reported that more than 93% of secondary-school adolescents in
Ikeja used social media for over 30 min daily, with nearly half engaging with food-related
content and influencer-driven advertisements. Although these findings suggest that
Nigerian adolescents are exposed to persuasive food marketing online, most available
evidence remains observational. Therefore, the reviewed studies support an association
between digital exposure and unhealthy food preferences, but they do not establish that
social media independently causes poor dietary outcomes. This distinction is important
because adolescent food choices are also shaped by household income, school food envi-
ronments, peer networks, food availability, parental influence, and cultural food prac -
tices. Similarly, Oguizu et al. [ 27] reported high consumption of ultra-processed foods
among adolescents in Umuahia North and observed a significant association between
body mass index and selected energy-dense snacks. Although the study did not directly
attribute this pattern to social media promotion, the authors acknowledged that per -
vasive advertising and celebrity endorsements likely reinforce these consumption
preferences.
Celebrity and influencer culture intensifies this digital dietary environment. Al-Haifi
et al. [ 28] found that 51% of adolescents were “often tempted” by food advertisements
on social media, with notable gender differences; females responded more strongly to
body-image-driven messages, while males were more influenced by performance- and
portion-related appeals. Comparable observations in Nigeria reveal that influencers
on Instagram, TikTok, and Snapchat leverage lifestyle imagery, humour, and perceived
authenticity to promote sugary drinks, snacks, and fast-food brands; unlike traditional
broadcast advertising, this online marketing remains largely unregulated. Beyond cor -
porate advertising, peer networks reinforce dietary decisions through a form of “social
proof, ” where likes, comments, and shares validate eating practices and signal social
desirability, operating less as direct instruction than as social normalisation. Bankole et
al. [ 7] found that 41.9% of adolescents described themselves as body-conscious about
what they consumed, while 37% reported visiting cafés or restaurants after seeing
friends post about food online. These findings illustrate how digital interactions convert
consumption into a performance of identity and social inclusion. Gender differences are

involving health professionals, educators, youth influencers, and content creators could
develop culturally resonant narratives that promote balanced eating, debunk dietary

harnessing this potential requires coordinated action across schools, regulators, and
technology companies, discussed further in Sect.  3.3.
Ultimately, the multifaceted role of social media in Nigeria, spanning commercial,
peer, and informational influences, reveals both structural and psychosocial determi -
nants of adolescent diet quality. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for design -
ing interventions that counteract harmful exposure while leveraging the medium’s
potential for sustainable adolescent nutrition and NR-NCD prevention.
3.2 Implications for nutrition-related noncommunicable diseases (NR-NCDs)
Nutrition-related noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), including cardiovascular dis -
eases, type 2 diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and certain cancers, represent leading causes
of global morbidity and mortality, with adolescence recognised as a critical prevention
window [31]. Among Nigerian adolescents, unhealthy dietary patterns are increasingly

Page 13 of 24
Ademiluyi et al. Discover Public Health         (2026) 23:1533
prevalent, marked by high consumption of refined carbohydrates, saturated fats, and
sugar-sweetened foods, coupled with low intake of fruits, vegetables, and fibre-rich
foods. These habits are closely associated with early manifestations of metabolic syn -
drome, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, abdominal obesity, and impaired glucose
metabolism [32]. Such metabolic disturbances heighten long-term vulnerability to dia -
betes and cardiovascular diseases [ 33]. The coexistence of undernutrition and overnutri-
tion, commonly described as the “double burden” of malnutrition, has intensified across
low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Nigeria, as traditional diets give
way to energy-dense, nutrient-poor foods amid rapid urbanisation [ 13, 34]. Empiri -
cal evidence from southwestern Nigeria shows that adolescents frequently fall short
of recommended dietary allowances for essential nutrients such as calcium, iron, and
protein, alongside inadequate fruit and vegetable consumption. This poor dietary qual -
ity aligns with the observed rise in overweight and obesity among students [ 17]. This
shift toward Westernised diets elevates adolescents’ NR-NCD susceptibility into adult -
hood [ 13]. Adolescent overweight and obesity have risen sharply worldwide over the
past four decades [ 13, 16]. Between 1975 and 2016, global adolescent obesity increased
by over 400%, with African prevalence estimated between 0.6% and 9.3% [ 35, 3]. The
World Health Organisation [15] reports that 5-16.5% of African adolescents are affected,
and obesity-related morbidity and mortality now surpass those from undernutrition.
In Nigeria, where adolescents represent approximately 25% of the population, obesity-
related disorders such as type 2 diabetes and hypertension are increasingly reported [ 15,
36]. The economic burden of obesity is significant, projected to exceed US$18 trillion
globally by 2060, accounting for 3.29% of global GDP [ 37]. The escalation of adoles -
cent obesity correlates with a parallel rise in prehypertension and hypertension, both
of which are strong predictors of adult cardiovascular disease [ 38]. Although regional
data from Nigeria are limited, available evidence underscores the positive associa -
tion between body mass index (BMI) and blood pressure among adolescents [ 39, 40].
Given the continuity between adolescent and adult hypertension, more context-specific
research is needed to elucidate weight-blood pressure dynamics across Nigeria’s diverse
geographic and socioeconomic regions [ 39]. Urban-rural disparities and socioeco -
nomic stratification further mediate adolescent NCD risk, as urbanisation accelerates
dietary shifts toward calorie-dense, processed foods [ 41]. Urban adolescents exposed to
fast-food outlets, sedentary lifestyles, and high consumption of sugary beverages show
higher obesity risk compared to rural counterparts [ 42]. Nonetheless, the penetration of
processed foods into rural markets is gradually narrowing this divide [ 43, 44]. Socioeco-
nomic status, parental education, and income strongly influence food preferences and
access to health care, with higher-income households displaying greater consumption of
convenience foods but better access to health information [ 1, 45]. Effective prevention,
therefore, requires multi-level interventions addressing not only dietary behaviour but
also structural determinants of food access and education [46].
The proliferation of digital food marketing and influencer culture has introduced a
new pathway linking social media exposure to NR-NCD risk. Multinational fast-food
companies have invested billions in social media advertising, targeting youth through
algorithmic personalisation and influencer collaborations [ 26, 1]. Despite the World
Health Organisation’s (2020) call for restrictions on unhealthy food marketing to minors,
enforcement across LMICs remains inconsistent [ 47]. In Nigeria, such unregulated

Page 14 of 24
Ademiluyi et al. Discover Public Health         (2026) 23:1533
digital spaces enable aggressive marketing strategies that frame fast food as aspirational
and modern, encouraging overconsumption and fostering obesogenic behaviours [ 10,
48, 49].
Social media also acts as a conduit for psychological and behavioural pathways that
exacerbate diet-related health risks. Image-centric platforms such as Instagram and
TikTok promote idealised body types that foster social comparison, body dissatisfac -
tion, and disordered eating behaviours [ 17, 50]. These dynamics, though once consid -
ered Western, now permeate adolescent cultures in LMICs, including Nigeria, leading
to increased body-image anxiety and unhealthy dietary restriction [ 40, 51]. Algorithmic
reinforcement further traps users in echo chambers of diet-related content, intensifying
exposure to harmful narratives [52]. At the environmental level, limited access to healthy
foods and pervasive exposure to energy-dense options reinforce poor dietary habits [ 1,
53]. As adolescents often rely on online sources for dietary information, strengthening
home and school food environments, digital health literacy, and responsible-advertising
enforcement are pivotal strategies for reducing social media-driven NR-NCD risk. This
influence is further shaped by gendered socialisation patterns, with body image serv -
ing as a key determinant of self-worth [ 1, 54]. Female adolescents are more frequently
exposed to beauty-centric content emphasising thinness and perfection, which is associ -
ated with greater social comparison and disordered eating risk [ 55]. Conversely, male
adolescents encounter pressures toward muscularity and athleticism, reinforced by fit -
ness influencers and sports culture [56, 57].
These gender-specific ideals extend beyond self-perception to influence dietary hab -
its such as restrictive dieting among girls and high-protein supplementation among
boys, both of which may contribute to imbalanced nutrition and long-term metabolic
risk [58]. Addressing these disparities requires gender-sensitive interventions that pro -
mote positive body image, digital literacy, and media awareness. Programs should also
empower adolescents to critically evaluate online messages and celebrate diverse body
representations, fostering resilience against harmful beauty and dietary norms in Nige -
ria’s evolving digital landscape.
3.3 Public health and policy implications
Social media has emerged as a dominant information source among Nigerian youths,
with 86.1% using it daily. However, a substantial proportion remains unaware of disin -
formation (58.3%) and lacks formal media literacy training (34.3%) [ 59]. Media literacy,
therefore, represents a vital public health strategy not only for countering political and
social misinformation but also for equipping adolescents to evaluate food advertise -
ments, influencer promotions, and nutrition-related content encountered online. The
proliferation of unverified food promotions, influencer marketing, and algorithm-driven
advertising can distort adolescents’ understanding of healthy eating and nutritional
risk. Empowering youths through media literacy education will enhance their capac -
ity to resist manipulative tactics such as emotional branding, peer-influencer persua -
sion, and celebrity endorsement commonly employed by fast-food corporations [ 2].
Schools are uniquely positioned to deliver structured, credible, and sustained nutrition
education that can offset the influence of misleading food messages prevalent on social
media. Evidence from Lagos State demonstrates that integrating digital nutrition edu -
cation into the curriculum significantly improves adolescents’ dietary knowledge and

behaviours, resulting in healthier body weight and reduced disordered eating tendencies
[60]. Embedding digital nutrition learning modules within school health curricula would
ensure that students receive continuous exposure to evidence-based dietary informa -
tion aligned with national dietary guidelines. Atoloye et al. [ 61] found that combining
digital tools such as WhatsApp groups with experiential learning initiatives like school-
based gardening effectively improved adolescents’ dietary choices and engagement with
local foods. Similarly, a Delta State intervention reported a strong positive correlation
between nutrition education and healthier household food consumption ( r = 0.723,
p = 0.039), marked by decreased intake of sugary beverages, snacks, and instant noodles
[62]. These findings affirm that early, consistent, and digitally integrated nutrition educa-
tion can cultivate lifelong healthy habits, reduce NR-NCD risk, and promote intergen -
erational health literacy [63].
The aggressive marketing of ultra-processed foods to adolescents via social media
platforms has become a growing public health concern in Nigeria. Fast-food companies
increasingly deploy emotionally engaging, youth-targeted campaigns on Instagram, Tik -
Tok, and Facebook, using slang, challenges, and competitions to deepen brand engage -
ment [7]. These strategies normalise consumption of energy-dense, nutrient-poor foods,
embedding them within adolescent social culture. As a result, government-led regula -
tion of digital food marketing is urgently required to protect adolescents from harm -
ful commercial influences. Existing voluntary marketing codes have proven inadequate
to control the persuasive reach of online advertising [ 64]. Effective policy frameworks
should therefore mandate stricter controls, including: (1) clear age-based advertising
restrictions; (2) transparency in influencer partnerships and sponsorships; (3) warn -
ings for unhealthy food promotions; and (4) restrictions on interactive features such as
“likes, ” shares, and competitions that foster viral engagement.
Addressing the nutritional consequences of unregulated digital marketing requires
multi-sectoral collaboration across policy, academia, and industry. Joint frameworks
between the government, nutrition experts, and technology companies can ensure
accountability and promote ethical advertising standards on digital platforms. Klemm
[65] underscores the importance of professional guidelines such as the Guidance for
Professional Use of Social Media in Nutrition and Dietetics Practice for ensuring that
nutrition information disseminated online is evidence-based, accurate, and socially
responsible. As Menakaya and Unuigbe [ 66] note, effective counteraction against misin -
formation demands coordinated actions across institutions. Collaboration between the
Federal Ministry of Health, the National Broadcasting Commission, and social media
companies could facilitate the monitoring of digital advertisements, promote algorith -
mic transparency, and amplify credible nutrition content. While social media has often
been implicated in promoting Western-style, processed diets, it equally presents oppor -
tunities for positive engagement. When strategically harnessed, these platforms can
disseminate culturally grounded nutrition education, emphasising traditional Nigerian
foods rich in legumes, tubers, fruits, and vegetables. Integrating indigenous dietary pro -
motion into digital interventions such as WhatsApp-based nutrition groups or influ -
encer-led “Eat Local” campaigns can foster pride in local food systems while enhancing
dietary diversity [59].
Evidence from Delta State supports this approach, linking nutrition education with
reduced consumption of ultra-processed foods and greater appreciation for traditional

campaigns that promote traditional diets in Nigeria, representing a critical gap for future

3.3.1 Processed food sector growth and adolescent public health sustainability
A major policy challenge is the tension between the economic growth of the processed
food sector and the sustainability of adolescent public health. However, when growth is
driven by aggressive promotion of energy-dense and nutrient-poor products to young
consumers, the long-term health costs may be transferred to households, schools, and
the health system through rising obesity, hypertension, diabetes risk, and disordered
eating.
This tension requires a balanced public health approach. The food industry should not
be treated only as a threat but as a sector requiring stronger accountability. Public health
sustainability would require product reformulation, clearer nutrition labelling, respon -
sible marketing standards, and restrictions on youth-targeted advertising. In this regard,
food-sector sustainability should include not only profit and operational performance,
but also the nutritional consequences of products marketed to adolescents.
The comparative study by Thongmun et al. [ 66] on food-processing SMEs provides a
useful broader lens for understanding food-sector sustainability as a multidimensional
issue involving agility, innovation, and governance. Although not Nigeria-specific, it
supports the argument that food-sector growth should be aligned with social respon -
sibility and public health protection, particularly where young consumers are a major
marketing target (Table 3).
4 Discussion
This review demonstrates that social media has emerged as a significant environmental
determinant of adolescent dietary behaviour in Nigeria, operating through commercial,
peer, psychological, and algorithmic pathways. The convergence of influencer marketing,
peer social validation, algorithmic content personalisation, and nutrition misinforma -
tion creates a digital food environment that may promote consumption of ultra-pro -
cessed, energy-dense foods while normalising body ideals inconsistent with diverse and
nutritionally balanced diets. These dynamics amplify Nigeria’s ongoing nutrition transi -
tion, potentially increasing the risk of overweight, obesity, hypertension, and early-onset
metabolic syndrome among adolescents.
The algorithmic dimension of this digital food environment deserves particular cau -
tion in interpretation. Recommender systems that rank content by predicted engage -
ment, and advertising systems that classify users from behavioural signals such as
viewing history and engagement patterns, are structurally capable of repeatedly sur -
facing calorie-dense food content, appearance-focused material, and targeted food or
diet advertising to adolescents who have previously engaged with similar content. This
mechanism is well documented in the global digital-media literature, but no studies
identified in this review directly measured algorithmic exposure or platform recommen-
dation pathways among Nigerian adolescents; the pathway is therefore presented here as
a plausible, theory-driven hypothesis rather than an established empirical finding, and is
a priority for future Nigeria-specific research using digital trace data or platform-audit
methods.

Comparing findings across Nigerian, African, and global evidence reveals both con -
vergence and important divergence. Nigerian studies, particularly Bankole et al. [ 7],
Okonkwo and Dlamini [29], and Danjuma et al. [30], are consistent with global literature
in identifying associations between social media engagement and unhealthy food pref -
erences, body dissatisfaction, and eating-disorder risk. However, several mechanisms
extensively documented in Western settings, including direct measurement of social
media algorithms on dietary intake, longitudinal tracking from digital exposure to NR-
NCD biomarkers, and randomised digital intervention trials, remain absent from the
Nigerian evidence base. This represents a significant evidence gap, not merely a replica -
tion gap.
The evidence also contains important contradictions and heterogeneities that require
acknowledgement. Several studies suggest that higher socioeconomic status and private-
school enrolment are associated with both greater social media exposure and better
dietary knowledge. This creates an apparent paradox: adolescents with greater digital
access may simultaneously be more exposed to commercial food marketing and better
equipped with health literacy to critically evaluate it. This contradiction suggests that
social media’s dietary impact is not uniform and that its net effect depends heavily on
co-occurring factors, such as digital literacy, school quality, parental education, and food
access, that vary substantially across Nigeria’s socioeconomic and geographic landscape.
Urban-rural disparities in digital dietary risk are increasingly documented but under -
theorised. Urban adolescents in Lagos, Abuja, and Port Harcourt report higher daily
social media engagement and fast-food consumption, consistent with a co-exposure
hypothesis; however, expanding rural mobile internet access and cheap ultra-processed
snacks in informal markets are narrowing this divide, demanding a shift from urban-
centric toward national policy frameworks.
The gendered dimensions of digital dietary influence require distinct policy and inter -
vention responses. Female adolescents bear disproportionate exposure to thinness- and
beauty-focused content, with body dissatisfaction emerging as the stronger predictor of
disordered eating than social media exposure per se [29]. This finding is clinically signifi-
cant: it suggests that interventions targeting social media exposure alone may be insuf -
ficient, and that building psychological resilience through body-positive programming,
self-esteem strengthening, and critical media literacy may be equally or more important.
Male adolescents face a parallel but distinct risk: muscularity-oriented content drives
unverified protein supplement use and body-building dietary patterns that may compro-
mise nutritional balance. Gender-responsive digital health interventions designed sepa -
rately for female and male adolescent populations are warranted.
The commercial determinants of health framework offers a useful lens here: multi -
national food corporations, advertising platforms, and influencer economies form a
commercially organised system whose financial incentives are structurally aligned with
promoting energy-dense, high-margin foods to young consumers. Platform design fea -
tures, algorithmic recommendations, infinite scroll, social validation mechanisms, and
frictionless in-app purchasing are commercially optimised to maximise engagement
and advertising revenue. Public health responses relying solely on individual behaviour
change or media literacy are therefore unlikely to be sufficient without structural regula-
tory complements such as advertising restrictions, algorithmic transparency, and influ -
encer disclosure requirements.

4.1 Limitations and future research
Although this review provides important insights into how digital media influences
adolescent nutrition in Nigeria, significant research gaps remain. Most existing studies
emphasise general dietary patterns without exploring the specific mechanisms through
which digital exposure shapes adolescents’ food preferences and nutritional behaviours.
Recent studies by Uthman-Akinhanmi et al. [ 25] and Fretes et al. [ 1] highlight the ris -
ing consumption of energy-dense snacks and low dietary diversity among adolescents,
though they provide limited analysis of how digital marketing or online food environ -
ments contribute to these patterns. Moreover, few Nigerian studies have examined the
long-term impacts of digital dietary exposure on measurable nutrition outcomes such
as obesity risk, micronutrient deficiency, or disordered eating. There is also a paucity of
research examining gender, socioeconomic, and regional disparities in adolescents’ digi -
tal diet behaviours, despite evidence of unequal access to technology and varying dietary
norms across Nigeria [ 7]. Future research should therefore employ mixed-method and
longitudinal designs to examine causal pathways between digital food marketing and
adolescent health outcomes. Comparative studies across urban and rural contexts are
also needed to capture regional disparities in digital access and dietary behaviour. Fur -
ther studies should also assess the effectiveness of digital nutrition education interven -
tions and social media campaigns, as well as how adolescents interpret and interact with
online nutrition content. These findings will inform culturally sensitive and evidence-
based digital strategies for improving adolescent dietary practices in Nigeria.
5 Conclusion and recommendations
This structured narrative review demonstrates that social media is an increasingly influ -
ential environmental determinant of adolescent dietary behaviour and nutritional health
in Nigeria. Commercial digital food marketing, algorithmic content personalisation,
influencer culture, peer social validation, body-image pressures, and nutrition misinfor -
mation collectively constitute a digital food environment that may promote consump -
tion of ultra-processed, energy-dense foods while normalising nutritionally poor dietary
patterns. These mechanisms are theoretically coherent and are supported by an emerg -
ing Nigerian evidence base, although direct causal evidence linking social media expo -
sure to measured NR-NCD biomarkers among Nigerian adolescents remains limited.
The findings of this review should therefore be interpreted as evidence of plausible and
policy-relevant pathways requiring urgent empirical investigation rather than as defini -
tive causal conclusions.
Priority public health actions should include: (1) regulatory restrictions on digital
marketing of unhealthy foods to minors, enforced through NAFDAC and the National

Page 21 of 24

Broadcasting Commission; (2) mandatory disclosure of paid influencer partnerships
involving food and beverage brands; (3) integration of digital nutrition literacy and
critical media literacy into the National Curriculum for secondary schools; (4) national
monitoring of online nutrition misinformation, with authority to mandate corrections
on major platforms; and (5) government-supported digital campaigns promoting afford -
able traditional Nigerian foods, dietary diversity, and body-positive representations that
reflect the diversity of Nigerian adolescent populations.
Future research must employ longitudinal, mixed-methods, and digital analytics
designs to establish causal mechanisms and population-level effects. Priority research
questions include: How do specific platform algorithms interact with socioeconomic
vulnerability to shape adolescent dietary outcomes across Nigeria’s diverse urban-
rural and regional landscape? What digital intervention designs effectively shift both
knowledge and dietary practice, particularly among lower-income and public-school
adolescents? How do gendered patterns of social media food exposure translate into
differential NR-NCD risk trajectories? Answering these questions will be essential for
designing evidence-based, contextually appropriate digital public health strategies for
Nigerian adolescents.
