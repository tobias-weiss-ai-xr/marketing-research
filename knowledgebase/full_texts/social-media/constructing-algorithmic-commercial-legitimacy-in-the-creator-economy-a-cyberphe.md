ABSTRACT: The creator economy has transformed digital commerce by positioning creators as entrepreneurial actors operating
within algorithm-driven platform ecosystems. Existing studies primarily explain influencer credibility, consumer trust, and social
commerce from consumer -centric perspectives, providing limited understanding of how creators construct commercial
legitimacy through their lived experiences. This study addresses this gap by exploring the experiences of TikTok affiliate cr eators
using a qualitative cyberphenomenological approach. Data were collected through in -depth semi -structured interviews and
digital observations and analyzed using iterative coding, thematic development, and theory triangulation. The findings identi fy
four interconnected dimensions of commercial legitimacy: digital identity construction, algorithmic visibility negotiation,
marketplace trust curation, and adaptive commercial learning. These dimensions interact dynamically, demonstrating that
creators continuously negotiate legitimacy through interactions with platform algorithms, audiences, and marketplace actors
rather than relying solely on persuasive communication or consumer evaluation. Based on these findings, the study proposes
Algorithmic Commercial Legitimacy as a novel theoretical construct that extends Source Credibility Theory, Signaling Theory,
Social Commerce Theory, and Creator Economy literature. The study contributes by offering a creator -centered explanation of
legitimacy construction and advancing cyberphenomenology as an interpretive approach for understanding human –algorithm
interaction in digital commerce.
KEYWORDS: Creator economy; Algorithmic commercial legitimacy; Cyberphenomenology; Social commerce; TikTok affiliate;
Digital entrepreneurship.

I. INTRODUCTION
The emergence of the creator economy has fundamentally transformed how economic value is created, distributed, and
monetized within digital platform ecosystems  (Bleier et al., 2024) . Unlike traditional marketing systems, where firms primarily
control communication and commercial activities, the creator economy enables individuals to become independent economic
actors who simultaneously function as content creators, entrepreneurs, marketers, and community builders. Supported by
artificial intelligence, recommendation algorithms, and platform technologies, creators increasingly generate economic value
through digital content, audience engagement, and platform  mediated commercial interactions. The creator economy has
evolved into a global ecosystem valued at more than USD 100 billion and continues to expand rapidly, positioning creators,
platforms, consumers, and firms as interdependent stakeholders in digital value creation. Consequently, the creator economy
has become one of the most important emerging research agendas in contemporary marketing, with scholars calling for deeper
investigations into how creators establish their brands, optimize content, build audiences, monetize digital interactions, an d
create sustainable commercial ecosystems within platform environments  (Peres et al., 2024).
Among contemporary digital platforms, TikTok represents one of the most influential examples of algorithm -driven social
commerce. Unlike conventional social networking platforms that primarily rely on follower -based information diffusion, TikTok
employs an artificial intelligence -powered recommendation system that continuously evaluates user interactions, engagement
signals, viewing behavior, and content relevance to determine content visibility. This algorithmic architecture fundamentally
reshapes digital entrepreneurship because creators' commercial success increasingly depends not only on creative capabilities
but also on their ability to understand, interpret, and continuously adapt to algorithmic mechanisms governing audience

Despite these important contributions, three important theoretical gaps remain. First, existing research primarily explains
what influences consumer behaviour but provides limited understanding of how creators themselves experience and interpret

explanation regarding how creators themselves construct credibility through repeated interaction with platform algorithms,
audiences, and marketplace infrastructures. Consequently, the experiential mechanisms underlying creators' commercial
adaptation remain insufficiently theorized despite becoming increasingly central to the creator economy.
Addressing these limitations requires an interpretive perspective capable of revealing the meanings creators construct
through their everyday interactions with digital platforms. From a cyberphenomenological perspective, digital platforms are n ot
merely technological tools but lived experiential environments in which human experiences, technological affordances, and
algorithmic processes continuously shape one another. Rather than simply producing promotional content, TikTok affiliate
creators constantly interpret algorithmic feedback, evaluate audience responses, negotiate marketplace relationships, refine
commercial strategies, and adapt content production based on ongoing digital experiences. Commercial performance therefore
emerges through an iterative process of meaning construction, adaptation, and negotiation occurring within algorithmically
mediated social commerce ecosystems.
Accordingly, this study adopts Cyberphenomenology to investigate the lived experiences of TikTok affiliate creators operating
within algorithm-driven social commerce. Instead of conceptualizing affiliate marketing solely as a promotional mechanism, this
study positions creators as adaptive digital entrepreneurs who continuously negotiate visibility, audience trust, marketplace
relationships, and commercial sustainability through ongoing interaction with platform algorithms. By shifting analytical
attention from consumer responses toward creators' lived experiences, this research extends current discussions on creator
economy, social commerce, and digital entrepreneurship beyond conventional marketing perspectives.

II. THEORITICAL REVIEW
2.1 Creator Economy: The Transformation of Digital Value Creation
The creator economy represents one of the most significant structural transformations in contemporary marketing, shifting
value creation from firm -centric production toward creator -driven digital ecosystems  (Filieri et al., 2023) . Unlike conventional
marketing systems in which organizations primarily control communication, branding, and commercial exchange, the creator
economy enables individuals to generate, distribute, and monetize digital content through continuous interaction with
audiences, digital platforms, brands, and artificial intelligence technologies. Consequently, creators have evolved from pass ive
content producers into entrepreneurial actors who simultaneously perform the roles of marketers, innovators, community
builders, and business owners. This transformation has prompted scholars to recognize the creator economy as a strategic
research domain requiring new theoretical perspectives capable of explaining value creation, platform governance,
monetization mechanisms, and creator–platform relationships beyond traditional marketing theories (Farrell et al., 2026).
Recent literature further argues that the creator economy should not be understood merely as an extension of influencer
marketing. Rather, it constitutes an interconnected ecosystem involving creators, consumers, firms, platforms, advertisers,
artificial intelligence, and digital infrastructures that collectively shape commercial value creation. Within this ecosystem ,
creators compete not only through content quality but also through their ability to develop personal brands, cultivate audien ce
communities, maintain authenticity, and continuously respond to platform dynamics. Accordingly, commercial performance
increasingly depends upon creators' capacity to adapt to evolving technological environments rather than solely upon
persuasive communication or promotional effectiveness. This perspective fundamentally redefines creators as adaptive
entrepreneurs operating within digitally mediated markets instead of marketing intermediaries who merely influence consumer
purchasing decisions (Bhargava, 2022).
The emergence of algorithm -driven platforms has further intensified this transformation by fundamentally altering the
mechanisms through which commercial opportunities are created. Rather than relying on organizational resources, creators
increasingly depend upon platform algorithms to obtain visibility, audience exposure, and monetization opportunities. Platfor m
algorithms therefore function not simply as technological infrastructures but as economic gatekeepers that regulate creators'
access to audiences and commercial opportunities. Consequently, success within the creator economy requires creators to
continuously interpret algorithmic feedback, experiment with content strategies, optimize audience engagement, and adapt
entrepreneurial practices in response to changing platform conditions. This algorithmic dependence distinguishes the creator
economy from conventional entrepreneurship and highlights the need to understand creators' lived experiences within
platform-mediated environments (Wong et al., 2024).
Despite the rapid development of creator economy research, several conceptual limitations remain. Existing studies have
predominantly focused on creator monetization, branding strategies, audience engagement, platform governance, and firm
creator collaboration (Tafesse & Dayan, 2023) . While these perspectives substantially advance understanding of platform -based
business models, they primarily examine creators from organizational, managerial, or consumer viewpoints  (Rieder et al., 2023).
Consequently, relatively little attention has been devoted to understanding how creators themselves experience algorithmicall y
mediated commercial environments, construct meanings from continuous interaction with platform infrastructures, and
develop adaptive entrepreneurial practices through everyday digital experiences. This limitation suggests that current creato r
economy literature remains largely outcome -oriented while providing limited insight into the experiential processes through
which commercial legitimacy is constructed within algorithm driven ecosystems(Edeling & Wies, 2024) .
Accordingly, this study positions the creator economy not merely as a commercial ecosystem but as an experiential environment
in which creators continuously negotiate technological affordances, audience expectations, and marketplace dynamics. By
adopting a cyberphenomenological perspective, this study extends current creator economy research beyond observable
marketing outcomes toward a deeper understanding of how creators interpret, negotiate, and construct commercial legitimacy

2.2 Algorithm-Driven Platforms and Algorithmic Visibility
The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence has transformed digital platforms from passive communication infrastructures
into intelligent ecosystems capable of continuously organizing, filtering, and recommending information based on algorithmic
decision-making. Unlike traditional social networking platforms where content dissemination was largely determined by users'
existing social connections, contemporary platforms increasingly rely on algorithmic recommendation systems to determine
which content is distributed, to whom it is delivered, and when it receives visibility. Consequently, algorithms have become
central actors in digital platform governance, significantly influencing creators' opportunities to gain audience attention,
maintain engagement, and generate commercial value (Zhang et al., 2025).
Within this context, TikTok represents one of the most sophisticated examples of algorithm -driven platforms. Its
recommendation system continuously analyzes user interactions, watch duration, engagement patterns, content relevance, and
behavioral signals to personalize content exposure. Rather than rewarding creators solely based on the number of followers,
TikTok dynamically evaluates each piece of content according to its predicted relevance for individual users. This
recommendation mechanism fundamentally changes the logic of digital visibility because commercial success depends
increasingly upon creators' ability to continuously adapt to algorithmic preferences rather than merely accumulating follower s
or publishing promotional content (Sardianos & Briana, 2026).
The growing dependence upon algorithmic recommendation systems has introduced new forms of entrepreneurial uncertainty
within platform ecosystems. Creators are required to continuously experiment with content formats, publishing schedules,
audience interaction strategies, and engagement techniques to maintain visibility within constantly evolving algorithmic
environments. Consequently, commercial performance becomes the outcome of an ongoing adaptive process through which
creators interpret algorithmic responses and modify subsequent actions accordingly. Rather than functioning as static
technological infrastructures, algorithms increasingly shape creators' strategic decision -making by continuously influencing the
opportunities available for audience exposure, market participation, and commercial growth  (Krouglov, 2024).
Recent studies increasingly acknowledge that algorithmic systems actively shape digital entrepreneurship, platform
participation, and creator performance  (Liu, 2025) . Nevertheless, the majority of existing research conceptualizes algorithms
primarily as technological mechanisms affecting measurable marketing outcomes, including engagement, recommendation
accuracy, and content diffusion. Such perspectives provide valuable explanations regarding algorithm effectiveness but offer
limited understanding of how creators themselves experience algorithmic governance during their everyday commercial
activities. More specifically, relatively little is known about how creators interpret algorithmic feedback, negotiate changi ng
platform expectations, and construct adaptive commercial strategies through repeated interaction with recommendation
systems (Serttaş et al., 2025).
From a cyberphenomenological perspective, algorithms should not be understood merely as computational procedures
operating behind digital platforms. Instead, algorithms constitute experiential environments that contin uously shape creators'
perceptions, actions, and commercial decisions. Every interaction with platform analytics, audience responses, declining reac h,
increasing visibility, or changing engagement patterns becomes part of creators' lived digital experiences  (Lin, 2025) .
Consequently, creators continuously construct meanings regarding how algorithms function, what types of content are
rewarded, and which commercial strategies are considered legitimate within platform ecosystems. These interpretive processes
gradually influence subsequent entrepreneurial decisions and ultimately shape the way creators understand commercial success
in algorithmically mediated environments (Heřmanová et al., 2026).
Accordingly, this study argues that algorithmic visibility should not be conceptualized solely as an outcome of recommendatio n
systems but also as an experie ntial process through which creators continuously negotiate their commercial existence within
digital platforms. Understanding this process requires moving beyond technological explanations toward an interpretive
understanding of how creators construct meaning through ongoing interaction with algorithmic infrastructures. Such a
perspective provides an important theoretical foundation for explaining how commercial legitimacy emerges within creator
economy ecosystems and establishes the conceptual basis for developing the notion of Algorithmic Credibility explored in the
present study.

2.3 Social Commerce Trust and Creator Credibility
The rapid evolution of social commerce has fundamentally transformed online purchasing from transaction -oriented exchanges
into relationship-driven commercial ecosystems where trust functions as the primary mechanism facilitating consumer decision -
making (Al-Kfairy et al., 2023). Unlike conventional e-commerce, social commerce integrates commercial transactions with user -
generated content, interpersonal communication, social interaction, and community participation, allowing consumers to
evaluate products through creators' experiences rather than relying solely on firm -generated information. Consequently,
creators increasingly function as trust intermediaries who reduce consumers' uncertainty by providing product demonstrations,
experiential reviews, and authentic recommendations. Recent studies consistently demonstrate that trust remains one of the
strongest predictors of consumer engagement, purchase intention, and long -term participation within social commerce
ecosystems (Hsu & Hu, 2024).
Within influencer marketing research, creator credibility has traditionally been explained through Source Credibility Theory,
which proposes that consumers evaluate information based on the perceived expertise, trustworthiness, and attractiveness of
the communicator  (Alkhalifah, 2022) . This perspective has significantly contributed to understanding why credible influencers
generate stronger persuasion, higher engagement, and greater purchase intention than less credible communicators. Similarly,
Signaling Theory argues that observable signals  including professional appearance, audience size, verified identity, and content
quality reduce information asymmetry and increase consumers' confidence in commercial recommendations. Together, these
theoretical perspectives suggest that credibility functions as a strategic resource capable of improving marketing effectiven ess
within digital environments (Lee, 2025).
More recent studies have expanded these perspectives by emphasizing authenticity as a central determinant of creator
effectiveness. Rather than responding merely to expertise or attractiveness, consumers increasingly evaluate whether creators
appear genuine, transparent, and personally connected with the products they recommend. Authentic storytelling, consistent
self-presentation, and transparent commercial relationships have therefore become critical components of successful creator
branding . Consequently, creator credibility is increasingly understood as a multidimensional construct emerging from
continuous interaction between personal identity, audience relationships, and commercial communication rather than from
individual characteristics alone.
Despite these theoretical developments, current literature continues to conceptualize credibility primarily from the perspect ive
of consumers. Existing studies explain how consumers evaluate creators, why consumers trust creators, and how credibility
influences purchasing behaviour  (Tseng, 2023). Comparatively little attention has been devoted to understanding how creators
themselves construct, maintain, and negotiate credibility while operating within algorithmically governed commercial
environments. In practice, creators continuously monitor audience reactions, algorithmic recommendations, marketplace
ratings, content performance, and seller reputations before deciding how products should be presented. Consequently,
credibility is not simply possessed by creators but is continuously negotiated through ongoing interaction among algorithms,
audiences, marketplaces, and commercial experiences(Lee, 2025).
This limitation becomes increasingly important within TikTok social commerce because creator success depends upon more than
consumers' perceptions of trustworthiness. Affiliate creators must simultaneously satisfy multiple stakeholders, including
platform algorithms that regulate visibility, audiences that determine engagement, and marketplace sellers that influence
commercial reputation through product quality and service performance. These interconnected relationships require creators to
continuously balance audience expectations, platform requirements, and commercial objectives while preserving their
professional identity. Such conditions indicate that creator credibility should be understood as a dynamic and relational pro cess
rather than a static individual characteristic.
Accordingly, this study argues that existing perspectives on source credibility and signaling remain insufficient for explain ing
creators' lived experiences within algorithmically mediated commercial ecosystems. Instead of viewing credibility exclusively  as
a consumer evaluation, this research conceptualizes credibility as an adaptive process continuously constructed through
creators' interactions with platform algorithms, audience engagement, marketplace dynamics, and commercial decision -making.
This perspective provides the theoretical foundation for developing the concept of Algorithmic Credibility, which extends
existing credibility theories by incorporating the experiential realities of creators operating within contemporary social
commerce ecosystems.

2.4 Cyberphenomenology as an Interpretive Lens for Understanding Creator Experiences
The increasing integration of artificial intelligence, algorithmic recommendation systems, and digital platforms has
fundamentally transformed human experience by blurring the boundaries between physical reality and digitally mediated
environments. Contemporary creators no longer interact solely with audiences but continuously engage with algorithmic
systems that influence visibility, participation, commercial opportunities, and entrepreneurial decision -making. Consequently,
creators' commercial activities cannot be fully understood through behavioural or technological perspectives alone because
their experiences emerge through continuous interpretation of digital environments in which algorithms actively participate i n
shaping everyday practices. This transformation requires an interpretive perspective capable of explaining how meaning is
constructed through ongoing interactions among humans, digital technologies, and platform ecosystems  (Babayev, 2025).
Cyberphenomenology extends classical phenomenological inquiry by examining lived experiences occurring within digitally
mediated environments where technological systems become integral components of human consciousness and everyday
practices (Rofiah, 2024b). Rather than conceptualizing technology as an external instrument, cyberphenomenology views digital
platforms as experiential spaces in which individuals continuously interpret technological affordances, negotiate digital
identities, construct social relationships, and assign meaning to interactions occurring within virtual environments. Accordi ngly,
knowledge is generated not simply through observable behaviour but through understanding how individuals experience and
interpret digital realities embedded within technologically mediated contexts  (Rofiah, 2024a).
Within TikTok social commerce, creators continuously experience algorithmic uncertainty. Decisions regarding content
production, publication timing, product selection, audience engagement, seller collaboration, and commercial adaptation are
rarely determined solely by predefined marketing strategies. Instead, creators constantly observe changes in content visibili ty,
audience responses, recommendation patterns, engagement metrics, and marketplace performance before interpreting
whether particular  actions are rewarded or constrained by platform algorithms. These repeated interactions gradually shape
creators' understanding of how commercial success can be achieved within the platform ecosystem. Consequently, creators'
entrepreneurial practices emerge through continuous cycles of observation, interpretation, adaptation, and reflection rather
than through static strategic planning (Hou et al., 2025).
From this perspective, algorithms function as active participants within creators' lived experiences rather than merely
technological infrastructures operating in the background. Every increase or decline in audience reach, every unexpected
recommendation, every successful affiliate transaction, and every unsuccessful promotional effort contributes to creators'
evolving understanding of platform behaviour  (Chodak, 2024) . These experiences subsequently influence future commercial
decisions, including product selection, content design, marketplace partnerships, publishing strategies, and audience
engagement practices. Therefore, algorithmic interaction becomes a meaningful experiential process through which creators
gradually construct commercial knowledge and adaptive entrepreneurial capabilities  (Issar, 2024).
Applying cyberphenomenology to TikTok affiliate creators enables this study to move beyond dominant marketing perspectives
that primarily explain measurable behavioural outcomes. Instead, this research seeks to uncover the underlying meanings
creators assign to algorithmic interactions during their everyday commercial activities  (Gerbaudo, 2026) . Such an interpretive
approach facilitates a deeper understanding of how creators perceive algorithmic visibility, evaluate marketplace credibility ,
negotiate audience expectations, and construct sustainable entrepreneurial practices within digital platform ecosystems  (Yang,
2025).

III. RESEARCH METHODS
3.1 Research Design
This study employed a qualitative research design grounded in Cyberphenomenology to explore how TikTok affiliate creators
construct commercial credibility through their lived experiences within algorithm -driven social commerce. Cyberphenomenology
extends interpretive phenomenology by positioning digital environments not merely as technological platforms but as
experiential spaces in which individuals continuously construct meaning through interactions with algorithms, digital
communities, and platform affordances  (Rofiah, 2024b) . This approach is particularly appropriate because the phenomenon
investigated in this study cannot be adequately understood through observable behavioural outcomes alone. Instead, it require s
an in -depth interpretation of creators' subjective experiences as they interact with algorithmic recommendation systems,
audience engagement, marketplace actors, and commercial activities within TikTok.
Unlike conventional phenomenology that primarily investigates lived experiences in physical contexts, Cyberphenomenology
recognizes that digital experiences are simultaneously shaped by human consciousness and computational infrastructures.
Consequently, creators' decisions regarding content production, product promotion, seller selection, publishing schedules, an d
audience interaction are interpreted as meaning -making processes emerging from continuous engagement with algorithmically
mediated environments. This perspective enables the present study to reveal not only what creators do but also how they
understand, negotiate, and reconstruct commercial reality within TikTok's social commerce ecosystem.
Accordingly, this study adopts an interpretivist paradigm in which reality is viewed as socially and digitally constructed th rough
continuous interaction among creators, audiences, marketplace actors, and platform algorithms. Knowledge is therefore
generated through interpreting participants' lived experiences rather than measuring objective behavioural relationships.
3.2 Research Context
The research was conducted within the TikTok social commerce ecosystem, specifically focusing on creators actively
participating in the TikTok Affiliate Program. TikTok represents one of the fastest -growing social commerce platforms globally,
integrating short -form video content, artificial intelligence -based recommendation systems, livestreaming, e -commerce, and
affiliate marketing into a single digital ecosystem. Within this environment, creators continuously interact with algorithmic
recommendation systems while simultaneously maintaining audience engagement and commercial relationships with online
sellers.
TikTok Affiliate provides a particularly suitable research context because creators' commercial success depends upon dynamic
interaction among multiple actors, including platform algorithms, consumers, sellers, products, and marketplace infrastructur es.
These interactions create complex digital experiences that extend beyond traditional marketing activities, making the platfor m
an appropriate setting for cyberphenomenological inquiry.

3.3 Participants and Sampling Strategy
Participants were selected using purposive sampling , consistent with interpretive qualitative research. The sampling strategy
focused on information-rich cases capable of providing deep insight into creators' experiences within TikTok Affiliate.
The participant selection criteria included:
1. Active TikTok Affiliate creators.
2. Minimum one year of experience managing affiliate activities.
3. Actively producing promotional content through TikTok.
4. Directly involved in selecting products, interacting with sellers, and managing audience engagement.
5. Willing to participate in in-depth interviews.
Two key informants fulfilled these criteria and possessed substantial experience managing affiliate -based commercial activities.
Although the number of participants appears limited, phenomenological inquiry prioritizes experiential depth rather than
statistical representation. The richness of participants' narratives provided sufficient information to understand the
phenomenon under investigation.
3.4 Data Collection
Primary data were collected through semi-structured in -depth interviews  conducted online. A semi -structured interview
protocol was developed to provide sufficient flexibility for participants to describe their lived experiences while ensuring
consistency across interviews.
The interview guide explored four broad domains:
1. Creators' digital identity and account development;
2. Content creation and algorithmic visibility strategies;
3. Marketplace and seller selection;
4. Affiliate marketing experiences and commercial adaptation.
Rather than following rigid interview sequences, probing questions were continuously employed to explore participants'
reflections regarding algorithmic experiences, commercial decision -making, and adaptive entrepreneurial practices. Interviews
were audio-recorded with participants' consent and subsequently transcribed verbatim for analysis.
To enhance contextual understanding, interview data were complemented by digital observations of participants' TikTok
accounts, including publicly available content, engagement patterns, profile characteristics, and commercial activities. This
triangulation allowed interview narratives to be interpreted within their natural digital environments.
3.5 Data Analysis
Data were analyzed using a Cyberphenomenological Interpretive Analysis (CIA) developed for this study. Unlike conventional
thematic analysis that primarily categorizes textual patterns, Cyberphenomenological Interpretive Analysis seeks to uncover t he
meanings participants construct while interacting with digital environments.
The analysis consisted of six iterative stages.
Stage 1: Familiarization
Interview transcripts were repeatedly read to obtain holistic understanding of participants' digital experiences.
Stage 2: Meaning Unit Identification
Statements reflecting significant experiences related to TikTok Affiliate activities were identified as meaning units.
Stage 3: Initial Coding
Meaning units were transformed into conceptual codes representing participants' perceptions, actions, and interpretations.
Stage 4: Cyberphenomenological Interpretation
Codes were interpreted within participants' broader experiences of interacting with algorithms, audiences, marketplace actors ,
and commercial environments.
Stage 5: Theme Development
Interpretations sharing conceptual similarities were synthesized into higher -order themes representing essential dimensions of
creators' lived experiences.
Stage 6: Theory Triangulation
Emerging themes were compared with contemporary theories, including Source Credibility Theory, Signaling Theory, Social
Commerce Theory, and Creator Economy literature. This process enabled identification of theoretical convergence, divergence,
and extension, ultimately facilitating the development of research propositions and the conceptual model proposed in this
study.

5. DISCUSSION
5.1 Theory Triangulation
The findings reveal that TikTok affiliate creators continuously construct commercial legitimacy through an iterative interact ion
among digital identity, algorithmic visibility, marketplace trust, and adaptive commercial learning. This experiential proces s
extends existing marketing theories by demonstrating that commercial credibility is not a fixed personal attribute but a dyna mic
capability emerging from creators' continuous interaction with algorithmically mediated social commerce environments. To
explain this phenomenon, the present study triangulates the empirical findings with four complementary theoretical
perspectives: Source Credibility Theory, Signaling Theory, Social Commerce Theory, and Creator Economy Theory.
Theme 1: Constructing Digital Identity and Algorithmic Credibility
Source Credibility Theory proposes that consumers evaluate information based on the communicator's expertise,
trustworthiness, and attractiveness. Previous influencer marketing studies similarly demonstrate that authenticity and expert ise
positively influence consumer trust and purchase intention. The present findings partially support these perspectives by
confirming that audience trust remains essential within TikTok Affiliate. However, the empirical evidence further indicates t hat
creators themselves actively construct credibility through continuous management of digital identity, including follower grow th,
niche consistency, audience interaction, and profile legitimacy. Consequently, credibility emerges not solely through consume rs'
evaluation but also through creators' strategic interpretation of algorithmic and social signals.
From the perspective of Signaling Theory, followers, engagement metrics, profile completeness, and audience interaction
function as observable market signals reducing uncertainty. Nevertheless, participants did not interpret these indicators mer ely
as market signals but as evolving representations of their professional identity. This finding extends Signaling Theory by
suggesting that digital signals simultaneously communicate commercial competence to audiences while reinforcing creators'
own understanding of legitimacy within platform ecosystems.
Theme 2: Negotiating Algorithmic Visibility
Existing algorithmic marketing literature generally conceptualizes recommendation systems as technological mechanisms
optimizing content distribution. The present findings demonstrate a more complex process. Participants experienced algorithms
as dynamic environments requiring continuous observation, experimentation, and interpretation. Rather than treating
algorithms as deterministic systems, creators actively negotiated visibility by modifying content strategies based on algorit hmic
feedback. Consequently, algorithmic visibility emerged as an adaptive capability developed through lived experience rather th an
a passive outcome generated by computational systems.
This finding extends current platform governance literature by positioning creators as active interpreters of algorithmic
behaviour instead of passive recipients of algorithmic decisions. Visibility therefore becomes a negotiated achievement resul ting
from continuous interaction between creators and intelligent platform infrastructures.
Theme 3: Curating Marketplace Trust
Social Commerce Theory argues that trust represents the principal mechanism facilitating online commercial transactions.
Previous studies generally conceptualize trust as consumers' confidence in sellers, platforms, or influencers. The present fi ndings
support this proposition while simultaneously extending it by demonstrating that creators themselves actively curate
marketplace trust before consumers evaluate products.
Participants continuously interpreted seller ratings, customer reviews, fulfilment quality, and after -sales service before deciding
whether commercial collaboration should occur. Marketplace trust therefore emerged through creators' interpretive evaluation
rather than marketplace systems alone. Consequently, trust functions as a reciprocal process involving creators, audiences,
sellers, and digital platforms rather than a one-directional consumer perception.

However, existing studies primarily explain entrepreneurial success through monetization strategies, branding, or audience
engagement. In contrast, the present findings reveal that creators continuously transform everyday digital experiences into
entrepreneurial knowledge through reflection, experimentation, and adaptation.

future strategic decisions. Adaptive commercial learning therefore represents an experiential capability developed through

6. CONCLUSION
6.1 Conclusion
This study explored how TikTok affiliate creators construct commercial legitimacy through their lived experiences within
algorithm-driven social commerce by adopting a cyberphenomenological perspective. Moving beyond conventional consumer -
oriented approaches, the findings reveal that creators are not merely promotional intermediaries but adaptive digital
entrepreneurs who continuously interpret, negotiate, and respond to algorithmically mediated environments. Their commercial
practices emerge through ongoing interactions with platform algorithms, audiences, marketplace actors, and digital
technologies rather than through predetermined marketing strategies.
The empirical analysis identified four interrelated dimensions that collectively explain this process: digital identity const ruction,
algorithmic visibility negotiation, marketplace trust curation, and adaptive commercial learning. These dimensions operate
recursively, forming an iterative process through which creators continuously construct, maintain, and reinforce commercial
legitimacy. Rather than representing independent marketing activities, they constitute an integrated experiential mechanism
shaping sustainable entrepreneurial performance within TikTok social commerce.
The study further demonstrates that existing theories —including Source Credibility Theory, Signaling Theory, Social Commerce
Theory, and Creator Economy Theory —individually explain only partial aspects of creators' experiences. While these
perspectives effectively explain consumer trust, information signaling, and platform participation, they provide limited
explanation regarding how creators themselves experience algorithmic environments and continuously construct credibility
through digital interaction. By integrating these theoretical perspectives with cyberphenomenological interpretation, this
research extends current knowledge beyond observable marketing outcomes toward a deeper understanding of creators' lived
digital experiences.
Accordingly, this study introduces Algorithmic Credibility as a novel theoretical construct describing the dynamic process
through which creators establish and sustain commercial legitimacy by continuously interacting with platform algorithms,
audiences, marketplace actors, and entrepreneurial learning processes. Unlike traditional credibility perspectives that
conceptualize credibility as a relatively stable individual attribute evaluated by consumers, Algorithmic Credibility is
conceptualized as an adaptive capability that evolves through repeated interpretation, experimentation, reflection, and
commercial adaptation within algorithmically mediated ecosystems.

6.4 Limitations and Future Research
Several limitations should be acknowledged.  First, this study was conducted within the context of TikTok Affiliate, which may
limit transferability to other platform ecosystems such as Instagram, YouTube, or Shopee Live. Future studies may compare
multiple creator ecosystems to examine similarities and differences in algorithmic credibility construction across platforms.
Second, the research adopted a qualitative cyberphenomenological approach involving a limited number of information -rich
participants. Although this approach provides substantial interpretive depth, future research may employ mixed -method or
quantitative designs to empirically validate the proposed conceptual framework.  Third, the study focused primarily on creators'
experiences without directly incorporating perspectives from audiences, sellers, or platform managers. Future research could
employ multi -actor approaches to develop a more comprehensive understanding of commercial legitimacy within creator
ecosystems. Finally, future investigations may explore how emerging artificial intelligence technologies  including generative AI,
virtual influencers, intelligent recommendation systems, and AI -assisted content production  reshape creators' lived experiences
and influence the evolution of algorithmic credibility within future digital commerce environments.

commercial success is no longer determined solely by persuasive communication or marketing expertise but increasingly
depends upon creators' capacity to continuously interpret, negotiate, and adapt to algorithmically mediated realities. By
introducing Algorithmic Credibility as an adaptive and experience -based theoretical construct, this study provides a new
conceptual lens for understanding how commercial legitimacy is constructed within intelligent platform ecosystems. As digital
commerce becomes progressively shaped by artificial intelligence and algorithmic governance, understanding creators' lived
experiences will become increasingly essential for advancing both marketing theory and digital entrepreneurial practice. .
