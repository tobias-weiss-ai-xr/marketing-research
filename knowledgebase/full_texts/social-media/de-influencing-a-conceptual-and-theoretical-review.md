Abstract
Influencer marketing has evolved into a significant form of marketing
communication that shapes consumers’ perceptions of products, brands, and
lifestyles, as well as their consumption preferences, through the interactions
it establishes with social media users. The visibility that influencers give to
trends, luxury products, and idealized lifestyles in their social media content
may lead followers to compare themselves with these consumption patterns and
motivate them to adopt similar lifestyles. When considered in conjunction with
social comparison, concerns about falling behind, and parasocial relationships
established with influencers, this phenomenon may reinforce excessive
consumption and materialistic tendencies, potentially giving rise to various
concerns regarding individual well-being and environmental sustainability
and, consequently , contributing to growing criticism of influencer marketing.
In this context, de-influencing, which gained visibility on TikTok in 2023,
emerged as a critical response to the consumption-oriented nature of influencer
marketing and evolved into a social media practice that encourages consumers
to refrain from purchasing certain products, reconsider their consumption
preferences, and question excessive consumption. Despite its rapid proliferation
on social media, the theoretical framework surrounding the concept remains
at an early stage of development in the academic literature. The relatively
recent emergence of de-influencing in the academic literature underscores the
need for further research to delineate its conceptual boundaries and clarify its
theoretical foundations.
This study aims to examine the phenomenon of de-influencing in terms of
its conceptual development and theoretical foundations. Accordingly , the
study first addresses the concept of de-influencing and its key characteristics,
followed by an examination of its emergence and evolution. It also explores

1. INTRODUCTION
Influencer marketing is a form of marketing communication that leverages
the influence of individuals who have attained a certain degree of visibility
and engagement on social media platforms over their follower bases to
promote brands and products (Elsantil et al., 2025). Influencers who create
content within specific domains and reach large audiences have evolved
beyond merely promoting products or services; they have become cultural
intermediaries capable of shaping their followers’ lifestyles, preferences, and
consumption practices (Kılıç & Polat, 2025; Temiz, 2026). The relatively
close and continuous communication that influencers maintain with their
followers contributes to their being perceived as more authentic, accessible,
and trustworthy , distinguishing them from traditional advertising actors.
These characteristics enable influencers to effectively engage their followers
by integrating commercial messages into everyday and personal content
(Schouten et al, 2020).
Product and brand recommendations made by influencers may be
understood not merely as direct commercial messages, but also as components
of content that followers associate with influencers’ everyday lives and compare
with their own lives. Presenting products within influencers’ lives as markers of
particular lifestyles, aesthetic orientations, or social identities may contribute to
transforming consumption from a practice primarily concerned with fulfilling
functional needs into one that also facilitates the acquisition of social and
symbolic meanings. In this context, products may be positioned not merely as
objects that satisfy particular needs, but as symbolic means of attaining desired
lifestyles and social identities (Azhar et al., 2025). Accordingly , consumer
desires may be shaped not only by existing needs but also by the lifestyles,
consumption practices, and symbolic meanings that gain visibility within
social media environments.
This influence of influencer marketing on consumption has also brought
critical debates concerning the boundaries and consequences of consumption
to the fore. Influencer activities have been criticized for encouraging excessive
consumption and materialism, intensifying parasocial interactions with

followers, and potentially exerting adverse effects on individuals’ well-being
(Michalidou et al., 2026). In particular, alongside discussions surrounding the
environmental, economic, and social consequences of consumption practices,
criticisms of the intensified product recommendations and persistent appeals
to consumption on social media have paved the way for the emergence of
alternative communication practices that call consumption into question. In
response to forms of communication that continually encourage consumers
to purchase more, alternative discourses that promote reducing consumption,
critically evaluating products, or reconsidering purchasing decisions have
begun to gain visibility (Wood, 2025; Rasool et al., 2026). As one such
alternative discourse, de-influencing has attracted particular attention as a
critical content practice directed at consumption-oriented influencer culture,
especially on social media platforms. Following its increased visibility on
TikTok in 2023, de-influencing evolved into a social media practice that
subsequently became more widespread across different product categories
and social media platforms (Apostol & Asiminei, 2025; Sheng & Y eşiloğlu,
2025; Elhajjar & Itani, 2026).
Despite the growing visibility of de-influencing practices on social
media, the concept’s conceptual and theoretical foundations have not yet
been systematically established in the academic literature (Kılıç & Polat,
2025; Michaelidou et al., 2026). In particular, the literature requires a more
comprehensive examination of how the boundaries of the de-influencing
concept should be delineated, how it relates to closely associated concepts
such as influencer marketing, greenfluencing, anti-consumption, anti-haul,
minimalism, electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM), comparative advertising, and
demarketing, and which theoretical perspectives can be employed to explain it.
This renders the systematic examination of the conceptual development and
theoretical foundations of de-influencing—an increasingly visible phenomenon
in social media environments—an important area of research.
This study aims to examine the conceptual and theoretical foundations
of de-influencing through a comprehensive literature review. Accordingly , it
investigates the core characteristics of the de-influencing concept, its emergence
and development, its connections with related concepts, and the theoretical
perspectives that may contribute to explaining the phenomenon within an
integrated framework. In doing so, the study seeks to contribute to a clearer
understanding of the conceptual positioning and theoretical underpinnings of
de-influencing, a phenomenon that has gained increasing visibility in research
on social media and consumption.

2. THE CONCEPT OF DE-INFLUENCING
De-influencing refers to a content creation and communication practice
on social media aimed at discouraging consumers from purchasing particular
products or engaging in specific consumption behaviors. The concept has
gained visibility particularly through content creators’ negative evaluations of
products or brands based on their personal experiences and their encouragement
of followers to refrain from purchasing the products in question (Kılıç & Polat,
2025; Taheran et al.,2026).
A defining characteristic of de-influencing content is its emphasis on
encouraging consumers to question their purchasing decisions and reassess
their consumption needs. Such content may take the form of direct calls to
“not purchase this product,” but it may also involve recommending lower-
cost alternatives, encouraging consumers to use existing products for longer
or sharing suggestions related to minimalist lifestyles (Karpuz, 2026).
Accordingly , de-influencing should not be understood as an approach that seeks
to reject consumption altogether or to eliminate personal consumption entirely
(Koivunen et al., 2025). A substantial proportion of de-influencing content
instead offers alternatives aimed at facilitating more conscious, moderate,
economical, ethical, or sustainable forms of consumption. Therefore, de-
influencing discourse is based not on a call to “not consume,” but rather on
encouraging consumers to question what they consume and why they consume
it. In this respect, de-influencing content encourages consumers to reconsider
the necessity , manner, and extent of their consumption.
Within this framework, de-influencing content discourages consumers from
making a purchase based on two primary rationales. The first rationale centers
on the social and environmental consequences of consumption that extend
beyond the individual. The environmental degradation caused by excessive
consumption, the proliferation of unnecessary consumption, and consumers’
exposure to misleading or aggressive advertising practices constitute ethical
grounds for discouraging consumption (Koivunen vd. 2025; Yıldız, 2025). In
such content, de-influencing encourages consumers to consider not only their
own economic interests but also the environmental and social consequences
of their consumption behavior.
The second rationale, by contrast, relates directly to the product itself
and the consumer’s individual interests. A high price, poor quality , failure
to deliver the expected performance, or the absence of a genuine consumer
need are among the primary reasons given for recommending against the
purchase (Koivunen et al., 2025; Yıldız, 2025). From this perspective, de-
influencing content encourages consumers to use their financial resources

more judiciously and to refrain from impulsive or unnecessary purchases.
Indeed, the literature indicates that de-influencing supports avoidance of
excessive spending and encourages consumers to consider more economical
alternatives (Khalil vd., 2025).
De-influencing content may be shared under headings such as “De-
influencing,” “Worth Buying or Not,” “Do Not Buy These Products,”
“Products Y ou Should Not Waste Y our Money On,” and “Products I Regret
Buying” (Geçit et al., 2024). Under these headings, content creators evaluate
popular products, share their personal purchasing experiences, encourage
their followers to avoid particular products, or recommend more affordable
alternatives. In this way , personal experience becomes an important persuasive
element of de-influencing communication. The content creator’s account of
“I bought and experienced this product” takes the form of an experience-
based consumer recommendation rather than a generalized anti-consumption
discourse (Elsantil et al., 2025; Gürbüz, 2025).
Individuals who create de-influencing content are referred to as de-
influencers (Karpuz,2026). By encouraging consumers to make more conscious
and reflective consumption choices, de-influencers support the development of
responsible consumer behavior. Their emphasis on social benefit and consumer
awareness, together with the perception that they are motivated by social
responsibility rather than financial objectives, may contribute to de-influencers
being perceived by consumers as more authentic and trustworthy (Elsantil et
al., 2025; Michalideou et al., 2026).
De-influencing is a form of communication positioned in opposition to
influencer marketing and may therefore be characterized as the “negative mirror
image” of influencer marketing (Yıldız, 2025). Nevertheless, de-influencing
does not imply the complete elimination of influencing activities (Elsantil et
al., 2025; Temiz, 2026). On the contrary , calls for consumers not to purchase
a particular product, to opt for a less expensive alternative, or to adopt a
more minimalist lifestyle are, ultimately , forms of communication intended
to influence individuals’ preferences (Michaelidou et al., 2026). Accordingly ,
rather than eliminating influencer influence altogether, de-influencing can be
understood as a form of influencing that alters the direction of influence and
the nature of the recommendation concerning consumption behavior (Kılıç
& Polat, 2025; Temiz, 2026).
Like influencing content, de-influencing content is created and circulated
on social media platforms, where it also seeks to gain visibility (Yıldız, 2025).
Therefore, rather than constituting a form of opposition situated entirely
outside influencer culture, de-influencing can also be understood as a critical

influencer communication. In this sense, de-influencing can be conceptualized
as a social media communication practice that uses the tools of influencer

reconsideration of consumption decisions.
3. THE EMERGENCE AND DEVELOPMENT OF THE
CONCEPT OF DE-INFLUENCING
De-influencing gained visibility on TikTok in early 2023 as a content
practice encouraging users to refrain from purchasing products that had
become popular on social media, and it subsequently evolved into a viral
phenomenon. The rapid dissemination of de-influencing on TikTok ensured
that the concept did not remain a limited form of content focused solely on
criticizing particular products. Initially prominent in the fashion and beauty
sectors, the trend soon expanded into other areas of consumption beyond
these domains, with a wide range of product categories and consumption
practices becoming subjects of criticism (Apostol & Asiminei, 2025; Sheng
& Y eşiloğlu, 2025). During the same period, de-influencing content extended
beyond TikTok and began to appear on other social media platforms, including
Instagram, Facebook, and Y ouT ube (Elhajjar & Itani, 2026). Thus, the content
format—initially shaped around a “do not buy this” discourse targeting specific
viral products—has progressively evolved into a broader critique of the hyper-
consumption culture normalized on social media.
With this transformation, the scope of de-influencing became no longer
limited to discouraging consumers from purchasing specific products. On the
one hand, content was produced to criticize popular products and brands;
on the other, more affordable alternatives were recommended. Some content
creators also began to question the relationship established by consumer culture
between consumption and social status, success, and prestige. As Bainotti
(2023) points out, de-influencers therefore do not constitute a homogeneous
group. While some reject idealized lifestyles centered on excessive consumption,
others highlight alternative products to popular brands, and still others use
de-influencing discourse as a means of enhancing their own visibility and
personal brands.
The emergence of de-influencing should not be understood merely as a
trend that suddenly appeared on TikTok in 2023 and consisted solely of calls to
“not purchase products.” The practice that gained visibility on TikTok in 2023
should be considered in conjunction with previously developed forms of anti-
consumption communication. In particular, anti-haul contents—produced by

creators who subverted the acquisition and consumption practices showcased
in traditional haul videos popularized on Y ouT ube—are considered direct
precursors to de-influencing. (Apostol & Asiminei, 2025).
The emergence of de-influencing trends is also closely associated with the
proliferation of influencer culture on social media. As influencer marketing
became increasingly prevalent across social media platforms, products came
to be not only promoted but also displayed as part of everyday life, while
consumption increasingly became represented as an indicator of lifestyle.
Consumer products have gone beyond merely being utilized in social media
content; they have been staged, displayed, and presented to followers as
elements of a specific lifestyle. (Bayer, 2026). In particular, the continuous
recommendation of products by high-follower influencers through brand
collaborations has made the commercial nature of influencer endorsements
increasingly prominent. In this process, followers have also begun to question
the extent to which influencers’ evaluations of products are independent
and authentic (Geçit et al., 2024). The dissatisfaction experienced by users
regarding the pressure to follow every new trend, acquire popular products,
and exhibit these items via social media has laid the groundwork for a critical
reexamination of consumption behavior (Sheng & Y eşiloğlu, 2025). In this
context, de-influencing can be considered as the visibility of the desire to
mitigate hyper-consumption and avoid unnecessary purchasing behaviors
through social media content.
4. DE-INFLUENCING AND RELATED CONCEPTS
To conceptually position and better understand the concept of de-
influencing, it is first necessary to distinguish it from closely related concepts
with which it shares similar functions, objectives, or forms of communication
and with which it intersects at various points. Accordingly , this section
examines the conceptual relationships between de-influencing and influencer
marketing, greenfluencing, anti-consumption, anti-haul, minimalism, e-WOM,
comparative advertising, and de-marketing. By discussing the dimensions
in which these concepts overlap with and diverge from de-influencing, the
distinctive conceptual position of de-influencing is clarified.
a.) Influencer Marketing: Influencer marketing is a digital marketing
communication strategy in which brands utilize influencers to reach their
target audiences and influence consumer decision-making (Okonkwo &
Namkoisse, 2023). Influencers are individuals who create and share content,
have substantial numbers of followers, and influence their followers’ attitudes
and behaviors through this content (Michalideou et al., 2026).

Within influencer marketing, influencers encourage purchasing behavior by
highlighting the benefits of products and services. In contrast, de-influencing
content encourages consumers to refrain from purchasing particular products
or services, reconsider their purchase decisions, or turn to alternative products
(Khalil et al., 2025; Karpuz, 2026; Elsantil et al., 2025). Such content highlights
factors including the unnecessary nature of a product, the availability of more
affordable alternatives, and the ethical, social, or environmental consequences
of consumption (Zote, 2024, as cited in Temiz, 2026). The distinction between
influencer marketing and de-influencing is evident not only in the messages
directed toward consumer behavior but also in the motivations underlying the
influence process. While influencers are noted to drive engagement through
extrinsic factors such as income and fame, de-influencers are argued to act
out of a desire to create positive social and environmental impacts (Elsantil et
al., 2025). Furthermore, while influencers are noted to frequently overlook
the social and environmental impacts of products in their recommendations,
de-influencing content encourages consumers to consider these impacts and
make more conscious choices (Zote, 2024, as cited in Temiz, 2026).
De-influencing has emerged as a reaction against influencer marketing
and influencer culture, representing a redirection of the influence exerted by
influencers on consumption. In this context, de-influencing can be considered
an evolving form of influencer culture (Apostol & Asiminei, 2025; Kılıç &
Polat, 2025; Temiz, 2026).
b.) Greenfluencing: Greenfluencing is a form of influencer marketing
focused on sustainability and carried out by greenfluencers (Koivunen et al.
2025). Active on social media platforms (Hartmann et al., 2025), greenfluencers
are specialized social media influencers who focus on sustainability and seek
to raise environmental awareness while promoting sustainable lifestyles and
conscious consumption (Conte et al., 2025). Their content features various
topics sharing a common thematic framework centered on sustainability ,
such as sustainable fashion, slow travel, waste reduction, and environmental
activism (Knupfer et al., 2023).
There are certain overlaps and differences between de-influencing and
greenfluencing. Although both approaches can encourage more conscious
consumption behaviors, greenfluencers present products and consumption
patterns almost entirely within a positive framework grounded in a sustainable
lifestyle philosophy . In contrast, de-influencers question and warn against
unnecessary and excessive consumption, while promoting sustainable
behaviors such as purchasing less, repairing, and reusing (Elsantil et al., 2025).
Nevertheless, de-influencers’ recommendations to refrain from consumption or

turn to alternative products do not always stem from sustainability concerns;
they may also be based on considerations such as perceiving products as
overpriced or excessively promoted (Temiz, 2026).
c.) Anti-Consumption: Anti-consumption, defined as “resistance to,
distaste of, or even resentment or rejection of consumption” (Zavestoski,
2002, as cited in Michaelidou et al., 2026), encompasses a range of practices,
including boycotting, brand avoidance, reuse, self-producing, and voluntary
simplicity . Anti-consumption can also manifest as a form of selective opposition
directed at specific brands, products, or companies (Başçı, 2014; Makri et
al., 2020). Accordingly , anti-consumption refers to a broader framework
that encompasses not only the outright rejection of consumption but also
the questioning of modes of consumption, consumption preferences, and
consumption practices. Furthermore, research on anti-consumption in recent
years has been observed to be associated with approaches such as consumer
resistance, green/sustainable consumption, voluntary simplicity , and ethical/
moral consumption (Makri et al., 2020; Michaelidou et al., 2026).
Although there is a clear overlap between de-influencing and anti-
consumption, the two concepts are not entirely identical. De-influencing
content acquires an anti-consumption character when it encourages avoiding
unnecessary consumption, questioning excessive consumption, or refraining
from purchasing specific products. In this regard, de-influencing can be
considered a practice that enables the articulation and circulation of anti-
consumption discourses within the context of social media and influencer
culture (Yıldız, 2025). However, not all de-influencing content aims to reject
consumption entirely . Particularly in content where alternative products are
recommended instead of specific ones, consumption itself is not rejected;
rather, the consumer’s preference for a specific product or brand is redirected
toward another option (Apostol & Asiminei, 2025).
d.) Anti-Haul: Anti-haul content is defined as critical content intended
to raise awareness of the continuous introduction of new products into the
market and the excessive consumption practices that accompany this process
(Koivunen et al., 2025). This content format, which has gained visibility
particular on Y ouT ube, is centered on content creators listing products they
do not intend to purchase and explaining to their audiences why they do not
wish to purchase these products (Wood, 2020).
Similar to de-influencing content, anti-haul content may involve content
creators questioning consumers’ purchase of heavily promoted goods, products,
or services, or their tendency to follow market-driven trends, and attempting to
discourage individuals from engaging in such behaviors (Apostol &Asiminiei,

entirely identical.
While anti-haul content is predominantly structured around justifications
regarding why certain products or product groups should not be purchased, the
de-influencing discourse can carry this discussion into a broader consumption
framework by also incorporating the critique of overconsumption, purchasing
impulses, and influencer-driven consumption pressure.In other words, de-
influencing may seek answers not only to the question, “What should I not

as substitutes for the products being discouraged (Sheng & Y eşiloğlu, 2025).
The widespread adoption of anti-haul content on Y ouT ube, facilitated by
the platform’s longer and more explanatory video format, has enabled content
creators to provide detailed explanations of their reasons for refraining from
purchasing particular products. In contrast, content that has gained visibility
on TikTok and circulated under the de-influencing hashtag may employ a
shorter and more direct mode of communication, reflecting the platform’s
short-form and rapidly consumed video format (Apostol &Asiminiei, 2025).
e.) Minimalism: Minimalism and de-influencing substantially overlap
in terms of questioning excessive consumption and encouraging more
conscious consumption choices. Minimalism is a lifestyle approach centered on
preferences for eliminating unnecessary possessions, owning fewer items, and
reducing clutter. This approach cannot be explained solely by environmental
sustainability concerns; it can also be adopted through different motivations
such as financial status, life satisfaction, simplification, and personal values
(Pangarkar et al., 2025).
Minimalism approaches the reduction of consumption within a broader
lifestyle and value system. De-influencing overlaps with several principles of
minimalism, including consuming less, avoiding unnecessary purchases, and
consciously evaluating consumption choices (Gürbüz, 2025). This overlap
indicates that, although the two concepts share certain commonalities, they
are not identical. While minimalism frames reduced consumption in terms
of a lifestyle and value system, de-influencing constitutes a specific form of
communication and persuasion through which consumption decisions are
questioned in digital environments. In other words, whereas minimalism
provides a lifestyle and value framework centered on consuming less, de-
influencing can facilitate the questioning of the consumption norms and

purchasing tendencies embedded within this framework through digital
content (Apostol &Asiminiei, 2025).
f.) e-WOM: e-WOM is defined by Hennig- Thurau et al. (2004) as “any
positive or negative statement made by potential, actual, or former customers
about a product or company , which is made available to a multitude of
people and institutions via the Internet”. The key point of overlap between
de-influencing and negative eWOM lies in the online sharing of negative
evaluations and information concerning products or brands. However, the
two concepts differ in terms of the source of communication, the level of
reach, and the nature of the relationship established with followers. Individuals
who generate negative e-WOM do not necessarily need to have large follower
bases or be recognized as content creators. By contrast, de-influencers are
digital content creators who have substantial followings and have established
a certain level of social connection with their followers. Therefore, unlike
e-WOM communication generated by individual consumers within their own
social networks, de-influencing can be regarded as a communication practice
capable of reaching a broader online audience, with its influence on followers
being more visible (Taheran et al., 2026).
g.) Comparative Advertising: Comparative advertising refers to
advertisements in which firms directly or indirectly identify one or more
competitors and claim that their own products or brands are superior to those
of their competitors (Y enipazarlı, 2023). One of the primary objectives of
comparative advertising is to positively influence consumers’ evaluations of
products and brands by emphasizing the superior attributes of the products
offered by a brand relative to those of its competitors (Malhotra, 2018).
In the context of de-influencing, applications where alternative products
are presented to audiences can give rise to a comparative communication
based on consumers evaluating two or more products. In this respect, the
relationship between comparative advertising and de-influencing cannot
be established through the identity of the concepts, but rather through the
convergence of comparison-based persuasion and guidance mechanisms in
certain de-influencing practices. While comparative advertising is regarded as
a marketing strategy and a form of persuasive communication (Y enipazarlı,
2023), de-influencing has been associated with concepts such as moral
responsibility , sustainability , and anti-consumption (Apostol & Asiminei,
2025). Furthermore, in many cases, de-influencing also encompasses practices
aimed not at redirecting the consumer toward an alternative brand, but rather
at abstaining from purchase, consuming less, or questioning the necessity
of purchasing a product. In this context, whereas comparative advertising

h.) Demarketing : Kotler and Levy (1971), define demarketing as “the
aspect of marketing which deals with discouraging customers in general or a
certain class of customers in particular on a temporary or permanent basis”
(Lawrence & Mekoth, 2023). Accordingly , demarketing is fundamentally a
marketing approach used to control demand in situations where production
decreases or when it is necessary to limit excessive demand, scarcity , or specific
consumption patterns, contrasting with traditional marketing focused on
stimulating consumption. The primary objective of demarketing is not to
eliminate demand altogether, but rather to reshape its level and composition
in accordance with existing capacity , resource constraints, or organizational
objectives (Cullwick, 1975, pp. 51-53). Initially conceptualized as a means of
controlling demand in retail operations, demarketing has subsequently been
applied across a range of domains, including healthcare, tourism, hospitality
and restaurant management, energy , transportation, textiles, and public policy .
The example discussed by Kotler and Levy (1971) concerning the impact of
excessive tourist flows on Bali’s natural and authentic appeal illustrates the
use of demarketing to manage excessive demand in tourism. More recently ,
demarketing has also been associated with social and environmental objectives,
such as smoking cessation, reducing alcohol consumption, environmental
protection, and promoting sustainable consumption (Lawrence & Mekoth,
2023).
The conceptual overlap between demarketing and de-influencing lies in
the fact that both approaches, contrary to the conventional marketing mindset
centered on increasing consumption, are concerned with reducing specific
consumption patterns or encouraging consumers to reassess their purchasing
behaviors.Nevertheless, the two concepts do not fully overlap in terms of their
objectives and modes of application. While demarketing treats the reduction
or reshaping of demand as an organizational-level marketing approach, de-
influencing approaches a similar demand-reduction or redirection function as
a practice carried out within digital communication environments (Apostol
& Asiminei, 2025). In this context, the fundamental difference between the
two approaches emerges through the level at which the intervention aimed
at reducing demand takes place and the mechanisms employed. However, de-
influencing does not merely refer to reducing demand for a specific product or
service; it can also encompass consumers reassessing their purchasing decisions,

alternative products.
5. Theoretical Foundations of De-Influencing
The multifaceted structure of the de-influencing phenomenon—associated
with consumption behaviors, consumer decisions, social influence, and social
media communication—makes it possible to examine it within the framework
of different theoretical perspectives. Building on the conceptual characteristics
and related concepts outlined in the previous sections, this section evaluates
theories and models that may contribute to explaining the de-influencing
phenomenon and understanding its effects on behaviors. In this context,
social comparison theory , social impact theory , the persuasion knowledge
model, and the theory of planned behavior are addressed to explain consumer
behavior and changes in consumption preferences driven by de-influencing.
5.1. Social Comparison Theory
Social Comparison Theory posits that individuals use other people as
reference points to evaluate themselves and the circumstances in which they
are situated (Festinger, 1954). The theory suggests that individuals tend to
obtain accurate information about themselves by comparing their lifestyles,
achievements, appearances, or possessions with those of others, particularly
in situations where objective and definitive criteria for self-evaluation are
unavailable (Reid-Partin et al., 2023). According to the theory , the direction
of these comparisons can vary depending on the individual being compared
against. When individuals compare themselves with people whom they
perceive as more successful, attractive, or affluent than themselves, an upward
social comparison occurs. Conversely , comparing oneself with individuals
perceived to be in a more disadvantaged position constitutes a downward
social comparison, whereas comparisons with individuals who possess similar
characteristics are regarded as lateral comparisons (Reid-Partin et al., 2023).
Social media provides individuals with the opportunity to continuously
observe the lives of a far greater number of people than they would ordinarily
encounter within their traditional social environments. Social media content
frequently brings to light the selected and desired aspects of individuals’ lives
(Bailey et al., 2020). The continuous display of idealized lifestyles—particularly
in domains such as physical appearance, success, travel, luxury consumption,
fashion, and everyday life—can set the stage for users to compare their own
lives with these contents.

Influencers are not merely individuals promoting specific products,
but rather social actors who project a particular lifestyle, appearance, and
consumption pattern onto their followers. The notion that influencers possess
idealized self-presentations—which are generally evaluated as attractive and
desirable due to their popularity—can cause the follower to position themselves
lower (Vrontis et al.,2021; Tian et al., 2023). An important consequence
of upward social comparison in terms of consumer behavior is that when an
individual perceives a trait or lifestyle possessed by the comparison target
as desirable, they may turn to consumption in order to approach the social
position represented by that target and align themselves with it (Liang et al.,
2024). In this case, a simple comparison framed as “They have it, I do not”
can transform into a consumption desire articulated as “If I also acquire this,
I can approach the attributes they possess.” During this process, influencer
content transcends merely providing information about a product and evolves
into a comparison standard against which the individual evaluates themselves.
De-influencing content can alter the framework of social comparison and
facilitate the reevaluation of this comparison, thereby preventing the individual
from perceiving the difference in question as a deficiency . By discussing
the necessity of a product, its frequency of use, its actual benefits, or the
reasons for purchasing it, a de-influencer may alter followers’ evaluations
of the product. Consequently , instead of the follower initially interpreting
the discrepancy between themselves and the influencer as a deficiency and
attempting to bridge this gap through consumption, it becomes possible for
them to question whether this discrepancy actually needs to be bridged. In this
way , the meaning that individuals attribute to the difference between themselves
and the influencer may change. The difference, which was previously perceived
as a deficiency that needed to be remedied through consumption, can be
evaluated as a natural or acceptable divergence following de-influencing.
De-influencing content may also shift the target of social comparison and
offer followers an alternative consumption standard. While followers may use
the products owned by an influencer as a reference point for themselves, they
may also come to regard the de-influencer’s consumption practices as a new
reference point. For example, a de-influencer who refrains from purchasing
trending products, utilizes their existing possessions over an extended period,
or questions whether they genuinely need a product prior to purchase can
represent an alternative lifestyle and consumption pattern for the follower.
Rather than merely asking, “Why do I not possess what they have?”, the follower
may begin to ask questions such as, “Can I also consume less like them?” or
“Do I truly need this product?”. Accordingly , a de-influencer may establish
an alternative comparison standard to the consumption-oriented comparison

created by an influencer. Here, individuals continue to evaluate themselves
by using another person as a reference point; however, the comparison is
now directed not toward consuming and possessing more, but toward more
conscious consumption and questioning the necessity of ownership.
In short, Social Comparison Theory helps explain how de-influencing
content may reshape individuals’ consumption-related social comparisons.
De-influencing content can prevent the difference between the influencer and
the follower from being evaluated as a deficiency , thereby contributing to the
individual’s reevaluation of the meaning they attribute to this difference. In
addition, by shifting the target of social comparison, it may provide individuals
with an alternative consumption standard.
5.2. Social Influencer Theory
“Social influence can be described as the change in individuals’ thoughts,
feelings, communication, or behavior resulting from the influence of others”
(Oliveira et al., 2025). Kelman (1958), identified three fundamental mechanisms
of social influence—compliance, identification, and internalization—to explain
how social influence operates on individuals. These mechanisms account for
the reasons why individuals accept social influence and the extent to which
the attitudes or behaviors resulting from such influence become enduring
for the individual.
Compliance occurs when an individual accepts the influence of an actor
(i.e., the influencing person) in order to obtain a favorable response from
the actor or from others. In the compliance process, the individual’s primary
motivation does not stem from believing in the content of the behavior
exhibited by the actor or accepting this content in accordance with their
own values and beliefs. Rather, the fundamental motivation in this process
is to obtain an anticipated reward, approval, or acceptance from the actor or
the individual’s social environment, or to avoid punishment or disapproval
(Choudhury , 2026).
Identification occurs when an individual accepts the influence of an actor
in order to establish or maintain a satisfying, self-defining relationship with
another individual or group (Kelman, 1958). In this process, the foundation
of social influence lies in the attractiveness and relational value that the actor
or group holds for the target individual. As long as the actor’s perceived
value in the eyes of the influenced individual persists, the social influence will
continue; once this value disappears, the behavior resulting from the influence
will extinguish (Kağıtçıbaşı, 1999, p.93). Similar to the compliance process,
in the identification process, the individual may not adopt the attitudes and

28 | De-Influencing: A Conceptual and Theoretical Review
behaviors entailed by social influence in accordance with their own beliefs and
values. In the identification process, the individual’s primary motivation is
the desire to establish or maintain a satisfying social relationship (Choudhury ,
2026).
Internalization occurs when an individual accepts an actor’s influence
because they recognize the personal significance of the content presented by
that actor (Kelman, 1958). In other words, individuals accept the ideas and
actions presented by the actor because they perceive them as intrinsically
rewarding, beneficial, and consistent with their existing value system (Hwang,
2016, as cited in, Choudhury , 2026). The primary motivation here is to satisfy
the need to believe that one is acting correctly (Kağıtçıbaşı, 1999).
The mechanisms of compliance, identification, and internalization can also
be used to explain the effects of de-influencers and de-influencing content
on followers’ consumption decisions. In this regard, Social Impact Theory
provides a theoretical foundation for explaining how the social, emotional, and
cognitive processes emerging within the de-influencer–follower relationship
are translated into followers’ consumption behaviors. In the context of
de-influencing, each of the mechanisms of compliance, identification, and
internalization explains a follower’s decision not to purchase a particular
product or to alter their consumption behavior through a distinct underlying
motivation.
Within the mechanism of compliance, followers may adopt an attitude or
behavior advocated by a de-influencer not necessarily because it is consistent
with their personal beliefs, but rather to obtain the social approval they expect
from their social environment or to avoid negative social evaluations and
social sanctions (Choudhury , 2026). In other words, followers may engage
in consumption behaviors consistent with de-influencing messages in order
to conform to group norms and avoid being excluded from the group. For
example, when a follower observes that people around them also evaluate
the same product as an indicator of unnecessary or excessive consumption,
they may not want to be perceived as someone who “purchases products
mindlessly” or “exhibits consumption behaviors that harm the environment.”
In line with the theory , it can be stated that the primary determinant of
the follower’s behavior in the identification mechanism is the value that the
relationship established with the de-influencer and the identity represented by
the de-influencer hold for the follower. A de-influencer may project an identity
associated with a minimalist lifestyle, conscious consumption, avoidance
of unnecessary spending, or sustainable consumption. Followers may find
this lifestyle and identity appealing, perceive the de-influencer as similar to

Ali Alper Akgün | 29
themselves, develop an emotional bond with them, or regard them as a role
model. In such cases, followers are drawn not only to the information provided
by the de-influencer but also to the identity and lifestyle that the de-influencer
represents. Motivations such as “I want to be a conscious consumer like them”
or “the consumption approach they adopt aligns with the person I aspire to
be” can lead the follower to adopt the de-influencer’s attitudes and behaviors
regarding consumption.
The mechanism of internalization, explains the acceptance of a message
presented by a de-influencer when the follower perceives that message as
consistent with their own values, beliefs, and needs (Kelman, 1958). De-
influencer content often demonstrates that consumers do not actually need
the product in question, that existing alternatives can be utilized, that the
product’s price is disproportionate to its utility , or that it has only gained
popularity because of a trend, alongside highlighting its environmental impacts
or relation to overconsumption. When the follower finds the presented message
meaningful, useful, and correct in terms of their own value system, they may
accept the message and abandon the decision to purchase.
5.3. Theory of Planned Behavior
The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), developed by Ajzen (1991)
provides a theoretical framework for understanding human behavior. TPB
proposes that behavior is shaped by attitudes toward the behavior, subjective
norms, and perceived behavioral control (Ajzen, 1985, 1991).
The first core component of TPB, attitude, refers to an individual’s beliefs
regarding the consequences of a particular behavior. An individual’s attitudes
are shaped according to whether their beliefs concerning the consequences of
that behavior are positive, negative, or neutral (Long et al., 2024). The stronger
these beliefs become in a positive direction, the stronger the individual’s
positive attitudes toward the behavior (Y alçıner Smit, 2022).
The second component, subjective norm, refers to the individual’s perception
of the expectations of people or groups they consider important regarding
whether or not to perform a specific behavior. The views of reference groups,
such as family members, friends, and other members of the individual’s social
environment, may influence the formation of behavioral intentions (Long
et al., 2024).
The third component, perceived behavioral control, refers to the individual’s
perception of how easy or difficult it is to perform a specific behavior, as well
as their belief regarding whether they possess the necessary skills, resources,
and opportunities to perform that behavior (Ajzen, 1985, 1991). As the level

30 | De-Influencing: A Conceptual and Theoretical Review
of perceived behavioral control increases, the individual’s intention to perform
the behavior in question also increases (Y alçıner Smit, 2022).
The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) provides a theoretical framework
that can be used to explain the effects of de-influencing content on consumer
behavior. Within this framework, de-influencing content may be expected to
influence consumers’ attitudes toward purchasing behavior, their perceived
social norms regarding purchasing behavior, and their perceived behavioral
control over purchasing behavior. Accordingly , the potential effects of de-
influencing on the three core components of the TPB can be considered
separately .
De-influencing content may prompt consumers to question and reconsider
their existing attitudes toward a product, thereby altering their evaluations of
the product’s necessity , utility , or rationale for purchase (Apostol & Asiminei,
2025). For example, by discussing how frequently a popular product is actually
used, whether consumers genuinely need the product, or whether the product
delivers the benefits it promises, a de-influencer may prompt followers to
reconsider their favorable evaluations of the product. Such contents may
influence consumers’ beliefs regarding the expected outcomes of purchasing
behavior, thereby contributing to the development of a more unfavorable
attitude toward purchasing. This shift may contribute to weakening the
individual’s behavioral intention to purchase and making the decision not
to buy more likely .
De-influencing content can also enable the reevaluation of social norms
that influence an individual’s purchasing decision. While influencer content
presents certain products as trendy , current, and must-have items, de-influencers
can question this consumption expectation and demonstrate an alternative
behavioral pattern to the follower. Particularly , considering the consumption
trends prevalent on social media and the social pressure exerted by influencers,
de-influencing content can offer an alternative perspective to these perceived
social norms, prompting the individual to reassess their purchasing decision.
Consequently , the individual may begin to attribute less importance to the
influence of peers or general social media trends regarding the purchase of
a specific product.
De-influencing may strengthen the individual’s sense of perceived control
over the purchasing decision (Apostol & Asiminei, 2025). Realizing that they
are not obligated to conform to social media trends or follow influencers’
recommendations, consumers may conclude that they can make decisions
aligned with their own needs, budget, and preferences. This may enable

consumers to evaluate their purchasing decisions independently of external
influences and to regard non-purchase behavior as a deliberate choice.
5.4. Persuasion Knowledge Model
Consumers exposed to various persuasive attempts by marketers seek to
interpret these messages and develop appropriate responses to them. In making
sense of how and why marketers attempt to persuade them, consumers draw
upon knowledge they possess and have developed over time (Aytekin & Ay ,
2014). Termed persuasion knowledge, this knowledge refers to consumers’
knowledge and beliefs regarding the persuasion motives and attempts of
persuasive actors, the underlying motives and tactics of these attempts, and
how the persuasion process operates (Friestad & Wright, 1994).
The Persuasion Knowledge Model explains the nature and development of
persuasion knowledge, as well as how consumers utilize this knowledge in the
processes of interpreting, evaluating, and responding to persuasion attempts
(Friestad & Wright, 1994). According to the model’s central assumption, as
consumers encounter different persuasive attempts over time, they develop
knowledge about the goals, motives, and tactics employed by persuasive
agents. This knowledge is conceptualized as a structure that develops over time,
remains available in memory , and is activated when a persuasive message is
recognized (Boerman&Müller, 2022). As their persuasion knowledge develops,
consumers are able to compare the benefits offered to them with the benefits
expected to be gained by the marketer or influencer, and can more easily discern
commercial motives, ulterior motives, and potential manipulative tactics.
The recognition of a persuasion attempt can lead to critical evaluations and
coping responses to negativity (Friestad & Wright, 1994; Eisend & Tarrahi,
2022; Waltenrath, 2024).
Cues indicating that a message carries a persuasive intent can play a crucial
role in activating the consumer’s persuasion knowledge. In particular, post
characteristics such as sponsorship disclosures, content or source characteristics
such as influencer-brand incongruence, and the transparent disclosure of
influencers’ commercial relationships or objectives can trigger consumers
to recognize the persuasive intent behind the message and evaluate it more
critically (Pan et al., 2025).
Realizing that the influencer is not merely sharing a product experience,
but rather attempting to guide them toward developing a specific attitude
or behavior regarding a product—and that there is a commercial motive
behind the communication—can lead consumers to question the persuasive
attempt in the content more thoroughly (Boerman vd., 2018; Boerman &

32 | De-Influencing: A Conceptual and Theoretical Review
Müller, 2022). This awareness can activate persuasion knowledge, enabling
the consumer to evaluate the influencer’s recommendation and the persuasive
intent behind it, while also leading the consumer to develop a more skeptical
attitude toward the recommended product.
The Persuasion Knowledge Model (PKM) provides an important
theoretical framework for explaining how the phenomenon of de-influencing
shapes consumers’ responses to influencer content. From this perspective, de-
influencing can be conceptualized as a form of communication that activates
consumers’ persuasion knowledge regarding influencer marketing, enabling
them to interpret and cope with persuasion attempts in accordance with this
knowledge.
From a PKM perspective, the first stage of the de-influencing process involves
consumers recognizing the persuasive intent underlying an influencer’s message.
This awareness may prompt consumers to evaluate the influencer’s motivations
and the purpose underlying the message. In particular, the perception that an
influencer has an economic interest or maintains a commercial relationship
with a brand may lead consumers to question the credibility and impartiality
of the message. When consumers perceive the influencer’s communication as
being motivated by commercial interests, this may facilitate more active use
of persuasion knowledge and provide a basis for developing different coping
strategies in response to the persuasion attempt (Boerman & Müller, 2022). In
the context of de-influencing, these strategies may manifest as questioning the
influencer’s product recommendation, reassessing the necessity of the product,
consulting alternative information sources, postponing the purchasing decision,
or deciding against the purchase. From this perspective, de-influencing can
be evaluated as a form of coping and resistance that prompts the consumer
to question and resist the persuasion process toward purchasing generated
by the influencer.
According to the PKM, de-influencing content does not merely tell
consumers “what they should not buy”; rather, it provides a cognitive
framework through which consumers can critically examine the influencer’s
message itself. Consequently , instead of directly accepting the desire to purchase
generated by the influencer, consumers may reassess the necessity of the
product, the influencer’s motivations, and the persuasive nature of the message.
6. CONCLUSION
This study aimed to examine the conceptual development, core
characteristics, connections with related concepts, and theoretical foundations

Ali Alper Akgün | 33
of the de-influencing phenomenon, which is gaining increasing visibility in
social media environments.
Based on the evaluation of the conceptual literature, it is evident that
de-influencing is not merely a social media trend developed against the non-
purchase of certain products; rather, it constitutes a broader domain aimed
at questioning the consumption-driving communication forms of influencer
marketing. In this context, de-influencing can be regarded as an alternative
communication practice that encourages consumers to reconsider their
consumption preferences and purchasing orientations.
At this point, it is remarkable that de-influencing generates anti-consumption
or consumption-questioning messages directly within the framework of social
media and influencer culture. De-influencing content is produced by influencers
who act as “de-influencers.” Therefore, rather than eradicating influencer
impact altogether, de-influencing can be conceptualized as a form of influencing
that redirects this impact and alters the nature of recommendations concerning
consumer behavior. Moreover, rather than developing as an independent
anti-consumption discourse outside the influencer economy , de-influencing
emerges as a practice that critiques influencer culture by utilizing the very
building blocks of that culture, such as visibility , personal experience sharing,
and social interaction. These two aspects expose a striking inherent tension
within the concept.
When the relationship between de-influencing and related concepts is
considered, the phenomenon can be seen to intersect, at various points, with
approaches such as influencer marketing, greenfluencing, anti-consumption,
anti-haul, minimalism, e-WOM, comparative advertising, and de-marketing.
Nevertheless, conflating de-influencing entirely with any of these concepts
risks obscuring the unique dimensions inherent to the phenomenon itself.
Consequently , de-influencing ought to be addressed as a distinct phenomenon
possessing independent conceptual boundaries, rather than as a synonym for
these notions.
Theoretical models and frameworks were utilized to explain the effects of
de-influencing content on consumer behavior. In this context, social impact
theory , social comparison theory , the theory of planned behavior, and the
persuasion knowledge model were employed. Social impact theory explains
how the social, emotional, and cognitive processes emerging within the de-
influencer–follower relationship can translate into followers’ consumption
behaviors. Social comparison theory elucidates the psychological foundation
upon which de-influencing content can exert its effects, whereas the theory
of planned behavior accounts for how such content can shape behavioral

perceptions. Finally , the persuasion knowledge model indicates how de-
influencing content can assist followers by reinforcing their persuasion
knowledge, thereby enabling them to cope with marketing stimuli.
By bringing together the definition, emergence, and development of the
concept of de-influencing, its related concepts, and its theoretical foundations,
this study contributes to the evaluation within an integrated framework
of explanations that are dispersed across different strands of the literature.
The theoretical framework developed in the study is expected to provide a
conceptual basis for understanding how de-influencing practices operate
and to offer guidance to those who produce content in this field, while also
serving as a foundation for future empirical research.
