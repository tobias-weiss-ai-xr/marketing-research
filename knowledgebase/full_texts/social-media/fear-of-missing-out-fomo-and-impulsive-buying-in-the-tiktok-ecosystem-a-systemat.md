 The integration of entertainment, social interaction, and
commerce on TikTok has created a digital environment in which
consumers are continuously exposed to trends, influencers,
limited-time promotions, and instant purchasing facilities. This
study systematically synthesizes evidence on Fear of Missing
Out (FOMO) and impulsive buying within TikTok and related
live-social commerce environments and evaluates the
phenomenon from an Islamic economics perspective. A
Systematic Literature Review was conducted using 26 verified
empirical studies, supported by methodological and Islamic
consumption literature. The synthesis demonstrates that FOMO
is consistently associated with impulsive buying, either as a
direct predictor, a mediator linking scarcity, discounts,
influencers, and streamer-related stimuli to purchases, or a
moderator strengthening value-driven purchase urges.
Parasocial interaction, social presence, hedonic motivation,
trust, electronic word-of-mouth, payment convenience, and
self-control further shape the process. From an Islamic
economics perspective, spontaneous purchasing is not
inherently prohibited. Ethical concerns emerge when FOMO-
driven consumption becomes excessive, wasteful, financially
irresponsible, or detached from genuine utility. Maslahah,
qana'ah, wasathiyah, self-control, and the avoidance of israf and
tabdzir provide a normative framework for responsible digital
consumption and ethical affiliate marketing.

INTRODUCTION
The development of social commerce has transformed the process by which consumers
recognize, evaluate, and purchase products. TikTok has become one of the digital
trading spaces bringing together entertainment, social interaction, algorithmic
recommendations, promotion, and transaction facilities within an almost
uninterrupted usage flow (Van et al., 2024). Consumers can transition from watching

videos to making purchases in just a few steps. Such a condition differs from
conventional e-commerce which is typically initiated by deliberate product searches
(Chen, 2024). On TikTok, product exposures frequently appear when users are seeking
entertainment, following creators, or interacting with content. Such an environment
magnifies the opportunity for purchase decisions that occur swiftly because marketing
stimuli accompany information regarding trends, responses from other users,
promotional time lim its, product popularity, and recommendations from figures
already known to consumers (Li, 2025).
TikTok Affiliate constitutes an important part of this ecosystem. Affiliates promote
products through videos, usage demonstrations, reviews, live streaming, experience
stories, and product links by earning commissions when transactions occur (Fan, 2024).
This format enables promotional messages to blend seamlessly with entertainment
content and personal communication. Consumer relationships with affiliates can also
resemble social closeness because audiences follow creator activities repeatedly
(Mohammad et al., 2025). The research of Li et al. (2025) demonstrates that streamer
attractiveness can drive impulsive purchasing through parasocial interaction and Fear
of Missing Out (FOMO). Such findings indicate that promotional figures in content -
based commerce operate not merely through product information, but also through
psychological relationships between audiences and creators.
Impulsive buying is understood as purchase decisions that arise quickly, are relatively
unplanned, and are accompanied by a strong urge to buy immediately (Amarjargal et
al., 2025). Beatty and Ferrell (1998) explain that impulsive buying is shaped by the
convergence of situational factors and individual character, such as the availability of
time and money, shopping enjoyment, affective conditions, search activities, as well as
purchase urges. The digital environment broadens such stimuli with features like live
streaming, flash sales, paylater, reviews, comments, viewer counts, influencer
recommendations, and instant transactions (Xavior, 2025). Consumers who initially
did not plan purchases can experience surges in urges when products appear as
limited, trendy, or recommended opportunities by figures considered attractive and
credible (Alexandrovich et al., 2023).
FOMO serves as one of the psychological mechanisms widely studied in digital
purchasing. Przybylski et al. (2013) define FOMO as the apprehension that others are
acquiring valuable experiences while individuals are not partaking in them,
accompanied by the  desire to maintain connectivity with others' activities. In digital
commerce, such apprehension can develop into the fear of losing popular products,
promotions, trends, social opportunities, or consumption experiences currently being
discussed (Zhong, 2023). Hong-Ngoc (2025) finds that the dimensions of the need to
feel accepted and anxiety over alienation relate strongly to the impulsive buying of
young TikTok Shop users. Ramdhani et al. (2025), Kumalasari and Aditama (2025), as
well as Mufti and Hariyanto (2025) also find positive relationships between FOMO and
impulsive buying.
Empirical findings indicate that FOMO frequently emerges after consumers receive
marketing stimuli (Babu et al., 2026). Adyantari et al. (2025) find that scarcity and
discounts during live streaming increase FOMO, whereas FOMO subsequently
mediates the formation of impulsive buying behavior. Fitria et al. (2025) demonstrate
the role of FOMO within the relationship between influencer marketing and shopping
lifestyle with impulsive buying. Pangastuti and Kusumaningtyas (2025) find that

Recent research illustrates increasingly complex mechanisms. Pramudita and
Rufaidah (2026) find that FOMO relates significantly to impulsive buying when
consumers encounter Twin Date Sales programs. Aresti et al. (2026) obtain significant
relationships among FOMO, digital content marketing, and impulsive buying among
Generation Z skincare consumers. Hadiyati and Luthfia (2026) show that impulsive
purchasing within TikTok Live involves FOMO, narrative engagement, telepresence,
parasocial interaction, and soc ial presence. Li et al. (2026) position FOMO as a
moderator strengthening the relationship between perceived value and the urge to buy
impulsively. Such results signify that FOMO can occupy the position of a predictor,
mediator, or reinforcing condition.
Self-control capacity also constitutes an important element. Ernanda et al. (2026) find
that FOMO relates to increased impulsive buying and decreased self -control among
Wardah consumers through TikTok Live. Phang et al. (2026) demonstrate that self -
control moderates the relationship between perceived value and impulsive buying
intentions in live streaming. Lestari and Nurhadi (2026) find FOMO and ease of use as
positive predictors of impulsive buying among Generation Z TikTok Shop Live users.
This series of  findings demonstrates that spontaneous consumption behavior results
from the interaction between marketing stimuli, psychological responses, value
perceptions, and self-regulation capacities.
Such phenomena are relevant to examine within Islamic economics because
consumption decisions relate to the utilization and safeguarding of wealth. Islam
recognizes consumption as a permitted activity as long as objects and transactions
fulfill sharia prov isions, yet imposes limits on excessive behavior and wastefulness.
The orientation of Islamic consumption does not halt at satisfaction, but rather
considers benefit or maslahah (Yulianti & Maryati, 2023). Budiman (2025) explains
israf as consumption excee ding reasonable limits and tabdzir as the utilization of
resources that is non -productive or in vain. This framework is important because
spontaneous purchases cannot be directly categorized as prohibited acts merely
because they occur without planning. Th eir evaluation needs to consider product
benefits, financial capacity, consumption objectives, frequency, and consequences of
wealth utilization.
Scientific gaps still persist in the available literature. Most research discusses TikTok
Shop and TikTok Live, whereas TikTok Affiliate as a commission -based promotion
mechanism is less frequently isolated as an object. The literature is also dominated by
marketing and consumer psychology approaches. Systematic integration among
empirical evidence regarding FOMO, affiliate promotion mechanisms, impulsive
buying, and Islamic economic principles remains limited. This research is intended to
fill that space t hrough the synthesis of more than 25 empirical studies whose
bibliographic identities have been verified.
The research aims to explain how FOMO and digital marketing stimuli drive
impulsive buying within the TikTok environment and to evaluate such phenomena

METHOD
The research employs a Systematic Literature Review method with narrative-thematic
synthesis. Systematic review is utilized because the literature regarding FOMO and
impulsive buying is scattered across research in digital marketing, social commerce,
live-commerce, consumer psychology, and Islamic economics. Tranfield et al. (2003)
emphasize the necessity of a transparent search, selection, evaluation, and synthesis
process in management-field systematic reviews. Snyder (2019) adds that a literature
review treated as a research method must possess clear questions, search strategies,
selection criteria, and explainable synthesis procedures. The reporting of this research
refers to PRISMA 2020 principles (Page et al., 2021), particularly transparency in source
identification, screening, eligibility, and the determination of final articles.
Literature searches and verifications were conducted through October 1, 2026, using
Google Scholar, Garuda, DOAJ, national journal portals, and international publisher
websites such as Taylor & Francis, Wiley, Frontiers, Elsevier, and original journal
pages of the articles. Keywords encompass "Fear of Missing Out", "FOMO", "impulsive
buying", "impulse buying", "TikTok Shop", "TikTok Live", "TikTok Affiliate", "live -
streaming commerce", "flash sale", "scarcity", "influencer marketing", "content
marketing", " paylater", "self -control", and "Islamic consumption ethics". References
were included if they constituted academic articles with verifiable publication
identities, provided methodological information and findings, and remained relevant
to FOMO, impulsive b uying, TikTok, live/social commerce, or Islamic consumption
ethics. Undergraduate theses, master's theses, news articles, popular pieces, sources
lacking verifiable bibliographic identities, and duplicate article versions were
excluded.
The final corpus consists of 26 primary and supporting empirical studies on impulsive
buying mechanisms, supplemented by theoretical, methodological, and Islamic
economic sources. Each article was verified through its original journal/publisher
page, DOI, Garuda, or official academic metadata. Data were extracted based on
authors and year, objects, sample sizes when available, analytical methods, constructs,
and findings relating to the research questions. Analysis was conducted through cross-
study comparis ons and grouping into five themes: the direct relationship between
FOMO and impulsive buying; marketing stimuli shaping FOMO; relational
mechanisms and value perceptions; transaction facilities and convenience; and self -
control. These empirical themes were subsequently examined utilizing the concepts of
maslahah, wasathiyah, qana'ah, israf, tabdzir, and responsibility toward wealth.
Initial search logs in previous drafts did not consistently record result counts for each
database, duplication, and exclusion. For reasons of academic integrity, counts at the
initial stages of the PRISMA diagram are not artificially reconstructed. This r esearch
reports the verified final corpus, whereas full numerical PRISMA diagrams can only

FOMO Mechanism in the Formation of Impulsive Buying within the TikTok
Affiliate Ecosystem
Synthesis results indicate a highly consistent pattern that FOMO possesses a positive
relationship with the tendency toward impulsive buying, although the strength and
position within models vary. Hong -Ngoc (2025), Ramdhani et al. (2025), Kumalasari
and Aditama (2025), Mufti and Hariyanto (2025), Hasna et al. (2025), Pramudita and
Rufaidah (2026), Aresti et al. (2026), Dilara et al. (2026), Natadilaga et al. (2026),
Nopfianti et al. (2026), and Wuryan et al. (2026) uniformly demonstrate FOMO as a
predictor associated with increased spontaneous purchasing. Such consistency is
found among students, Generation Z, skincare consumers, fashion consumers, and
TikTok Shop users across several cities.
The finding of Hong-Ngoc (2025) provides a more detailed psychological explanation.
FOMO does not merely take the form of a fear of missing cheap prices, but rather
involves the need to belong to a group and anxiety regarding alienation. When TikTok
displays products as part of trends being followed by others, purchasing decisions can
function as actions to maintain symbolic connectedness with groups. This finding
helps explain why fashion items, skincare, and viral products are highly dominant in
research. Such products are easily associated with identity, appearance, and social
recognition.
Marketing stimuli increase the likelihood of FOMO through the creation of scarcity
and urgency. Adyantari et al. (2025) show that scarcity and discounts in live-streaming
increase FOMO prior to influencing impulsive buying. Ardana et al. (2026) also

position scarcity messages, flash sales, visual appeal, and FOMO within the same
model. Wuryan et al. (2026) show that flash sales continue to exert a positive
relationship with impulsive buying when tested together with paylater and FOMO.
Such a pattern i ndicates that time or stock limitations operate primarily by altering
consumer perceptions regarding psychological costs if decisions are delayed.
Despite this, time-limited promotions do not produce uniform effects. Pangastuti and
Kusumaningtyas (2025) find that flash sales possess a positive yet nonsignificant
direction, whereas FOMO and live stream shopping are significant. This difference
indicates that price promotions alone are not necessarily sufficient to generate
spontaneous decisions. Consumers can be aware of discounts without feeling
compelled to buy. FOMO becomes stronger when such promotions are accompanied
by social impressions that pro ducts are popular, widely used, or opportunities to
acquire specific experiences will vanish.
Real-time interaction constitutes the next important stimulus. Li et al. (2025) find that
streamer attractiveness influences impulsive buying through parasocial interaction
and FOMO. Hadiyati and Luthfia (2026) demonstrate that parasocial interaction and
social presence bridge various communication stimuli toward impulsive purchase on
TikTok Live. Sari et al. (2026) show that FOMO shapes trust and strengthens the effect
of electronic word-of-mouth. Such findings indicate that consumer relationships with
streamers, affiliates, and digital communities can alter how commercial messages are
perceived.
Such mechanisms are highly relevant to TikTok Affiliate. Affiliates send
advertisements and build narratives through personal usage, demonstrations,
recommendations, comments, direct responses, and content repetition. When
audiences have followed creators for a certain period, product recommendations
acquire social and relational dimensions. Consumers can process promotions as advice
from familiar figures. FOMO subsequently increases pressure to follow such
recommendations when products are also depicted as  viral, quickly sold out, or
available at limited prices.
Influencers and content marketing yield parallel results. Fitria et al. (2025) show that
FOMO plays a role in the relationship of influencer marketing and shopping lifestyle
with impulsive buying. Mufti and Hariyanto (2025) find content marketing and FOMO
to be equally significant. Aresti et al. (2026) obtain a similar pattern among Generation
Z skincare buyers. Nopfianti et al. (2026) show that FOMO, live streaming selling, and
influencer marketing simultaneously explain impulse buying. Such findings
demonstrate that the strength of TikTok Affiliate lies in the combination of messages,
figures, and interaction design, rather than a single promotional instrument alone.
Hedonic motivation also amplifies responses to digital exposure. Hasna et al. (2025)
find that hedonic shopping motivation and FOMO relate positively to impulse buying.
Al Madinah et al. (2025) examine hedonic shopping motivation as a moderator of the
relationship between marketing stimuli and impulsive buying. Adha et al. (2025) show
that hedonic value together with utilitarian, symbolic values, trust, and customer
engagement play a role in the formation of impulsive buying. This finding indicates
that consumers do not always buy due to practical benefits. Pleasure, entertainment,
identity, and social experiences also constitute values sought after.

Li, Arham, and Majid (2026) provide a finding that clarifies the position of FOMO.
FOMO functions as a direct antecedent, and can act as a reinforcing condition between
perceived value and the urge to buy impulsively. Meaning, FOMO works more
strongly when consumers previously evaluate products as interesting or useful. This
finding prevents excessive conclusions that FOMO alone causes every spontaneous
purchase. Products still need to possess value perceived by consumers in order for the
pressure of missing opportunities to generate transaction decisions.
Transaction convenience shortens the distance between urges and actions. Dilara et al.
(2026) find that paylater and FOMO exert positive effects on impulsive buying.
Wuryan et al. (2026) also obtain positive relationships for flash sales, paylater, and
FOMO. Lestari and Nurhadi (2026) find ease of use to exert a positive effect alongside
FOMO. These facilities do not always create the desire to buy, but lower barriers when
urges have already formed. In affiliate commerce, product links, yellow baskets, saved
payments, and instant credit can make decisions take place before consumers conduct
re-evaluation.
Self-control serves as a limiting mechanism. Ernanda et al. (2026) find that FOMO
relates negatively to self -control, whereas self -control plays a role in suppressing
impulse buying. Phang et al. (2026) show that self -control moderates the relationship
between perceived value and impulsive buying intention. Lestari and Nurhadi (2026)
find self-control to be nonsignificant partially, indicating that its effect is inconsistent
across studies. This difference indicates that self -regulation does not operate in the
same form across every group, yet remains an important construct to explain why
consumers with similar promotional exposure and FOMO can yield differing
decisions.
Differences in product characteristics, respondents, locations, and analytical models
help explain variations in results. The majority of studies use Generation Z and
products with rapid trend cycles. This limits generalizability to consumers of other
ages or goods with high purchase considerations. The majority of studies also use
cross-sectional surveys, so statistical relationships should not be read as absolute
causal evidence. Nonetheless, the consistency of relationships across many studies
provides a  strong basis for placing FOMO as an important mechanism in social
commerce consumption behavior.
FOMO functions as a psychological mechanism that accelerates transitions from digital
stimulus exposure toward spontaneous purchasing. Scarcity, time-limited promotions,
influencers, affiliates, parasocial interaction, social presence, social proof, percei ved
value, hedonic motivation, and transaction facilities can reinforce such a process.
TikTok Affiliate itself cannot be deemed a sole cause of impulsive buying because
many studies examine TikTok Shop or TikTok Live broadly. Available evidence is
more su pportive of the conclusion that affiliate mechanisms operate within an
environment possessing various stimuli that have repeatedly been found related to
FOMO and impulse buying.

FOMO-Triggered Impulsive Buying in the Perspective of Islamic Economics
Islamic economics evaluates consumption activities by considering the lawfulness of
objects, transaction methods, utility, expenditure proportions, financial capabilities,
and the consequences of wealth usage. Yulianti and Maryati (2023) explain that Islamic

259
consumption is oriented toward maslahah and does not stop at the pursuit of
maximum satisfaction. Such a framework makes the evaluation of impulse buying
different from consumer psychology approaches. The term "impulsive" constitutes a
description of decision processes, whereas categories of permissible or impermissible
require broader normative evaluations.
Spontaneous purchases do not automatically conflict with sharia. Consumers can
spontaneously buy halal goods that provide benefits, remain within capabilities, and
do not disrupt financial obligations. Issues arise when such decisions produce patterns
of e xcess, waste, uncontrolled debt, or the redirection of funds from more primary
needs. Such differentiation is important so that the Islamic economic perspective does
not simplify every spontaneous action as a violation.
The principle of maslahah constitutes the primary basis for evaluating consumption
decisions. Products currently popular are not necessarily devoid of utility, yet
popularity also cannot serve as a measure of need. FOMO risks shifting consumer
evaluations from actual benefits toward the fear of being left behind. When purchasing
decisions are made primarily to alleviate social anxiety, consumers need to evaluate
whether goods provide value comparable to the resources expended.
The principle of moderation or wasathiyah provides limits toward consumption
patterns. QS. Al -A'raf [7]:31 teaches that consumption should not be performed in
excess. Such a principle is relevant to ongoing social commerce exposure. A consumer
may be able to buy one viral product without issue, yet patterns of following every
trend can shift consumption toward a state of excess. Sharia evaluations are more
appropriately directed toward patterns and consequences rather than isolated
transactions.
Budiman (2025) explains israf as consumption that exceeds reasonable limits. Such a
concept is highly relevant when FOMO causes consumers to buy repeated products
with identical functions, spend funds exceeding reasonable proportions, or pursue
trends without considering capabilities. Empirical findings regarding paylater amplify
attention toward this issue because credit facilities can enable consumption to occur
before payment capabilities are truly available.
Tabdzir possesses a different emphasis because it relates to the utilization of resources
in a vain or unproductive manner. QS. Al -Isra [17]:26–27 provides warnings against
waste. Within social commerce, risks of tabdzir can emerge when products are bought
due to trends yet remain unused, are immediately discarded, or fail to provide
meaningful utility. Spontaneous purchases occurring occasionally do not necessarily
constitute tabdzir, yet repetitions of purchasing patterns resulting in the accumulation
of goods without utilization reinforce indicators of waste.
The principle of balance is also reflected in QS. Al -Furqan [25]:67 concerning
expenditures that are neither excessive nor miserly. Balance demands consumers to
consider needs, capabilities, savings, obligations, and long -term goals. From this
viewpoint, F OMO can be viewed as a disruption to balance if consumers place
excessive weight on social pressures compared to financial planning. Findings by
Dilara et al. (2026) and Wuryan et al. (2026) regarding paylater demonstrate the direct
relevance of the principle of wealth safeguarding.
Qana'ah can function as an ethical principle opposing the social comparison
mechanisms underlying FOMO. Qana'ah is not a rejection of consumption, but an

Findings concerning self -control bring together consumer psychology and Islamic
economic values. Ernanda et al. (2026) and Phang et al. (2026) show that self-regulation

an ethical dimension because it relates to capabilities to safeguard wealth and control
desires. Although Lestari and Nurhadi (2026) do not find self-control to be significant
partially, such inconsistency instead indicates the necessity of research examining
relationships among self-control, religiosity, qana'ah, and financial literacy.
The sharia perspective also possesses implications for affiliates. Commissions from
promotions essentially constitute economic incentives that are acceptable if objects and
transaction mechanisms comply with provisions. Ethical issues emerge if affiliates
convey false information, conceal product weaknesses, create artificial scarcity, or use
claims purposely enlarging consumer anxiety. Honesty, trustworthiness,
transparency, and prohibitions against deception constitute principles limiting how
FOMO can be utilized as a marketing strategy.
Transparency of affiliate relationships is also relevant. Consumers need to know that
creators possess economic interests from transactions conducted through their links.
Such information helps audiences evaluate recommendations more objectively.
Responsible promotions should enhance consumer information quality, rather than
reduce their capacity to make decisions. Affiliates can continue utilizing creativity,
storytelling, live streaming, and product demonstrations without having to rely on
misleading claims or artificial pressures.
The study by Kartika et al. (2025) concerning impulse buying in e -commerce
promotions from the perspective of Islamic consumption ethics reinforces the necessity
of viewing behavioral motivations and consequences, not merely the presence of
spontaneous tra nsactions. Budiman (2025) positions israf and tabdzir as ethical
boundaries toward daily consumption. Yulianti and Maryati (2023) emphasize
maslahah as the objective of consumption. If the three perspectives are combined,
impulsive behavior can be mapped f rom what remains acceptable to what is
problematic based on utility, capability, intensity, and the impacts of wealth usage.
FOMO-triggered impulse buying does not possess a uniform normative status. Sharia
evaluations need to be conducted through several questions: whether objects and
transactions are halal; whether goods possess proper utility; whether purchases reside
within financial capabilities; whether primary needs remain fulfilled; whether
amounts and frequencies exceed reasonableness; and whether goods are utilized or
end up as waste. Maslahah, qana'ah, wasathiyah, self-control, and wealth safeguarding
form ethical mechanisms to prevent FOMO from developing into israf and tabdzi.

CONCLUSION
A Systematic Literature Review of 26 empirical studies indicates that FOMO
constitutes one of the most consistent psychological mechanisms related to impulsive
buying on TikTok Shop, TikTok Live, and social commerce environments relevant to

TikTok Affiliate. FOMO can function as a direct predictor, a mediator following
exposure to scarcity, discounts, influencers, or streamers, as well as a moderator
reinforcing the relationship between perceived value and impulsive urges.
Spontaneous purchases are also influenced by live streaming, parasocial interaction,
social presence, content marketing, hedonic motivation, transaction convenience,
paylater, trust, E -WOM, and self -control. Available evidence does not yet permit
claims that TikTok Affiliate  alone causes impulse buying, yet affiliate mechanisms
operate within an environment robust in generating FOMO stimuli. From Islamic
economics, impulsive buying is not automatically prohibited. Issues emerge when
consumption loses utility, exceeds capabilities, ignores priority needs, or develops into
israf and tabdzir.
Theoretically, study results support a layered model positioning digital stimuli as
triggers, FOMO and perceived value as psychological processes, and impulsive buying
as a response. The role of self-control indicates that consumer decisions are not entirely
determined by platforms. Academically, TikTok Affiliate research needs to separate
affiliate effects from the effects of TikTok Shop, live streaming, prices, and payment
methods. The Islamic economic perspective broadens the discussion by adding
dimensions of utility, moderation, and wealth safeguarding. Practically, Muslim
consumers need to build decision pauses, evaluate utility, examine financial
capabilities, and distinguish needs from trend pressures. Affiliate practitioners need to
maintain honesty, commission transparency, claim accuracy, as well as avoid creating
misleading scarcity or urgency.
Subsequent research needs to recruit consumers who can be proven to conduct
transactions through affiliate links or baskets so that TikTok Affiliate constitutes an
object truly separated from TikTok Shop generally. Research models can
simultaneously test a ffiliate credibility, parasocial interaction, FOMO, self -control,
qana'ah, sharia financial literacy, religiosity, and impulse buying. Experimental studies
are necessary to test the effects of scarcity, countdowns, social proof, and commission
disclosures more robustly compared to cross -sectional surveys. Longitudinal studies
are also required to observe whether impulse buying develops into excessive
consumption patterns. Subsequent SLR research needs to store complete search logs
from the beginning, includ ing databases, search dates, search strings, result counts,
duplications, exclusions, article elimination reasons, and quality assessments so that
entire PRISMA diagrams can be reported numerically and replicated.
