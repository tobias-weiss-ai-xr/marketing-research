Abstract
This study investigates the impact of influencer marketing on impulsive buying behavior and overconsumption among
young consumers by utilising the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S- O-R) framework and the Persuasion Knowledge
Model (PKM). In an era of massive global advertising expenditur e, this research examines how digital stimuli and
parasocial interactions trigger spontaneous purchasing decisions. Adopting a descriptive and exploratory quantitative
design, data was collected via an online survey focusing on the  young consumer demographic. The results identify a
“Persuasion Knowledge Paradox,” where a significant majority of  participants express skepticism toward paid
advertisements, yet nearly half engaged in immediate impulsive purchases. These findings suggest that the “Illusion
of Friendship,” or parasocial interaction, functions as a psychological bypass that allows affective triggers to override
cognitive critical defences. Notably, a substantial portion of the sample reported post-pur chase regret, owning items
purchased via social media that remain largely unused. While li mited by the use of a convenience sample, the study
highlights the risks of digital overconsumption and financial v ulnerability for young consumers in the modern social
commerce landscape. Ultimately, this paper contributes to the literature by explicitly linking the relational intimacy of
influencer marketing to the physical manifestation of overconsu mption, providing a nuanced understanding of how
contemporary digital environments facilitate a transition from cognitive awareness to spontaneous consumption.
1. Introduction
In today’s digital landscape, overconsumption has become an alm ost unavoidable consequence of contemporary
capitalist systems. More precisely, the rapid advancement of technologies has accelerated the expansion of the digital
economy and reshaped how people consume. The concept of overcon sumption spans multiple disciplines, from
business to environmental studies. Within this literature, it c ommonly refers to consumption patterns that exceed the
planet’s capacity to regenerate resources and absorb waste, a condition that Raworth (2017) describes as a “full world”.
Prior research shows that excessive consumption undermines personal well-being, in addition to posing environmental
challenges (Cherrier, 2025). Consumption-oriented lifestyles centred on frequent purchasing, brand engagement, and
material acquisition have been associated with feelings of empt iness and short-lived satisfaction, often resulting in
saturation, financial strain, stress, and anxiety (Lipovetsky, 1987; Gergen, 1991). Such patterns may also erode the
enjoyment derived from simple yet meaningful activities, including social interaction and everyday leisure (Thoreau,
1854). Given that overconsumption is a broad and complex phenom enon, consumer scholars have increasingly
examined its manifestation at the individual level through spec ific negative buying behaviours. Within this context,
scholars have examined excessive consumption through specific b ehavioural lenses, most notably impulsive buying,
compulsive buying (oniomania) , and conspicuous buying. Collectively, these behaviours are referred to as negative
buying behaviours (Pradhan et al., 2018). Accordingly, this paper focuses on imp ulsive buying behaviour (Cubukcu,
2025)—purchasing occurs as a result of the instant stimulus, either internal or external—and examines how it unfolds
within social media environments.
Evidence suggests that more than 50% of internet purchases are made impulsively (Zheng et al., 2019), with consumers
spending approximately $5,400 per year (O’Brien, 2018). E-comme rce environments liberate consumers from many
of the constraints associated with traditional physical retail settings, such as limited opening hours, travel time to stores
or shopping malls, and inconvenient store locations, which contributes to increased levels of online impulsive buying.
Prior research indicates that intensive internet use—particularly social media engagement—can significantly influence
impulsive consumer behaviour, especially among teenagers and young adults (Pellegrino & Shannon, 2021).

Social media environments are characterised by personalised advertising, seamless interfaces, and frictionless payment
systems, which collectively reduce deliberation and heighten consumers’ propensity for impulsive purchases, including
through credit options such as “buy now, pay later” (Fook & McN eill, 2020; Jain & Bara, 2024; Kasser & Kanner,
2004).
The emergence of social media influencers, largely driven by the widespread adoption of social media platforms where
individuals increasingly share aspects of their personal lives and attract substantial followings, has fundamentally
altered how consumers perceive digital advertising (Liu et al.,  2023). Rather than responding to direct advertising
messages, commonly referred to as paid advertisements, consumer s increasingly favour the perceived authenticity,
interaction, and relational closeness offered by influencers. As a result, followers exhibit a strong propensity to trust
influencers’ recommendations, adopt the products they promote, and engage in impulsive purchasing behaviour (Lee
et al., 2021). This dynamic highlights a notable Persuasion Kno wledge Paradox, serving as a theoretically grounded
behavioural inconsistency where consumers advertising literacy and spontaneous buying actions diverge in digital
environments.

2. Theoretical Background
2.1 Impulsive Buying Behaviour in Traditional and Digital Contexts
Impulsive buying has been widely examined as a form of excessiv e consumption driven by immediate internal or
external stimuli (Cubukcu, 2025). It is typically characterised by sudden and intense emotional urges, limited cognitive
self-regulation, and behaviour that is largely reactive rather than planned (Weinberg & Gottwald, 1982). Rather than
resulting from deliberate evaluation, impulsive purchases tend to occur spontaneously, often bypassing reflective
decision-making processes.
From a consumer behaviour perspective, impulsive buying represents a key pathway through which overconsumption
manifests at the individual level . Because such purchases are d riven by affective responses rather than need-based
considerations, they may accumula te over time, contributing to unnecessary consumption, financial strain, and
diminished well-being (Pereira Heath & Chatzidakis, 2011). Prior research further suggests that impulsive buying can
generate negative emotional outcomes, including guilt and stress, and, in more severe cases, interpersonal or financial
conflict (Beikverdi et al., 2022).
Impulsive buying has been conceptualised from multiple perspectives, reflecting the complexity of the construct. Early
research primarily examined this behaviour within the context o f physical retail environments and identified it as a
form of unplanned purchasing triggered by situational cues and characterised by limited p re-purchase deliberation
(Beatty & Ferrell, 1998). Subsequent work moved beyond purely situational explanations to incorporate psychological
and affective drivers, highlighting the role of emotions, reduced self-control, and immediate gratification in impulsive
decision-making (Rook, 1987; Rook & Fisher, 1995; Lutz, 1981). Later conceptualisations further emphasised the
experiential and hedonic nature of impulsive buying, framing it  as rapid, affect-driven behaviour in which the speed
of the decision constrains reflective evaluation of alternatives (Piron, 1991; Kacen & Lee, 2002).
However, with the progression of digitalisation, technological developments—particularly in marketing and electronic
commerce—have enabled consumers to make purchases more spontaneously. As a result, the scope and interpretation
of impulsive buying have evolved alongside these changes, pavin g the way for more recent research examining
impulsive behaviour in online consumption contexts.
The Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) model is the foundation for most investigations in this area. According to
this psychologically grounded theory, a stimulus is an external  environmental cue that carries a message. Response
refers to the results, reactions, replies, and answers of an or ganism, which is a single person (Gao & Bai, 2014). The
S-O-R model conceptualises how consumers respond to stimuli, providing a framework for understanding the complex
underlying consumer responses and decision-making (Chen & Yao, 2018).
Studies examining impulsive buying within the S-O-R framework t ypically evaluate how features of online
consumption environments shape consumer responses. In this cont ext, external stimuli—such as website
characteristics that have utilitarian and hedonic attributes, as well as online marketing campaigns—play a central role
in triggering impulsive purchasing behaviour (Habib & Qayyum, 2 017). These environmental stimuli interact with

organism-level factors, including consumers’ affective states and personality traits, which together fuel the impulse to
purchase (Olsen et al., 2016). With regard to external stimuli,  prior research has identified a range of online
environment features that can facilitate impulsive purchasing, including website security, usability, visual appeal,
interactivity, and ease of use. Th ese characteristics are enabl ed by digital technologies and are specific to online
consumption contexts. Empirical studies suggest that many such features are positively associated with impulsive
buying behaviour, although their relative influence may vary across settings and consumer groups (Zhao et al., 2022).
In addition to interface-related stimuli, online promotional st rategies—such as time-limited offers and quantity
restrictions—represent salient external cues that can further s timulate impulsive purchasing by increasing perceived
urgency and attractiveness (Luo et al., 2021).
Within the context of this study, the influencer and the content that they share on social media function as the Stimuli
(S). Followers’ affective responses and perceived enjoyment alo ng with trust when engaging with influencer-led
content represent the Organism (O), while impulsive purchasing behaviour constitutes the Response (R). This
conceptualisation is consistent with prior applications of the S-O-R framework in online consumption settings, as
originally advanced by the researchers (Eroglu et al., 2003).
To further understand the Organism (O) component of this model,  prior research has approached impulsive buying
through the lens of hedonic consumption, highlighting the role of pleasure-oriented motives in spontaneous purchasing
decisions. Hedonic consumption, often regarded as a defining characteristic of modern consumption patterns, refers to
purchasing behaviour driven not solely by functional or utilita rian considerations but by the pursuit of pleasure,
enjoyment, and emotional gratification (Akgun & Diktas, 2017). Although impulsive buying behaviour is a complex
construct, existing studies consistently demonstrate a strong association between hedonic consumption and impulsive
purchasing (Babin et al., 1994). The affect-driven and urge-bas ed nature of impulsive purchasing (Rook, 1987),
together with the pleasure—and satisfaction—characteristics of hedonic consumption, reduces consumers’ ability to
resist material desires. As a result, such consumption patterns may foster repetitive purchasing behaviour which, when
accumulated over time, contribute to overconsumption. This phenomenon has frequently been examined in relation to
hedonic goods, including food products, alcohol, tobacco, and other major product categories, and is associated with
significant environmental and social consequences, such as the depletion of natural resources, environmental pollution,
and increased global inequality (Cubukcu, 2025).
2.2 Social Media & Influencer Marketing as a Stimulus-Rich Consumption Environment
Over time, various communication channels have been employed to  engage consumers; however, in the twenty-first
century, social media has emerged as a dominant environment for  interaction between consumers and firms. Today,
social media is widely utilised by individuals and organisations of all sizes as a key promotional and engagement tool,
enabling brands to establish an online presence and cultivate o ngoing relationships with consumers (Saravanakumar
et al., 2012). Beyond its communication function, social media operates as a multifaceted platform in which users
actively express preferences and opinions about products, brands engage directly with the audience, influencers create
and disseminate branded content while building personal followi ngs, and paid promotional mechanisms coexist with
embedded social commerce features (Zhou et al., 2013). Within t he social media marketing domain, influencer
marketing has emerged as a prominent contemporary approach to m arketing (Rathod et al., 2023). Social media
influencers are typically ordinary individuals who, through the ir online presence, are able to affect consumers’ brand
perceptions and purchasing decisions. Importantly, influence is not restricted to a specific professional group; rather,
any individual with perceived credibility and visibility can shape others’ consumption-related attitudes and behaviours
(Singh, 2010). Owing to its persuasive capacity and widespread adoption, influencer marketing has been recognised
as a powerful and influential marketing practice within modern digital environments.
The rapid expansion of influencer marketing has accelerated scholarly interest in this domain. Recent industry evidence
(Dentsu, 2025) suggests that in 2026 global advertising expendi ture is projected to surp ass the historic $1.08
trillion threshold for the first time, with digital channels re presenting 68.7% (approximately $742 billion) of the total
investment. Within this digital landscape, social media advertising has emerged as the dominant force, with projected
expenditures reaching approximately $297 billion. Furthermore, the specialized influencer marketing industry itself is
estimated to be valued at $32.5 billion this year (WARC, 2026). This growth underscores social media’s central role in
shaping contemporary consumer influence and engagement.

focuses on identifying influential characteristics that enhance  influencers’ effectiveness in brand promotion (Lou &
Yuan, 2019; Chen et al., 2019). A second stream examines the po tential negative consequences associated with
influencer-generated content (Kim & Kim, 2021). A third stream investigates the effects of influencer marketing on

consumer behavioural outcomes (Jin et al., 2021). Taken togethe r, these streams suggest that while influencer
marketing has been widely examined across multiple dimensions, relatively limited attention has been paid to its role
in triggering impulsive buying behaviour, indicating a clear gap in the existing literature.
Recent literature reviews (Kanaveedu & Kalapurackal, 2022) iden tify the persuasive influence of digital influencers
on consumer behaviour as one of the most salient themes in this research stream. In particular, prior studies frequently
examine the persuasive effects o f influencer-generated content through established frameworks such as the source
credibility model and the persuasion knowledge model.
From an S-O-R perspective, existing studies identify a range of  influencer attributes that operate as external stimuli
within social media environmen ts, with source credibility recei ving particular attention (Burnasheva & Suh, 2022).
Through their profiles, influencers commonly engage in self-dis closure by sharing professional activities, personal
opinions, and lived experiences (Ki et al., 2020; Luarn & Chiu, 2015), alongside glimpses into their everyday routines.
This form of disclosure enhances perceived familiarity and rela tability, thereby shaping users’ internal affective and
cognitive states. Consequently, such influencer characteristics function as stimuli that trigger consumer responses and
behavioural outcomes (Lou & Yuan, 2019).
The majority of existing studies in this research stream emphas ise the pivotal role of influencer credibility in the
persuasion process (Kanaveedu &  Kalapurackal, 2022). In this re gard, prior research has  identified a range of
influencer characteristics, including source credibility, popul arity, authenticity, influencer type, inspirational appeal,
and perceived influence, as key factors shaping consumer respon ses. Building on these perspectives, more recent
studies have extended the analysis to parasocial interaction (P SI), highlighting its role in strengthening the relational
and psychological bonds between influencers and their followers.
Parasocial relationships refer to one-sided, illusory bonds formed between audiences and mediated figures (Bi & Zhang,
2022). Such relationships may develop with both fictional chara cters and real individuals, including celebrities and
public figures (Kurtin et al., 2018). In contemporary social me dia contexts, influencers have emerged as influential
mediated actors who, similar to traditional celebrities, exert a significant impact on consumer behaviour.
Parasocial interaction was initially conceptualised within trad itional media contexts such as television and radio,
describing relationships formed between media consumers and on- screen personalities (Horton & Wohl, 1956). With
the rise of social media, this phenomenon has evolved as public  figures and celebrities increasingly engage with
audiences through interactive digital platforms. A similar dyna mic is observed in influencer–follower relationships,
wherein influencers cultivate perceived intimacy and an illusio n of friendship with their audiences, thereby exerting
meaningful influence on consumer behaviour (Ki & Kim, 2019). Th e persistence of such parasocial bonds is largely
driven by the frequency of interaction and ongoing exposure (Sokolova & Kefi, 2019). To strengthen these perceived
connections, influencers regularly share content related to their daily lives and personal experiences (Lee & Watkins,
2016; Choi & Lee, 2019). In addition, they often engage in interactive practices, such as question-and-answer sessions,
to address followers’ inquiries regarding both personal and professional matters. This reciprocity is supported by social
media architectures—such as those of YouTube, Instagram, and Facebook—which facilitate two-way communication
through comments and engagement features. (Sokolova & Kefi, 2019; Shan et al., 2019).
These repeated interactions contribute to the development of enduring parasocial relationships, which can subsequently
shape consumer attitudes and behaviours. Over time, parasocial interaction has evolved into a more interactive form
of mediated communication involving reciprocal engagement (Horton & Wohl, 1956). Influencers provide entertaining
and informative content, while f ollowers express appreciation t hrough engagement behaviours such as likes,
subscriptions, and comments (Choi & Lee, 2019). As a result, au diences often develop a sense of familiarity with
influencers and place trust in t heir recommendations, frequently relying on their advice in a manner comparable to
consulting a trusted acquaintance when making purchasing decisions (Labrecque, 2014).
Prior research (Lee & Watkins, 2016) suggests that consumers wh o perceive influencers as similar to themselves are
more likely to develop parasocial interaction with them, which in turn is associated with brand evaluations that align
with those expressed by the influencer. From an S-O-R perspective, this indicates that influencers function as external
stimuli that shape consumers’ brand perceptions, ultimately influencing purchase-related responses.
However, with the rapid advancement of digitalisation, internet  users have become increasingly sophisticated and
aware of digital advertising tactics. Within this context, the Persuasion Knowledge Model (PKM) describes how
consumers develop and apply intuitive theories regarding persua sion, including beliefs about marketers’ motives,
strategies, and tactics. Originally conceptualised by Friestad and Wright (1994), this framework centres on consumers’
ability to detect persuasive intent, allowing them to decode and react to marketing strategies effectively (Campbell &

While persuasion knowledge has long been a central concern in a dvertising research, the unique nature of influencer
marketing necessitates a re-evaluation of these mechanisms (Mikołajczak-Degrauwe & Brengman, 2014) specifically,
evaluating the impact of influen cers requires considering addit ional variables such as attractiveness, credibility, and

Research suggests that when consumers possess higher persuasion  expertise, the effect of these influencer traits on
perceived quality diminishes. In this regard, empirical finding s indicate that the influence of an endorser’s attributes
on consumer perceptions is significantly regulated by both parasocial interaction and the consumer’s own persuasion
knowledge (Iqbal et al., 2023). Consequently, the level of consumer interaction with influencers, combined with their
awareness of persuasive strategies, plays a critical role in de termining the effectiveness of influencer marketing
campaigns.
In the context of the S-O-R framework, the interplay between these influencer stimuli and the consumer’s organismic
state relates to the behavioural  response. While high persuasio n knowledge typically acts as a cognitive barrier to
purchase, the relational intimacy of parasocial interaction often mitigates this defence mechanism. Consequently, when
consumers perceive an influencer as a trusted peer rather than a marketer, they may be less likely to critically evaluate
the purchasing impulse. This cognitive relaxation, combined wit h the structural ease of social commerce, facilitates
the transition from a mere urge to an actual impulsive buying b ehaviour. Thus, the literature suggests that influencer
marketing does not merely persuade; it frequently intercepts cognitive deliberation to trigger spontaneous consumption
(Lee et al., 2021).
3. Methodology
3.1 Research Design
This study adopts a descriptive and exploratory quantitative re search design. The primary objective is to investigate
the consumption habits of social media users and examine the pr evalence of impulsive buying and overconsumption
in the context of influencer marketing. According to Malhotra ( 1999), descriptive research  is appropriate when the
research problem is clearly structured and the goal is to descr ibe the characteristics of a relevant group, such as
consumer segments. Given the complexity of consumer behaviour, this approach allows for the profiling of respondents’
interactions with influencers, their susceptibility to impulsive urges, and the subsequent post-purchase consequences.
3.2 Sampling and Participants
The population of interest consists of individuals who actively use social media and follow at least one influencer on
social media platforms.
Sampling Method: Participants were recruited using a non-probability convenience sampling technique (Etikan, 2016).
The questionnaire was disseminated online through social media channels and online survey platforms (SurveyCycle
and SurveySwap) in order to reach a heterogeneous group of users.
Inclusion Criteria: To guarantee the relevance of the collected  data, an initial screening question was included in the
survey (Lavrakas, 2008). Only respondents who indicated that th ey currently follow one or more influencers were
allowed to complete the questionnaire.
Sample Characteristics: The study seeks to reflect a wide range  of demographic profiles. Participants are classified
according to variables such as a ge, gender, employment status, and household income, enabling comparisons across
different consumer groups.

4.2 Influencer Interaction & Platform Dominance
To capture the Stimulus (S) environment, participants were aske d about their primary social media platforms for
following influencers and their level of daily exposure. Instagram emerged as the dominant platform for 50.5% of
the sample, followed by YouTube (19.4%) and TikTok (18.4%). In terms of exposure intensity, the majority of
respondents spend significant time on social media, with 65.0% reporting between one and three hours of daily use.
While the influencer lists appear relatively curated—with 75.8% of participants following 10 or fewer influencers—
the frequency of exposure within these focused environments is consistently high.
Interaction frequency varied among the group; while only 10.7% reported “always” interacting with influencer content
(likes, comments, shares), the majority engaged on a “sometimes ” (30.1%) or “rarely” (35.9%) basis. This suggests
that even “passive” followers, who do not frequently engage in active digital communication, remain consistently
exposed to the persuasive stimuli presented by influencers through their daily browsing habits.
4.3 The Persuasion Knowledge Paradox: Trust & Scepticism
The internal state of the consumer, or the “Organism” (O) compo nent of the S-O-R framework, is characterized by a
significant tension between cognitive scepticism and affective connection. When evaluating the sample through the
lens of the Persuasion Knowledge Model (PKM), results indicate a high level of advertising literacy. A total of 87.4%
of participants reported that the presence of a “paid partnersh ip” disclosure reduces their trust in a recommendation,
with 57.3% becoming more sceptical and 30.1% reporting a significant loss of trust. Furthermore, a majority  o f
respondents indicated that they find it relatively easy to distinguish between organic content and paid advertisements.
Despite this high degree of scepticism, the data highlights the  underlying power of Parasocial Interaction (PSI).
Although 35.0% of participants reported no sense of personal connection to in fluencers, the remaining 65.0%
experienced varying levels of perceived “friendship” or connection. Specifically, nearly 40.0% of the sample reported
a moderate to high sense of relational closeness.

4.4 Impulse Buying Behaviour and Post-Purchase Outcomes
The behavioral response to influencer-led stimuli is evidenced by the frequency of immediate, unplanned purchases
within the sample. Despite the high level of skepticism reporte d in the previous section, 48.5% of participants
confirmed making at least one pur chase immediately after seeing  an influencer’s recommendation in the preceding
three months. When examining the intentionality behind these tr ansactions, 35.0% of respondents noted that while
they had previously considered the product, the influencer functioned as the primary accelerator for the final decision.
Additionally, 8.4% of purchases were described as entirely spontaneous, with no prior intention of buying.
The data further clarifies the specific external cues that are strongly associated with these internal buying urges.
Curiosity about the product was identified as the primary trigg er for 36.9% of the sample. This was followed by
promotional tactics such as discount codes or limited-time offe rs (18.4%) and the visual appeal or aesthetics of the
post (17.5%). Notably, trust in the influencer’s expertise was cited by only 10.7% of participants, suggesting that the
“Illusion of Friendship” and visual stimuli appear to be more p rominent correlates of impulsive behavior than
professional credibility.
The consequences of these impulsive responses highlight a direc t link to overconsumption and psychological
discomfort. A total of 47.6% of participants admitted to regretting an influencer-based purchase, with 4.9% reporting
frequent regret. This dissatisfaction is mirrored in the accumu lation of unused goods; 47.6% of the sample currently
own items purchased via social media that have never been used or used only once.
Finally, the study examined the resulting financial pressure. While 37.9% of respondents reported no financial strain
(Scale 1), approximately 38.0% indicated moderate to significant stress (Scales 3 to 5) resul ting from their social
media shopping habits. This finding underscores the potential f or influencer-triggered impulsive buying to move
beyond simple consumption and correlate with financial vulnerability for nearly four in ten young consumers.

5. Conclusion
This study demonstrates that influencer marketing serves as a p otent stimulus within the S-O-R framework, strongly
shaping impulsive buying behaviour among young consumers. The r esults reveal a striking “Persuasion Knowledge
Paradox” characterised by a notable behavioural inconsistency; a highly educated sampl e exhibits significant
scepticism toward paid content yet remains emotionally vulnerab le to influencer-led triggers. While 87.4% of
participants recognize and devalue explicit marketing tactics, nearly half of the responden ts engaged in immediate,
unplanned purchases in the preceding three months. This suggest s that the “Illusion of Friendship” or parasocial
interaction, may function as a p sychological pathway where affe ctive connection overrides cognitive and critical
defences. Consequently, the influencer-follower relationship is associated with a shift in the consumption environment
from a space of deliberate evaluation into one of spontaneous, reactive purchasing.
Beyond the theoretical implications, these findings highlight t he tangible risks of digital overconsumption, as
evidenced by the high rates of po st-purchase regret and the acc umulation of unused goods within the 18–34 age
segment. With 47.6% of participants reporting both regret and t he possession of items never used, influencer-driven
impulses appear to correlate with unsustainable consumption patterns and financial vulnerability.
However, this study is not without its limitations, as the expl oratory nature of the research relied on a convenience
sample of 103 qualified participants primarily located in professional and academic networks. Future research should
aim to expand the sample size a nd explore whether these pattern s hold across different cultural contexts or specific
product categories. Ultimately, these insights provide a founda tion for both consumers and policymakers to address
the ethical challenges of social commerce and promote more intentional digital engagement.
