Abstrak
Artikel ini mengulas studi empiris tentang pemasaran hijau desa wisata, karena sering kali hanya dianggap sebagai
aktivitas promosi dan bukan sebagai bagian integral dari pengembangan destinasi berkelanjutan. Studi ini
bertujuan untuk mengidentifikasi penentu utama, hasil utama, dan arah teoritis pemasaran hijau desa wisata.
Metode yang digunakan adalah tinjauan literatur sistematis dengan kriteria penyaringan empiris saja. Setelah
mengecualikan sumber konseptual, berbasis tinjauan, dan sumber non -empiris lainnya, 17 studi primer
dipertahankan. Temuan menunjukkan bahwa pemasaran hijau desa wisata efektif bila didukung oleh
keberlanjutan operasional, partisipasi aktif masyarakat, kemampuan komunikasi digital, dan kolaborasi pemangku
kepentingan. Strategi seperti label ramah lingkungan, branding ramah lingkungan, iklan lingkungan, penceritaan
komunitas, komunikasi media sosial, promosi influencer, segmentasi pengunjung, dan branding destinasi yang
transparan dikaitkan dengan kepercayaan hijau yang lebih kuat, citra destinasi yang lebih baik, niat kunjungan
dan pembelian yang lebih tinggi, niat kunjungan berulang, dan perilaku wisatawan yang lebih bertanggung jawab
terhadap lingkungan. Artikel ini berkontribusi dengan mengusulkan kerangka kerja integratif yang
menghubungkan kemampuan komunitas, proposisi nilai hijau, saluran komunikasi, mediator psikologis, dan hasil
keberlanjutan, sekaligus menyoroti perlunya pengukuran dampak longitudinal, verifikasi anti-greenwashing yang
lebih kuat, analisis tata kelola komparatif, dan konsolidasi teoretis yang lebih besar.

Abstract
This article reviews empirical studies on green marketing in community-based tourism (CBT), as it is often treated
merely as a promotional activity rather than an integral part of sustainable destination development. The study
aims to identify the key determinants, major outcomes, and theoretical directions of green marketing in CBT. The
method used is a systematic literature review with an empirical -only screening criterion. After excluding
conceptual, review-based, and other non-empirical sources, 17 primary studies were retained. The findings show
that green marketing in CBT is effective when supported by operational sustainability, active community
participation, digital communication capability, and stakeholder collaboration. Strategies such as eco-labels,
eco-branding, environmental advertising, community storytelling, social media communication, influencer
promotion, visitor segmentation, and transparent destination branding are associated with stronger green trust,
improved destination image, higher visit and purchase intention, repeat-visit intention, and more environmentally
responsible tourist behavior. This article contributes by proposing an integrative framework linking community
capability, green value propositions, communication channels, psychological mediators, and sustainability
outcomes, while also highlighting the need for longitudinal impact measurement, stronger anti-greenwashing
verification, comparative governance analysis, and greater theoretical consolidation.
Keywords: Tourism Village, Digital Marketing, Green Marketing, Sustainable Tourism, Tourist Behavior

INTRODUCTION
Community-based tourism (CBT) is commonly associated with a development model in which
tourism benefits are expected to remain in the locality, involve local residents more directly, and support
the stewardship of cultural and environmental resources (Lo & Janta, 2020). In this setting, tourism
growth is not judged only by visitor numbers or revenue, but also by whether local people participate
in decision-making, represent their place authentically, and receive tangible economic benefits (Ngo et
al., 2018a; Yang & Bi, 2024). Under such conditions, sustainability communication becomes more than
a branding exercise. CBT destinations must convince visitors that environmental and community values
are embedded in the tourism experience rather than presented as symbolic claims (Hendrayani & Zen,
2025; Kurniawati & Kohar, 2025). This pressure has made green marketing a strategic concern for CBT
destinations seeking to communicate sustainability credibly without separating the message from local
practice.
The main problem is the credibility of green claims. In the CBT context, green marketing
extends beyond eco-labels or short promotional slogans (Chin et al., 2018). It includes the design of
low-impact tourism products, the visibility of waste -management and conservation practices,
community-led storytelling, environmental interpretation, destination branding, social media
communication, and relationship-building with tourists and institutional partners (Alhomaid, 2025;
Kilipiri et al., 2023; Kurniawati & Kohar, 2025). Literature shows that green claims are more persuasive
and trusted when visitors can observe operational evidence, such as local sourcing, environmental
management, participatory governance, or distinctive community experiences (Alhomaid, 2025; Chin
et al., 2018). The evidence, therefore, suggests that green marketing serves as a bridge between what a
destination actually does and how it is represented to outsiders (Alhomaid, 2025). Its effectiveness
depends not only on persuasive messages, but also on the community’s ability to demonstrate
environmental commitment, coordinate among stakeholders, and deliver benefits that local residents
regard as legitimate (Hendrayani & Zen, 2025; Ngo et al., 2018a).
Green marketing in the context of CBT can undeniably achieve sustainability outcomes, too. For
the purposes of this review, sustainability outcomes are grouped into environmental, social, and
economic dimensions. Environmental outcomes include reduced waste, greener operations, lower
resource use, conservation awareness, and more responsible visitor behavior (Chang et al., 2025). Social
outcomes include participation, empowerment, social cohesion, local legitimacy, and the preservation
of cultural meaning within tourism development (Kurniawati & Kohar, 2025; Ngo et al., 2018a).
Economic outcomes include competitive advantage, visit or revisit intention, local income generation,
entrepreneurship, and reduced leakage from the community. This three-part definition is important
because the empirical studies do not all measure sustainability in the same way. Some focus on tourist
behavior and intention, while others focus on destination governance and community process. The
three-dimensional definition allows the review to interpret green marketing in terms of multiple forms
of value rather than reducing success to demand stimulation alone (Kurniawati & Kohar, 2025; Lo &
Janta, 2020; Silva et al., 2021).
Furthermore, the literature remains dispersed across different methodological traditions, ranging
from behavioral surveys and digital communication studies to qualitative fieldwork on collaboration
and community development. Other than that, two gaps recur across this literature. First, tourism
marketing outcomes and sustainability outcomes are often examined separately. Some studies focus on
tourist-side variables such as segmentation, trust, concern, destination image, purchase intention, revisit
intention, or environmentally responsible behavior (Alhomaid, 2025; Chin et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2026;
Lee & Jan, 2019; Sinsup & Phumsathan, 2025; Vilkaite-vaitone & Skackauskiene, 2026), whereas
others focus on community participation, collaborative marketing, CSR support, digital capability,
market design, or governance constraints at the destination level (Hendrayani & Zen, 2025; Kurniawati
& Kohar, 2025; Ngo et al., 2018a). When these strands are not synthesized together, it becomes difficult
to explain how operational sustainability, communication strategy, and psychological response interact
in community-based settings. Second, theory use remains uneven. A small number of studies clearly
draw on named frameworks such as the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), Customer Engagement
Theory (CET), Attachment Theory, or the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) model (Alhomaid,
2025; Chang et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026), while several case-based studies rely more loosely on
stakeholder, participation, collaboration, or empowerment logics (Deeprasert & Yimprasert, 2023;

accumulating disconnected findings rather than building a coherent explanation of green marketing in

Recent empirical literature also indicates that CBT-based sustainability communication should be
supported by social entrepreneurship, community empowerment, stakeholder governance, and local
resource-management capacity. Studies in Kenya, Vietnam, Iran, the Philippines, South Africa, and
Indonesia show that community awareness, local social capital, conflict management, empowerment,
and stakeholder role clarity influence whether tourism initiatives can translate sustainability narratives
into equitable and credible destination practices (Curcija et al., 2019; Ghasemi et al., 2024; Juma &
Khademi-Vidra, 2019; Musavengane & Kloppers, 2020; Nguyen et al., 2022; Rosalina et al., 2023;
Roxas et al., 2020; Van Tuyen et al., 2023). This fragmentation is the reason the topic requires an SLR:
the evidence is dispersed across methods, countries, and theoretical lenses, making it difficult to see
how green communication, local practice, and tourist response work together.
Based on this argument, the first objective of this study is to identify the green marketing strategies
most frequently discussed in relation to CBT and closely related community-oriented destinations. The
second is to explain the organizational and behavioral mechanisms through which those strategies
influence tourists, communities, and destination performance. The third is to identify which theories or
theory-informed lenses are used in the empirical studies and how they are operationalized. The fourth
is to highlight the research gaps that emerge from the empirical evidence base. The contribution of this
article is threefold. Conceptually, it positions green marketing in CBT as an embedded socio-marketing
capability rather than an isolated promotional variable. Theoretically, it connects consumer-behavior
models such as TPB and S -O-R with organizational lenses such as stakeholder collaboration,
participation, and empowerment. Practically, it offers an integrative framework that links community
capability, visible green practice, green value propositions, communication channels, psychological
mediators, and sustainability outcomes. By consolidating the empirical evidence, this review provides
guidance for destination managers, community actors, policymakers, and future scholarship.
METHODOLOGY
This review uses a systematic design based on a seed-reference corpus extracted from Scopus-
indexed articles on green marketing in community-based tourism. The initial map contained 77
references spanning tourism studies, marketing, sustainability, and community development. Because
this wider set included journal articles, book chapters, proceedings, and contextual references, an
explicit screening process was required before synthesis.
Studies were only included when they met four criteria (see Table 1 and Figure 1). First, they
explicitly addressed green marketing, sustainable marketing, destination branding, green
communication tools, digital promotion, or closely related constructs. S econd, they were directly
connected to CBT, rural or community destinations, ecotourism, or sustainable destinations with clear
implications for community-based tourism. Third, sufficient abstract or full-text information was
available to identify the research design, context, and main findings. Fourth, the study had to be a
primary empirical contribution, such as a survey, qualitative field study, mixed-methods study, case
study, model-development study with field or expert input, or another form of origin al data-based
analysis.

For each included study, the review extracted the author(s), publication year, article title, research
design, theory and/or theoretical lens, sample information, analytical approach, main findings, and
novelty. The synthesis then proceeded in two linked stages. First, descriptive reading was used to
identify dominant strategic, behavioral, organizational, and theoretical issues across the 17 empirical
studies. Second, a narrative-thematic synthesis connected those findings into broader explanatory
categories, especially the links among community capability, visible sustainability practice, digital
communication, tourist segmentation, psychological mediation, and outcomes at tourist and destination
levels. Particular attention was paid to whether the studies used explicit formal theories, relied on
theory-informed constructs, or interpreted their findings through governance and empowerment lenses.
This two-stage process preserved methodological diversity while producing a more coherent analytical
structure.

Green Marketing as Value Creation Rather Than Mere Promotion
Across the corpus, green marketing is most credible when it is tied to visible environmental
benefits rather than presented as a purely symbolic message. The early rural tourism study by Chin et
al. (2018) already treated eco-brand, eco-label, and environmental advertising as substantive marketing
tools linked to green purchasing behavior, implying that these tools matter only when tourists regard
them as relevant and believable (Chin et al., 2018). More recent empirical work strengthens this
interpretation by showing that digital communication becomes more persuasive when it is linked to
observable environmental practice, such as waste management, green technology, or destination -
branding criteria that visitors can recognize (Chen et al., 2025; Kurniawati & Kohar, 2025). In other
words, the evidence suggests that green marketing in CBT works best when communication is backed
by operational substance. The message does not create sustainability on its own; rather, it translates
enacted sustainability into forms that tourists can notice, interpret, and trust.
This finding has direct implications for community-based destinations. Community enterprises
cannot rely on sustainability claims if the destination experience, resource management, and local
participation do not support those claims (Chen et al., 2025). The mixed-methods case of Pantai Tiga
Warna shows that communication through websites, Instagram, WhatsApp, and Facebook was
strengthened by environmental management practices that visitors could associate with the destination
(Kurniawati & Kohar, 2025). Likewise, model-development and branding studies indicate that eco-
friendly markets and green destination positioning require concrete criteria,s visible practices, and
coherent product differentiation rather than generic green language (Utarasakul & Sunandha, 2021).
The empirical evidence therefore reframes green marketing as a process of value articulation grounded
in real practice, not merely a creative promotional tactic.
Collaborative and Community-Centered Marketing Capability
A recurring theme in the empirical corpus is that green marketing in CBT is inherently
collaborative. The multi-case study by Ngo et al. (2018) shows that community -based tourism
enterprises in Vietnam adopt different collaborative marketing logics, ranging from commercially
driven approaches to community-development-driven approaches and more balanced arrangements
(Ngo et al., 2018a). This finding is important because it suggests that marketing in CBT is not simply a
matter of message design or promotional skill. It is also a question of who participates in strategy, whose
interests are prioritized, and how benefits and responsibilities are negotiated among community actors
and outside partners. Marketing capability in CBT is therefore partly relational and institutional. Its
credibility depends on how successfully communities coordinate with local authorities, NGOs, private
partners, and intermediaries while maintaining local legitimacy.
Other case studies reinforce the same conclusion. The Nglanggeran village case demonstrates that
multi-stakeholder engagement and strategically organized CSR can strengthen long-term destination
sustainability by helping communities mobilize resources, add ress environmental and economic
problems, and project a coherent identity (Hendrayani & Zen, 2025). The resident-centered study of
Muen Ngoen Kong shows the issue from another angle: tourism development may generate benefits,
but unresolved ownership conflicts, uneven participation, financial constraints, and leakage risks can
weaken local support (Lo & Janta, 2020). Together, these studies indicate that community participation
is not a decorative background condition. It is an enabling capability for credible green marketing
because it shapes who has a voice, who can verify sustainability claims, and whether the destination’s
public image corresponds to local experience.
Digital Communication, Social Media, and Visitor Segmentation
The studies show a clear shift toward digitally mediated green marketing. In the Indonesian Pantai
Tiga Warna case, websites and social media channels contributed to growing visitation and stronger
repeat-visit intention when combined with visible eco-tourism practices and waste-management rules
(Kurniawati & Kohar, 2025). The Thai and Chinese new-media case similarly emphasizes that local
authorities and community eco-tourism initiatives must connect digital communication with local
economic and environmental narratives rather than treating online promotion as a detached media
exercise. These findings suggest that digital channels do more than distribute information (Yang & Bi,
2024). They shape the visibility, emotional tone, and social reach of sustainability narratives, and they

can help smaller community destinations amplify their distinctiveness when the underlying
sustainability practice is tangible.
A second digital pattern concerns audience differentiation. The literature indicates that
communication becomes more effective when content is matched to the audience's profile and behavior.
Lee and Jan (2019) show that CBT tourists are not homogeneous; they can be segmented into distinct
clusters based on environmentally responsible behavior, with different strategic implications (Lee &
Jan, 2019b). Sinsup and Phumsathan (2025) similarly develop a social-media-oriented segmentation
framework for nature-based tourism (Sinsup & Phumsathan, 2025), while Kilipiri et al. (2023) show
that Instagram influencers can shape sustainable destination choice (Kilipiri et al., 2023). Taken
together, these studies suggest that green marketing in CBT is moving away from generic destination
promotion toward segmented communication that considers tourist values, media habits, and behavioral
readiness. This shift is especially important for community destinations with limited budgets because
segmentation can make communication more efficient and more relevant.
This digital turn is consistent with newer green-marketing studies showing that social media,
digital green campaigns, eco-labels, green advertising, and clustering-based visitor segmentation can
strengthen sustainable destination positioning when they build green trust, green brand image, pro-
environmental behavior, and revisit intention (Akram et al., 2024; Gheorghe et al., 2023; Hysa et al.,
2022; Sanjaya et al., 2024; Vicente, 2024). These findings strengthen the argument that digital
marketing in CBT should not only increase exposure but also translate sustainability evidence into
credible and behavior-shaping messages.
Psychological Mechanisms Shaping Tourist Response
The quantitative portion of the empirical corpus provides a more detailed explanation of why green
marketing matters. Its dominant message is that the effect of green marketing is usually indirect.
Marketing practices influence tourist behavior through psychological mediators such as green trust,
green concern, eco-friendly attitudes, emotional value, connectedness to nature, brand engagement, and
green consumption value (Chang et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026; Silva et al., 2021; Vilkaite-Vaitone &
Tamuliene, 2024). This means that green marketing works not simply because tourists receive
information, but because that information changes how they evaluate the destination, how morally
appropriate it appears, how emotionally resonant it feels, and whether it becomes w orthy of trust
(Alhomaid, 2025). The presence of these mediating mechanisms is one of the strongest and most
consistent findings in the retained empirical literature.
Several studies also show that these mechanisms are conditional rather than universal. Chang et al.
(2025) find that eco-friendly attitudes and green destination trust help translate green marketing
communication into intention to visit sustainable destinations, while biospheric values moderate the
process (Chang et al., 2025). Alhomaid (2025) shows that green brand trust and brand engagement
mediate the effect of green marketing on tourism purchase intentions (Alhomaid, 2025). Hu et al. (2026)
demonstrate that emotional value and connectedness to nature mediate the relationship between
sustainable marketing and tourists’ sustainable consumption behavior, with green consumption value
acting as a moderator (Hu et al., 2026). Vilkaite-Vaitone and Skackauskiene (2026) add that green trust
and green concern can mediate perceptions of green marketing, with age operating as a contextual
boundary condition (Vilkaite-Vaitone & Tamuliene, 2024). Together, these studies show that tourist
response depends on both message content and the psychological architecture through which that
content is interpreted.
Theoretical Foundations Used in The Retained Empirical Studies
The synthesis shows that explicit formal theory is present, but unevenly distributed across the 17
retained studies. Only a small number of articles clearly identify a named formal theory and use it to
organize variables, hypotheses, or explanatory pathways. The clearest case is Chin et al. (2018), which
uses the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) to explain how green marketing tools such as eco-brand,
eco-label, and environmental advertising relate to green purchasing behavior in rural tourism. In that
study, TPB supplies a recognizable behavioral logic by connecting marketing stimuli to attitudes,
intention-related reasoning, and behavioral readiness . Its importance lies in this formal clarity: it shows
how green marketing can be embedded in a theory of action rather than treated as a descriptive
collection of tools (C.-H. Chin et al., 2018).

A second clear theoretical stream appears in recent survey-based work that adopts S-O-R logic or
theory-informed mediation structures. In these studies, green or sustainable marketing functions as the
stimulus; trust, concern, attitudes, emotional value, or connectedness to nature function as organism-
level internal states; and purchase intention, visit intention, or sustainable behavior function as the
response. This logic is most visible in  (Hu et al., 2026; Vilkaite-vaitone & Skackauskiene, 2026), and
it is strongly echoed by the mediator-moderator structures in (Alhomaid, 2025; Chang et al., 2025; Hu
et al., 2026). These studies operationalize theory through structural relationships among latent
constructs, typically estimated with PLS-SEM, rather than through descriptive observation alone. Their
contribution is to clarify the internal mechanisms by which green communication becomes behaviorally
meaningful.
Other theories that come up from the corpus are Attachment Theory and Customer Engagement
Theory (CET). Attachment Theory is applied to emphasize the emotional and psychological
connections that tourists develop with brands, providing a perspective for understanding how brand
engagement and green brand trust function as key mediating factors in the decision-making process
(Alhomaid, 2025). On the other hand, CET serves as an umbrella term for green marketing as an
engagement initiative that can build stronger connections with eco-friendly destinations (Chang et al.,
2025).
The qualitative and case-based studies rely less on a single named theory and more on broader
interpretive lenses. Ngo et al. (2018), Lo and Janta (2020), Yang and Bi (2024), and Hendrayani et al.
(2025) mobilize stakeholder, collaborative governance, participation, and community-empowerment
logics to explain how tourism development, green positioning, and destination communication depend
on local voice, institutional support, and negotiated legitimacy (Hendrayani & Zen, 2025; Lo & Janta,
2020; Ngo et al., 2018b; Yang & Bi, 2024). In these studies, theory is used less to estimate causal paths
and more to explain how actors coordinate, how benefits are distributed, and why community-rooted
claims gain or lose credibility. Theory use is therefore interpretive and organizational rather than strictly
positivist. This matters because CBT requires explanation at more than one level: visitor cognition is
important, but so are community processes and institutional arrangements.
The remaining studies use theory more lightly. Segmentation studies such as Lee and Jan (2019)
and Sinsup and Phumsathan (2025) do not primarily test a grand theory, but they still rely on behavior-
based and audience-differentiation logics that are theoretically meaningful for marketing practice. Chen
et al. (2025) use a prioritization framework for green destination branding, while Kurniawati and Kohar
(2025) emphasize green technology and digital communication as strategy-linked capabilities. These
studies are theory-informed even when they do not foreground a single named theory. The overall
pattern suggests that the field contains active constructs and active models, but still lacks a fully
consolidated theoretical center. In practical terms, the empirical literature already offers building blocks
for theory development, yet those blocks remain distributed across behavioral, organizational, branding,
and communication traditions.

Sustainability Outcomes and Persistent Barriers
The literature attributes several benefits to green marketing in CBT and closely related community-
oriented destinations. At the tourist level, the most commonly reported outcomes are stronger
destination image, higher green trust, more favorable attitudes, greater visit or purchase intention,
repeat-visit intention, and more sustainable consumption or environmentally responsible behavior
(Chang et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026; Kurniawati & Kohar, 2025). At the destination and community
levels, the studies suggest enhanced visibility, improved differentiation, and greater local economic
participation when marketing aligns with community practice (Alhomaid, 2025; Silva et al., 2021).
These outcomes indicate that green marketing can support both market performance and sustainability
performance, but only when communication is tied to visible operational substance and credible
community involvement.
At the same time, the empirical evidence shows that these outcomes are not automatic. Barriers
repeatedly appear in the form of weak governance, uneven participation, low digital literacy,
infrastructure limitations, financial constraints, ownership conflict, leakage risk, and the possibility that
sustainability claims may outpace actual practice (Hendrayanti & Terini, 2021; Lo & Janta, 2020; Ngo
et al., 2018a; Yang & Bi, 2024). These barriers are especially visible in the qualitative and case-based
studies, which show that community support and stakeholder coordination remain fragile when
institutional arrangements are weak. In that sense, green marketing can magnify both strengths and
weaknesses. It can help communities communicate value more effectively, but it can also expose gaps
between narrative and practice if operational capability and governance do not keep pace with the
message.
The literature further confirms that barriers in CBT are multi -dimensional rather than purely
promotional. Participation does not automatically produce empowerment, and community -based
destinations often require deliberate mechanisms for conflict management, stakeholder coordination,
capacity building, and benefit distribution before green positioning can be sustained (Curcija et al.,
2019; Gutierrez, 2023; Lee & Jan, 2019b, 2019a; Zielinski et al., 2020). This reinforces the need to treat
green marketing as part of destination governance and community capability building.

Based on the thematic synthesis, green marketing in community-based tourism can be understood
as a multi-level process that links community -side capability, visible sustainability practices,
communication strategy, tourist psychology, and sustainability-related outcomes. The reviewed studies
show that green marketing is not effective when treated only as promotion, because its credibility
depends on community participation, stakeholder coordination, operational sustainability, and
transparent communication (Kurniawati & Kohar, 2025; Lo & Janta, 2020; Ngo et al., 2018b). In this
context, green marketing becomes a bridge between what the destination actually practices and how
those practices are communicated to visitors through branding, storytelling, digital platforms, and green
signals (C. H. Chin et al., 2018; Kilipiri et al., 2023; Yang & Bi, 2024). Figure 3 presents an integrative
framework that summarizes these relationships and clarifies how green marketing operates from
internal destination capability to tourist response and final outcomes.

 Figure 2 illustrates that green marketing in CBT begins with two foundational elements:
community capability and governance, and visible green practices. Community capability includes
participation, stakeholder involvement, CSR support, and local legitimacy, while visible green practices
include waste management, green technology, local sourcing, and low-impact visitor rules (Hendrayani
& Zen, 2025; Lo & Janta, 2020; Ngo et al., 2018b). These two elements form the basis of a credible
green value proposition. This value proposition is then communicated through eco-labels, eco-branding,
storytelling, influencer communication, and social media, while also being adjusted to visitor segments
based on ecological values, media habits, and behavioral readiness (C. H. Chin et al., 2018; Kilipiri et
al., 2023; Lee & Jan, 2019b; Sinsup & Phumsathan, 2025). These communication and segmentation
strategies influence tourists’ psychological responses, including trust, concern, attitudes, emotional
value, and a sense of connectedness to nature (Alhomaid, 2025; Vilkaite-vaitone & Skackauskiene,
2026). In turn, these responses contribute to outcomes such as visit, purchase, and revisit intentions,
green behavior, and competitive advantage.
Thus, the empirical evidence supports an integrative interpretation of green marketing in CBT.
Community capability, stakeholder coordination, and visible sustainability practices shape the
destination's green value proposition (Ngo et al., 2018a). That value proposition is then translated
through branding, certification signals, digital media, storytelling, influencer communication, and
visitor segmentation (Hu et al., 2026; Kurniawati & Kohar, 2025; Lee & Jan, 2019b) . These
communication processes influence trust, concern, attitudes, emotional value, connectedness to nature,
and related internal states, which in turn contribute to visit intention, purchase intention, revisit
intention, sustainable behavior, and destination competitiveness (Alhomaid, 2025; Chang et al., 2025).
This overall chain is moderated by contextual conditions such as audience profile, age, environmental
values, digital capability, resource availability, and governance quality. Green marketing in CBT should
therefore be understood as a connected system of organizational capability, communication strategy,
and tourist psychology.
The corpus also makes clear where the evidence is strongest and where it remains thin. Green
marketing is most persuasive when it is linked to community-rooted practice, visible sustainability
signals, credible stakeholder arrangements, targeted digital co mmunication, and clearly modeled
psychological mediators (Chang et al., 2025; Hendrayani & Zen, 2025; Kurniawati & Kohar, 2025). By
contrast, longitudinal environmental outcomes, distributional equity, and cross-context comparison
remain less developed than intention-based outcomes. The stricter empirical-only screening strengthens
evidentiary consistency and makes these gaps easier to identify.

Several theoretical families stand out. The first is the consumer-behavior family, which includes
TPB, S-O-R logic, others are Attachment Theory, and CET, and the rests are related mediator -

so future theory building will be strongest when it connects the two levels in a single explanatory model.
CONCLUSION
This review demonstrates that green marketing in CBT is effective only when it is embedded in
genuine community participation, operational sustainability, and transparent communication. Across
the reviewed studies, green marketing is consistently shown not as a mere promotional device, but as
an integrated mechanism linking community capacity, visible sustainability practices, digital
communication, destination differentiation, and tourist responses. Accordingly, the strength of green
marketing in CBT lies less in the attractiveness of sustainability claims than in the credibility of their
implementation. The review also advances several theoretical implications. First, green marketing in
CBT should be understood as an embedded socio-marketing capability rather than an isolated
promotional variable. Sustainability messages become persuasive only when they are supported by
community-recognized practices and by the organizational capacity to deliver on environmental
promises. Second, tourist behavior models in this context gain stronger explanatory power when trust,
environmental concern, attitudes, emotional value, connectedness to nature, and biospheric or green
consumption values are treated as mediating or moderating mechanisms rather than periphe ral
constructs.
SUGGESTIONS
Practical Suggestions
From a practical perspective, the findings suggest that green marketing should begin with
operational integrity rather than promotional intensity. Waste management, low-impact visitor rules,
local sourcing, community participation, and transparent standards need to be established before
sustainability claims are amplified. Communication is most effective when it renders existing practices
visible, understandable, and credible to visitors. In addition, communication strategies should be
segmented and platform-specific, with messages tailored to different visitor groups and supported by
credible sustainability signals. Policymakers and supporting institutions likewise play an important role
in strengthening digital capabilities, stakeholder coordination, and e vidence-based branding,
particularly in CBT destinations with limited resources.
Theoretical Suggestions
Several priorities for future research emerge from this review. A first priority is stronger outcome
measurement. While much of the existing literature focuses on intentions, attitudes, trust, and image,
future studies should pay greater attention to observable outcomes such as waste reduction, energy
savings, repeat visitation, local spending, and leakage reduction. A second priority is expanding
comparative and longitudinal designs to determine which green marketing mechanisms are context-
specific and which are generalizable across destinations, governance arrangements, and visitor
segments. A third priority is the development of stronger anti-greenwashing verification tools, including
indicators of transparency, evidence quality, community-based verification, and consistency between
sustainability claims and operational practice. This is particularly important in CBT, where misleading
claims may damage not only destination reputation but also community livelihoods.
Overall, this review suggests that the future of green marketing in CBT depends on stronger
theoretical integration, more rigorous measurement, deeper institutional collaboration, and more
credible sustainability communication. For scholars, the field offers substantial opportunities to build
multilevel frameworks connecting community governance and tourist behavior. For practitioners, the
