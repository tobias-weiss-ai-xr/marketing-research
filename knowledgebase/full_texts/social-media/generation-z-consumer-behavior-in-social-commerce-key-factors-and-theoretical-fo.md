# Generation Z Consumer Behavior in Social Commerce: Key Factors and Theoretical Foundations

Generation Z Consumer Behavior in Social Commerce:
Key Factors and Theoretical Foundations

Petre Danci*
Bucharest University of Economic Studies, Doctoral School of Marketing, Romania
dancipetre24@stud.ase.ro

Abstract

This article analyzes the purchasing behavior of Generation Z consumers on social media
platforms. Generation Z, which includes individuals born between 1997 and 2012, is currently one
of the world's largest consumer groups. The article reviews recent academic literature to understand
what distinguishes this generation from previous ones, how they think and act online, and what drives
their purchasing decisions. Three theoretical frameworks are used to explain their behavior: the
Technology Acceptance Model, Uses and Gratifications Theory, and the Stimulus-Organism-
Response model. The article then examines six factors that influence how Generation Z purchases
on social platforms: peer influence, platform design and algorithms, relationships with influencers,
fear of missing out, the balance between emotional and rational decisions, and differences across
cultures and regions. The findings offer valuable guidance for businesses and marketers working in
social commerce.

Key words: Generation Z, social commerce, consumer behavior, influencer marketing, parasocial
interaction

1. Introduction

Social commerce has gone from a niche concept to one of the most important forces in today’s
digital retail landscape. The combination of social media and e-commerce has created a unique space
where people can discover products, interact with others, and make purchases without leaving the
app. For Generation Z, born between 1997 and 2012, this is not something they have had to adapt to,
it is the world they have grown up in. Their shopping habits are very different from those of previous
generations, as they are always connected, spend a large part of their lives on social media, and are
completely accustomed to algorithms that show them exactly what they want before they even realize
it.
According to Statista (2024), Generation Z represents approximately 25 to 30 percent of the
world’s population. In developed markets in Europe, North America, and Asia, they are entering a
stage where their purchasing power is at its peak. For companies, understanding what truly drives
social commerce behavior is no longer optional, and for researchers, it remains a topic that continues
to generate new questions. This article reviews recent academic literature to answer two key
questions. First, what distinguishes Generation Z consumers from social commerce and how do they
behave? Second, what factors influence their purchasing decisions within these platforms? The
article uses a systematic review of the literature to organize the analysis and extracts six main
categories of influence. The results are then discussed in terms of their significance for social
commerce strategy and future research directions.

2. Literature review

2.1 Understanding Generation Z: characteristics, digital natives and consumer psychology

Generation Z is generally defined as people born between 1997 and 2012 Dimock, M, (2019).
Unlike Millennials, who adapted to digital technology as it emerged, Gen Z grew entirely within it.
Smartphones, social media and always-on connectivity were ordinary features of their childhood,
this shapes how they communicate, form opinions and make purchasing decisions in ways that are
different from previous generations.
Francis, T. and Hoefel, (2019) talk about Gen Z as True Gen, a generation defined by a desire for
authenticity, if they feel a brand is being fake, they’ll usually just stop following or even worse, call
them out in the comments for everyone to see. McKinsey (2023) sustains that Gen Z chooses brands
based on social values and peer trust. To them being authentic and honest matters much more than
having a big production budget. Twenge, (2023)being the first generation to spend their entire teens
on a smartphone has had a real impact. It’s led to higher anxiety levels and a feeling that their ‘online
self' and ‘real self’ are basically the same thing.
Content consumption patterns are also unique. Djafarova & Bowes (2021) found that Gen Z relies
heavily on social media for product discovery with impulse purchases influenced significantly by
visual content. Their engagement is often described as a short attention span, but Priporas et al.,
(2017)offer a more precise description, Gen Z is not distracted, but highly selective. If content gets
their interest within the first few seconds, they engage, if not they disengage. The generation is also
mostly mobile-first, Lim & Lee (2024) found that purchasing decisions are frequently made in real
time using smartphones, often alongside other activities, creating fast commerce experiences.
Dhir et al. (2018) found that Gen Z is aware of platform data collection practices and many express
discomfort yet continue using platforms because the social cost of withdrawal is too high. McKee et
al., (2024)talk about a pragmatic paradox, on one hand, they value honesty and data privacy, yet they
comply with the platforms data collection practices. It’s a constant trade-off between their personal
values and the realities of digital life. This contradiction is the key to understanding Gen Z, any brand
or researcher that ignores this tension is going to miss the mark on how they make decisions.

2.2 Generation Z compared to previous generations

Much of the existing marketing literature was built around Millennial consumer behavior.
Applying those frameworks to Gen Z without adjustment produces strategies that don’t perform. The
two generations share a preference for peer recommendations over brand advertising, but the way
this happens differs significantly. Sokolova & Kefi (2020) show that interactions with influences
drive purchase intentions across both generations, authenticity is a significantly stronger factor for
Gen Z. An influencer who posts too many paid ads too visibly loses trust fast and that credibility
rarely recovers.
Williams & Hodges (2022) studied Gen Z attitudes toward sustainable and responsible fashion
consumption, found that Gen Z is more likely to research brand values and check for ethical practices.
Even though they have strong principles, there's a clear value-action gap, in the heart of the moment
convenience and a price offer almost always win over ethical concerns. On platform behavior, the
differences are clear, Millennials built social identities on Facebook and early Instagram, while Gen
Z are attracted to TikTok, YouTube, and Snapchat, platforms built around short-form video and
unfiltered content. This fast format has revolutionized how products are discovered and purchased.
Kaur et al. (2020)found that Gen Z adopt social commerce features faster than Millennials, they’re
tech-savvy and follow their friends lead, so they pick up new social commerce habits faster than
older generations.

2.3 Theoretical frameworks

Three frameworks provide the most useful insights for analyzing Gen Z social commerce
behavior. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), originally formulated by Davis (1989),
proposes that technology adoption is driven by utility and ease of use.  extended TAM to the context
of social commerce, adding social influence and trust as predictors. Their findings show that, for Gen
Z in particular, social influence is a stronger predictor of platform adoption than any of the individual
functional factors; it turns out that, for Gen Z, platform quality is secondary; they do not choose
platforms based on their utility, but rather choose them because they want to be where their
community is.
Uses and Gratifications Theory (UGT), developed by Katz et al (1973), say that media users
actively select channels to satisfy their specific needs including entertainment, social connection, and
identity expression. Cheung et al. (2021)applied UGT to social media marketing and found Gen Z
users are primarily motivated by entertainment and social interaction, not information. Research by
Kamboj & Sharma (2023) shows that livestreams are incredibly effective because they offer
everything in one place. They combine entertainment with actual interaction, making the whole
experience feel much more personal and engaging than just scrolling through a website.
The Stimulus Organism Response (SOR) framework, developed by (Retnosari, 2024)Mehrabian
and Russell (1974), maps environmental stimuli to internal states to behavioral responses. In social
commerce, platform features and content (stimuli) trigger emotional response of the user (organism),
producing behaviors such as browsing or purchasing (responses). Xiang et al. (2016)applied SOR to
identify parasocial interaction as the key internal state between social and visual stimuli and
unplanned purchasing. Mardhiana (2024) extended this to live-stream commerce, finding that
emotional motivation is the primary state driving Gen Z impulse buying.

2.4 Social influence and peer behavior

Social influence is a constant driver of Gen Z purchasing decisions in social commerce. Huwaida
et al. (2024 peer approval is a massive driver for Gen Z. Their research points out that Gen Z is
heavily influenced by their peers and this is the strongest indicators of whether they’ll end up buying
a product or not. Sarker et al. (2020) shows that social proof mechanisms in platform design,
including visible review, trending labels and real-time viewer counts directly impact and stimulate
Gen Z consumers. Li et al. (2020) distinguish on strong-tie influence, which is most powerful
influencer of final purchase, and weak-tie influence, which is more effective at helping discover new
products, platforms that make strong-tie activity visible to users combine both forms of influence.
Fitri et al. (2024) found that user-generated content, particularly product reviews and unboxing
videos drives Gen Z purchasing decisions more effectively than polished brand content, aligned with
the authenticity preference mentioned earlier.

2.5 Platform design and algorithmic architecture

Every design decision in a social commerce platform serves the goal of maximizing engagement
and purchasing. The recommendation algorithm is the most important element. Poh et al. (2024)
found that algorithm driven content discovery was the main factor in unplanned purchases among
Gen Z TikTok users, with products surfaced through personalized feeds regularly resulting in
purchases that were not previously planned. Li et al.(2020)found that irrelevance, information and
the perception of being advertised as the main driver of ad avoidance. Huang & Benyoucef (2013)
identified four structural layers that effective social commerce platforms must integrate: individual
identity, conversation, community, and commerce. Erobathriek et al. (2024) confirmed that
accessibility, interactivity, and perceived convenience are the features most strongly connected to
Gen Z purchase intention. Retnosari (2024) discovered that Gen Z is more interested and alerted with
deals than previous generations. Because they are always looking for a discount, a perfectly timed
notification about a sale can be the final nudge they need to stimulate the purchase.

2.6 Influencer dynamics and parasocial bonds

The relationship between a Gen Z consumer and a trusted influencer is a social bond, a one-sided
emotional connection that feels genuine despite being entirely virtual. Babu, Joseph and Aboobaker
(2024) found that parasocial interaction stimulates credibility, inspirational content, and social
presence on purchase intentions, meaning that the stronger the bond, the more powerfully an
endorsement becomes a purchase. Mardhiana (2024) found that among Gen Z live-stream shoppers,
parasocial interaction was one of the strongest predictors of impulse buying. Chetioui et al.,
(2020)showed that this bond dissolves rapidly when followers feel commercial motivation over
genuine content. This explains the growing success of micro-influencers, their smaller, more engaged
audiences generate stronger bonds and higher conversion rates per follower than celebrity accounts
Sokolova and Kefi (2020).

2.7 FOMO, urgency, and scarcity effects

Przybylski et al. (2013) defined Fear of Missing Out (FOMO) is basically the social anxiety of
feeling left out. In social commerce, this anxiety is activated through limited-time sales, countdown
timers, low-stock warnings and trending labels. Buglass et al. (2017) found that social network use
amplifies FOMO, creating a feedback loop especially relevant for Gen Z, greater social media
exposure increases awareness of peer purchasing behavior which stimulates FOMO, which drives
further platform engagement and purchasing. Tran (2021) found that fear of social exclusion
mediated the relationship between social media exposure and unplanned purchasing among high-
FOMO consumers. However, overuse of urgency mechanics produces demotivation and negative
trust effects with a generation experienced enough to recognize manufactured content.

2.8 Emotional and rational decision-making

Gan and Wang (2017) point out that value in social commerce isn't just about being hedonic, it’s
also about being fun and social, which describes most Gen Z social media sessions. Chen et al. (2019)
describes this as social window shopping, pleasure-driven scrolling that regularly converts into
unplanned purchases while the user believes they are simply browsing for entertainment. This does
not mean Gen Z purchases are irrational, Molinillo et al. (2018) found that perceived quality, price
advantage and seller credibility still determine the final purchase decision. Retnosari (2024) the flow
state is a major factor in social commerce and plays a major role between platform engagement and
purchase success. Effective social commerce for Gen Z requires a two-stage design: an emotional
first stage that sparks interest through interesting content and a rational second stage that removes
friction from the path to purchase.

2.9 Cultural and geographical differences

Gen Z social commerce behavior varies significantly across cultural and geographical
environments. Sun and Xu (2019) describe Chinese mobile social commerce as reflecting an
omnichannel lifestyle, with platforms like Douyin and WeChat that integrate messaging, content,
and purchasing into a single continuous environment. Hofstede (2001) provides a useful lens through
cultural dimensions, in societies across East and Southeast Asia, individual purchasing decisions are
more heavily influenced by group norms, sustaining the social influence mechanisms discussed
above. Hew et al. (2016), studying Malaysian consumers, found that privacy concerns had little
negative effect, with social validation over discomfort about data use, challenging the assumption
that privacy barriers are universal. Huwaida et al. (2024) found that in Indonesia, infrastructure
factors including mobile connectivity and platform availability play a stronger role than in more
developed digital markets. Djafarova and Bowes (2021), studying UK Gen Z consumers, found that
fashion and lifestyle categories generate the highest social commerce conversion rates, driven by
visual content and influencer authenticity. These variations confirm that market-specific adaptation
is essential for businesses targeting Gen Z globally (Akram et al., 2021).

3. Research methodology

This paper uses systematic literature review methodology, consistent with the approach
recommended for exploration and theoretical studies in marketing and consumer behavior research,
adopting the methodology proposed by Vladimirova et al. (2024). The review was conducted across
the following academic databases: Google Scholar, Statista, Scopus, Web of Science and
ScienceDirect. The primary search terms included combinations of 'Generation Z', 'social commerce',
'consumer behavior', 'influencer marketing', 'parasocial interaction', 'FOMO', and 'platform design'.
The inclusion criteria were peer-reviewed journal articles and conference papers published
between 2016 and 2024, with emphasis placed on studies published from 2020 onwards to ensure
currency. Some classic theoretical works outside this timeframe (Davis, 1989; Katz, Blumler and
Gurevitch, 1973; Mehrabian and Russell, 1974; Hofstede, 2001) were included because they
provided the foundational frameworks applied in more recent studies. In total, 41 sources were
reviewed and included in the analysis. I organized the findings by theme, grouping them into six
main categories that were consistent in the literature. Everything was filtered through the three most
common theoretical frameworks used to study Gen Z, which helped give the research some clear
structure. During the preparation of this paper, the author used AI writing tools to help with grammar
checks, rephrasing sentences for clarity and organizing search terms during the initial database
search. All decisions regarding which sources to include, how to interpret the findings, and how to
structure the analysis were made by the author.

4. Findings

The literature review brings out some clear conclusions. Firstly, Generation Z constitutes a truly
distinct group of consumers whose social commerce behavior cannot be explained by theoretical
frameworks developed for millennials or older consumers. The combination of their digital naivety,
their search for authenticity, and their platform-specific behaviors require specific theoretical and
empirical solutions.
Secondly, three theoretical frameworks provide the most useful analytical tools: the Technology
Acceptance Model (TAM), extended to include social influence, explains platform adoption; the
User Belief Theory (UGT) explains why Generation Z prioritizes entertainment and social interaction
over informational content on social networks; and the Generation Z Frame of Reference (SOR)
explains the emotional and dynamic nature of purchase decisions in social commerce environments.
Third, six key factors shape Generation Z’s behavior in social commerce. Social influence and
peer interaction consistently emerge as the main drivers, with subjective norms and user-generated
content having a greater impact than formal brand communication. Platform design and algorithmic
architecture act as powerful purchase stimuli, especially personalized content feeds. Parasocial
connections with influencers drive purchase conversion rates but are fragile and sensitive to the

5. Conclusions

This article applied a systematic approach to the literature to investigate the behavior of
Generation Z consumers in social commerce, identifying defining characteristics of this group, the
most appropriate theoretical frameworks for analyzing their behavior and six main factors that
determine their purchasing decisions in social commerce environments.
The main conclusion is that Generation Z does not approach social commerce as a shopping
activity, but rather as entertainment and social engagement that sometimes translates into a purchase.
The commercial element is part of the social experience. Companies that design their social
commerce presence around this reality, prioritizing an authentic community experience, credible
relationships, emotionally engaging content, and seamless purchases, are better positioned to connect
with this generation than those that apply the traditional logic of e-commerce to social media.
This study has several limitations that should be considered. The review was based primarily on
published academic sources, which may not adequately represent rapidly evolving business practices.
The literature has a documented bias toward Asian markets, particularly Chinese and Indonesian
samples, which may limit the generalizability of some findings to Western contexts. It is important
to note that given the rapid evolution of social commerce platforms, some findings regarding the
specific characteristics of these platforms may quickly become outdated.
Future research should examine the relationships between the factors identified in this study
across cultural contexts. Longitudinal studies that track Gen Z purchasing behavior as this cohort
grows older and gains purchasing power would also contribute to this field. Finally, the convergence
of emerging technologies, particularly AI-based personalization and augmented reality shopping
features, with Gen Z behavior represents a significant frontier for both academic and industry
research.
