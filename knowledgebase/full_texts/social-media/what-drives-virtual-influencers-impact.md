Abstract
Amid the influencer marketing boom, companies are increasingly shifting resources from real to virtual (computer-gener -
ated) influencers. While virtual influencers can influence consumers, what makes their content more (or less) impactful?
The current research examines whether social tie presence (i.e., appearing in photos with humans with whom they seem to
have social interactions) shapes consumer engagement. A multimethod investigation, combining automated image and text
analysis of thousands of social media posts with controlled experiments, demonstrates that social tie presence increases
impact above and beyond the mere presence of a human (i.e., without interaction). These effects are driven by anthro -
pomorphism and trust: social tie presence makes virtual influencers seem more human, which in turn makes them seem
more trustworthy and, consequently, increases engagement and choice. Supporting our theorizing, the results indicate that
the effects of social ties are stronger when the virtual influencer looks less humanlike, but are attenuated when the human
other is merely present, when the companion is another virtual entity, and when the poster is a human influencer. These
findings shed new light on consumer responses to virtual influencers, explain why social ties affect consumer perceptions,
and provide actionable insights for designing more impactful social media content.

Our work addresses these gaps by theorizing about the
presence of social ties (i.e., interaction with a human; see
Fig. 1), a visual cue beyond a virtual influencer’s appear -
ance. We propose that social tie presence makes virtual
influencers seem more human, and thus more trustwor -
thy, thereby increasing engagement. We demonstrate this
through a multimethod investigation combining image and
text analyses of nearly 10,000 virtual influencer posts with
four controlled experiments. In doing so, our work offers
concrete estimates of the magnitude of the engagement
effects and identifies boundary conditions under which the
effect of social ties is amplified or attenuated. Accordingly,
we ask: Does social tie presence in virtual influencer photos
influence post impact? If so, when and why?
This work makes important theoretical and practical
contributions. Theoretically, we extend research on social
influence and influencer effectiveness (e.g., Cascio Rizzo,
2026; Libai et al., 2025; Wies et al., 2023) to the less fre -
quently studied domain of virtual influencers and the factors
that drive consumer engagement (Zhou et al., 2024). Build-
ing on evidence that visual content strongly shapes online
engagement (Cascio Rizzo et al., 2026; Farace et al., 2026;
Hartmann et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024), we explore which
visual features matter for virtual influencers. Specifically, we
show that social tie presence makes virtual influencers seem
more human, which increases trust and ultimately boosts the
impact of their posts. In doing so, we extend research on the
role of social ties in influencer content. Prior work (Chung
et al., 2023) demonstrates that featuring strong social ties
(e.g., family members or best friends) in human influencer
posts increases engagement by making the influencer seem
more authentic. Our findings reveal a different pattern for
virtual influencers: images that portray them simply inter -
acting with a social tie boost engagement and choice.
Along the way, we contribute to research on anthro -
pomorphism (Hildebrand & Bergner, 2021; Miao et al.,
2022). Prior work has largely explained non-human agent
effectiveness through a “human  = good” lens, emphasizing
deficits in realism and trustworthiness (Gerrath et al., 2024;
Ozdemir et al., 2025). The present research moves beyond
this view by examining how non-human agents can evoke
humanlike responses through social context rather than
physical appearance. Specifically, we test whether signals
of social interaction can trigger anthropomorphism and
thereby restore trust and engagement.
Practically, our field data indicate that virtual influencer
posts featuring social ties generate nearly 21% more engage-
ment. Given that only about 15% of virtual influencer posts
depict a social tie (according to our field data), this represents
a clear, underused content strategy for marketers. Moreover,
we argue that the effect of social tie presence on engage -
ment depends on the extent to which it signals an underly -
ing social mind. Consistent with this reasoning, the positive
effect is stronger for less humanlike virtual influencers, and
mitigated when there is no genuine social interaction (e.g.,
when a person merely appears in the photo) or when the
companion is another virtual entity. Finally, we show that
social tie presence does not benefit human influencers, and
we provide comparative evidence on which features matter
for virtual versus human influencer content.

Virtual influencers
Social influence is a fundamental driver of market -
ing effectiveness. Decades of research demonstrate that
people are more likely to adopt attitudes and behaviors
endorsed by others, particularly when those others are
perceived as credible or trustworthy (Cialdini, 2001).
Consumers know that companies are trying to convince
them, so they are less likely to trust traditional ads or
brand-generated content (Wies et al., 2023). Online
reviews and social media have also made it ever easier
to see (and rely on) what others think, so others’ opin -
ions now have a substantial impact on consumer behavior
(Cascio Rizzo et al., 2023).
Reflecting this shift, many brands hire online influencers
to promote their products and services. Working with influ-
encers, however, presents challenges. Unlike owned media
(e.g., brand posts), where brands control the communica -
tion (i.e., who says what and when), influencers are harder
to control. It may be difficult to find an influencer who is
willing to work with the brand and has the desired charac -
teristics (e.g., appearance, preferences), and brands do not
always have the final say over what gets posted. Moreover,
influencers have their own image to manage, which may not
align with the brand’s vision, and misbehavior or misalign-
ment in their personal lives can spill over to damage the
brand (Leung et al., 2022a).
As an alternative, virtual influencers can overcome
most of these challenges. These computer-generated
entities maintain social media profiles, produce content,
and engage with audiences in ways analogous to human
influencers, while varying widely in their visual form and
degree of realism (Zhou et al., 2024). Some are designed
to closely resemble humans, whereas others are inten -
tionally stylized, cartoon-like, or fantastical. Despite this
diversity, many virtual influencers cultivate large follow -
ings and attract substantial brand partnerships, as Table 2
illustrates. 1 Once virtual influencers have built an audi -
ence, brands pay their management company to post
about products and services, just as a human influencer
might. For example, virtual influencer Lil Miquela’s 2020
Samsung #TeamGalaxy campaign generated more than
126 million organic views and increased brand mentions
by 12% (P2P, 2022).
1 A few virtual influencers look like cartoon characters or animals
(e.g., Barbie), but because their posts usually depict unrealistic envi -

narrower scope allows us to examine social tie presence’s effects and

By working with virtual influencers, brands can raise
consumer awareness and drive online discussions, while
maintaining control over what they post (Open Influence,
2022).2 In addition, companies can create their own vir -
tual influencers, with the exact mix of demographics and
image features they seek. Yet as companies continue shift -
ing resources to virtual influencers, a key question remains:
How effective are they, as reflected in consumer responses
to their posts?
The effectiveness of virtual influencers depends on the
extent to which followers trust them (Sands et al., 2022).
In general, people are more persuaded by others whom
they perceive as trustworthy, because trusted sources seem
sincere and motivated to provide accurate information
(Packard et al., 2016). In these settings, however, con -
sumers engage with influencers whom they do not know
personally, so establishing trust requires additional effort.
Moreover, social media often makes the artificial nature
of virtual influencers salient through biographical notes or
posts explicitly labeling them as fake, further reducing trust
(Open Influence, 2022). Therefore, when virtual influenc -
ers promote brands, their persuasion motives are especially
conspicuous because audiences recognize that they cannot
have personally experienced the product or service they
recommend.
The impact of social tie presence
This work seeks to identify ways to increase consumers’
trust in virtual influencers, thereby enhancing their impact.
Specifically, we predict that social tie presence (i.e., when a
virtual influencer appears with a human with whom it seems
to have a social interaction) makes virtual influencers seem
more human, and in turn more trustworthy, which should
increase the impact of their posts. While recent work has
examined the strength of social ties (e.g., family members
versus strangers; Chung et al., 2023), we focus on tie pres -
ence. In our context, a tie is a human interacting with a vir-
tual influencer. Importantly, we examine the effects of these
ties not for the actors involved (as prior work has done),
but for the observing audience—specifically, how seeing a
virtual influencer interact with a human can affect followers’
perceptions.

phism? Perceptions of non-human entities as humanlike, or
anthropomorphism (Epley et al., 2007), can be evoked in

Having lunch with someone, for example, is something only
humans do. Thus, seeing a virtual influencer depicted din -

if it has real human relationships) and make it seem more
human. Rather than leading viewers to question whether the
influencer is “truly” virtual, these depictions provide cues of
social interaction, prompting attributions of humanlike qual-
ities. This inference does not require observers to believe
that the interaction is spontaneous or unstaged. Social cog -
nition research shows that people routinely infer mental
states, intentions, and humanness from depicted interac -
tions, even when those interactions are known to be staged
or mediated (e.g., Reeves & Nass, 1996). What matters is
not the true nature of the interaction, but whether the scene
conforms to core expectations of human social behavior,
such as reciprocity, joint attention, and coordinated action.
Thus, even when viewers recognize that something is arti -
ficially produced, the presence of a human interaction part -
ner provides a cue that the entity participates in relational
dynamics typically reserved for humans. In this way, social
tie presence functions less as evidence of literal authenticity
than as a heuristic signal of social mindedness that observ -
ers use to infer humanness.
By making virtual influencers seem more human, social
tie presence should also increase trust. One defining trait
of humans is their capacity for thought and intention. Con -
sequently, traits or actions that make non-human agents
seem more human should increase perceptions that they can
think, evaluate, and act intentionally (Epley et al., 2007).
This, in turn, should make them seem more trustworthy.
Just as people are more likely to trust a person who seems
thoughtful, anthropomorphized non-human agents also gar-
ner more trust when they appear to have motives and judg -
ment (Waytz et al., 2014). In short, social tie presence makes
virtual influencers seem more human, which increases the
likelihood that consumers trust them.3
Finally, the more trustworthy virtual influencers seem,
the more likely consumers are to engage with and be per -
suaded by their posts. As noted, when people trust a source
more, they assign more weight to that source’s opinions;
accordingly, trust often increases persuasiveness (Packard
et al., 2016). Trust should also increase engagement: people
like trustworthy others more, and find their input more valu-
able, so they should be more likely to engage with the con -
tent these sources post (Cascio Rizzo et al., 2024).

Boundary conditions
We argue that there are situations in which the positive
effect of social tie presence on engagement is attenuated or
strengthened, depending on whether the tie provides diag-
nostic evidence  of an underlying social mind. Research
on mind perception suggests that observers rely on cues
of agency, reciprocity, and intentional social engagement
when inferring humanness (Epley et al., 2007). Accord -
ingly, social tie presence should be less effective when these
cues are ambiguous, redundant, or nondiagnostic, and more
effective when they meaningfully reduce uncertainty about
a virtual influencer’s social-mindedness. Building on this
logic, we outline two situations in which the effect is miti -
gated (when the companion is a virtual other, and when the
poster is a human influencer) and one in which it is strength-
ened (when the virtual influencer looks less humanlike).
Companion type: Human versus virtual
The impact of social tie presence depends on the nature of
the companion. Just as influencers can be human or virtual,
so can the others they appear with. If social tie presence
increases engagement and choice because it makes virtual
influencers seem more human, then its effect should depend
on companion type. A human friend is more likely to suggest
that a virtual influencer participates in real social relation -
ships and is thus more human, because a social tie implies
cross-agent reciprocity with a real person—something
observers treat as a diagnostic marker of a functioning social
mind. In contrast, a virtual companion can be dismissed as
part of the same synthetic system, offering little incremental
evidence of humanness. Thus, whereas being with a human
other should increase anthropomorphism (and thus trust and
the downstream consequences), the same presence with a
virtual other should yield weaker effects. Formally,

Influencer type: Virtual vs. human
If social tie presence boosts engagement and choice because
it makes virtual influencers appear more human and
trustworthy, then its impact should be reduced when the
poster is already known to be human. For human influenc -
ers, perceived humanness and baseline trust are typically high
(Sands et al., 2022), so additional cues of social interaction
provide little incremental diagnostic value (a ceiling effect in
mind perception and trust). For virtual influencers, by con -
trast, whose artificial nature creates inherent skepticism (Miao
et al., 2022), social ties supply missing interaction cues that
foster anthropomorphism and thereby increase trust. Thus,
the effect of social tie presence is contingent on whether the
poster is a human or a virtual influencer. Formally,
H3 The positive effect of social tie presence is mitigated
when the poster is a human (versus a virtual) influencer.
Virtual influencer humanlikeness
Virtual influencers vary in how closely they resemble real
people. Some (e.g., Rozy Gram) appear almost indistin -
guishable from humans, whereas others (e.g., Noonoouri)
are more stylized and clearly artificial. If the effects of social
tie presence operate through increased anthropomorphism,
as we theorize, they should be especially pronounced for
less humanlike virtual influencers. Because these avatars
start from a lower baseline of perceived humanness, a
social tie provides a stronger diagnostic cue, signaling par -
ticipation in humanlike social behavior and thereby elevat -
ing mind perception (e.g., Epley et al., 2007). For highly
humanlike avatars, by contrast, social tie presence adds less
incremental evidence of humanness, yielding a smaller mar-
ginal increase in anthropomorphism, trust, and downstream
consequences such as engagement. Formally,
H4 The positive effect of social tie presence is stronger for
virtual influencers who look less humanlike.
Empirical package
To test our predictions, we conducted a multimethod inves-
tigation combining a field study with controlled experiments
(see Fig. 2 for the conceptual model and Table 3 for an over-
view of the studies). Study 1 uses text and image analysis of
nearly 10,000 virtual influencer posts to examine whether
photos that include social ties receive more engagement;
ancillary analyses begin to test the hypothesized process and
highlight boundary conditions. The controlled experiments
then directly test the causal impact of social ties and explore
the hypothesized process through both mediation and mod-
eration. Studies 2–5 manipulate social tie presence, testing
whether it boosts engagement (Studies 2–5) and choice like-
lihood (Studies 2 and 5) by making virtual influencers seem

Measurements
In line with prior research (e.g., Cascio Rizzo et al., 2023,
2024), we measure engagement as the number of likes and
comments received (M = 18,597, SD = 47,312). To measure
social ties, we used the photos associated with the posts. In
total, our dataset included 19,158 photos (some posts had
more than one). To detect social tie presence, we applied
the Google Cloud Vision API’s face detection to identify
the number of faces in each photo. A test of a random sam-
ple of 100 photos indicated 100% accuracy in detecting
virtual influencers’ faces; manual checks ensured that each
photo included the virtual influencer (rather than multiple
people or a meme). Any post with at least one photo that
featured more than one face was flagged as potentially
involving a social tie. A research assistant reviewed all
flagged posts to ensure that they included both a virtual
influencer and a companion. Consistent with our definition
of social tie presence, the research assistant was instructed
to code a post as featuring a social tie only if (1) the virtual
influencer was clearly present, and (2) a human appeared
in the same image in a manner implying social interaction
(e.g., eating together, facing each other, or more generally
engaging in a joint activity). Posts with passive co-appear -
ance (e.g., a human in the background) were not coded as
social ties. The dataset also included 1,047 videos, which
a research assistant reviewed them to assess social tie
presence. These efforts identified 1,556 posts that featured
social ties ( M = 1.6).

Model
To estimate the effect of social tie presence on engagement,
we use a negative binomial regression (the dependent vari -
able is a count and is overdispersed, p < .001, likelihood
ratio test). To provide interpretable effect size measures,
coefficients are expressed as incidence rate ratios (IRRs),
so a value greater than 1 indicates a positive effect and one
below 1 a negative effect. The base model is:
Engagementijt = β1SocialTiePresenceijt + Xijt′γ + εijt,
where Engagementijt is the number of likes and comments
for post i by virtual influencer j at time t. Social Tie Presen-
ceijt indicates whether post i shared by virtual influencer j at
time t features a social tie (dummy coded). The matrix X′ijt
is the set of covariates, which include aspects of the influ -
encer, the image, the text, others’ follower count, and time
effects. Table 4 provides a summary of the controls. See Web
Appendix A for further details on the controls, and Tables
WA1 and WA2 for descriptive statistics and correlations.
Results
Consistent with our theorizing, model-free evidence indicates
that posts including a social tie in photos receive more engage-
ment (IRR = 1.699, SE = .068, z = 13.24, p < .001). Even after
accounting for all preceding control variables, posts featuring
social tie presence receive more engagement (IRR = 1.208,
SE = .047, z = 4.82, p < .001; Table 5, Model 1). Such posts are
associated with a 20.7% increase in engagement, correspond-
ing to 290 additional likes or comments, on average.

Exploring boundary conditions
We also used the data from Study 1 to test the roles of (1)
companion type (human versus virtual), (2) influencer type
(virtual versus human), and (3) the degree to which the vir-
tual influencer looks humanlike.
The role of companion type: Human vs. virtual
companion
We consider whether the companion is virtual or human. We
proposed that, if social tie presence increases engagement
because it makes virtual influencers seem more human,
then appearing with virtual (versus human) companions
should mitigate the effect (H2). To test this possibility, we
manually annotate whether each companion depicted in the
posts was a human (1,383 posts) or a virtual character (173
posts).5 While social tie presence increases engagement
when the companion is human (IRR = 1.213, SE = .050,
z = 4.71, p < .001; Table 5, Model 2), as theorized, this effect
is mitigated when the companion is another virtual charac -
ter (IRR = 1.177, SE = .119, z = 1.62, p = .105; Table 5, Model
3, Fig. 3).
Moderating role of influencer type: Vrtual vs. human
influencer
We proposed that, if social tie presence increases engage -
ment by making virtual influencers seem more human, and
thus more trustworthy, then its effect should be mitigated
when the poster’s humanness is not in doubt (H3). To test
this, we examined the moderating role of influencer type,
comparing virtual and human influencers. We worked with
the agency to acquire a sample of 22,411 Instagram posts
from 1,429 human influencers. The posts include 38,607
images and cover similar industries (see Table WA5 for
descriptive statistics and correlations). Given that there
might be systematic differences between virtual and human
influencers (e.g., in post frequency or tendency to share
videos), propensity score matching was used to identify
human influencers who were as similar as possible to virtual
influencers in terms of traits and posting behavior (i.e., text,
image, and posting time; see Web Appendix A for details).
We used all the covariates of our original engagement model
in the matching equation predicting whether the influencer
was virtual. The resulting matched sample included 19,532
posts, half from virtual and half from human influencers
(see Table WA6 for variables before and after matching).

Discussion
The field data in Study 1 provide initial support for our
theorizing. Across almost 10,000 virtual influencer posts,
we find that social tie presence prompts nearly 21% more
engagement. By including multiple controls at various lev -
els, we rule out several alternative explanations and demon-
strate the generalizability of the effects.6
Additionally, ancillary analyses provide preliminary evi-
dence that the observed effects are driven by anthropomor -
phism, while underscoring important boundary conditions.
The effect of social tie presence is mitigated when the com-
panion is a virtual other or when the poster is a human influ-
encer, and is larger when the virtual influencer seems less
humanlike. Studies 2–5 test the predicted process in detail,
along with alternative explanations, using experiments.

Method
Participants (N = 190, Prolific) were randomly assigned to a
condition in a 2 (social tie present vs. control) between-sub-
jects design. Web Appendix B provides further information
about the sample size determination, exclusions, participant
demographics, and materials for all experiments.
Everyone was shown an Instagram post endorsing a café.
Participants were told that the virtual influencer Isla Robin
posted it and that “virtual influencers are computer-gener -
ated influencers that look human but are not. They post on
social media as real influencers do.” The only difference
between the conditions was social tie presence. In the con -
trol condition, only the virtual influencer appeared in the
photo; in the social tie condition, a human friend appeared
with them (see Fig. 4). Participants were told that the com -
panion was human and were given information about which
figure was the virtual influencer.
Next, we collected measures to tap the hypothesized serial
process. Participants rated anthropomorphism on a 9-item scale
adapted from Crolic et al., (2022; e.g., “To what extent does
the virtual influencer come alive (like a person) in your mind/

all, 7 = to a great extent; α = .97). To indicate how trustworthy
the virtual influencer seemed, they used a 2-item scale adapted
from Lin et al., ( 2021; “trustworthy” and “honest”; r = .89).

they would be to engage with the post (i.e., to like it or com-
ment on it; 1 = not at all likely, 7 = very likely; Cascio Rizzo et
al., 2023, 2024; Valsesia et al., 2020). In addition, to explore
whether social tie presence affects choice, we included an item
to measure how likely they would be to choose the endorsed
café (1 = not at all likely, 7 = very likely). Finally, participants
completed two attention checks and demographic items.

Study 3: Social interaction or mere human
presence?

Study 3 provides further direct causal evidence that social

made them seem more trustworthy, which boosted impact.

robustness of the findings.

social interaction but also reduced visual salience of the
companion. However, the broader pattern across studies
(particularly the next two studies) provides convergent evi-

presence, drives the effect.

Discussion
Study 5 underscores the hypothesized underlying role of
trust through both mediation and moderation. First, consis -
tent with the prior studies, including a social tie in an image
increased virtual influencers’ impact because it made them
seem more trustworthy. Second, consistent with the notion
that these effects are driven by trust, when the influencer was
seen as human, and thus more trustworthy to begin with, the
effect was attenuated. Specifically, the effect reversed: the
presence of a social tie reduced trust for human influenc -
ers. One possible explanation is that, for human influencers,
appearing alone may signal greater autonomy or indepen -
dence (Valsesia et al., 2020), whereas the presence of others
may introduce ambiguity about motives or reduce perceived
focus on the endorsed product.
Importantly, these findings also clarify the relative dis -
advantage of virtual influencers compared with human
influencers. Consistent with prior work, virtual influencers
generally elicit lower engagement than human influenc -
ers; however, the presence of social ties meaningfully nar -
rows this gap. Furthermore, we do not mean to suggest that
anthropomorphism is the only factor that varies between
virtual and human influencers. They differ on several other
factors (e.g., having a companion may be seen as more
novel for virtual influencers) that could also contribute to
any observed effects. That said, the findings suggest that
anthropomorphism at least contributes to the observed
moderation.
General discussion
Despite rapidly growing research on human influencers (e.g.,
Barari et al., 2025; Lanz et al., 2019, 2024), we still know
relatively little about what drives consumer engagement
with virtual influencers . This research advances under -
standing of virtual influencer effectiveness by addressing
three gaps in prior literature. First, rather than emphasizing
appearance-based anthropomorphic cues (Ozdemir et al.,
2025; V olles et al., 2024), we introduce an interaction-based
lens, showing that social tie presence humanizes virtual
influencers through social interaction cues. Second, while
most studies focus on textual cues or profile images and rely
on either experiments or field data (e.g., Kim et al., 2024;
Yu et al., 2024), we adopt a multimethod approach com -
bining large-scale image and text analyses with controlled
experiments across multiple industries. Third, whereas prior
research has identified some boundary conditions through
experiments (e.g., Xia et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024), our
work quantifies these effects in the field and demonstrates
when and why social tie presence enhances or attenuates
engagement.
Specifically, our automated image and text analyses dem-
onstrate that virtual influencers’ posts prompt more engage-
ment when they appear with social ties (Study 1). The
experiments provide direct causal evidence for this effect:
including social ties in photos makes consumers more will-
ing to like and/or comment on the post (Studies 2–5) and
choose the endorsed offering (Studies 2 and 5). We also illu-
minate the sequential effects of anthropomorphism and trust
in these links. The presence of social ties increases engage-
ment by making virtual influencers seem more human, and
thus more trustworthy (Studies 2–5). Furthermore, reflect -
ing the impact of anthropomorphism, the effects are stron -
ger when virtual influencers seem less human to begin with

Theoretical contributions
This research makes important theoretical contributions.
First, more broadly, we extend work on non-human agents
such as avatars, chatbots, and other AI-generated entities by
showing that perceptions of humanness are shaped not only
by intrinsic features of the agent (e.g., appearance, voice,
or naming) but also by relational context. Prior work dem -
onstrates that design choices such as voice characteristics
(Wang et al., 2007) or naming conventions (Crolic et al.,
2022) can increase anthropomorphism and improve down -
stream responses. We show that cues external to the agent
(i.e., depicted social interaction with a human) can simi -
larly influence anthropomorphic inferences. This shifts the
focus from what the agent is to what the agent appears to
do socially, suggesting that humanness judgments are con -
structed from contextual signals of social participation. By
demonstrating that these cues increase trust and engagement,
we broaden anthropomorphism theory and clarify how rela-
tional context shapes persuasion by non-human agents.
Second, this work contributes to influencer market -
ing research by identifying interactional cues as drivers of
effectiveness. Much prior work focuses on influencer char -
acteristics, content features, or message framing (e.g., Cas -
cio Rizzo et al., 2024; Leung et al., 2022b). We show that
the social context in which virtual influencers are depicted,
and specifically whether they appear to engage in social
interaction with a human, can shape perceived humanness,
trust, and engagement. Importantly, this effect is asymmet -
ric: social tie presence increases the impact of virtual influ -
encers but not human influencers, suggesting that relational
cues compensate for a humanness deficit rather than univer-
sally enhancing persuasion. This distinction refines under -
standing of when social context matters and highlights that
identical cues can operate differently for human versus non-
human sources.
Together, these findings advance theory by demonstrat -
ing that relational cues (i.e., social ties) can elicit anthropo-
morphism, which in turn shapes the trust and effectiveness
(e.g., engagement and choice) of virtual influencers.
Managerial implications
The findings offer actionable guidance for marketers seek -
ing to design more effective virtual influencer campaigns.
Many brands already rely on virtual influencers, seeking the
unique benefits they offer (e.g., greater control). Yet virtual
influencers also create potential risks. Only 15% of consum-
ers regard virtual influencers as trustworthy, in part because
they know these influencers are not human and thus can -
not experience products in real life (Real Researcher, 2022).
Our results suggest that depictions of virtual influencers as
having social ties can increase engagement by about 21%,
on average. Considering that approximately 15% of virtual
influencers’ posts included a social tie (i.e., an interaction
with a human), this represents a clear opportunity for mar -
keters to build upon.
When social ties help and when they are less beneficial
On the one hand, our findings underscore the value of social
ties for less humanlike virtual influencers. Although some
virtual influencers are highly humanlike in appearance (e.g.,
Lil Miquela), others look distinctly artificial (e.g., Noono -
ouri). When the virtual influencer appears less humanlike,
showing a social connection with another human helps
convey humanness and can make even a highly synthetic
influencer seem more trustworthy. On the other hand, this
research also delineates boundary conditions, offering
guidelines on content composition. First, while appearing
with someone else boosts virtual influencers’ impact when it
seems the two share a relationship (e.g., posing together), it

Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science
has no effect when there is no social interaction (e.g., when
the person is just in the background or merely appears in
the picture). Second, although social ties are beneficial for
virtual influencers, there is less benefit when the companion
is another virtual character.
Virtual vs. human influencers
Ancillary analyses of Study 1 also show other features that
have different effects for virtual and human influencers (see
Fig. 9 for a summary). We argue that these differences offer
valuable insights for virtual influencers’ content strategies.
First, in line with prior research, language high in positive
valence and arousal (e.g., “this book is amazing”) receives
higher engagement (Berger & Milkman, 2012). However,
such language does not seem effective for virtual influenc -
ers. One possible reason is that highly positive evaluations
may seem less credible coming from entities that lack real
experiences with the products or services they endorse. Sec-
ond, concrete language seems more beneficial for virtual
influencers than for human ones. Because concrete state -
ments (e.g., “I am wearing this blue shirt”) convey direct
experience rather than evaluation (Packard & Berger, 2021),
they may make virtual influencers’ opinions appear more
realistic and grounded. Third, social words (e.g., family,
friends, others, group) present another interesting linguistic
feature. Although recent research suggests that such words
have negligible or even negative effects on click-through
rates (Banerjee & Urminsky, 2025), our analysis shows
that they have a clear positive impact for virtual influenc -
ers. This aligns with the idea that social words evoke social
connectedness—much like social ties in images—thereby
fostering trust and engagement. Lastly, prior work shows
that emojis generally increase engagement (Luangrath et
al., 2023), but we find that this effect is nearly five times
stronger for virtual influencers. This may reflect a consis -
tency effect, wherein paralinguistic cues such as emojis are
especially effective for entities that are themselves artificial
and digitally expressive.
Limitations and further research
This work raises interesting questions for research. First,
other nonverbal cues might encourage anthropomorphism.
As research on humanizing voices indicates, hearing some-
one’s voice can make them seem more human (Schroeder
& Epley, 2016). We do not find many examples of virtual
influencers using voice, but technological advances make
this possible, and online channels are seeing an increase
in content created by virtual influencers (YouTube Official
Blog, 2025).
Second, individual differences among followers appear
relevant. When people interact with non-human agents they
perceive as human, they feel less socially excluded (Chen et
al., 2017). Differences in followers’ need for social belong-
ing might therefore amplify the effect of social ties that
allow consumers to fulfill their social needs. People who
feel that they lack control over the objects they interact with
are also more affected by anthropomorphism (e.g., Epley
et al., 2007), because greater familiarity with how humans
(versus non-humans) behave makes anthropomorphized
objects seem more predictable and therefore controllable.
We leave it to further research to determine whether these
consumers are also more affected by social tie presence.
Third, our observational data cannot control for the
potential endogeneity arising from algorithmic selection
processes. Social media platforms, such as Instagram,
Facebook, and TikTok, select who will see each post using
algorithms designed to boost engagement. Although we
controlled for the follower counts of both the virtual influ -
encer sharing the content and the accounts tagged in posts,
we do not have data about the actual number of impressions
(i.e., views) each post generates. Such concerns motivated
our experiments, which attempted to rule out these alterna -
tive explanations.
Fourth, future research could build on these findings
by exploring contexts in which non-humanness itself may
enhance appeal. While our work focuses on situations where
humanizing cues (e.g., social interactions) increase trust and
engagement, virtual influencers’ artificial nature may some-
times be advantageous—such as when consumers value nov-
elty, fantasy fulfillment, or perceived objectivity. Relatedly,
future work could examine whether the effectiveness of
social tie presence attenuates as such depictions become
more frequent and less novel over time. Examining when
leaning into virtuality, rather than mitigating it, boosts
effectiveness would deepen our understanding of consumer
responses to synthetic agents and complement the present
work’s focus on when and why humanizing cues help.
Fifth, the existence and impact of virtual influencers also
raise some ethical considerations. Is it appropriate for virtual
and human influencers to appear together? More broadly,
should the use of virtual influencers even be allowed at all? If
they cannot experience products and services, is it ethical to
program them to claim that they like something, particularly
if such recommendations sway consumer behavior? Similar
challenges could be leveled at influencers in general, who
often endorse products and services solely in exchange for
payment. These pertinent issues deserve further attention.
Finally, note that across our studies, we observe rela -
tively low absolute ratings for engagement, a pattern that
prior work has also documented for human influencers (e.g.,

1 3
Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science
Cascio Rizzo et al., 2023; Valsesia et al., 2020; Wies et al.,
2023). Such low levels may partly reflect the survey con -
text, where consumers tend to underreport intentions to like
or comment relative to observed field behavior. At the same
time, they also underscore an important drawback of virtual
influencers more broadly: compared with human influenc -
ers, they appear to elicit less enthusiasm and interaction,
consistent with prior work highlighting their lower trust
and authenticity (Miao et al., 2022). We regard this both as
a limitation and as a meaningful boundary condition. On
the one hand, it suggests that brands should be cautious
in expecting virtual influencers to generate high absolute
engagement. On the other hand, our results demonstrate
that social tie presence can meaningfully boost engagement,
offering a pathway to mitigate this central disadvantage.
