Abstract
Social media data analytics (SMDA) now feeds directly into how firms make marketing decisions. The research on that
value, though, has grown one marketing domain at a time, and it still lacks a single firm-level construct able to capture
the phenomenon as a whole. This article develops and validates a measure of SMDA-Enabled Marketing Strategy (SEMS),
defined as the degree to which a firm’s marketing strategy, across its core strategic domains, is informed and supported
by social media data analytics. We treat SEMS as the social-media instance of a wider construct family, data-enabled
marketing strategy. The scale was built following Churchill’s (1979) paradigm together with more recent measurement
guidance: items came from a systematic review of 120 articles and from expert interviews, were purified through explor -
atory factor analysis using survey data, and were then assessed with partial least squares structural equation modeling.
The final 42-item instrument recovers five dimensions and performs well on reliability as well as on convergent and dis -
criminant validity. The findings reveal strong psychometric properties, while nomological validity is established. Beyond
the measurement tool, the study offers an integrative reading of evidence that had until now remained fragmented and
domain specific. This research contributes to both theory and practice by offering an effective tool to easily assess the
SMDA-Enabled Marketing Strategy. Future research could use this scale by applying it in different contexts, industries,
or technological environments.

This conceptual gap motivates the development of
SMDA-Enabled Marketing Strategy (SEMS), defined as
the degree to which a firm’s marketing strategy, across its
core strategic domains, is informed and supported by social
media data analytics. Reading marketing as something
increasingly steered by data at the organizational, strategic,
and operational levels is by now well established (Wamba
et al., 2017; Wedel & Kannan, 2016; Chaffey & Ellis-Chad-

The main aims of the article are: (1) To provide research-
ers and practitioners in the field of marketing with a reliable
tool that makes it easy to assess SMDA-Enabled Marketing
Strategy by covering in depth all types of marketing strat -
egies, considering their complexity and complementarity.
(2) Identify the dimensions and components that need to be
integrated to evaluate the marketing strategy activities in a
more comprehensive and in-depth way.
Three questions organize the study. RQ1: what are the
underlying dimensions of SEMS? RQ2: how can these
dimensions be operationalized into a reliable and valid mul-
tidimensional scale? RQ3: does the proposed scale demon -
strate satisfactory psychometric properties and behave as
theory predicts within a nomological network?
This article is structured as follows. We first review
the existing literature related to SMDA concepts and data
driven marketing as well as the existing scales in the studied
fields. Second, we outline our methodology for develop -
ing the SEMS scale. The third section describes the data
analysis procedures as well as the results of the validation
procedures. The fourth section discusses the theoretical con-
tributions and managerial implications of the study. Finally,
the article concludes by summarizing the main findings, dis-
cussing the study limitations, and outlining future research
directions.
2 Literature review and conceptual
development
Social media data analytics refers to the analytical tech -
niques and computing tools used to monitor, gather, and
analyze social media data so as to draw out useful pat -
terns and insights (Kordzadeh & Young, 2022). Its strate -
gic weight has drawn two fairly distinct lines of research.
One applies SMDA to a specific setting, reading com -
petitors’ promotional moves (Sahatiya, 2019), supporting
digital marketing in textiles (Almashaleh et al., 2025), or
guiding tourism choices (Sudrajat, 2024). The other treats
it more broadly, whether to support decision making in
general (Yang et al., 2022) or to bring together the SMDA
techniques, data, and platforms that matter most for market-
ing (Benslama & Jallouli, 2024). The labels have multiplied
along the way, social media data-driven decision making
(Yang et al., 2022), data-driven targeting (Upadhyay et al.,
2024), customer engagement and data capabilities (Zang et
al., 2025), social media data-driven marketing (Rolando &
Mulyono, 2024), which says something about how alive the
topic is, and also about how scattered it has become. What
runs through all of it is a steady finding that SMDA helps
marketing decisions; what is missing is any account of that
help at the level of the strategy taken whole, rather than
domain by domain.
2.1 Existing measures of social media data analytics
and their limits
Data enabled marketing and Data-driven marketing con -
cepts have been studied from several angles: organization -
ally, as a matter of building a data-driven culture (Wamba
et al., 2017); strategically, as a way of steering decisions
(Wedel & Kannan, 2016); and operationally, as targeting
and personalization (Chaffey & Ellis-Chadwick, 2022). It
carries forward older ideas: technology enabled strategies
(Roch et al., 2022), technology-based marketing, marketing
intelligence, marketing analytics, that all rest on the same
logic of putting data and technology to work for market -
ing ends (Raju et al., 2025). For all that maturity, validated
instruments are scarce, and each of the ones that exist covers
only a slice.
Gupta and George ( 2016) built a big data analyt -
ics capability scale and tied it to firm performance. It is a
useful instrument, but a generic one: it addresses big data
at large rather than social media data in particular, and it
looks at organizational resources rather than at the reach of
a marketing strategy. Fullerton et al. ( 2019) measured the
effectiveness of technology-based marketing through six
dimensions, though from the consumer’s side rather than
as a firm-level property. Varela-Neira et al. ( 2023) devel -
oped and validated a social media marketing system scale,
real progress on measuring social media marketing, yet still
short of capturing how far analytics enables strategy across
every strategic domain. Others stay with a single lever, such
as data-driven segmentation (Purnamasari et al., 2022) or
how consumers perceive a firm’s data enabled marketing
image (García et al., 2025).
Placed side by side, these scales are either partial, one
lever, or one vantage point, or adjacent, in that they are not
specific to social media data analytics. None of them mea -
sure, at the firm level and across the full span of a market -
ing strategy, how far that strategy runs on social media data
analytics. This can be explained by the lack of consensus

2.2 Toward an integrative construct
Judging a marketing strategy in full means covering every
domain it works through. Here the literature is fairly settled:
it structures marketing strategy into five domains: target -
ing and positioning; product, service and brand; commu -
nication and influence; channel and logistics; and pricing
(Armstrong et al., 2014; Campbell et al., 2020; Chebil et al.,
2025). That five-part reading squares with earlier accounts
of the marketing strategy process and has been taken up in
the data-driven marketing literature (Pandey et al., 2021;
Raju et al., 2025).
According to Nadube and James ( 2018), the market -
ing strategy process starts with a clear vision and mission
statement and ends with the target market selection and the
development of a well-defined mix marketing and position-
ing. Owomoyela et al. ( 2013) also viewed marketing strat -
egy as a set of actions aimed at ensuring a quality product
or service (product/service strategy) that is capable of meet-
ing and satisfying customer needs (targeting and positioning
strategy), offered at a fair price (pricing strategy), widely
distributed (distribution strategy), and supported by effec -
tive communication and promotion (communication/pro -
motion strategy). Benslama and Jallouli (2022, 2024) make
the further point that a marketing strategy should not be
assessed as a handful of isolated objectives, but as a process
running through all of its strategic parts. What the field is
short of, then, is not one more single-domain measure. It
is an integrative construct that captures, across these five
domains, how far a firm’s marketing strategy is enabled by
social media data analytics (Table 1). We name that con -
struct SEMS and develop its conceptualization in the fol -
lowing section.

2.3 Conceptualization of SMDA-enabled marketing
strategy (SEMS)
This research is founded on Resource-Based View theory
(Wernerfelt, 1984), according to which SMDA represents
both an informational and analytical resource that pro -
vides strategic advantages for improving the quality of
marketing decisions, and also on Knowledge-Based View
theory (Grant, 1996), given that the SMDA is considered a
source of knowledge that can enrich marketing strategies.
We define SEMS as the degree to which a firm’s market -
ing strategy, across its core strategic domains: targeting and
positioning; product, service and brand; communication and
influence; channel and logistics; and pricing, is informed
and supported by social media data analytics, as perceived
within the firm. On this definition SEMS is a graded, firm-
level property of the strategy. It is the extent to which a
firm’s marketing strategies, domain by domain, are in fact
leveraged by social media data. This follows the logic of
graded strategic constructs that are measured through the
domain-level activities in which they show up: the market
orientation tradition works this way, reading the degree to
which a firm’s practices embody an orientation off concrete
activities spread across the organization (Narver & Slater,
1990; Kohli et al., 1993).
The construct is inherently multidimensional because
marketing strategy is conceptualized as a set of distinct but
interrelated strategic domains. Its five dimensions are not
five separate benefits but five domains through which one
underlying degree of data-enablement expresses itself: a
firm whose marketing strategy is strongly SMDA-enabled
will draw on social media data analytics in targeting and
positioning; in product, service and brand; in communica -
tion and influence; in channel and logistics; and in pric -
ing alike (Benslama & Jallouli, 2022). We keep these five
dimensions because they match the established structuring
of marketing strategy set out above, which covers the strate-
gic domains comprehensively and without overlap.

lytics, which no single scale could establish, and it is not
an organizational capability, which would speak to what a

3 Methodology
We follow Churchill’s (1979) paradigm for scale devel -
opment, whose standing in marketing is well established
(Boateng et al., 2018; DeVellis, 2016; Teeluckdharry et al.,
2021), and align the procedure with more recent measure -
ment guidance (MacKenzie et al., 2011). It runs in three
phases: item development, scale development, and scale
evaluation (Fig. 1).
3.1 Item development
To fix the conceptual domain of SEMS we began with a
systematic literature review of 120 articles addressing both
SMDA and marketing strategy, chosen to hold across firm
fields and sizes. As documented by Benslama and Jallouli
(2022), this analysis identified the core marketing strate -
gies and their components, leading to the establishment of
the construct’s five dimensions, one per strategic marketing
domain (Fig. 2).
Item generation also drew on foundational treatments of
marketing strategy that remain among its most complete
operationalizations. Thus, it is worth noting that this phase
also drew on other previous research not included in our
SLR, notably: Filipe Lages et al. ( 2008), Filipe Lages and
Montgomery (2004), Chen et al. (2015), Chaudhuri (2002),

keting and information technology, who checked how well
each represented its domain; 19 items were dropped and five
reworded. A second pass by an expert practitioner added
two items and removed three. The result was an initial scale
of 47 items across the five dimensions (Table 3).

following Chen et al. ( 2015) and Mahmood and Soon

The questionnaire was administered in English to facilitate

The prompt asks respondents to rate how far SMDA use
has supported each marketing process, and those ratings
are exactly what operationalize the construct. A respon -
dent who reports that social media data analytics strongly
supports a given strategic domain is reporting, in effect, a
high degree of data-enablement in that domain of the strat -
egy. As with graded strategic constructs measured through
domain-level activities (Narver & Slater, 1990; Kohli et al.,
1993), the perceived support across the five domains is the
empirical manifestation of how far the marketing strategy is
SMDA-enabled.
A pretest with 30 marketing and data analytics profes -
sionals checked clarity and flow (Jolibert & Jourdan, 2011);
it took about ten minutes on average. In addition, to purify
our scale even more, we applied exploratory factor analysis
(EFA) and reliability tests to the preliminary data collected.
This step led to the elimination of three items: general prod-
uct, general distribution, and general price, taken from The-
odosiou and Leonidou (2003) due to their lack of clarity for
respondents, leaving a 44-item scale for the main collection
(Appendix 2). The final survey ran online, since the study
was not tied to any one place, through Google Forms (Bhat-
tacherjee, 2012). The target population consists of profes -
sionals worldwide having knowledge of both SMDA and
marketing strategy. We applied the quota sampling method
which allows to obtain a balanced and representative sam -
ple while taking into account the constraints of access to this
specific population (Andrade, 2020). In order to increase
the credibility of the results, we ensured geographical and
sectoral diversity in our sample (Boateng et al., 2018). We
defined therefore specific quotas based on criteria deemed
essential for our study, including sector of activity, company
size, country, and department (Appendices 3 and 4).
Sample size adequacy was considered prior to data col -
lection at two levels. For the structural model, the inverse
square root method (Kock & Hadaya, 2018) indicated
that 100 to 160 respondents would be sufficient to detect
a medium path coefficient (0.20–0.30; Cohen, 1988) with
80% statistical power. For the measurement model, Marsh
et al. ( 1998) show that solution stability depends less on
total sample size than on the number of indicators per fac -
tor, with three to four well-loading indicators per factor
yielding stable solutions even with modest samples; each
of the five SEMS dimensions was measured with at least
five items, exceeding this threshold. This is consistent with
PLS-SEM’s own tolerance for smaller samples, as the tech-
nique does not require multivariate normality and remains
statistically robust even when the number of items is com -
paratively large relative to sample size.
The questionnaire went to 600 professionals and returned
317 responses, 132 of them from people who reported apply-
ing SMDA in their firm’s marketing strategy. After cleaning,
removing missing values and outliers (Hair et al., 2022),
123 responses were retained for analysis. Respondents came
mostly from IT (39.4%), marketing (11.0%), and production
(10.1%) departments. Technology was the best-represented
sector (37.8%), ahead of automotive (11.2%) and education
and culture (7.4%). Most firms were SMEs (63.5%), based
mainly in Tunisia (61.5%), then France (18.0%) and Mexico
(7.6%).

3.3.1 Dimensionality and reliability
A final round of purification and factor extraction used
principal component analysis and reliability testing (using
SPSS) (Hair et al., 2018). In the first iteration two items
loaded strongly on more than one factor and were removed,
leaving 42 (Appendix 2). The second, with Varimax rota -
tion, returned a Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of 0.902,
a significant Bartlett’s test of sphericity ( p = 0.000), and a
non-zero determinant. The extracted factors accounted for
74.523% of the variance, with every communality and load-
ing clearing the usual thresholds (Hair et al., 2018), and
they reproduced the expected five-factor structure: targeting
and positioning (14 items), communication and influence (9
items), product, service and brand (8 items), channel and
logistics (5 items), and pricing (6 items). Internal consis -
tency was high for the overall construct and for each dimen-
sion (Table 4): the reliability test was applied, proving that
the internal consistency of the SEMS scale and its five
dimensions was verified by giving an excellent Cronbach’s
alpha result. Consequently, the final scale introduced in this
study offered the best fit for the model.
Because the constructs rest on single-source, self-
reported data, we checked for common method bias with a
full collinearity test (VIF < 5) (Kock, 2015) and, as a cross-
check, Harman’s single-factor test. Common method bias
was not a serious concern.
Although the reliability of our constructs is ensured, fur-
ther validation of the scale is necessary to ensure its prelimi-
nary validation within the selected sample and to support the
coherence of the scale, thus it is important to move on to the
next step of validation in order to validate the psychometric
criteria and the found results. This step is performed using
the fourth version of the SMART-PLS software. This choice
is explained by the fact that the PLS approach allows for the
simultaneous testing of nomological validity, which indi -
cates that the scale is well connected to other theoretical
constructs (consistency with the literature), and predictive
validity, which indicates that the scale has practical utility
(Hair et al., 2020).
3.3.2 Measurement model validation
PLS-SEM was preferred to covariance-based SEM given
the modest sample, the higher-order and multidimensional
structure of the construct (Hair et al., 2020). Following the
decision criteria of Jarvis et al. ( 2003) and Coltman et al.
(2008), the constructs were specified as reflective since they
are interchangeable expressions of the same domain-level
data-enablement. At the higher order, SEMS is likewise
modeled as reflective on its five dimensions: a common
underlying degree of data-enablement should lead firms to
lean on social media data coherently across all five, so that
the dimensions covary and share antecedents and conse -
quences, which is what the strong inter-dimensional correla-
tions show (Hair et al., 2022). Collinearity was acceptable,
with all item VIF values below 5 (Hair et al., 2018). Using
the consistent PLS-SEM algorithm, which suits reflective
models (Dijkstra & Henseler, 2015), indicator reliability
held with all loadings above 0.70. Composite reliability and
Rho_A above 0.70 supported internal consistency, average
variance extracted above 0.50 supported convergent valid -
ity, and heterotrait-monotrait ratios below 0.85 (Appendix
5) supported discriminant validity (Henseler et al., 2015).
Table 5 gathers these results.

To do so, we selected a well-documented variable from
the existing literature based on a detailed empirical study
(Benslama & Jallouli, 2024) that consolidated the results
of numerous previous studies on social media data analy -
sis. This variable is known as SMDA (Social Media Data
Analytics), which is operationalized in the literature in six
dimensions: Analysis Method, General Data, Specific Data,
General Platforms, Specific Platforms, and Professional
Platforms. SMDA is then an analytical practice, an input to
decision making, while SEMS is how far that input works its
way into the marketing strategy across its domains, a strate-
gic state. Having the practice does not guarantee the transla-
tion: firms differ in how much of their analytical output is
absorbed into strategic marketing decisions (Wedel & Kan-
nan, 2016; Cao et al., 2021). The stronger and more embed-
ded a firm’s social media data analytics practice, though,
the more its strategy is likely to become data-enabled across
domains. We therefore expect a positive impact and read it
as evidence of nomological embedding.
H1. A firm’s social media data analytics practice (SMDA)
has a positive impact on the degree to which its marketing
strategy is SMDA-enabled (SEMS).
We therefore report the discriminant validity between
SMDA and SEMS and treat it as a precondition for the test
itself. We tested H1, estimating a structural and external
models with SMDA as the exogenous and SEMS as the
endogenous variable (Fig. 3). Before reading the path we
established that the two constructs are empirically distinct:
the HTMT ratio between SMDA and SEMS fell below the
recommended threshold (0,748 < 0,85), so that despite their
conceptual closeness they are not redundant.
Together these results give first evidence of the nomo -
logical validity of SEMS: the construct behaves as theory
predicts, positively and significantly tied to the firm’s social
media data analytics practice while staying empirically dis-
tinct from it. We call the evidence first rather than conclu -
sive because it rests on one antecedent-side relationship.
The tougher test lies on the consequence side, in the link
between SEMS and marketing performance, which we set
out as a dedicated agenda for future research.
In this level, we started by using the lower-order construct
(LOC: first order model) to check the external model qual -
ity of the SMDA variable (internal reliability, convergent
and discriminant validity), results show that this variable
fulfills all reliability rules (Appendix 6). Then we moved
on to a higher-order construct (HOC: second order model).
The adoption of HOC is recommended by researchers, espe-
cially when the studied concept is multidimensional, and
when the objective is to test the overall impact of a multi -
dimensional concept rather than each dimension separately.
HOC enables us to capture the multidimensionality of the
variables in an integrative way, thereby strengthening the
robustness of the estimates (Becker et al., 2022; Hair et
al., 2018). To do so, we used the Bootstrapping PLS-SEM
algorithm, which uses several selected subsamples of data
to estimate the β, p, and t coefficients for each relationship
(Hair et al., 2017). The results showed that the SMDA →
SEMS path is positive and significant (t = 2.57; p < 0.01).
Furthermore, the calculated effect size (f² = 0.709 > 0.35)
confirms the practical relevance of this relationship, and the
index (R² = 0.415) shows that SMDA explains 41.5% of the
variance in SEMS. These results confirm that our scale per-
forms as expected and fits into the existing network of rela-
tionships established in the literature, thus providing strong
support for its nomological validity.
4 Discussion
The study conceptualized and operationalized SMDA-
Enabled Marketing Strategy, a graded, firm-level property
capturing how far a marketing strategy is informed and
supported by social media data analytics across its core
domains, and produced a validated 42-item, five-dimension
instrument to measure it. Several things follow. The first is
a shift in how the value of social media data can be read.
Where earlier work treated that value as a set of separate

SEMS is also distinct from its neighbors. It is not the big

does not span the full scope of a marketing strategy. More -

is positive and significant, their empirical distinctiveness
demonstrates that engaging in analytical practices is not
equivalent to embedding analytics into marketing strategy.
This finding reinforces the view that the mere availability of
analytical capabilities does not automatically translate into
strategic integration (Wedel & Kannan, 2016).
A validated construct and instrument open several lines of
inquiry. The nearest is the consequence side of the network:
placing SEMS as an antecedent of marketing performance,
agility, or competitive advantage, or as a mediator between
analytical investment and strategic outcomes. A second fol-
lows from the conceptual positioning: the instrument can
be adapted and validated for other strategic data sources,
including internal information systems (e.g., ERP, billing,
transactional, and email data), customer relationship man -
agement (CRM) systems, website and e-commerce click -
stream data, as well as industry and market intelligence,
within the data-enabled marketing strategy family, once
measurement invariance across data types is established.
For managers, the scale is a structured, quick, and consis-
tent way to gauge how far their firm’s marketing strategy is
enabled by social media data analytics, and to see where that
enablement is strongest and where it is thin which in turn
supports more deliberate investment in data-driven market-
ing (Raju et al., 2025).
5 Conclusions, limitations and future
research
This study conceptualized, developed, and validated a
multidimensional scale for SMDA-Enabled Marketing
Strategy. Its main contribution is conceptual before it is
instrumental: we conceptualize a new, integrative construct,
SMDA-Enabled Marketing Strategy, that draws previously
fragmented, domain-specific evidence on the value of social
media data analytics into a single frame; we show it to be
multidimensional across the five core domains of market -
ing strategy: targeting and positioning; product, service and
brand; communication and influence; channel and logistics;
and pricing; and we give it a first empirical operationaliza -
tion. On this reading, the scale follows from the concep -
tualization rather than standing as an end in itself, and the
construct is placed within a wider family, data-enabled mar-
keting strategy, that sets up a larger research program. Work-
ing from a systematic literature review and an exploratory
phase with professionals, the study identified five dimen -
sions matching the core strategic marketing domains, estab-
lished the scale’s reliability and dimensionality through
exploratory factor analysis, and confirmed convergent and
discriminant validity together with first evidence of nomo -
logical validity through PLS-SEM. For practice, the instru -
ment gives managers a way to evaluate and benchmark the
data-enablement of their marketing strategy in a structured,
comparable manner, and to invest more deliberately across
strategic domains.
Some limits qualify these results and mark out where the
work should go. The sample, diverse as it is across geogra -
phies and sectors, is modest against the number of items and
was drawn by quota sampling, which supports representa -
tiveness but not statistical generalization; its concentration
in a few countries narrows generalizability further. The data
are single-source and cross-sectional, and the exploratory
and confirmatory analyses were run on the same sample.
The nomological evidence rests on a single antecedent-side
relationship; the two constructs are empirically distinct
(HTMT below threshold), which we read as direct evidence
that possessing analytics practice is not the same as embed-
ding analytics into marketing strategy (Wedel & Kannan,
2016), but an additional test of nomological validity is
recommended on the consequence side (e.g., marketing
agility, marketing performance). The relatively high reli -
ability coefficients observed for the overall construct and its
dimensions are consistent with the number of items retained
per dimension, since alpha tends to increase with item count
independently of redundancy (Cortina, 1993); nonetheless,
values in this range are also commonly associated with item
overlap (Streiner, 2003; Tavakol & Dennick, 2011), and
examining item-level redundancy within each dimension
remains a useful step for future refinement of the scale.
Future work should therefore replicate the scale on
larger, independent, and more varied samples, ideally with a
separate confirmatory sample; extend the network by relat -
ing SEMS to marketing performance and other strategic
outcomes, which is the next study we plan; and adapt the
instrument to other data sources within the data-enabled
marketing strategy family, subject to tests of measurement
invariance.
The study offers careful conceptualization and a full
instrument for gauging how far a firm’s marketing strategy
is enabled by social media data analytics across every facet
