This study examines the effectiveness of civil protection mechanisms against
misleading advertisements disseminated through social media influencers,
adopting comparative analytical approach between the Jordanian and French
legal frameworks. It argues that  influencer marketing, as a technologically
driven and psychologically mediated practice, has fundamentally challenged
traditional doctrines of advertising, contractual consent, and civil liability by
exploiting virtual trust and para-social relationships.
The research demonstrates that contemporary influencer practices particularly
covert advertising, the use of visual filters, and artificial intelligence tools
generate significant risks of consumer deception, leading to defects in consent
and structural im balances in contractual relations. It critically evaluates the
adequacy of the Jordanian legal system, including the Civil Code, Consumer
Protection Law No. (7) Of 2017, and Cybercrime Law No. (17) Of 2023, revealing
key deficiencies such as the absence of a clear legal definition of influencers, the
rigidity of evidentiary rules, and the lack of procedural mechanisms suited to
the digital environment.
In contrast, the study highlights the proactive nature of the French regulatory
model, particularly the Commercial Influencer Law of 2023 and the European
Digital Services Act (DSA), which establish strict transparency obligations,
mandatory disclosure req uirements, formalized contractual frameworks, and
statutory joint liability among influencers, advertisers, and platforms. The
analysis further explores the adaptation of civil liability elements fault, damage,
and causation within the digital context, emp hasizing the growing reliance on
evidentiary presumptions to alleviate the burden of proof.

1. Introduction
1.1 Study Topic
This study centers on the novel legal and judicial challenges facing the consumer protection
framework and electronic transactions within the contemporary digital space, as manifested in the
phenomenon of influencer marketing across transcontinental social  media platforms (Aade, 2025).
Through a critical and comparative analysis, this research highlights the mechanisms of civil
protection established to safeguard consumers from misleading advertisements and deceptive
commercial practices executed by these v irtual actors (Badenas -Boldó, 2026). The core issue
crystallizes in this promotional pattern's reliance on exploiting "digital trust" and psychological
influence (Triadinda et al., 2026). This exploitation leads to the erosion of well -established concepts
and legal statuses, giving rise to what is known as "hidden or covert advertising" where commercial
promotion is seamlessly blended with spontaneous, personal daily content (Mishra & Bajpai, 2025).
Furthermore, it employs highly sophisticated technical too ls, such as visual filters, deep fake
technologies, and generative artificial intelligence, which causes a severe imbalance in contractual
equilibrium and defects the consumer's contractual intent by inducing error or silencing them
through electronic frau dulent misrepresentation (Duivenvoorde, 2025; Faruk et al., 2026; Prados -
Peña & Crespo-Almendros, 2026).
The research aims to measure the adequacy and adaptability of the general rules of tort liability
within the Civil Code and traditional consumer protection legislations to accommodate this
technological shift, establishing a direct legislative and judicial confrontation between the Jordanian
and French legal systems (Ballal et al., 2025). In doing so, the study examines the dimensions of
"legislative stagnation" in Jordan and the reliance on the scope of traditional rules established under
the Consumer Protection Law No. (7) of 2017, demonstrating the procedural obstacles that impose
the burden of complex technical proof entirely on the aggrieved consumer. Additionally, the research
elucidates how the binding authority of criminal judgments issued under the Jordanian Cybercrime
Law No. (17) of 2023 can be leveraged to definitively establish and prove civil fault (Ratmaja, 2026).
Conversely, the study examines the "proactive legislative revolution" of the French legislator, who
carved out a specialized regulat ory framework under the Commercial Influencer Regulation Law of
2023, the European Digital Services Act (DSA), and artificial intelligence regulations (Busch et al.,
2026; Duivenvoorde & Goanta, 2023). This framework enforces strict transparency obligation s
through mandatory disclosures, formalizes written contracts linked to the economic public order,
prohibits the promotion of sensitive goods, and protects minor influencers. Ultimately, it establishes
the principle of statutory joint liability between the  influencer and the advertiser, determines the

previously confined by classical advertising mediums such as print newspapers, roadside billboards,
and television or radio commercials they have today become the direct target of a novel and highly
complex pattern known as influencer marketing (Pan et al., 2025).
The economic dimensions of this modern marketing pattern lie fundamentally in its reliance on the
influencer’s digital social capital, manifested in their fame, follower base, and capacity to produce
engaging and eye-catching digital content (Hsiao & Lin, 2025). More
Importantly, this marketing feeds on the concept of virtual trust and the relationship of par asocial
interaction that develops between the influencer and their followers (Lee, 2026). Consequently, a
false sense of intimacy and spontaneous friendship is ge nerated within the follower toward the
influencer, leading them to perceive the influencer's opinions and recommendations as sincere,
fraternal advice from a caring friend, rather than a paid commercial offering aimed at achieving
lucrative ends for the advertising companies (Mehra et al., 2026).
This intricate overlap between the influencer’s personal, entertainment, and daily content on one
hand, and the hidden promotional advertisement on the other, breeds a fertile environment for
deceptive commercial practices that inevitably mislead the consumer and induce error (Padmavathy
& Khadir, 2026). This covert pattern of virtual psychological and visual steering contributes to a defect
in the consumer's contractual intent upon entering into a contract, as their behavior becomes guided
and programmed under the influence of this virtual bond, thereby stripping them of the capacity to
make a free and well-informed consumption decision (Kamasak et al., 2026).

professional influencer" within the virtual space (Mehra et al., 2026). The study further aims to
modernize the concepts of civil liability theory and adapt them to the digital environment (Ratmaja,
2026). This is pursued by finding legal solutions to the obstacles of proving fault, damage, and the

1.3 Importance of The Study
This study aims to address the phenomenon of conceptual overlap introduced by the digital
influencer into legal thought. While traditional consumer protection rules established a clear
distinction separating the producer, provider, advertiser, and consumer, the virtual environment has
erased these traditional differences (Peltier et al., 2026). The traits of a spontaneous consumer, a
professional advertiser, and a commercial broker merge within the influencer’s activity, making the
analysis and characterization of this overlap an urgent scientific necessity to determine the nature of
their pre-contractual obligations (Lee, 2026).
The significance of the study also manifests in demonstrating the inadequacy of statutory texts and
the burden of proof (Cocks et al., 2026). The judicial system faces challenges when attempting to
apply the general provisions of consumer protection laws t o newly developed electronic misleading
methods. Advertising via influencers no longer assumes an overt promotional form; rather, it
infiltrates as covert advertising blended with daily life, reinforced by technical deception tools such
as filters and artificial intelligence (Faruk et al., 2026; Prados -Peña & Crespo-Almendros, 2026). The
significance lies in understanding the capacity of the general rules of tort liability to accommodate
these patterns, and determining how the consumer can establish the cau sal link between the
influencer's virtual clout and the incurred damage, particularly given that the Jordanian legal system
lacks statutory presumptions of implied fault in this domain (Ballal et al., 2025).
These dimensions integrate with the legislative perspective and economic policyat a time
characterized by a vital need for modernization (Busch et al., 2026). While the Jordanian legislator
contents itself with relying on the provisions of the Consumer Pro tection Law and the Cybercrime
Law, the French legislator rushed to enact a proactive, specialized, and cross -border legislation to
regulate commercial influence. The legislative significance here emerges through conducting an in -
depth analytical study of this newly introduced French regulation, standing upon its specific
provisions related to advertising transparency and shared liabilityamongparties, with the ultimate
goal of providing legal recommendations that support the Jordanian legislator in drafting special rules
that ensure contractual equilibrium and safeguard the weaker party in the digital environment (Aade,
2025).

protection established to safeguard consumers from misleading advertisements via influencers on
social media platforms (George et al., 2025). It further examines how the Jordanian legal framework

and the advertiser, and what is the extent of liability fo r digital platforms themselves (as content

1.5 Study Methodology
This study relies on the descriptive -analytical comparative method as a primary analytical tool to
conduct a mutual evaluation between the legislative environment in Jordan and its counterpart in
France (Ballal et al., 2025). The methodology aims to describe the digital and marketing phenomenon
of influencers, analyze the statutory texts in force in Jordan specifically within the Consumer
Protection Law No. (7) of 2017, the rules of tort liability, and the general theory of contract in the
Jordanian Civil Code No. (43) Of 1976, as well as the Cybercrime Law No. (17) Of 2023 and compare
this national framework with the legislative and judicial system in France (Ratmaja, 2026). This
legislative and judicial comparison aims to detect areas of weakness and inade quacy within
traditional texts, and stand upon the newly introduced mechanisms in comparative law to determine
the feasibility of mapping and adopting them into the Jordanian legal system in a manner that ensures
modernizing civil protection for the digital consumer (Busch et al., 2026).

2. Literature Review
Legal studies have shown increasing interest in consumer protection within the digital environment,
particularly with the rise of influencer marketing. These studies have mainly focused on civil liability
and misleading advertising within traditional frame works. However, they have not sufficiently
examined the unique nature of this model, which is driven by psychological influence and modern
technology. They also often overlook comparative Approaches and recent legislative developments.
Accordingly, there is a need for a modern and comparative analysis to address these research gaps
(Al-Hammouri et al., 2025).
2.1 Cluster on Civil Liability of Influencers and Digital Platforms
This cluster includes studies that focused on establishing the civil liability of social media influencers
and digital platforms in the context of misleading advertisements and deceptive content. In this
regard, the study by Al-Abdallat (2024). Is considered one of the most prominent contributions, as it
examined the legal nature of influencer liability through an analysis of both general and special rules
governing civil accountability, with the aim of obligating influencers to compensate for material and
moral damages resulting from rumors and misleading advertisements. The study also addressed the
regulatory role of digital platforms in limiting the spread of such content or contributing to its
dissemination. However, this approach remained confined withi n the traditional framework of civil
liability based on fault, damage, and causation, without expanding into comparative legislative or
modern technological dimensions. In contrast, the present study advances beyond this framework
by introducing a comparat ive legislative analysis with the French Commercial Influencer Regulation
Law of 2023 and the European Digital Services Act (DSA) (Aade, 2025; Duivenvoorde & Goanta, 2023).
It further examines preventive technical obligations prior to the occurrence of damage. Additionally,
the studyuniquelyanalyzes the legal structure of influencer contracts and their connection to
economic public order (Mehra et al., 2026), while proposing the concept of statutory joint liability
among the parties involved in the advertis ing process. It also highlights modern procedural
mechanisms such as consumer protection association lawsuits and urgent judicial orders for content
blocking (Badenas-Boldó, 2026).
2.2  Cluster on Consumer Protection against Misleading Electronic Advertising
This cluster encompasses studies that addressed consumer protection from misleading electronic
advertising within the framework of traditional legislation. The study by Al -Khalaileh (2023).
represents a key contribution in this field, as it aimed to evalua te the adequacy of general rules in
the Jordanian Civil Code in addressing misleading electronic advertisements, with particular emphasis
on the theory of defects of consent especially error and fraudulent misrepresentation as tools for
protecting consumers before and after contract formation. Despite its theoretical importance, this
study did not address more complex contemporary patterns, particularly influencer marketing, which
is based on psychological influence and digital trust. Accordingly, the prese nt study distinguishes
itself by focusing specifically on this advanced advertising model through the introduction of a
comparative analysis with the French Commercial Influencer Regulation Law of 2023 (Ballal et al.,
2025), and by assessing its ability to  accommodate rapid digital transformations. Furthermore, the

This cluster represents the qualitative contribution of the present study, reflecting a shift from
traditional legal analysis toward a modern comparative approach grounded in the interaction
between national legislation and international regulatory develop ments. It involves linking the
Jordanian legal framework with European legislative advancements, particularly the French
Commercial Influencer Regulation Law of 2023 and the European Digital Services Act (DSA) (Aade,

contemporary technical and regulatory dimensions associated with influencer contracts (Mehra et

obligations related to artificial intelligence and digital media (Busch et al., 2026). It also considers the

2.4 Research Gap
Previous studies, including Al-Abdallat (2024) and Al-Khalaileh (2023), remain largely confined within
traditional frameworks of civil liability and consumer protection, focusing primarily on ex post
remedies such as compensation and defects of consent. Ho wever, they do not adequately address
the complexity of influencer marketing as a model driven by technology and psychological influence.
There is also a clear deficiency in comparative analysis with modern legislative systems, such as the
French law of 20 23 and the Digital Services Act (Aade, 2025; Duivenvoorde & Goanta, 2023), in
addition to insufficient attention to preventive technical obligations such as disclosure and
transparency (Aade, 2025; Busch et al., 2026). Moreover, these studies have overlooked the analysis
of the legal structure of influencer contracts and their relationship to economic public order (Mehra
et al., 2026), as well as the necessity of establishing joint liability among the parties involved in the
advertising process (Badenas -Boldó, 2026), and the role of criminal judgments in establishing civil
fault (Ratmaja, 2026). Accordingly, this study seeks to fill these gaps by adopting a contemporary
comparative legal approach.

traditional concepts within the general theory of obligations and consumer protection law. One of
the most prominent manifestations of this transformation is the phenomenon of digital influencers,
who have evolved from me re content creators into influential  economic actors capable of shaping
consumer preferences and directing purchasing behavior. The legal risk associated with such activity

3.1.1 The Legal Concept of the Digital Influencer
The consolidation of civil protection frameworks for consumers relies fundamentally on the discipline
and precision of the legal concepts regulating digital market participants. The problem of drafting a
precise definition for the digital influencer emerge s as the foremost jurisprudential and legislative
challenge, given that the concept of "influence" is rooted in social, psychological, and economic
sciences before entering legal thought and being formulated into mandatory provisions (Pan et al.,
2025). As a result of this overlap, the process of establishing an exclusive standard for this virtual
actor required a study of its flexible nature. This revealed a fundamental divergence in the legislative
vision between the French legislator, who adopted a speci alized approach by enacting the
Commercial Influence Regulation Law of 2023, and the Jordanian legislator, who preferred to
sufficewith the general rules and established traditional concepts within the Consumer Protection
Law of 2017 (Ballal et al., 2025).
The transition from a purely conceptual legal characterization to a functional and operational
understanding of influencer activity requires reliance on empirical indicators of influence,
engagement, and market impact.

3.2.1 The Obligation to Disclose the Commercial Capacity
Transparency represents the fundamental cornerstone and the inevitable gatewayto ensuringafree
consent, devoid of defects, for the digital consumer (Anselmo et al., 2025). Imposing the obligation
to disclose the commercial capacity of the content transcend s market regulation to directly target
the protection of consumer awareness and guarantee their right to prior knowledge of the nature of
the material they receive through their virtual screens (Cocks et al., 2026). In cyberspace, this
obligation requires tools and techniques that radically differ from traditional means of disclosure.
Comparative jurisprudence and judiciary have introduced what is known as mandatory technical tags
and clarificatory disclosures to distinguish promotional content from purely personal content,
resulting in a significant legislative and regulatory divergence between the procedural rigor of the
French legislator and the substantive generality of the Jordanian legislator (Ballal et al., 2025).The
procedural evolution in French law  manifests as a proactive and advanced model. The French
legislator did not merely rely on the general obligation of disclosure; rather, through Article (5) of the
Commercial Influence Regulation Law of 2023, a direct technical obligation was imposed upon
influencers, requiring them to explicitly denote the commercial capacity of the post (Aade, 2025).

clear, unambiguous language such as the phrase "adverti sement" or "commercial partnership"
provided that this disclosure is displayed visually or audibly, in a prominent and legible font, and

4.1 The Elements of the Influencer's Civil Liability for Damages Arising from Deception
If proving the digital influencer's breach of transparency and disclosure obligations represents the
foundation for the existence of a deceptive commercial practice, activating the remedial civil
protection mechanisms for the aggrieved consumer inherently requires examining the legal structure
of the elements of civil liability and analyzing its substantive components (Anselmo et al., 2025). This
is because the influencer cannot be obligated to redress the damage through financial compensation
without establishing conclusive evidence of the availability of the elements of liability: fault, damage,
and the continuous causal link between them (Ballal et al., 2025). The study of these elements in
cyberspace acquires a complex nature due to the non-material character of the harmful conduct, as
a jurisprudential dispute arises regarding the standard of the influencer's civil fault and how to
measure their deviation from the conduct of a prudent professional or influencer. This is especially
true with the emergence of a highly important regulatory pillar manifested in the intersection of the
civil aspect with the strict penal protection prescribed under the texts and legislation of combating
cybercrimes, which has come to play a pivotal role in facilitating the burd en of civil proof before
judicial authorities.
4.1.1 The Influencer's Tortious Fault and the Criteria for Deviation from the Conduct of a Prudent
Professional:
The element of fault constitutes the fundamental pillar and the driving force of tort liability claims
(the harmful act) against the consumer (Ratmaja, 2026). Given the absence of any direct contractual
relationship binding the public to the digital influe ncer in most marketing models, this leads to the
application of general tort liability rules as the legal framework through which compensation
judgments are obtained.
Within this context, civil fault is established when the influencer deviates from the customary course
of conduct or breaches the legislative duties imposed upon them, particularly the duty of vigilance
and economic foresight within the digital environment . Such a breach gives rise to civil liability,
especially in cases where the influencer promotes or advertises counterfeit or harmful products,
therebytriggeringliabilitydirectlyas aconsequenceof this violation(Busch et al., 2026).The complexity
of this is sue lies in the necessity of formulating a specialized legal benchmark to assess the
influencer’s harmful conduct, particularly in light of the intersection between freedom of expression
as a personal activity and the commercial professional nature of infl uencer practices. This requires
the identification of criteria governing digital deviation and its legal qualification within the
framework of civil fault, taking into account the unique nature of virtual trust exploited by influencers
in their relationshi p with their audience. In this regard, the role of criminal judiciary has become
increasingly significant especially through cybercrime legislation in shaping and establishing the
element of civil fault. A final criminal conviction acquires the authorityof  res judicata before civil

4.1.2 Damage and the Presumed Causal Relationship in the Digital Environment
The civil obligation for compensation requires, as an inevitable substantive condition, the occurrence
of material, physical, or moral damage on the consumer's end, based on a direct or constructive
causal link (Cocks et al., 2026). Material damage is embo died in the direct financial diminution
suffered by the consumer's estate, whereas physical damage is realized when the influencer
promotes herbal remedies or unauthorized dietary supplements (Lee, 2026). The scope of civil
liabilityexpands to encompass moral damage that affects the consumer's psychological state, due to
their feeling of deception and exploitation (Kamasak et al., 2026).The French trend has settled on the
possibilityof inferring the causal link through the presumption of consumer psychologi cal influence
and sway (Busch et al., 2026). This presumption turns into conclusive material evidence if it is proven
that the consumer used discount codes or referral links belonging to the influencer, as they constitute
a digital documentation of the consumer's awareness compliance under the virtual influence (Bucko
et al., 2025; Hsiao & Lin, 2025). On the level of comparative legislation, this standard in French law is
subject to highly stringent oversight that draws its principles from the theory of presume d
professional fault. When the French legislator, pursuant to the Law on Regulating Commercial
Influence of 2023, bestowed the description of an independent profession upon this category, the
objective measurement standard shifted accordingly to rise from the standard of the ordinary man
to the standard of a prudent and specialized professional. As a result of this transformation, the
French judiciary has come to characterize the influencer's fault as an abstract, material fault that is
established upon the mere proof of deficiencyin technical transparency measures, without requiring
evidence of intentional intent or bad faith to deceive.
The French approach mandates an obligation to exercise strict care upon influencers with broad
target audiences to ensure the source of the products. In the event that they broadcast promotional
materials concerning counterfeit or harmful goods, civil liability arises against them automaticallydue
to their breach of the duty of vigilance and economic foresight imposed within the virtual
environment.In contrast to this French development, the influencer's tortious fault in the Jordanian
legal system relies on the provisions of the general text of Article (256) of the Civil Code No. (43) of
1976, which establishes the principle that every injury caused to another binds the doer to make
amends. Given the absence of specific legislative regulation for the catego ry of digital influencers in
the Consumer Protection Law No. (7) of 2017, the Jordanian judiciary resorts to the traditional
standard of fault, whereby the influencer's conduct is measured against the behavior of an ordinary,
prudent advertiser or provider  placed in the same external circumstances. The material
manifestation of this fault is exemplified in broadcasting false, untruthful, or incomplete data that
automatically leads to misleading and deceiving the consumer in accordance with Article (8/A) of the
Consumer Protection Law.

4.1.3 Damage and the Presumed Causal Relationship in the Digital Environment
The civil obligation for compensation does not arise against the digital influencer upon the mere
proof of infringement or tortious deviation in their conduct; rather, it requires, as an inevitable
substantive condition, the occurrence of material, physical, or moral damage on the consumer's end,
based on adirect or constructivecausal link that attributes the harmful resultto that deceptive
conduct and nothing else. Civil compensation coexists with the damage in its existence and non -
existence, and its regulatory purpose is directed toward restoring financial balance and suppressing
the effects arising from forms of cyber deception. However, the adaptation of the elements of
damage and causality in virtual space generates complexities due to technological mediation and the
multiplicity of actors involved in content creation. This places the consumer  before a procedural
obstacle that forces them to prove that their contractual choice and psychological submission were
the product of the virtual influence and spiritual authorityexerted by the influencer through
smartphone screens (Lim et al., 2025).
The forms of consumer damage resulting from digital fraud vary to affect the vital and legitimate
interests of the consumer in their material, physical, and moral dimensions. Material damage is
embodied in the direct financial diminution suffered by the consumer's estate as a result of spending
their savings on acquiringauseless or low -value commoditycompared to the pricepaid, driven bythe
influencer's false data. Conversely, physical and health damage is realized when the influencer
promotes cosmeticproduc ts, herbal remedies, or unauthorized dietarysupplements, leadingto
theviolation of the recipient's physical integrity or their affliction with chronic diseases. The scope of
civil liability expands to encompass moral damage that affects the consumer's psyc hological and
emotional state, due to their feeling of deception and exploitation resulting from the defect in their
will and the compromise of their virtual contractual awareness. Legal and practical complexities arise
from the attempt to applythe element s of compensation and the concept of the causal link to the
reality of virtual marketing. Originally, pursuant to the established rules in Article (266) of the
Jordanian Civil Code, damages are assessed to the extent of the harm suffered by the aggrieved
partyand their lost profit, provided that the damage is a natural and direct result of the harmful act.

4.2.1 Joint and Several Liability of the Advertising Process Parties
The mechanism of redressing damage through compensation is considered the fundamental basis
and ultimate objective of the consumer civil protection system. This is because acknowledging the
occurrence of the influencer's fault and defining the features of their damages remain devoid of
realistic effectiveness unless they are based on a flexible procedural path that shields the consumer
from the risks of the influencer's insolvency or the hiding of transnational companies behind
cyberspace. The scope of this  dilemma expands within the digital environment as a result of the
overlap and multiplicity of actors involved in manufacturing and broadcasting promotional material.
This generates a clear legislative divergence between French legislation based on mandato ry legal
joint and several liability to protect the weaker party and Jordanian legislative policy, which still
restricts the rules of joint liability and subordinates them to the provisions of tortious liability for
indemnity according to discretionary authority (Busch et al., 2020). Within this framework, the 2023
French regulation introduces an advanced model by imposing joint and several liability by operation
of law. Article (8) thereof resolved the dispute by decreeing automatic and direct joint liability among
the commercial influence practitioner, their business agent, and the advertising company in favor of
the consumer aggrieved by misleading advertisements. The regulatory purpose of this provision is
directed toward surrounding the consumer with ab solute financial solvencythat empowers them to
sue the influencer individually and recover full compensation for material or physical damages,
without being confronted with the defense of division or the referral of litigation to the original
producing company. This approach lifts the hardship of tracking foreign and covert entities off the
consumer's shoulders and places the consequences of investment risks upon professionals and
influencers as the most financially and technically capable party in the virt ual marketplace (Loos &
Luzak, 2016).
In contrast to the legislative and technical system in France, the Jordanian legislator adopts a different
position emanating from the traditional provisions of the harmful act. The Jordanian civil system
establishes, as a general rule, the impermissibilit y of presuming joint liability among those
responsible for damage unless based on an explicit legal text or a prior contractual agreement.
Indeed, examining Article (265) of the Jordanian Civil Code No. (43) of 1976, we find it explicitly states
that if those responsible for a harmful act are multiple, each of them is liable in proportion to their

text in the context of digital disputes, it becomes evident that the general rule in Jordanian law, when
there are multiple contributors to consumer damage  (the influencer and the advertising company),
is proportional liability according to the roleof each partyin causingthe damage. Meanwhile, joint and
several liabilityis not byoperation of law but is a purely optional choice subject to the court's
discretionary authority. This imposes a burden upon the Jordanian consumer to prove the
conjunction of the influencer's fault with the company's fault to convince the judge to rule jointly and
severally an arduous proof that mayend in depriving the consumer of rec overing their right if
proportional liability is ruled and the influencer's insolvency is revealed, or if it proves impossible to
serve notice to the cross-border producing company. The texts of the Jordanian Consumer Protection
Law No. (7) of 2017 do not aid in addressing this deficiency, as the specialized framework fails to
establish automatic joint liability due to the incompatibility of its provisions with the nature of the
activity. Although Article (20) of the Jordanian Consumer Protection Law recogn ized a form of joint
and several liability, it restricted this effect among providers when they are aware of or cause a defect
in the  commodity. This is a text whose scope is too narrow to apply to the influencer, who is
characterized jurisprudentially as an intermediary advertiser rather than a provider who directly
engaged in the sale or manufacturing process. In light of this fragment ation and the absence of
automatic joint liability in the laws, influencers in Jordan are granted a broad application scope to
evade redressing damages by pleading that the responsibility for verifying the commodity's quality
and legality rests solely on t he advertising company. This empties civil protection of its actual
substance and leaves the consumer's consent and will at the mercy of cross -border promotional
contracts that lack direct procedural guarantees imposed jointly and severally by operation of  law,
as is the case in French legislation.
4.2.2 Procedural Protection for Consumers and the Liability of Social Media Platforms
Substantive and joint rules of liability remain devoid of their realistic effectiveness unless supported
by a procedural structure characterized by flexibility and immediate responsiveness. This is because
the continuous display of misleading advertising m aterials across social media networks generates
consecutive and cumulative consumer damages. Hence, the legal importance of deterrent procedural
means capable of halting fraud emerges, particularly by activating the mechanism of collective
lawsuits for consumer protection associations, granting the expedited judiciary the authority to issue
immediate content removal orders, as well as establishing the secondary liability of digital platforms
hosting this activity, confronting the emerging technical deceptio n powered by generative artificial
intelligence techniques, and eliminating the obstacles of cross-border jurisdiction (Busch et al., 2020;
Duivenvoorde & Goanta, 2023).
When measuring the procedural characteristics of collective protection, the clear difference between
the development of the French approach and the deficiency of the Jordanian system becomes
manifest. While French legislative policy, in integration with Eu ropean directives, adopts granting
consumer associations the authority of collective litigation to ban deceptive practices and delete
misleading videos on behalf of the public, French laws have granted the competent administrative
bodies, such as the Direc torate-General for Competition, Consumer Affairs and Fraud Control

Jordanian and French frameworks. In Article (21) of the Consumer Protection Law No. (7) of 2017,
the Jordanian legislator establishes a definitive principle dictating the nullity of any agreement or
clause that cancels or restricts consumer rights provided for in the law, or exempts the provider from
liability for their obligations. This is a substantive text that carries highly significant procedural value
before trial courts, as it blocks the attempts of influencers and companies to evade accountability by
placing silent digital disclaimers within their accounts (such as the influencer's claim that the
purchase is at the follower's sole risk). These clauses are absolutely null and void and do not prevent
their judicial prosecution for compensation. This substantive deterrent integrates with an advanced
procedural capacity granted by Article (15) of the same law to accredited consumer protection
associations, aiming to enable them to defend the collective interests of consumers,  as the article
explicitlyauthorizes them to file lawsuits on behalf of consumers or to intervene in lawsuits filed by
them, provided they adhere to Article (23), which prohibits these associations from receiving any
financial aid or material benefits from providers or advertisers to ensure their neutrality and integrity
when prosecuting influencer deception (Busch et al., 2020).
Conversely, the procedural role in Jordanian legislation is characterized by a practical deficiency
arising from the material tendencyof traditional texts. In Article (12) of the Consumer Protection Law
No. (7) of 2017, the Jordanian legislator grants auth orized ministry employees the status of judicial
police, empowering them with the right to enter shops, warehouses, and commercial establishments
to carry out monitoring and inspection work on goods and services to ensure the provider's
compliance with the provisions of the law. Upon deconstructing this text, it becomes evident that the
powers of the civil judicial police were formulated to confront violations in their traditional, material
forms within brick-and-mortar establishments on the ground, renderi ng them technically incapable
of exercising digital law enforcement to monitor influencers' personal accounts online. Furthermore,
the role of Jordanian consumer protection associations, regulated under Articles (14) and (15) of the
same law, does not exceed the character of advice, guidance, receivingcomplaints, and referringthem
to the ministry. Jordanian associations completely lack the procedural capacity to initiate
independent collective lawsuits to prosecute commercial influence parties or to compel platforms to
delete misleading content (Loos & Luzak, 2016).

associated with it until the core dispute is resolved, based on the fulfillment of the twin conditions of
urgency and the  risk of irreparable harm. This protection is easily achieved before the French
expedited judiciary due to the existence of explicit requirements obligating commercial influence

5. Conclusion &Recommendations
This study presents a comparative analytical framework highlighting the shortcomings and
advancements in regulating civil liability for misleading advertisements disseminated by influencers
in the digital environment. Based on its findings, a clear legisla tive gap emerges, necessitating a
restructuring of legal protection mechanisms to align with rapid technological advancements.
Therefore, there is an urgent need to formulate a set of recommendations aimed at enhancing the
effectiveness of civil protection and restoring contractual balance in the digital market.

• Enacting a Specialized Legal Framework for Influencer Marketing the Jordanian legislator
should adopt a dedicated regulation governing digital marketing and commercial influence, providing
a precise legal definition of the influencer and recognizing influe ncer activity as a professional
commercial practice. This framework should clearly distinguish between personal expressions and
