Abstract
As social media increasingly permeates everyday life, ethical concerns about its use are coming into sharper focus. At the
same time, the ethical issues involved in social media marketing have received somewhat limited attention from market -
ing research and practice. Therefore, many scholars are calling out for a better understanding of the role of ethics in social
media marketing decisions. Current marketing ethics theories do not sufficiently consider the unique dynamics of social
media. To aid researchers, practitioners, and policymakers, we propose a theoretical framework to address issues in social
media marketing ethics built on theories of normative and descriptive ethics. This framework considers the normative and
descriptive ethics within firm-internal and firm-external factors in the fields of law, regulation and norms, platform condi-
tions, and stakeholder value. Then, applying a thematically built systematic literature review, we identify and discuss five
distinct themes of research in social media marketing ethics: (I) advertising and customer–brand relationships; (II) the dark
side of social media; (III) privacy; (IV) fake news; and (V) emerging research. These themes and their implications are
discussed using the proposed theoretical framework. Our study provides a comprehensive overview and synthesis of ethical
challenges in social media marketing and suggests possible research avenues for the future. In doing so, it outlines pressing
issues that require attention from researchers, policymakers, and practitioners to ensure an ethically sustainable approach
to social media marketing.

Introduction
Several attempts have been made to bring ethical decision-
making to the forefront of marketing science, notably by
Ferrell and Gresham (1985), Hunt and Vitell (1986) and
Laczniak and Murphy (1993). However, even after decades
of research, marketing ethics—and, in particular social
media marketing ethics—is not receiving the necessary
attention (Dwivedi et al., 2021). Acknowledging this void,
some scholars (e.g., Jacobson et al., 2020; Laczniak & Mur-
phy, 2019) suggest that researchers should focus more on
digital marketing ethics and privacy issues. Such studies are
urgently needed, as there are still gaps in our understanding
of ethics in privacy and other social media-related issues that
pose numerous avenues for future research.
Social media researchers are facing a multitude of issues
not previously present in science, such as those related to
individualised data use (Dwivedi et al., 2021), artificial
intelligence (AI) (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2019) and psycho-
logical harm (Marengo et al., 2018). This paper approaches
the issue by proposing a theoretical framework designed to
address the ethical issues specific to social media marketing.
Our proposed framework helps scholars in identifying and
studying new and emerging phenomena in their dynamic
context. Furthermore, this paper presents a review of litera-
ture on social media marketing ethics to chart the emerging
subject and to evaluate the applicability of the framework.

In this paper, we define social media as media forms based
on an evolving Internet infrastructure that facilitates social
interaction and user-generated content (UGC) (Kaplan &
Haenlein, 2010). Individuals use social media to learn,
connect, share and immerse themselves in information
(Jacobson et al., 2020). Examples of popular social media

and chart areas for future research addressing the ethical
issues raised in social media marketing, and conclude our
findings in Sect.7.
This paper contributes to social media research by provid-
ing a comprehensive approach to the ethical issues associ-
ated with marketing on social media. Our study also con-
tributes to the development of marketing ethics by tying
marketing ethics theories to the current issues raised in
social media marketing.
Definition of marketing ethics
Before we begin to chart the current state of research on
social media marketing ethics, it is vital to define marketing
ethics. Marketing ethics is not a unified theory; rather, it is a
macro-level domain that covers several constructs and theo-
ries (MacInnis, 2011). The study of marketing ethics concen-
trates on specific questions about ethical decision-making.
Marketing ethics is typically described as “the system-
atic study of how moral standards are applied to marketing
decisions, behaviors and institutions” (Laczniak & Murphy,
1993). The foundations of marketing ethics lie in moral phi-
losophy (Abela & Murphy, 2008). Moral philosophy can
be divided into deontological and teleological viewpoints.
Deontology focuses on actions and behaviours and setting
rules, whereas teleology looks at the consequences of these
actions and behaviours and on evaluations of them (Hunt &
Vitell, 1986).
Depending on the viewpoint, marketing ethics can be
either normative/prescriptive or positive/descriptive (Hunt
& Vitell, 1986; Laczniak & Murphy, 1993). Normative eth-
ics focuses on creating guidelines and rules for marketers
to assist ethical decision-making, while descriptive ethics
aims to explain how the ethical decision-making process
takes place. Noting that most theories in marketing ethics are
normative, Hunt and Vitell (1986) developed a descriptive
theory to explain the underlying ethical issues in decision-
making, known as the Hunt-Vitell (H-V) model. Compre-
hensive theories on marketing ethics should consider both
the deontological and teleological perspectives (Ferrell &
Gresham, 1985; Hunt & Vitell, 1986). This is important as
both kinds of assessments take place in real-life ethical eval-
uations. Using established norms only can be problematic
because there are always exceptions to the rule.
Ethics and ethical decision-making ought to be the fun-
damental starting point in any business decision (Murphy
et al., 2013; Torelli, 2021). Hence, ethics always precede
considerations of sustainability or responsibility, because
ethical judgments de facto shape the value of sustainability
efforts and other actions (Torelli, 2021). It is important to
distinguish ethics from the widely used concept of CSR.
The distinction between ethics and CSR is all the more vital
because even scholars can imply that these terms are synony-
mous (Ferrell et al., 2019; Murphy et al., 2013). CSR can be
defined as voluntary responsible actions and decisions that

will not harm firms’ stakeholders as well as actions taken to
communicate these efforts (Murphy et al., 2013). Normative
elements of CSR include values and principles in fulfilling,
for example, economical or ethical responsibilities (Ferrell
et al., 2019).
Marketing ethics is a part of business ethics, which is
contextual and socially negotiated (Ferrell et al., 2019).
Business ethics can all too often be simplistically catego-
rised as responsible actions, such as engaging in fair trade
or adopting environmentally sustainable practices. In the
intersection of business and philosophy, business ethics is
the moral principles guiding behaviour and actions, giv -
ing guidance in what is right or wrong (Ferrell et al., 2019;
Torelli, 2021). Normative elements of business ethics are
for instance the norms, codes of conduct and compliance
systems (Ferrell et al., 2019) that drive an ethical culture
within a firm (Murphy et al., 2013).
Abela and Murphy (2008) argue that a compartmentali-
sation of business and ethics persists in marketing science,
which makes it easy to neglect ethical issues. New research
and developments within marketing do not necessarily con-
sider the ethical dimensions of the phenomena being studied,
thus pushing ethics to the margins. Consequently, ethics is
not seen as being directly linked with business or profit,
despite research results showing that ethical or responsible
practices can increase the long-term profitability of a firm
(Murphy et al., 2013; White et al., 2019). This tendency to
neglect ethical judgements in decision-making, in turn, leads
to ethical tensions between the firm and its stakeholders.
Theoretical framework
Our paper develops a theoretical framework based on
prominent marketing ethics theories, adding elements
related to the unique characteristics of social media. We
use this framework throughout the paper to interpret and
analyse the findings, thereby enhancing the conceptual
contribution (MacInnis, 2011). The development of the
framework follows Jaakkola’s (2020) description of a con -
ceptual paper. Theory synthesis integrates accumulated
knowledge to offer a new perspective on a certain concept.
This paper presents a theory synthesis by summarising
existing theories and outlining the conceptual domain of
social media marketing ethics. By developing a theoretical
framework, this paper delineates a new entity and provides
a map to understand it (MacInnis, 2011). Thus, the aim is
not to develop a comprehensive theory of marketing eth-
ics, but to create a synthesis of current marketing ethics
theories (Yadav, 2010).
The theories used to develop the framework are Abela
and Murphy’s (2008) seven ethical tensions in market-
ing, Ferrel and Gresham’s ( 1985) contingency framework
for understanding ethical decision-making, and Hunt and
Vitell’s (1986) general theory of marketing ethics, as pre-
sented in Table  1. These theories have been widely used
and cited, and they represent different poles of marketing
ethics. The descriptive H-V model and the contingency
model have been impactful in the study of marketing ethics
for decades. In their normative approach, Abela and Mur -
phy are applying service-dominant (S-D) logic, a theory
that has been very influential in marketing thought during
the past two decades (Vargo & Lusch, 2017).
Abela and Murphy (2008) draw from the legacy of Lac-
zniak and Murphy’s (1993) normative theory of marketing
ethics. They focus on the relationship between business
and marketing ethics on the systemic level, following the
helicopter view approach of S-D logic. They identify ethi -
cal tensions between business and ethics that arise when
norms guiding the economic interests of the firms and the
wellbeing of stakeholders clash. Five of these dimensions
are created between the firm and its customers and two
between the firm and other stakeholders.
Ferrell and Gresham’s (1985) descriptive contingency
framework seeks to explain ethical decision-making in mar-
keting by identifying variables affecting it. They pinpoint
particularly the role of significant others within the organisa-
tion. In addition, they highlight the opportunity, meaning the
conditions that drive (un)ethical behaviour. The proximity
of significant others has a great impact on understanding
ethical decision-making, as does the role of rewards and
punishments after a successful or unsuccessful decision.

The H-V model (Hunt & Vitell, 1986) is a descriptive
theory of marketing ethics, explaining the ethical issues in
individual decision-making processes rather than prescrib-
ing courses of action. The model suggests that ”an individ-
ual’s ethical judgements…are a function of the individual’s
deontological evaluation… and the individual’s [teleologi-
cal evaluation]” (Hunt & Vitell, 2006, p. 145). Further, this
function is affected by four factors causing ethical variance
in decision-making (Hunt & Vitell, 1986 ). First, different
individuals interpret reality and the available options dif-
ferently. The second source of variance stems from an indi-
vidual’s interpretation of the probability and desirability of
teleological consequences. Thirdly, there is some individual
variance in deontological evaluation, that is, different people
adhere to different norms and rules. Fourth, individuals tend
to combine and emphasise deontological and teleological
evaluations differently. The authors conclude that under -
standing these mechanisms is an essential element of any
approach on marketing ethics (Hunt & Vitell, 1986).
While the most apparent distinction between these theo-
ries may lie in the normative–descriptive level, another
significant difference is the level of observation. The H-V
model acknowledges that industries and organisations have a
complex set of norms and codes, but it focuses on individual
decision-making (Hunt & Vitell, 1986). The contingency
framework (Ferrell & Gresham, 1985) has a similar scope,
placing the individual in pivotal position in an organisa-
tional setting. In contrast, Abela and Murphy (2008) pay
little attention to individual decision-makers and consider
the origin of ethical tensions to stem from the presence of
conflicting norms within the ethical systems that dictate how
humans ought to behave.
Developing a framework for social media marketing
ethics
Rather than embracing only normative or descriptive ethics
or considering only the individual or systemic level, the pre-
sented framework uses a combination of these approaches
to provide a nuanced representation of social media market-
ing ethics (Hunt & Vitell, 1986). From Abela and Murphy
(2008), we draw the organisation-centred, systemic approach
of ethical conflicts that affect different stakeholders as well
as the idea about ethical tensions between business and
ethics. The macro-level lens is motivated as organisational
pressure is shown to strongly put pressure on individual
decision-making (Ferrell & Gresham, 1985). Hunt and Vitell
(1986, 2006), meanwhile, cater to us with a refined approach
to individual factors, the impact of the environment and
weighing of the consequences for stakeholders. The con-
cept of stakeholders, along with internal culture and norms,
is based on the model developed by Ferrell and Gresham
(1985). Based on the implications of these theories, we
develop a theoretical framework for social media marketing
ethics presented in Fig. 1.
This framework places the firm in a focal position within
a system of internal and external factors. Meanwhile, it rec-
ognises that decisions are not made by organisations but
by individual decision-makers in an organisational environ-
ment, impacted by firm-external factors. It does not try to
understand how and why decisions are made (descriptive)
or to explain normative rules stating how things ought to
be done. Instead, the framework aims to frame the issue
of (un)ethical decision-making processes of social media
marketers. The framework is designed to enable its users to
consider social media marketing ethics on a systemic level,
rather than acquiring an understanding of individual factors
affecting ethical decision-making. It also helps managers
find and allocate the right resources to manage ethical issues
in social media marketing.
Internal and external factors
Ethical tensions in marketing arise when conflicting norms
are present (Abela & Murphy, 2008). The conflicts can stem
from when different objectives emerge from firm-internal
and firm-external factors. To illustrate, the firm may see as
its main objective to gain profit for its main stakeholders,
whereas the external social norms and beliefs might advo-
cate a stronger focus on responsibility.
Our model examines both internal and external factors as
the external environment significantly influences the work -
ing conditions and the types of (un)ethical judgments made
within an organisation. The impact of external factors on
firms in the realm of social media is often greater than in
traditional marketing. This has been repeatedly illustrated by
scandals involving the problematic or illegal use of personal
data (e.g., Jacobson et al., 2020). Therefore, there is increas-
ing pressure on firms to interpret external factors correctly
and adjust internal factors accordingly. Cavusgil and Zou
(1994) identify that a successful marketing strategy is an
interplay of internal and external factors. We suggest simi-
larly that identifying and finding a balance between firm-
internal and firm-external factors is essential for an ethical
marketing strategy.
Whereas many external factors are given and need to be
considered as such, affecting internal factors is often easier
for firms (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2019). However, the degree
of consideration given to external factors has a strong impact
on how internal factors are dealt with. Internal factors are
firm characteristics (Cavusgil & Zou, 1994) and can include
both individual and organisational factors (cf. Ferrell &
Gresham, 1985), such as firm employees, resources, and
competencies as well as organisational norms. Firm-external
factors include industry and market characteristics, such as
given circumstances in the form of law and regulation, but

also competitors, and industry- or culture-specific norms and
self-regulation (Hunt & Vitell, 1986; Kaplan & Haenlein,
2019).
Law, regulation and norms
All marketing is subject to international and local law, regu-
lation, and norms. According to Abela and Murphy (2008),
the current system encourages firms to increase their results
while adhering to the law, and consequently, there is an
increased demand for legislation and regulatory actions.
Moreover, the H-V model (Hunt & Vitell, 2006) encourages
scholars to focus on external factors of the cultural environ-
ment such as the legal system.
Considering external factors are considered more
important in social media marketing than in offline adver -
tising (Jacobson et al., 2020). Therefore, it is understand-
able that the focus is on the external factors of the legisla-
tive and regulatory bodies. However, it is also important to
acknowledge the role of external cultural norms and recog -
nise industry-specific self-regulatory practices as well as
firm-internal norm systems. Norms typical for marketing
are increasing customer satisfaction, maximising firm prof-
its and protecting consumers from abuse (Abela & Mur -
phy, 2008). Sometimes these norms clash with each other,
or even with law and regulation, creating ethical tensions.
According to normative ethics, this is where the normative
approach is useful in guiding marketers.
To navigate the complex and fast-paced environment
of social media, firms need to allocate sufficient resources
to interpret the legal and regulatory implications of their
actions. Hence, firms should be aware of the internal
implications of external factors. Internal factors within
this dimension include employee and manager competen-
cies in identifying and interpreting relevant legislation
and regulations. Firm culture and internal norms also play
major roles in how rules and behaviours are interpreted
and internalised, and how decisions are made. Within
the dynamic sphere of social media, external factors are
liable to change, and firms need to develop the flexibility
to update their internal norms and practices to match the
environment and ethical requirements (cf., Laczniak &
Murphy, 1993).

Platform conditions
In social media marketing, a great many external factors
are dictated by tech giants, which can change such factors
almost on a whim. Social media platforms can one-sidedly
change their algorithms which usually has a large impact on
firms’ marketing effectiveness (Saura et al., 2021).
Ceaseless changes in the external environment put a
heavy pressure on firm-internal factors, such as advancing
platform-specific competencies. Efforts to develop sufficient
social media competencies help firms navigate in a turbu-
lent social media environment and enhance their competitive
advantage (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2019; Saura et al., 2021).
Organisations need to be alert to the development of new
social media platforms and to evaluate where and how to
best serve their stakeholders. The internal platform com-
petencies include both ‘hard’ skills such as understanding
of the technology and algorithms, and ‘soft’ skills such as
ethical evaluations. The existence of hard skills can lead to
organisational virtue (Chatterjee et al., 2015), that is, to the
development of ethical considerations.
Stakeholder value
Social media constitutes a double-edged sword that poses
both risks and opportunities. Therefore, a framework for
social media marketing ethics needs to consider both the
possible gains and losses for different market actors (Hunt
& Vitell, 1986). A comprehensive approach to stakeholders
can help explain the intersections between marketing and
society (Hult et al., 2011; Murphy et al., 2013) and increase
understanding of how different stakeholders are affected by
but also how they affect firms’ marketing efforts.
Here, the framework draws inspiration from the H-V
model (Hunt & Vitell, 1986, 2006), which considers the
consequences of decisions for several different stakeholders.
The consequences of (un)ethical decisions can be intended
or unintended and can vary for different stakeholders. We
recognise the role of individual decision-making and, in par-
ticular, how teleological evaluations are used to consider
consequences. However, an individual decision-maker can-
not be expected to identify all potential stakeholders and
consequences; hence there is pressure to develop adequate
organisational competencies and norms (cf., Chatterjee
et al., 2015).
A broad stakeholder view is necessary in social media
marketing as—thanks to the interactive nature of social
media—decision can have consequences for a larger num-
ber of stakeholders than firms might expect. Taking a broad
view of stakeholders means considering that, in practice,
anyone can affect or be affected by the focal firm’s actions
(Hult et al., 2011).
The ethical value created for, with, or by stakeholders
can be positive or negative (Fry & Polonski, 2004). In other
words, both intended or unintended consequences can be
either positive or negative, and different stakeholders can be
affected in various ways by a specific decision. Identifying
and addressing the sometimes-conflicting needs of differ -
ent stakeholders require firms to balance on a tightrope (cf.
Abela & Murphy, 2008). Firms also need to dedicate effort
to identifying potential stakeholders and to consider the kind
of value each group is likely to receive (Chatterjee et al.,
2015; Hult et al., 2011).
Materials and methods
The purpose of this paper is twofold: first, to present a theo-
retical framework for social media marketing ethics, and
second, to review and synthesise accumulated knowledge
on social media marketing ethics in the light of the frame-
work. An SLR was chosen as the method, as it is a robust
tool for knowledge production, can provide a general over -
view in dispersed or emerging research areas (Snyder, 2019),
and is a transparent, objective, and reproducible method of
synthesising existing literature within a given area (Kraus
et al., 2020).
This study applies a thematic approach to the SLR, which
seeks to find concepts within a research area that create a
framework and are used to organise the review (Torraco,
2016). Our data set includes qualitative, quantitative, and
conceptual papers, in which case a thematic SLR is a highly
relevant analysis method to generate a holistic understanding
of the accumulated knowledge (Snyder, 2019). Addition-
ally, the conceptual objective of this study is to delineate the
entity of social media ethics research (MacInnis, 2011), and
we believe that the structured approach of a thematic SLR
is best suited to synthesise the information acquired to paint
a comprehensive image of the field.
A review protocol ensures the transparency and quality
of the process (Kraus et al., 2020). We defined the selec-
tion criteria (cf. Palmatier et al., 2018; Snyder, 2019) and
included papers explicitly addressing ethical issues related to
social media marketing from organisational and managerial
viewpoints. Consumer and other stakeholder perspectives
were included in the data set when relevant for firms from a
business viewpoint (e.g. customer satisfaction or employee
wellbeing).
For data extraction, we conducted a Web of Science
(WoS) Core Collection search using the keywords ‘social
media’ and ‘ethic.*’ To extract a representative body of
papers and ensure different markets were covered, the search
terms also included a list of the most used social media or
networking sites: Facebook, Instagram, Twitter, LinkedIn,
YouTube, TikTok, Snapchat, VKontakte, Weibo, and

WeChat. The search words were sought in the title, abstract
and keywords of the papers to ensure that social media mar-
keting ethics was a prominent theme in each paper. No time
limits for publication were set. The search was limited to
WoS’ predefined categories of marketing, business, and
management journals to guarantee a market-oriented per -
spective. Out of the results, we manually selected journals
classified A+, A, B or C by The German Academic Associa-
tion for Business Research (VHB) to guarantee the studies
were of high quality (cf. Bodolica & Spraggon, 2018; Kraus
et al., 2020).
The initial corpus extracted from WoS comprised 2,857
articles. Applying the selection criteria defined in the review
protocol resulted in a final data set of 205 papers (i.e., 7%
of the initially extracted corpus). The papers were published
between 2009 and 2022 in 51 different peer-reviewed jour -
nals. The journals with the most papers in the sample were
(number of papers in parentheses): Journal of Business
Research (21), Business Horizons (13), Journal of Adver -
tising (13), International Journal of Advertising (13), Tech-
nological Forecasting and Social Change  (12), Journal of
Management Information Systems (10), Journal of Business
Ethics (8), and Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services
(7).
During the analysis process, we systematically created
initial open codes based on keywords in either the title,
abstract, or keywords of each paper (Braun & Clarke, 2006).
Then, we grouped the codes into larger thematic groups to
identify recurring themes and check that they matched the
initial coding. The coding scheme was double-checked, and
both the initial codes and thematic coding were carried out
in two separate rounds to ensure the validity of the findings
(Eriksson & Kovalainen, 2008).
Findings
The past few years have seen a rapid growth in the number
of studies on social media marketing ethics. As many as 78%
of the papers were published in or after 2017. Before that,
fewer than 10 studies per year were published. Since 2017,
different themes have started to emerge, and the topics have
become diversified.
We analysed the data set based on the initial and thematic
coding (Braun & Clarke, 2006) presented in the previous
section. Five thematic entities emerged within the data: (I)
advertising and customer–brand relationships; (II) the dark
side of social media; (III) privacy; (IV) fake news; and (V)
emerging research. Table  2 presents the identified themes,
important topics and most influential papers and journals.
As shown in Table 2, the literature within each theme is
dispersed across several journals. Understandably, many of
the most cited papers were published several years ago and
have thus had time to accumulate citations. Some of the
topics were relevant to more than one theme, a fact that we
considered in the coding scheme. The proximity and over -
lapping of the themes as well as the relative size of each
theme are presented in Fig. 2.
When considering the themes through the theoretical
framework presented in Fig.  1, the emergence of firm-
related fake news is heavily based on firm-external factors,
as fake news rarely stem from inside the organisation. In
the other themes, on the other hand, firm-internal factors
such the interpretation of regulation or choice of collecting
and using user data are strongly emphasised. This indicates
that the firm may have more power to impact ethical ten -
sions within the other themes by adjusting internal norms,
resources and competencies. This is also mirrored in the fact
that fake news is distanced from the other themes, which are
more interrelated and share the notion of internal influence.
Fake news is also a more societally-oriented theme than the
others. Its normative approach is mirrored towards the phe-
nomena itself and at the society rather than on the focal firm
or the individual decision maker.
The themes identified in the literature are interrelated,
and a clear differentiation between them is therefore not
always possible. Among our identified themes, we found
that privacy most permeated the other themes and caused
the most concern among scholars. Moreover, this concern
is in line with the public interest shown by the media and
policymakers.
The findings of the SLR emphasise the need for a social
media-specific approach to marketing ethics, which we have
proposed to do with our framework. In Fig.  3, we illustrate

Advertising and customer–brand relationships
Advertising and customer–brand relationships is the largest
single theme in terms of number of papers published, with
nearly half of the identified articles coded here. This theme
was the first to emerge, appearing in 2009. It has remained
prominent, as shown by the fact that it includes many of the
papers with the most citations. It is also, understandably, the
most diverse of the themes.
Issues within the theme pose different ethical questions
addressing both firm-internal and firm-external factors, often
directly or indirectly related to profit generation and creating
stakeholder value, as discussed in Fig.  1. This theme has a
strict managerial focus. Most studies aim to increase under-
standing of how firms can use social media platforms for
better results. In doing so, they more likely take a descriptive
than normative stance and look at the organisational level
rather than the level of an individual decision maker. The

285AMS Review (2024) 14:278–296
focus is also normative in that they aim to develop guidelines
and best practices on how to make use of an external factor;
social media platforms and their algorithms. However, very
few of the studies look specifically at ethics and the (un)
ethicality of decision making becomes easily a side note,
as does the understanding of why and how firms make (un)
ethical decisions. This lines well with Abela and Murphy’s
(2008) statement about ethics being left at the outskirts of
business research where profit generation is the main issue.
Many of the topics, such as ad persuasiveness (Chang
et al., 2015), invasiveness (Noguti & Waller, 2020), or cred-
ibility (e.g., Harvey et al., 2011) raise issues about conse-
quences for external stakeholders. Studies on targeted or
personalised advertising are closely linked with the research
on data security and privacy (e.g., Hayes et al., 2021) and
connect this theme to the discussion on trust. Ethical use
of data, selecting suitable targeting options, and advertis-
ing messages require firms to be well aware of the external
regulations and rules and to develop suitable internal norm
systems. Firms also need to be careful to ensure internal
resources and competencies to address these questions and
consider the possible consequences of their advertising
choices.

advertising literacy of the influencers’ target groups, largely

compliance is not enough, but firms need to have norms and
skills to guide ethical decision making (see Fig.  1).
The dark side of social media
The dark side of social media has become an often-used
term in the study of various topics concerning the emotional
or psychological harm faced by social media users (Turel &
Qahri-Saremi, 2016). In contrast to the first theme focusing
on the opportunities of social media for firms, this theme
looks at the negative consequences of using social media,
mainly from a firm-external perspective (see Fig.  3). The
studies in this theme are difficult to place in a framework
of normative/descriptive ethics as they do not look at the
ethical decision making of firms. Rather, they tend to focus
on the role of external factors (cf. Hunt & Vitell, 1986) like
social media platforms and platform dynamics that enable
the emergence of negative consequences, thus taking a more
systemic lens (cf. Abela & Murphy, 2008). Nevertheless,
some studies recommend normative approaches on how
firms may avoid causing negative consequences by address-
ing customer-related issues on social media in a specific
manner.
The dark side of social media has aroused great public
interest over the past few years, and academic interest in
how this dark side relates to marketing has started to gain
momentum around 2019. Common topics include cyberbul-
lying (e.g., Chan et al., 2019; Lowry et al., 2016), mental
health (e.g., Staniewski & Awruk, 2022), and body image
issues (e.g., He, 2022). At first glance, the theme seems
consumer-focused, but it is in fact very relevant for firms.
The dark sides of social media can give rise to negative
unintended consequences for stakeholders and cause vast
damage to a firm’s reputation (Baccarella et al., 2018; Wil-
burn & Wilburn, 2016). Not keeping up with the develop-
ment of technology or editing ad images may throw firms
into a spiral in which their existing competencies—or the

social media content. They emphasise firms’ responsibil-
ity to protect consumers through active content moderation,
that is, by deleting users’ offensive comments and actively
combating trolling and cyberbullying. Firms’ responsibility
to take care of the wellbeing of their customers becomes
even more relevant on social media (Appel et al., 2020 ),
particularly when the advertised service is controversial or
sensitive, or when customers are in a vulnerable position.
Privacy
Privacy in social media marketing started to emerge as a
research topic around 2017. This technically focused theme
is characterised by discussion of data and targeted or per -
sonalised advertising. Privacy issues are closely linked to
advertising and customer–brand relationships as their aim
is to improve marketing efficiency, but they are also relevant
for the dark side of social media. Much of the research in this
theme is focused on how firms use data (e.g., Spiekermann
& Korunovska, 2017), but scholars are also interested in the
level of self-disclosure and users’ awareness of what and
how much information they give away about themselves,
and for what purposes (e.g., Ozdemir et al., 2017). The use
of data gives rise to a number of ethical tensions as norms
of profit-seeking and data protection easily collide (cf. Abela
& Murphy, 2008), and the value created can be negative to
customers (see Fig. 1).
The focus of privacy research is regulatory with norma-
tive inputs. While the normative focus on privacy ethics
arose with the Cambridge Analytica scandal (Michaelidou &
Micevski, 2019), the descriptive side of ethics is almost con-
spicuous in its absence. The level of observation is mainly
systemic or firm-centred, whereas the role of individual
decision making is blurred to the background (cf. Table  1).
We identify a growing concern for lack of appropriate
regulation, that is, an emphasis on firm-external factors
(see Figs. 1 and 3). Authors such as Jacobson et al. (2020)
increasingly call for more knowledge and state the need to
educate the public about the amount of personal information

social media users disclose by using these platforms. Mostel-
ler and Poddar (2017) find that trust can be stimulated if a
firm gives users control of how their personal information
is being used. Furthermore, firms that succeed in reassuring
their customers that they are protected from privacy viola-
tions benefit from increased consumer trust and simultane-
ously contribute to the wellbeing of their customers, directly
creating positive stakeholder value. Firms should understand
that consumers see privacy and agreeing to terms of use
as a mutual contract in which they both give and receive
(Bandara et al., 2020). Thus, firm-internal competencies in
understanding regulation and platform conditions are crucial
for building a foundation for fruitful stakeholder relations.
Ethical privacy issues arise when marketers attempt
to improve total marketing system efficiency (Abela &
Murphy, 2008) by collecting and using data (Mosteller &
Poddar, 2017). Simultaneously, privacy-related fears can
lessen consumer participation by creating tensions in the
firm–customer relationship (Quach et al., 2022). An effective
marketing system depends on high-quality user data, which
provides the basis for the advertising solutions offered to
third-party actors. As the collection and use of data may
create a perceived privacy risk at the same time (Bugshan
& Attar, 2020), platform providers as well as advertisers
need to find a balance between firm and consumer interest.
Privacy concerns impact consumer behaviour. A grow -
ing number of people are quitting social media or specific
platforms due to rising privacy concerns (Appel et al., 2020),
which in turn affects advertisers relying on social media.
Therefore, it is important that firms try to identify both the
intended and unintended consequences related to data and
privacy.
Fake news
Fake news is the most distinct and focused of the themes
and does not overlap with any of the other themes, as seen
in Fig. 2. The studies on fake news and mis- and dis-infor -
mation concentrate on the societal and larger-scale effects
of distributing information on social media, thus mainly
focusing on the systemic or the organisational level. The
focus is more external than internal. The central element is
the much talked-of spreading of fake news and mis- or dis-
information. Scholarly attention to fake news was awakened
in 2017, in the wake of the 2016 US presidential elections.
Due to the political events that arguably sparked interest in
this theme, the bulk of the research is concentrated on the
spreading of political fake news while some of the more
recent studies focus on the spread of mis- and dis-informa-
tion about COVID-19 (e.g., Islam et al., 2020). A common
denominator for the studies within this theme is the norma-
tive view that fake news ought to be fought actively to lessen
the threats to society, organisations and people.
Fake news become relevant for the scope of this study
because firms are likewise susceptible to the spreading of
mis- or dis-information about them (e.g., Pennycook et al.,
2020). In such cases, the financial risks can be substantial, as
can the damage to brand and reputation, caused by external
stakeholders (cf. Hunt & Vitell, 1986, see also Fig. 1). Some
studies even indicate that brand management is increasingly
impacted by the spread of fake news (Flostrand et al., 2020).
This means that firms have a growing need to develop inter-
nal norm systems and competencies to fight the effects of
fake news on their brand and reputation (see Fig. 1).
Even firms that develop internal competencies and try to
react to external events can find it difficult to directly address
tensions related to fake news. Thus, these ethical issues are
particularly challenging and not necessarily considered by
scholars as an ethical problem but rather a brand-related
issue. However, as Chen and Cheng (2020) emphasise, how
consumers interpret fake news about companies can have
consequences for both internal and external stakeholders
such as consumers, managers, and regulators.
One of the major factors differentiating fake news from
the other themes is the different dynamic between the firm
and its customers. Within the other literature streams, the
firm is usually the focal actor, but in this theme we see that
customers can take an operative role in creating negative
value for the firm both wilfully and inadvertently. Interest-
ingly, despite fake news and the dark side of social media not
having any overlap, the implications to firms are similar. In
both cases, scholars recommend that firms engage in active
brand management as well as curating and moderating social
media content actively.
Here, the ethical tensions are triggered by external dis-
turbances. For instance, disinformation can occur in cases
where service recovery has been unsatisfactory. External
parties become observers and actors, in the worst case accel-
erating the spreading of mis- or dis-information (cf. Bacile
et al., 2018). Firms need to actively manage their brands
and support media and information literacy to fight negative
consequences for stakeholders (Chen & Cheng, 2020). To
protect their customers from mis- or dis-information, firms
should engage in correcting false accusations and rumours,
and other forms of harmful and/or incorrect information.
Firms can do so by participating in social media conversa-
tions and deleting offensive comments but also by making
sure their tone of communication is respectful (Bacile et al.,
2018).
Emerging research
In addition to identifying four major themes within the
social media marketing ethics literature, we labelled 29
miscellaneous papers in the sample ‘emerging research’ as
we were unable to identify a single common theme across

289AMS Review (2024) 14:278–296
them. We expect many of these topics to gain more inter -
est in the near future as their relevance for social media
marketing ethics increases.
The birth of this theme can be tracked to Kaplan and
Haenlein’s (2010) description and conceptualisation of
social media. Initially, the attitudes towards social media
marketing were positive—if not idealistic. The ethical
issues were often left unattended, but sometimes instead
painting a dystopian future where humans no longer com -
municate with each other face-to-face (Kaplan & Haen-
lein, 2010). Some scholars also saw that social media can
increase unpredictability because of the diffusion of roles
among consumers, technology, culture, and government
(Berthon et al., 2012).
As the theme is so dispersed, a comprehensive analy -
sis is challenging. However, some common elements in the
approach to ethics can be discovered. Mainly, this theme
adheres to descriptive ethics as it is in nature exploratory
and aims to explain emerging phenomena. The focus is on
firm-internal factors; how can firms utilise new technology
and new possibilities, and what kind of norm systems and
internal capabilities are needed to harness these (see Fig. 1).
More important than regulation are the opportunities and
how to grab them.
Over the years, a wide variety of topics has been covered.
The role of social media in societal development and democ-
racy (Berthon et al., 2012), social change (Bhatti et al.,
2020) and activism (Etter & Albu, 2021) have awakened
interest. Firms are even affected by how power is dispersed
to consumers in social media and how brands and copy -
righted material become free prey (Berthon et al., 2012).
From 2019 onwards we identify a slight growth in tech-
nology-related topics such as AI and virtual reality (VR)
(Kaplan & Haenlein, 2019), textual analysis, and algorithms
(Etter & Albu, 2021). The development of AI in marketing
has both internal and external implications for firms (Kaplan
& Haenlein, 2019). These implications can be categorised as
confidence, change and control, in a division closely related
to the one proposed in our framework in Fig. 1. The internal
implications are related to the firm’s human, physical or dig-
ital resources, while the external ones relate to stakeholder
relationships with customers, competitors, and regulating
bodies (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2019).
Recently, there has also been growing interest in the role
of social media in business ethics (Rauf, 2021), responsibil-
ity, and CSR (Bhatti et al., 2020). How firms address these
issues is determined to some extent by external legislation
and regulation but to a larger degree by the ethical codes and
norms they have developed internally. Firms can actively
take the role of an ethical actor trying to be part of societal
change or deter negative societal events, and some com-
mentators think this is a role they should increasingly play
(Rauf, 2021).
Discussion
In this paper, we set out to build a framework for social
media marketing ethics and to synthesise the existing
research in this area. We wanted the theoretical framework
to include factors relevant to the dynamic nature of social
media. The framework is first divided into firm-internal
and firm-external factors, and within this division, we look
at law, regulation and norms, platform conditions, and
stakeholder value. The comprehensive approach is war -
ranted as the fast-paced world of social media puts unprec-
edented pressure on firm-internal factors and on how to
address these in marketing strategies. Sufficient internal
resources and competencies help firms gain competitive
advantage as they can adopt new trends; it is, however, of
uttermost importance to do so ethically so as to create trust
among their stakeholders. To achieve this, firms engag-
ing in social media marketing need to have a teleological
understanding of their ethical decision making processes
and based on this, develop normative approaches to guide
their actions.
In a SLR based on a data set of 205 articles, we identi-
fied four distinct themes in social media marketing eth-
ics: (I) advertising and customer–brand relationships;
(II) the dark side of social media; (III) privacy; and (IV)
fake news, as well as an additional theme of (V) emerging
research. Of these, advertising is the dominant one, both
in the number of papers and variety of topics addressed.
This theme is the starting point of the scholarly interest
in social media marketing ethics. This is not surprising
as at least superficially, advertising and customer–brand
relationships bring most value to the firm. In the future,
several themes and sub-themes will surely develop as the
field continues to grow. The currently visible maturation of
the themes within the past 15 years or so shows an attitude
change from more optimistic or idealistic (e.g., Kaplan
& Haenlein, 2010) to a realistic or an even dystopian
idea of the power of AI (e.g., Kaplan & Haenlein, 2019).
Instead of making marketers’ lives easier as predicted,
social media is causing new kinds of problems that were
unheard of a decade or two ago. However, we should not
forget that social media still has a light side, helping firms
to increase their marketing performance while enabling
consumers to network, access and share information and
become empowered (e.g., Dwivedi et al., 2021). Achiev -
ing a balance between an efficient marketing strategy and
wellbeing of stakeholders requires that ethical decision-
making guides firms’ actions. For this to happen, we need
a more comprehensive understanding or descriptive ethics
as well as suitable normative theories.
The conduct and decisions of tech companies not
only affect users of their platforms, but a range of other

290 AMS Review (2024) 14:278–296
stakeholders (see Figs.  1 and 3). We see that interest in
stakeholders has grown in recent years as, for example,
S-D logic scholars have emphasised the role of different
market actors as value co-creators and value beneficiaries
(Vargo & Lusch, 2017). The systemic lens is justified as
the actions of one market actor can have significant conse-
quences for others. For instance, the dominance of adver -
tising giants such as Meta and Google are pressing other
advertising platforms hard, making it difficult for them to
compete on price or reach. This externally-initiated situa-
tion leaves advertisers with few alternatives to using these
platforms. As a result, it further increases the power of
the already dominant market players and potentially per -
manently tipping the power dynamics of traditional and
new media.
Yet another external factor that causes tensions for firms
is the fast-paced technological development. AI has been a
part of social media marketing since such marketing began,
but marketing AI is rapidly advancing. The speedy devel-
opments of recent years are opening new avenues. Many
researchers agree on AI’s huge potential for targeting, pric-
ing, optimising, content creation and customer care (e.g.,
Dwivedi et al., 2021). The use of novel marketing technolo-
gies is still heavily reliant on external regulation, but norma-
tive systems are being developed in firms and on a societal
level (Dwivedi et al., 2021; Jacobson et al., 2020; Mosteller
& Poddar, 2017).
It is noteworthy that for an AI to be trustworthy, it should
be explainable, transparent, and fair, or in brief, ethical.
However, as recently as 2019, only 25% of 250 firms in a
study by PwC ( 2019) thought it was important to consider
ethical aspects prior to developing an AI application. This
apparent lack of interest in the ethical aspects of AI is clearly
illustrative of what Abela and Murphy (2008) call the com-
partmentalisation of business and ethics: firms are eager to
test and develop new technologies to enhance their business,
but the tension between profit and ethics persists. Further, it
emphasises the gap between firm-external and firm-internal
development.
Algorithms are an enormously important part of social
media marketing, but it is an external factor firms and their
stakeholders have little impact on. It is important to bear in
mind that the cultural, social, and political background of
software developers can impact what people see online and
how it affects them. Even these are external factors firms
should consider when opting for marketing on social media.
Thus, the pressure for developing firm-internal competencies
is ever-growing.
CSR and sustainable practices have been shown to have
business long-term business benefits helping firms boost
their innovativeness, and increasing consumer trust and lik-
ing (White et al., 2019). In recent years, the growth paradigm
has faced criticism from those calling for greater emphasis
on sustainable and ethical business practices that account
for the interests of all stakeholders (cf. White et al., 2019).
Even business executives are calling for morally motivated
business strategies and practices. A point in case is Sales-
force co-CEO Marc Benioff’s (2019) opinion piece in The
New York Times where he advocates for a ‘new capitalism’
that prioritises purpose alongside profit, promoting fairness,
equality, sustainability, and ethics. The calls from business
and academia alike underline the need for research to con-
sider ethicality alongside profit, rather than as opposed to it
(cf. Abela & Murphy, 2008).
The future of social media marketing will likely be char-
acterised by the fragmentation of the digital and social
media landscape (Appel et al., 2020), which will put further
pressure on firms to develop internal platform competencies.
There is an ongoing proliferation of social media as plat-
forms are omnipresent online, collecting data even on other
websites via application programming interfaces, or APIs.
These data can then be used to target messages on and out-
side social media platforms (cf. Acker & Kreisberg, 2020).
This being the case, social media is present in all phases of
consumers’ decision-making processes and is likely to influ-
ence them, whether they know it or not (Appel et al., 2020).
The omnipresence of social media is not only applicable to
marketing but is also shaping cultures and the way of think-
ing and seeing things in general (Appel et al., 2020).
Privacy issues will most likely increasingly characterise
social media marketing (Appel et al., 2020; Jacobson et al.,
2020). Social media users may be becoming more wary of
what data are being collected about them, why such data
are being collected, and how they are being used and shared
with third parties. Social media platforms are increasingly
relying on data collected externally, for example, users of
other websites may not even be aware that their data are
being collected and shared by companies such as Meta
(Acker & Kreisberg, 2020). Albeit research shows (e.g.,
Mosteller & Poddar, 2017), that both the platforms them-
selves and marketers using them should be increasing open-
ness and disclosure to build trust. However, there is also a
risk of developing further digital inequality if certain groups
have higher awareness and digital literacy than others.
Policy implications
In recent years, social media platforms have become a
battleground for political disputes. This development
has been seen, for instance, in the ban on the Chinese-
developed and owned social media platforms TikTok and
WeChat in India (Kumar & Thussu, 2023) and attempts
to ban it in the US (McMahon, 2024). Since Elon Musk’s
acquisition of X, previously blocked Russian accounts
have been restored and Russian propaganda has found new

291AMS Review (2024) 14:278–296
ways of spreading on social media (Mehta, 2023) while
disinformation leading up to elections has flourished on
the platform (O’Carroll, 2024).
At times, acting ethically is perceived as simply com-
plying with law. Efforts going beyond this depend on firm-
internal norms and decision-making. Taking an ethical
stance stems usually from the value base of the firm and
can be labelled as brand activism (Faull, 2022). So far,
the EU has been at the forefront of applying regulations
on new technology. In 2016, the EU adopted the GDPR,
which is said to be the strongest privacy and security law
in the world (European Council, 2022) and in 2024, the
EU also passed The EU Artificial Intelligence Act (Euro-
pean Parliament, 2024).
Other major players such as the US and China may have
less interest in applying strict regulations because big tech
companies are important stakeholders and taxpayers as well
as potentially valuable sources of intelligence. Nonethe-
less, protecting citizens by applying law and regulations to
restrict unethical activity should be a priority in all markets
to ensure wellbeing and digital equality as well as politi-
cal and societal stability. Furthermore, public interest and
social and cultural moral codes will most likely in future
have an even bigger impact on how social media platforms
are allowed to work and how marketing on these platforms
is being conducted.
Officials have increasingly stopped using social media
platforms (e.g., Norwegian Data Protection Authority,
2021) or banned TikTok use on staff devices (e.g., McCal-
lum, 2023). These actions are motivated by external fac-
tors such as privacy and security issues. Indeed, leaks from
former ByteDance employees have stated that the Chinese
government has access to TikTok users’ personal data (Fung,
2023). Thus far, firms have been relatively free to choose
where and how to meet their stakeholders, relying on their
own moral codes. Hence, it seems imperative that policy -
makers remain vigilant, follow closely how and where data
are being collected, used, and stored and issue regulations to
ensure the safety of state secrets as well as citizens’ privacy.
The EU may very well have a competitive head start on
other markets with its strict policies on privacy and data
use, but not even the EU can afford to rest on its laurels. The
strict regulation and risk of billion-euro fines may force tech
companies to consider the moral justification of their busi-
ness models and to adjust their activities to comply with the
ethical guidelines planned to increase the citizens’ digital
safety and wellbeing. Such companies may be tempted to
work their way around the regulations by offering different
services in Europe. However, if the EU is the only major
market issuing strict regulation, there may be opportunities
for other markets to use technology more freely and leave
Europeans lagging behind in terms of technical development
due to the EU’s strict ethical policies.
Managerial implications
The theoretical framework presented in Fig.  1 is aimed to
assist firms identify where and how ethical tensions in social
media marketing may arise and how they can be addressed.
Firms should focus on developing internal competencies and
work on their norm systems to assist individual decision
making (cf. Hunt & Vitell, 1986).
A case in point is customer data which are exception-
ally valuable for firms in helping them segment and target
customers better than ever before. Notwithstanding these
possibilities, firms and managers need to develop internal
competencies and be receptive to privacy issues, be the firms
big or small. It has been shown that openness and transpar -
ency in collecting and using customer data increase trust
in firms (Mosteller & Poddar, 2017). Acting this way gives
the customer a feeling that the firm is trustworthy while
strengthening their own sense of autonomy. To kindle trust
and to be perceived as an ethical firm may very well become
a competitive advantage in a world of climate crisis and
social injustice.
Ethical practices enhance trust and reputation (cf. Brun-
ing et al., 2020; Mosteller & Poddar, 2017), which is true for
firms regardless of size. While large firms may have more
requirements for reporting, small businesses may have a
bigger impact on their local community as the proximity
to many of the stakeholders is shorter. Thus, following the
community’s social norms is essential particularly to small
businesses. For instance, issues with bad customer experi-
ence and service recovery may be fought with active pres-
ence on social media (Bacile et al., 2018), thus potentially
preventing the spread of fake news and bad-will.
There is a large variance between the kinds of external
factors that affect social media marketing efforts in differ -
ent markets. In developed markets, people are becoming
increasingly aware of privacy risks and are less willing to
give their personal data to firms or social media platforms
(Ozdemir et al., 2017). Firms ought to give thought to apply-
ing international privacy standards in emerging markets that
may not yet have strict data regulation and where public
awareness of privacy and data security may still be lower,
thus utilising their internal competencies about market con-
ditions. Following external regulations may seem tempting
to avoid possible repercussions, but being ethical should be
seen as a value in itself. From a global perspective, acting
more ethically than required across markets increases trans-
parency and predictability and can in the best cases decrease
inequality within or between markets. As Laczniak and
Murphy (1993) have pointed out, unethical decisions may
have severe repercussions in the form of loss of goodwill for
firms, as illustrated by the infamous case of Nestlé and infant
formula or, more recently, by the boycotts of firms still oper-
ating in Russia. On the other hand, as research by Mosteller

292 AMS Review (2024) 14:278–296
and Poddar (2017) suggests, ethical decision-making can
increase trust in and goodwill toward the firm. The available
research implies that considering ethics in decision-making
may have significant business advantages and reduce pos-
sible risks.
An ethical marketer must make difficult decisions when
considering marketing on social media. As Appel et al.
(2020) urge, marketers should stand up against tech giants
and demand more accountability and transparency. Simul-
taneously, marketers should make decisions that reflect the
firms’ values and endear them to their customers. Some
firms have done so by joining a short-term boycott of Meta’s
platforms (He et al., 2021). Others have quit social media
platforms entirely, stating that they care about their custom-
ers’ privacy and wellbeing (Faull, 2022).

Based on our findings, we conclude that it is imperative to
accumulate more knowledge on the ethical development of
AI and use of data. AI and machine learning pose interesting
and practical research questions relating to how deepfakes
or virtual influencers will affect customers and their percep-
tion of the firm using them. Furthermore, research should
increasingly address questions related to algorithms and
their role in marketing. Algorithms mediate consumption
and have an impact on individual, collective, and market
level (Airoldi & Rokka, 2022) but neither users nor firms
have much impact on them. Research conducted on these
topics can be applied to develop regulation, norms and man-
agerial practices, minimising risks for social media users,
firms, and the platforms as well as for societies at large.
The findings show that research in social media mar -
keting ethics is largely focused on the customer. The firm
perspective and, in particular, the stakeholder perspective
require more investigation. As far back as 1993, Laczniak
and Murphy talked about the personal costs for managers
when making unethical decisions. They suggested studying
how managers use their moral judgement in their decision-
making to increase the understanding of the benefits of ethi-
cal behaviour. The nature of social media is such that many
marketing decisions can have unintended consequences for
unintended stakeholders.
We expect the emerging research to develop into several
streams of literature. One interesting avenue may be to look
at how ethical decision-making in social media marketing
can affect a firm’s long-term profit generation and brand
value. Sustainability and CSR have already been shown to
have economical up-sides (White et al., 2019), and open-
ness in data use is shown to increase brand trust (Mostel-
ler & Poddar, 2017). Yet, the role of ethics in social media
marketing to increase return on investment (ROI) or firm
profitability is, to our knowledge, yet to be fully revealed.
S-D logic and consumer culture theory (CCT) are just
a couple of examples of theoretical approaches that can
be used to shed light on social media marketing ethics. As
Abela and Murphy (2008) suggest, S-D logic can offer sig-
nificant opportunities for studying marketing ethics as it
avoids compartmentalisation by looking at the entire system
rather than just parts of it. CCT can be and has been used
to help understand consumer-related topics such as privacy
concerns or ethical issues related to influencer marketing in
an interpretivist manner. In general, we believe that systemic
and exploratory theories are well-suited to explaining these
issues and to shed light on arising ethical tensions.
Sadly, we found that developing countries are underrepre-
sented in research. We discovered that the topics addressed
by scholars in developed and developing countries are some-
what different. This difference is partly because the stream
in developed contexts started several years before the same
topics were studied in developing countries. In a world with
growing hybrid interference, we call out for more research
on fake news and digital literacy, particularly in developing
countries.
Limitations
The methodological choices used for the SLR rely on lit-
erature recommending strict selection criteria. Therefore,
some relevant studies may not be included in the data due to
publication in lower-ranking journals or in journals in other
fields than marketing and management, causing potential
selection and publication bias. Due to the emerging nature
of the field, some interesting findings may also be lacking
due to the exclusion of conference papers. The European and
North American dominance in the literature may be attrib-
uted to language selection criteria, as only papers published
in English were included.
The identified themes are based on subjective evaluations,
and other researchers may identify other significant ones.
The aim was to include themes that are large enough to draw
conclusions from, therefore some subtle themes may remain
uncovered. However, for instance the research within the
largest of the themes, advertising and customer-brand rela-
tionships, was so dispersed that dividing it into more small
themes may have proven unfruitful.
Conclusions
A comprehensive approach to social media marketing ethics
benefits academics, practitioners, policymakers, and con-
sumers alike. We believe that looking broadly at internal
and external stakeholders and how they are affected by (un)
ethical decision-making can help overcome decoupling of
ethics and business. Seeing how the presence or lack of ethi-
cal decisions influences one part of the marketing system
and acknowledging the trickle-down effects may increase
understanding of the role and impact of ethical decision-
making in social media marketing.
Social media marketing raises a multitude of ethical ques-
tions. As social media moves ever closer to omnipresence,
the importance of investigating ethical issues in social media
marketing will only increase. Firms are in ever-growing
need of ethical understanding and guidelines as they need
to consider a range of ethical questions and weigh their con-
sequences. Social media platforms have an unprecedented
power to influence politics, shape public opinion, and affect

consumer behaviour. The platforms will likely experience
the consequences of growing consumer sensibility concern-
ing privacy and ethicality in corporate conduct. They are
increasingly being targeted by enquiries and regulation by
policymakers. To date, platforms have proven extremely
resilient to efforts to render them more responsible, despite
several recent controversies. The storms of Frances Haugen,
record-breaking fines and repeated privacy infringements
seem to be forgotten quickly and people keep using the plat-
forms despite personal and societal losses.
Our research hopes to cater to the multiple involved
addressees by providing an integrative commentary of the
current state of research, and open interesting questions for
the future of social media marketing ethics.
We conclude that ethics research in social media mar -
keting is still developing. However, following Abela and
Murphy (2008), we wonder if ethics should perhaps not be
a research stream of its own at all. Rather, it could be inte-
grated in all research to avoid the existing compartmentalisa-
tion between business and ethics.
