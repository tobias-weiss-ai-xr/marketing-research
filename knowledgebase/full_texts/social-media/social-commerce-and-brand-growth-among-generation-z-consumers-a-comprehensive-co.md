Abstract
Social commerce has emerged as a disruptive paradigm in digital m arketing, fundamentally reshaping
how Generation Z (Gen Z) consumers discover, evaluate, and purchase products. This article presents a
comprehensive conceptual framework examining the multifaceted relationships between social
commerce antecedents and bran d growth outcomes among Gen Z demographics. Grounded in the
Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), Social Influence Theory, Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM),
and Uses and Gratifications Theory (UGT), the framework synthesizes influencer credibility, user -
generated content (UGC) quality, platform affordances, perceived authenticity, and algorithmic
personalization as key drivers of brand engagement and purchase intention. Gen Z consumers, born
between 1997 and 2012, exhibit distinctively digital behavioral profiles characterized by reliance on peer
validation, affinity for micro -influencers, cross -platform navigation fluency, and demand for values -
aligned brand narratives. The framework further incorporates moderating variables including digital
literacy, social media frequency of use, cultural disposition, and platform algorithm transparency. Six
research hypotheses are proposed, and practical implications for brand managers and directions for
empirical validation using Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) are discussed.

Keywords: Social Commerce, Brand Growth, Influencer Marketing, Platform Affordances, Purchase

1. Introduction
The convergence of social media and electronic commerce has engendered a powerful paradigm known
as social commerce, wherein d igital platforms simultaneously serve as spaces of social interaction,
content consumption, and transactional activity [1]. Unlike conventional e -commerce, which is
fundamentally transactional and unidirectional, social commerce is characterized by its emb eddedness
within peer networks, collaborative content ecosystems, and opinion -leader dynamics. Purchase

The economic significance of social commerce is substantial and growing. Global social commerce

Generation Z —individuals born between 1997 and 2012 —constitutes the world's largest consumer
cohort and the first generatio n of true digital natives. Gen Z consumers exhibit heightened skepticism
toward overt brand messaging, profound reliance on peer validation, nuanced sensitivity to authenticity
signals, and remarkable fluency in cross -platform digital navigation [4]. Their  consumption behavior is
constitutively shaped by digital channels: product discovery, evaluation, and social comparison occur
within algorithmically curated feeds, rendering traditional marketing interventions largely ineffective.
Despite the explosive gr owth of social commerce, the academic literature remains fragmented. Extant
studies tend to examine individual antecedents —influencer credibility, user -generated content, or
platform features —in isolation, without providing an integrated framework mapping the full causal
pathway from social commerce activities to measurable brand growth outcomes. This article addresses
this gap by proposing a theoretically grounded framework integrating five key antecedents of social
commerce—influencer credibility, UGC qua lity, platform affordances, perceived authenticity, and
algorithmic personalization —and mapping their effects on brand engagement, purchase intention, and
ultimately brand growth.

2. Theoretical Underpinnings of Social Commerce
2.1 Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)
The Technology Acceptance Model, originally formulated by Davis (1989), posits that digital
technology adoption is governed by perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness [5]. In social
commerce, TAM accounts for the functional intuitiveness o f social platforms and in -app shopping
features. Platforms such as Instagram Shopping, and Pinterest Buyable Pins have progressively reduced
navigational friction associated with in -platform purchasing. Simultaneously, AI -driven product
recommendations, pe rsonalized discovery feeds, and streamlined checkout flows have elevated
perceived usefulness for Gen Z users. Extended TAM models that incorporate social norm alignment,
community trust, and algorithmic transparency offer greater explanatory power in soci al commerce
contexts [6].
2.2 Social Influence Theory
Social Influence Theory distinguishes between normative influence —pressure to conform to group
expectations—and informational influence —the tendency to accept information from trusted sources as
evidence about reality [7]. Both are pervasive in social commerce. Gen Z consumers experience
normative influence through engagement metrics: products accumulating high likes, shares, and positive
comments exert significant conformity pressure. Informational infl uence operates through influencer
endorsements and peer reviews, which serve as epistemic shortcuts in information -overloaded digital

2.3 Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM)
The Elaboration Likelihood Model proposes two routes to persuasion: the central route, involving
careful cognitive elaboration of message arguments, and the peripheral route, relying on heuristic cues
such as source attractiveness [9]. In social commerce, influencer content operates along both routes
simultaneously. High -credibility micro -influencers providing detailed product evaluations activate
central route processing, fostering durable brand loyalty. Celebrity endorsements and visually
compelling content trigger peripheral processing, generating rapid but potentially less enduring
attitudinal shifts. High -involvement product categories —electronics, fashion, personal care —tend to
elicit central route pr ocessing, emphasizing the importance of influencer expertise and UGC
informativeness.
2.4 Uses and Gratifications Theory (UGT)
Uses and Gratifications Theory posits that media users actively select platforms and content formats to
gratify specific psychological, social, and cognitive needs [10]. Applied to social commerce, UGT
illuminates why Gen Z engages with platforms in commer ce-relevant ways. Social gratifications —
community belonging, identity expression, peer recognition —drive brand community participation and
UGC creation. Informational gratifications motivate product research and influencer content
consumption. Hedonic grat ifications sustain engagement with short -form video commerce and live -
stream shopping events. Platform features enabling participatory consumption —reaction mechanisms,
comment threads, community hashtags, creator monetization tools —are powerful affordances for brand
engagement and growth [11].

3. Generation Z Consumer Behavior in Social Commerce
3.1 Digital Nativity and Platform Fluency
Generation Z's consumer behavior is inseparable from their digital socialization. Unlike Millennials who
transitioned from  analog to digital media, Gen Z has been immersed in digitally mediated social
environments from early childhood, producing exceptional platform fluency —the ability to navigate
multiple platforms simultaneously, code -switch between platform -specific content norms, and leverage
algorithmic dynamics for community engagement [12]. In social commerce, this manifests as
sophisticated cross -channel behavior: a product discovery can trigger research on YouTube, price
comparison on Amazon, peer consultation on Redd it, and final purchase via Instagram Shopping —all
within a single decision episode. Brands lacking consistent, platform -native presences across this
fragmented ecosystem risk losing Gen Z consumers at critical purchase journey touchpoints.
3.2 Authenticity Orientation and Distrust of Traditional Advertising
A defining characteristic of Gen Z consumer behavior is deep, structurally embedded skepticism toward
traditional advertising [13]. Extensive childhood exposure to digital advertising has produced
sophisticated advertising literacy and heightened sensitivity to inauthenticity and commercial intent.
Polished, professionally produced brand content is frequently perceived as intrinsically suspect.
Conversely, Gen Z demonstrates markedly higher receptivity to  peer-generated content, unscripted
influencer narratives, and values -aligned brand activism. Research consistently demonstrates that UGC
is perceived as significantly more trustworthy and persuasive than equivalent branded content [14].

3.3 Micro-Influencer Affinity and Social Validation Dynamics
Gen Z demonstrates a  distinctive preference for micro -influencers—content creators with follower
counts from 1,000 to 100,000 —over macro -influencers or celebrity endorsers [15]. Micro -influencers
are perceived as more accessible, relatable, and less commercially compromised. Their communities
exhibit high engagement density, strong topical specificity, and elevated interpersonal trust. Engagement
metrics—likes, shares, saves, comments —function as quantitative proxies for community endorsement,
reducing uncertainty in the consumer decision process. Research demonstrates that Gen Z consumers are
significantly more likely to trust product -related content displaying high engagement metrics,
independent of content quality [16]. Engineering high -visibility community engagement is the refore as
strategically important as content quality in social commerce.
3.4 Live-Stream Commerce and Frictionless Purchase
Live-stream commerce —wherein creators or brand representatives conduct real -time product
demonstrations and sales events via social platforms—has emerged as a powerful channel for Gen Z
engagement [17]. The format combines informational richness with community participation dynamics
and real -time urgency cues. The integration of in -app checkout capabilities within live shopping and
short-video contexts eliminates platform transition friction, compressing the decision journey from
discovery to purchase. Platforms including Instagram Live Shopping, and YouTube Shopping have
invested substantially in these capabilities, creating seamless c ommerce experiences that convert social
engagement into transactional outcomes [18]. For brands targeting Gen Z, native platform commerce
capabilities represent a high-priority strategic investment.

4. Conceptual Framework and Research Hypotheses
4.1 Framework Overview
The proposed framework conceptualizes social commerce as a mediating ecosystem between brand
strategic activities and measurable growth outcomes. Five independent antecedent constructs are
identified: (1) Influencer Credibility, (2) UGC Quali ty, (3) Platform Affordances, (4) Perceived
Authenticity, and (5) Algorithmic Personalization. These antecedents exert direct and mediated effects
on two intermediate variables —Brand Engagement and Purchase Intention —which in turn drive the
dependent const ruct of Brand Growth, operationalized as brand awareness, loyalty, market share
expansion, and revenue growth. Four moderating variables —digital literacy, social media frequency,
cultural disposition, and algorithm transparency perception —account for bound ary conditions on the
proposed relationships.

4.2 Influencer Credibility
Influencer credibility is a multidimensional construct encompassing expertise (perceived knowledge and
competence in a product domain), trustworthiness (perceived honesty and integrity), attractiveness
(physical and social appeal), and homophily (perceived similarity between the influen cer and their
audience) [19]. Expertise activates central route processing (per ELM), leading to durable attitude
formation and higher purchase intention. Trustworthiness reduces perceived risk in high -involvement
categories. Attractiveness generates posit ive affect transfer from influencer to product. Homophily —
particularly pronounced in micro-influencer communities—facilitates social identification and normative
influence [20]. Influencer credibility is theorized to exert positive effects on both brand en gagement and
purchase intention, with perceived authenticity mediating the pathway to brand loyalty.
 H1: Influencer credibility positively influences brand engagement among Gen Z consumers in social
commerce contexts.
 H2: Perceived authenticity mediates the relationship between influencer credibility and brand
loyalty.
4.3 User-Generated Content Quality
UGC encompasses textual reviews, photographs, videos, ratings, and social media posts created by
consumers rather than brands [21]. UGC quality is concept ualized as integrating relevance (addressing
decision-relevant information), authenticity (perceived genuineness), informativeness (richness and
accuracy of product information), and virality potential (likelihood of amplification within social
networks). High-quality UGC reduces information asymmetry, provides peer -validated social proof,
generates organic brand visibility, and creates self-sustaining content ecosystems that reduce dependence
on paid advertising. For Gen Z, UGC represents the gold standard  of product information, superseding
branded content in credibility and persuasive impact [22]. Brands facilitating high -quality UGC through
product seeding, hashtag campaigns, and creator rewards are theorized to achieve superior brand
engagement and conversion outcomes.
 H3: User-generated content quality significantly and positively influences purchase intention among
Gen Z social commerce consumers.
4.4 Platform Affordances
Platform affordances refer to the functional capabilities of social media platforms that enable commerce-
facilitating consumer behavior [23]. Key affordances include in -app checkout functionality, live
shopping capabilities, augmented reality product try -on features, short -form video commerce, and AI -
powered product discovery feeds. In-app checkout eliminates platform transition—a major source of cart
abandonment—by enabling transactions within the social discovery environment. Live shopping
activates both informational and normative social influence in a single commerce event. AR try -on
features address fit and appearance uncertainty, a critical barrier to online apparel and beauty purchase
[24]. Platform affordances are theorized to moderate the relationship between social commerce
antecedents and purchase intention, amplifying conversion effects on higher-affordance platforms.

4.5 Perceived Authenticity
Perceived authenticity is perhaps the most  critical construct in social commerce marketing to Gen Z,
reflecting this cohort's pronounced sensitivity to genuineness, transparency, and values alignment [25].
Authenticity dimensions include transparency (openness of commercial relationship disclosure ), values
alignment (congruence between influencer or brand values and consumer values), narrative consistency
(coherence of brand storytelling across platforms and time), and aesthetic sincerity (deliberate use of
unpolished content to signal non -commerciality). Authenticity perceptions exert effects through both
affective mechanisms —generating positive brand affect, emotional identification, and psychological
closeness—and cognitive mechanisms, reducing skepticism and lowering persuasion defenses [26].
Brands investing in honest product communication, transparent influencer disclosure, and community -
centered content strategies achieve superior brand loyalty outcomes.
 H5: Perceived authenticity directly and positively influences brand engagement and mediate s the
effects of influencer credibility on brand loyalty.
4.6 Algorithmic Personalization
Algorithmic personalization is an increasingly important yet theoretically underexplored antecedent in
social commerce [27]. Platform algorithms dynamically curate co ntent feeds based on engagement
history, search patterns, and social graph characteristics, generating highly individualized product
discovery environments. Gen Z consumers exhibit a paradoxical relationship with algorithmic
personalization: appreciating relevance and serendipitous discovery while expressing concern about data
privacy and filter bubble effects [28]. The framework proposes that algorithmic personalization creates
virtuous cycles in which high-engagement content receives amplification generating further engagement
and brand visibility. Brands aligning strategies with platform algorithm dynamics —through consistent
posting schedules, trending content formats, and community interaction management —achieve
disproportionately superior brand awareness and market penetration.
 H6: Algorithmic personalization significantly amplifies the positive relationship between social
engagement metrics and brand growth outcomes among Gen Z consumers.

4.7 Moderating Variables
The framework incorporates four moderating variables delineating boundary conditions on the proposed
relationships. Digital literacy —encompassing platform -specific knowledge, content evaluation skills,
and algorithmic awareness —is expected to strengthen po sitive effects of platform affordances on
purchase intention, as more digitally literate consumers are better positioned to exploit in -app commerce
capabilities. Social media frequency of use is expected to amplify the effects of UGC quality and
influencer credibility, reflecting greater exposure among high -frequency users. Cultural disposition —
particularly collectivist versus individualist orientations —is expected to moderate the relative influence
of normative versus informational social influence mechani sms. Finally, algorithm transparency
perception is expected to moderate the relationship between algorithmic personalization and brand
engagement, with higher transparency awareness producing more deliberate consumer responses to
algorithmically curated content [29].

5. Implications and Future Research Directions
5.1 Theoretical Contributions
The proposed framework makes several important theoretical contributions. First, it provides an
integrated, multi-theoretical model synthesizing TAM, Social Influence Theory, ELM, and UGT within
a single coherent framework specific to Gen Z social commerce behavior. Second, it extends existing
social commerce models by incorporating algorithmic personalization as a formal antecedent construct,
acknowledging the growing centrality of platform AI in mediating consumer-brand interactions. Third, it
theorizes a mediating role for perceived authenticity, providing a more granular account of how
influencer credibility translates into brand loyalty —a relationship prior research  has largely assumed
rather than unpacked [30]. Fourth, the repositioning of platform affordances as moderating rather than
antecedent constructs is a theoretical refinement suggesting that platform capability investment without
corresponding content quality may fail to generate expected brand growth benefits.
5.2 Managerial Implications
The framework generates actionable implications for brand managers. First, investment strategies should
prioritize micro-influencer partnerships over macro-influencer or celebrity endorsements, given superior
engagement density, community trust, and perceived authenticity in micro -influencer ecosystems [31].
Influencer selection should be guided by credibility dimensions —expertise, trustworthiness, and
audience homophily —rather than reach metrics alone. Second, brands should invest systematically in
UGC facilitation infrastructure through hashtag campaigns, product seeding, consumer creator reward
mechanisms, and UGC amplification strategies, creating self -sustaining peer -validated content
ecosystems. Third, authenticity must be operationalized as a strategic imperative through transparent
influencer partnership disclosure, honest acknowledgment of product limitations, and values -driven
brand positioning consistent across plat forms. Fourth, brands should develop native platform commerce
capabilities—live-stream shopping programs, in -app product collections, AR try -on integrations, and
shoppable short -video content —to eliminate conversion friction [32]. Fifth, brand managers sho uld
develop algorithmic intelligence capabilities to optimize content strategies for platform amplification,
including consistent posting schedules, community interaction management, and paid amplification of
high-performing organic content.

6. Conclusion
This article has proposed a comprehensiv e conceptual framework for understanding the mechanisms
through which social commerce activities drive brand growth among Generation Z consumers.
Grounded in four complementary theoretical traditions —TAM, Social Influence Theory, ELM, and
UGT—the framework  integrates five antecedent constructs, two mediating constructs, and four
moderating variables within a coherent model of social commerce brand growth dynamics. Its
theoretical contributions lie in its integrative scope, the extension of existing models t o include
algorithmic personalization, the nuanced theorization of perceived authenticity as mediator, and the
repositioning of platform affordances as moderating variables. Its managerial contributions provide
actionable guidance for brand managers throug h micro -influencer partnerships, UGC ecosystem
investment, authenticity strategies, native platform commerce development, and algorithmic intelligence
capabilities.
Generation Z represents not only the largest current consumer cohort but also the template for the digital
consumer of the future. Understanding the social commerce dynamics governing their brand
relationships is a strategic imperative for brands seeking to capture their purchasing power today and to
build brand equities that will sustain compet itive advantage through the coming decades of digital
commerce evolution. The framework presented here represents a contribution toward that understanding
and a foundation for the empirical research program it invites.
