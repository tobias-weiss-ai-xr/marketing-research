Abstract
Political communication in Nigeria has undergone a fundamental transformation in the past
decade, with the rise of social media platforms reshaping how political actors engage the
electorate. What was once primarily a field of policy persuasion and ideological contestation has
evolved into a market of personality, image, and identity. This article examines how political
marketing and image -making have become defining features of Nigeria’s democratic process,
particularly during the 2023 general elections. The study situates Nigeria within the broader
African experience of digital democracy, arguing that the adoption of marketing logics, rooted in
advertising, branding, and influencer culture has redefined the meaning of political participation
and representation.  Drawing on Political Marketing Theory (Lees -Marshment, 2001) and the
Mediatization of Politics framework (Mazzoleni & Schulz, 1999), the paper critically analyses
how Nigerian politicians deploy digital platforms to construct appealing personal brands and
mobilise affective identities among citizens. Empirical observations from recent electoral cycles
demonstrate that campaign communication often privileges image over ideology and emotion
over deliberation (Abubakar, 2023; Ojebode & Akinola, 2022). For instance, Peter Obi’s
“Obidient” movement, Bola Tinubu’s “Emilokan” rhetoric, and Atiku Abubakar’s “Unified
Nigeria” slogan illustrate how digital messaging translated political choice into identity
performance, where affiliation to a candidate symbolised belonging to a cultural or generational
bloc (Olorunnisola, 2024).   While political marketing has expanded participation, especially
among young voters, it has also deepened Nigeria’s politics of spectacle. Studies show that
social media campaigns frequently accentuate ethno -religious symbols and celebrity
endorsement, reinforcing affective polarisation and diminishing policy substance (Odeyemi &
Mosan, 2023; Ezeibe et al., 2024). The absence of strong media regulation and low digital
literacy exacerbate these challenges, allowing misinformation and image manipulation to shape
voter perception (Adesina, 2023).   The article concludes that the Nigerian experience reflects

Introduction
Democracy in Nigeria has always been a story of voices seeking recognition and power-seeking
meaning. From the struggle for independence to the more recent calls for inclusion and
accountability, politics in the country has remained both an art of persuasion and a performance
of identity. Yet in recent years, the stage has changed. The digital revolution has transformed the
public sphere, turning campaigns into markets and citizens into consumers of political imagery.
The emergence of social media has rewritten the language of politics. What once depended on
the party manifesto or the moral weight of ideology now depends on the grammar of branding
and the currency of attention. On Twitter, Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok, politicians are not
simply leaders; they are curated personalities who sell emotions as much as ideas. The Nigerian
voter is no longer only a participant in democratic discourse but also an audience member within
a spectacle of personality and persuasion.
This transformation speaks to a deeper paradox at the heart of contemporary democracy. In
principle, democracy rests on reason, dialogue, and collective judgment. In practice, digital
communication privileges speed, visibility, and sentiment. During the 2023 general elections,
this paradox reached its loudest expression. Political messages were not designed to enlighten
but to excite. Slogans became identities. Bola Ahmed Tinubu’s “Emilokan,” Peter Obi’s
“Obidient,” and Atiku Abubakar’s “Unified Nigeria” were not just campaign phrases; they
became social movements with emotional and generational undertones. Each slogan invited
allegiance not merely to a policy idea but to a community of feeling and belonging.
What is unfolding is not merely a change in campaign strategy but a transformation in how
citizens imagine democracy itself. The logic of marketing has entered the heart of governance,
reordering relationships between leaders and the led. This study therefore explores how political
branding, image-making, and digital mobilization have reshaped Nigeria’s democratic landscape.
It asks what happens when political communication adopts the culture of advertising, and when
leadership becomes a matter of packaging rather than persuasion.
Two theoretical perspectives guide this exploration. The first is Political Marketing Theory, as
developed by Lees -Marshment (2001), which argues that politicians increasingly behave like
market-oriented actors, tailoring their image to match public preferences. The second is the
Mediatization of Politics Framework  articulated by Mazzoleni and Schulz (1999), which
explains how the media’s logic - its style, rhythm, and emphasis - has come to influence political
practice. Together, these theories provide a lens to understand how Nigeria’s digital environment
now defines not only how politics is communicated but how it is conceived.
In the African context, this transformation mirrors broader continental patterns where digital
media have both energised participation and complicated governance (Nyabola, 2018; Mare,

Literature Review
Political Marketing and the Personalization of Power
The language of marketing has gradually entered the bloodstream of politics. Once, politics was
about persuasion through policy and principle. Today, it is about persuasion through personality
and performance. Scholars of political marketing describe this shift as a transformation from
party-centered campaigning to market-oriented politics (Lees-Marshment, 2001). Politicians no
longer define their vision and seek followers; they study voters as consumers, identifying
preferences, tailoring messages, and packaging leadership as a desirable product.
In advanced democracies, this process is often managed by professional strategists who translate
political goals into marketable narratives (Scammell, 2015). In emerging democracies such as
Nigeria, this marketing culture has taken on a more emotional texture. The electorate, weary of
broken promises, often responds not to policies but to affective cues : gestures, slogans, religious
language, and ethnic symbols that provide a sense of familiarity and belonging. As Ojebode and
Akinola (2022) observed, Nigerian campaigns increasingly deploy imagery that appeals to
personal charisma and authenticity rather than programmatic vision.
This shift towards personalization is not without consequence. It deepens the politics of spectacle
where visibility becomes the measure of legitimacy. Politicians are judged less by what they
build and more by how they appear. A candidate with compelling online presence can eclipse
another with a richer policy agenda. Such image -centered politics risks reducing democracy to a
theatre of emotions, where civic reasoning yields to personal adoration.

Mediatization and the Power of the Digital Sphere
The Mediatization of Politics  theory, proposed by Mazzoleni and Schulz (1999), explains how
media no longer merely report politics but actively shape its content, tone, and rhythm. In
Nigeria, this has become even more pronounced with the rise of digital media. Television once
set the pace of political storytelling; now, social media algorithms do. Political actors learn to
speak the language of virality, brevity, and performance.
Mare (2021) notes that African politicians increasingly frame their messages to suit the aesthetic
of social platforms, where attention is fleeting and emotion is currency. Nigerian campaigns
during the 2023 elections demonstrated this vividly. A video clip, a tweet, or a meme could set
the day’s political agenda. Moments such as Bola Tinubu’s “Emilokan” speech or Peter Obi’s
youth-driven rallies were magnified through digital repetition, transforming political expression
into viral storytelling.
The effect of mediatization is that political communication becomes dependent on the logic of
media visibility. The citizen’s experience of governance is now filtered through digital frames.
What trends online often overshadows what happens on the ground. Olorunnisola (2024) argues

Identity, Emotion, and the Digital Citizen
Beyond communication and marketing lies the deeper question of identity. In Nigeria, political
identity has long been intertwined with ethnicity, religion, and region. The digital era has not
erased these divisions; rather, it has amplified them in new ways. Ezeibe, Kalu, and Oji (2024)
show that online discourse often mirrors offline cleavages, with ethno -religious sentiments
resurfacing in memes, hashtags, and digital debates.
At the same time, digital spaces have empowered new identities, especially among young
Nigerians who no longer see themselves merely as ethnic representatives but as members of a
global digital generation. The 2020 #EndSARS movement, and later the youth -driven
enthusiasm around Peter Obi’s candidacy, reveal how social media can channel frustration into
solidarity. Adesina (2023) cautions, however, that the same platforms that mobilise can also
misinform, allowing political actors to manipulate perception through coordinated propaganda
and image distortion.
This dual reality defines Nigeria’s digital democracy: a vibrant, participatory space where
citizens are more expressive than ever, yet also more vulnerable to manipulation. As Abubakar
(2023) notes, the 2023 elections saw unprecedented levels of digital engagement but also
unprecedented levels of misinformation. Political marketing thus functions in a fragile ecosystem
where hope and deception coexist.

Reframing Democracy in the Age of Digital Branding
The intersection of marketing and mediatization reveals a deeper philosophical challenge.
Democracy, in its original moral vision, was never meant to be a contest of images but a
conversation of ideas. When the art of governance is replaced by the art of branding, citizens risk
becoming spectators rather than participants. Scholars such as Stromer -Galley (2014) and
Coleman (2017) warn that digital campaigning often creates the illusion of inclusion without the
substance of deliberation.
For Nigeria, the challenge is to reclaim the ethical foundations of political communication. The
task is not to reject digital tools but to reimagine how they can serve the public good. Civic
education, media ethics, and responsible political leadership are essential to ensure that
technology amplifies democracy rather than distorts it. The goal is to build a political culture
where communication enlightens rather than entertains, and where political identity is rooted in
citizenship rather than spectacle.

Methodology
Research Design
This study adopts a qualitative research design rooted in interpretive inquiry. The goal is not only
to describe patterns of political communication but to understand their meaning within Nigeria’s
democratic culture. The interpretive approach allows for reflection on how image -making,
branding, and digital interaction shape political behavior and public perception. Rather than
focusing solely on measurable variables, it attends to symbols, language, and narratives that
define the experience of digital democracy.
The study is guided by the principles of Political Marketing Theory (Lees-Marshment, 2001) and
the Mediatization of Politics Framework  (Mazzoleni & Schulz, 1999), which together provide a

Scope and Case Selection
The study focuses on Nigeria’s 2023 general elections , a period that vividly captured the
convergence of marketing, identity, and digital mobilization. The elections marked a
generational turning point, with youth participation, online movements, and emotional branding
reshaping political engagement. Three major presidential candidates: Bola Ahmed Tinubu, Atiku
Abubakar, and Peter Obi  were selected as focal points of analysis because their campaigns
represented distinct models of political marketing and image construction.
The cases were examined not as personalities but as communicative symbols , each articulating
different logics of visibility and belonging. Tinubu’s “Emilokan” narrative was studied as a form
of populist branding rooted in personal entitlement and regional appeal. Atiku’s “Unified
Nigeria” campaign reflected a managerial brand of inclusivity, while Peter Obi’s “Obidient
Movement” embodied digital populism built on youth activism and authenticity.

Data Sources and Collection
Data were drawn from three main sources:
1. Digital Campaign Materials:
Content was collected from verified social media accounts, including Twitter (now X),
Facebook, Instagram, and YouTube, focusing on the official campaign period from
September 2022 to February 2023. Posts, videos, and hashtags related to the three major
candidates were sampled systematically.
2. Media and Scholarly Reports:
Credible newspapers such as The Guardian, Premium Times , and Channels Television,
alongside peer -reviewed works on digital democracy in Africa (e.g., Abubakar, 2023;
Odeyemi & Mosan, 2023), were reviewed to contextualize how these campaigns were
framed in public discourse.
3. Public Engagement and Observation:
The study also observed digital interactions and comment threads, particularly those
involving the “Obidient” online community, youth influencers, and fact -checking
platforms. This enabled a richer understanding of how citizens interpreted and performed
political identity online.

Data Analysis
The analysis followed thematic content analysis, guided by Braun and Clarke’s (2006) model for
identifying, analyzing, and interpreting patterns in qualitative data. Each campaign’s digital
footprint was examined to identify recurring themes of branding, emotional appeal, identity
construction, and media framing. Data were coded manually, and the findings were interpreted in
light of the two theoretical frameworks to reveal underlying meanings and contradictions.

Particular attention was given to how campaign communication expressed three

Trustworthiness and Ethical Considerations
Credibility was ensured through triangulation of data sources, comparing social media narratives
with news reports and scholarly analyses. Authenticity was enhanced by verifying digital content
through official campaign accounts and recognized fact-checking organizations such as Dubawa
and Africa Check.
Ethical standards were observed by maintaining the anonymity of online contributors and
respecting the integrity of publicly available information. No private data were accessed. The
study aligns with academic research ethics, valuing truth, respect, and responsibility in
interpreting human communication.

Philosophical Orientation
The study is grounded in pragmatic interpretivism , which recognizes that truth in political
communication is socially constructed and context -dependent. Pragmatism allows the researcher
to move beyond rigid theoretical boundaries and embrace multiple forms of evidence  like
textual, visual, and emotional. This orientation acknowledges that in the digital age, meaning is
not fixed but negotiated in real time between politicians and the public.

Findings and Discussion
1. From Manifesto to Market: The Rise of Political Branding
The first major finding of the study is the visible shift from ideological persuasion to personal
branding in Nigerian political communication. Campaigns during the 2023 elections were not
primarily designed to explain policies but to sell personalities. The language of politics echoed
that of consumer advertising, where the leader was the product and the voter was the buyer.
Across the social media ecosystem, candidates were not evaluated by the coherence of their
programmes but by the emotional texture of their presence. Bola Ahmed Tinubu’s “Emilokan”
narrative projected a sense of inevitability and entitlement - a personal history reframed as
destiny. Peter Obi’s “Obidient Movement” built a reputation of authenticity and moral
cleanliness, positioning him as the alternative to a corrupt establishment. Atiku Abubakar’s
“Unified Nigeria” message sought to embody inclusivity and managerial competence.
This movement from manifesto to market confirms Lees -Marshment’s (2001) observation that
modern politicians behave like market actors who tailor their image to match audience
expectations. In Nigeria’s case, political identity became a consumable product. Campaign
jingles, memes, and hashtags replaced policy debates. Citizens were invited to join movements
not by reasoned conviction but by emotional resonance.
While this branding logic expanded visibility, it also reduced politics to theatre. What emerged
was a democracy of impressions - what Ojebode and Akinola (2022) describe as “performative
governance,” where appearance substitutes for substance. The digital stage rewarded charisma,
humor, and spectacle over coherence.

2. The Mediatized Arena: When Visibility Equals Legitimacy
The second finding reveals the overwhelming power of mediatization. In the digital environment,
visibility became synonymous with legitimacy. The most visible candidate was often perceived
as the most viable. Social media posts, viral videos, and trending hashtags were not simply
campaign tools; they became instruments of perceived authority.
Peter Obi’s campaign best illustrates this phenomenon. His digital presence on Twitter and
TikTok gave him unprecedented cultural visibility, especially among urban youth. The
“Obidient” movement demonstrated how mediatization transforms citizens into active
participants in constructing a candidate’s image. As Olorunnisola (2024) notes, the movement’s
online engagement created a new language of belonging, where political support became a
declaration of moral and generational identity.
However, mediatization also produced distortions. Emotionally charged narratives circulated
more easily than factual information. Short video clips, dramatic images, and edited speeches
spread faster than policy documents. This reflects Mazzoleni and Schulz’s (1999) warning that
mediatization changes political logic: the rhythm of media, not the rhythm of governance, sets
the pace. In Nigeria, campaign strategies were redesigned to suit this rhythm. Candidates
measured success not by public understanding but by digital reach.

3. Emotion, Identity, and the Digital Citizen
The third finding highlights the centrality of emotion and identity in digital political
participation. Nigerian politics has always drawn heavily from ethnic and religious symbols, yet
the 2023 elections gave these old allegiances new digital life. Hashtags and memes carried
regional undertones. Visual symbols  like flags, traditional attire, and cultural gestures were
reinterpreted online as tools of solidarity.
Ezeibe, Kalu, and Oji (2024) note that online discourse in Nigeria often mirrors offline divisions,
but the digital sphere adds immediacy and intensity. The study found that discussions around
candidates frequently degenerated into moralised tribal debates. Support for a candidate became
a statement of cultural loyalty rather than policy alignment.
Yet the same digital platforms also fostered new forms of civic identity. Many young Nigerians,
especially first -time voters, found a sense of empowerment in participating online. They
produced graphics, podcasts, and campaign songs, expressing political consciousness that was
creative and assertive. Abubakar (2023) describes this as the “democratisation of voice” i.e a
process where every phone becomes a microphone. However, the abundance of voices also
generated confusion, as misinformation often blurred the line between truth and propaganda
(Adesina, 2023).
The emotional charge of digital politics made engagement passionate but volatile. Supporters
defended candidates as if defending faith. Online debates turned into battles of identity,
reflecting what Coleman (2017) calls the “affective turn” in democracy , where belonging and
feeling replace rational argument.

4. The Spectacle and Its Shadow: Consequences for Democratic Accountability
The fourth finding exposes a troubling paradox. While digital participation expanded democratic
expression, it simultaneously weakened accountability. Political marketing encouraged citizens
to relate to leaders as fans, not as employers. The spectacle of digital visibility masked the
absence of institutional depth.

Odeyemi and Mosan (2023) observed that many social media campaigns privileged celebrity
endorsement and emotional storytelling over governance plans. This spectacle -driven politics
blurred the boundary between entertainment and statecraft. Candidates mastered the art of being
seen but not necessarily the art of being understood.
The study found little evidence that digital visibility translated into policy literacy among voters.
Instead, it reinforced what Stromer -Galley (2014) describes as the “illusion of participation ”,
where citizens feel involved but have limited influence on real decision-making. For Nigeria, this
illusion is particularly dangerous because it replaces civic vigilance with emotional satisfaction.
Democracy becomes a brand to admire, not a process to question.

5. Towards a Communicative Democracy
Despite these contradictions, the digital transformation of Nigerian politics carries redemptive
potential. Social media has broken the monopoly of traditional media and opened channels for
marginalized voices. It has enabled youth movements, advocacy groups, and ordinary citizens to
speak directly to power. The challenge is how to convert this visibility into accountability.
For democracy to mature in the digital age, Nigeria must move from performative
communication to communicative democracy , a concept rooted in Habermas’ (1996) idea of
deliberative engagement. This means re -establishing spaces for thoughtful dialogue, fact -based
conversation, and civic education. Digital citizenship should cultivate discernment, not just
excitement. Political marketing must serve transparency, not manipulation.
The findings therefore call for a moral reawakening in Nigeria’s democratic communication.
Leadership must rediscover the humility to listen, and citizens must reclaim the courage to
question. Only then can the digital public sphere become not a market of personalities but a
meeting place of ideas.

Conclusion and Recommendations
The Nigerian experience demonstrates the twin -edged nature of digital democracy, its ability to
amplify voices and also distort political meaning. Political marketing, while an inevitable feature
of modern governance, has redefined the spirit of participation, shifting the emphasis from
ideology to imagery, from persuasion to performance, and from governance to popularity. The
digital space has become a theatre of identity and influence where citizens engage less as
informed voters and more as brand consumers. This new political rhythm, though vibrant and
participatory, risks weakening the ethical foundations of democracy if it continues without
deliberate institutional guidance.
The 2023 general elections revealed both the vitality and vulnerability of Nigeria’s digital public
sphere. Campaigns driven by branding and influencer culture attracted millions of young people
into the political conversation, but they also encouraged emotional partisanship, misinformation,
and shallow engagement with issues of governance. The spectacle of political marketing has thus
both illuminated and clouded Nigeria’s democratic horizon , making politics visible, yet often
void of substance.
To sustain the democratic promise amid these contradictions, Nigeria must move beyond
branding to genuine communication that informs, educates, and empowers. Democracy should
not be staged for optics; it must be lived through accountable leadership and enlightened
citizenship.

 The following policy-oriented recommendations emerge from this study:
1. Institutionalizing Media and Digital Literacy for Civic Engagement
There is a pressing need to integrate digital literacy and media ethics into Nigeria’s civic
education curricula, beginning from secondary schools through tertiary institutions and adult
education platforms. The National Orientation Agency, in partnership with civil society groups
and the Ministry of Education, should promote nationwide programmes that help citizens
critically evaluate online political content, discern misinformation, and understand the persuasive
techniques embedded in political marketing. Informed digital citizens will be less vulnerable to
manipulation and better equipped to hold leaders accountable in the digital space.

2. Establishing a Regulatory Framework for Political Communication Ethics
Nigeria’s Independent National Electoral Commission (INEC), in collaboration with the
Nigerian Broadcasting Commission (NBC) and the Advertising Regulatory Council of Nigeria
(ARCON), should develop a unified “Code of Political Communication Ethics.” This code
would set clear boundaries for digital campaigns, addressing issues such as misinformation, hate
speech, identity-based propaganda, and foreign-sponsored advertisements. By adopting standards
similar to those used in developed democracies, Nigeria can protect its electoral environment
from digital abuse and restore integrity to the communication process.

3.Strengthening Governance Communication and Institutional Transparency
Beyond elections, government institutions must embrace continuous, transparent, and evidence -
based communication with citizens. Ministries, Departments, and Agencies should employ
professional communicators who are trained in ethical political marketing and governance
reporting. Public communication should shift from personality projection to policy explanation ,
helping citizens see governance as a shared enterprise rather than a performance. When the state
leads by example in honest communication, political actors and the media ecosystem will follow
suit, gradually transforming the culture of spectacle into one of substance.
In sum, Nigeria’s democracy stands at a critical crossroads where image competes with integrity
and virality often replaces veracity. Yet, within this tension lies an opportunity  to rebuild a
democratic culture that honours truth, transparency, and trust. The future of Nigeria’s political
communication will depend not merely on how leaders brand themselves, but on how
meaningfully they speak, listen, and govern in the digital age.
