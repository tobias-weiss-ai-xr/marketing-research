Abstract
This paper examines the potential of social media as a supplementary planning
resource for understanding public space branding by relating digitally mediated
visibility and perception to established physical public space attributes. Despite
extensive literature on digital placemaking and hybrid public space, physical public
space assessment and digital engagement analysis remain methodologically
disconnected. Social media is often treated descriptively rather than as an operational
planning input, and planners lack replicable tools to diagnose alignment between
spatial quality and mediated perception. This study addresses that gap by framing
public space branding as a governance process, concerned with recognition,
legitimacy, symbolic continuity and shared meaning, rather than as a promotional
activity.
An exploratory mixed-methods design combines a desk-based physical public space
assessment, social media analysis, and a supplementary public perception survey.
Fifteen internationally recognized public spaces were evaluated across their physical
and digital dimensions. The social media dataset comprised 750 publicly visible
Facebook and Instagram posts collected via Meta Content Library API, published
between 1 January 2025 and 31 July 2026 and retrieved through a synchronized
data-collection session in August 2026. A separate online survey completed by
200 respondents provided complementary evidence for triangulating recognition,
prior social media exposure, perceived spatial qualities, and visitation interest. The
physical and digital composite scores showed only a weak, statistically non-significant
association (Spearman’s ρ = 0.32, p = 0.239), indicating that digital performance
should not be treated as a direct proxy for physical public space quality. A physical-
digital alignment matrix classifies the cases into four diagnostic conditions: aligned
success, physical under-recognition, low-profile performance, and digitally amplified
experience gaps.
The study contributes to digital placemaking and urban planning research by
treating social media as an exploratory analytical input rather than a marketing-
oriented measure of success. It proposes a five-phase diagnostic framework linking
physical assessment, digital diagnosis, strategic intervention, and monitoring. The
framework is comparative and exploratory; it uses physical-digital alignment to

Introduction
Public spaces have long been central to urban life, supporting social interaction, cultural
expression, and collective experience. Planning and urban design have traditionally eval-
uated them through physical and experiential attributes such as accessibility, comfort,
identity, sociability and diversity of activities. While these criteria remain fundamen -
tal, contemporary public spaces increasingly operate across both physical and digitally
mediated realms, extending beyond on-site experience into representational and net -
worked environments. The widespread adoption of social media has introduced a digital
layer through which public spaces are represented, interpreted, and circulated. User-
generated content—including images, reviews, hashtags, and check-ins—shapes public
awareness, expectations, and decisions to visit urban spaces.
This development also has important implications for public space branding. In this
study, public space branding is explicitly conceptualized as a governance and place-iden-
tity process rather than as a marketing activity, promotional campaign, visual-identity
exercise, or tourism-marketing strategy. It is understood as a continuing process through
which a public space acquires recognition, legitimacy, symbolic continuity, and shared
meaning. Public space brands are produced through the interaction of physical form,
everyday use, institutional representation, cultural memory, and user-generated nar -
ratives. Digital visibility therefore forms part of the governance of place identity rather
than an external layer of publicity.
As a result, public spaces are no longer experienced solely through direct physical
encounter; mediated representations also influence recognition, visibility, and place
identity. These digitally mediated encounters increasingly shape how public spaces are
anticipated, remembered, and evaluated. Despite this transformation, urban planning
practice has been slow to systematically incorporate digital evidence into public space
evaluation, management, and governance. Social media can therefore be considered a
supplementary planning resource that provides signals about public perception, repre -
sentation, and evolving place identity. However, concepts such as digital placemaking
and hybrid public space remain insufficiently operationalized within reproducible plan -
ning workflows.
Although interest in digital placemaking and hybrid public space has grown, three
methodological limitations remain. First, social media is frequently analysed descrip -
tively, with emphasis on representation or communication rather than integration into
planning workflows. Second, physical public space evaluation and digital engagement
analysis remain methodologically disconnected, limiting their combined analytical
value. Third, planners lack transparent and replicable tools for diagnosing how digitally
mediated representation aligns—or conflicts—with spatial performance. Addressing
these gaps requires moving beyond conceptual discussions toward methods that inte -
grate physical and digital evidence while retaining the limitations of each source.
This paper responds to these challenges by developing an exploratory diagnostic
framework that integrates physical public space assessment with digital indicators of
recognizing that platform metrics are partial, sample-dependent, and complementary
to field-based and participatory evidence.

The comparison is not intended to create a universal ranking of public spaces or to

demonstrate strong performance in both dimensions, strong physical quality with lim -

accompanied by specific experiential deficiencies. These conditions require different
governance responses.
Accordingly, the study addresses three research questions:
1. How can physical public space quality and digital visibility and perception be
integrated within a transparent planning-oriented assessment method?
2. What patterns of physical–digital alignment and misalignment emerge across the
fifteen selected public spaces?
3. How can these alignment patterns inform public space branding, spatial intervention,
management, and adaptive urban governance?
The contribution of the research is threefold. First, it operationalizes hybrid public space
through a comparative framework that examines physical performance alongside digi -
tally mediated representation. Second, it reframes public space branding as a governance
process concerned with the relationship among spatial value, public meaning, and medi-
ated recognition. Third, it translates patterns of alignment and misalignment into dif -
ferentiated planning responses rather than assuming that increased digital visibility is
desirable in every case.
Literature review
Physical public space quality
Public space quality is commonly assessed through physical and experiential attributes
such as accessibility, comfort, sociability, activity, and identity [ 1–4]. These attributes
influence whether people can reach and navigate a space, feel secure and comfortable,
remain for extended periods, participate in activities, and interact with others. Recent
reviews similarly show that connectivity, environmental conditions, amenities, perceived
safety, mixed uses, and opportunities for social encounter shape the social performance
of public spaces [5].
Public space quality, however, cannot be reduced to functional performance alone [ 6].
Public spaces acquire cultural and symbolic value through repeated use, collective mem-
ory, recognition, social relationships, and shared experience [ 7]. Place identity develops
through the interaction between physical setting, activities, and meanings attached to a
location over time [ 8, 9]. This understanding is consistent with Lefebvre’s view of space
as socially produced through the relationship among material form, everyday practices,
and representation [ 10]. Public space is therefore simultaneously a physical environ -
ment, a setting for social practice, and a symbolically interpreted place.

Place identity is not static or fully controlled by institutions. It develops through con -
tinuous interaction between internal understandings of a place and its external repre -
sentations [11]. A public space brand therefore cannot be created only through a logo,
slogan, or promotional campaign. It emerges from the relationship between physi -
cal quality, patterns of use, cultural memory, management, and the narratives through
which a space is represented and recognized.
Public space branding
This relationship between spatial quality and collective meaning is central to public space
branding [11, 12]. Earlier place-branding approaches often emphasized tourism, investment,
competition, and visual promotion. More recent literature treats place brands as dynamic
constructions shaped through interactions among spatial experience, institutional narratives,
residents, visitors, businesses, cultural practices, and public perceptions [11, 13].
In this study, public space branding is defined as a governance-related process through
which a space acquires recognition, legitimacy, symbolic continuity, and public meaning.
Promotion remains relevant, but it is treated as one component within a broader process
in which the represented image of a space should correspond to its lived physical and
social conditions.
Understanding branding as identity formation connects it directly to urban gover -
nance. Place branding increasingly forms part of local policy, urban planning, spatial
development, stakeholder coordination, and the management of urban reputation [ 12–
14]. It can influence which spaces become visible, memorable, visited, funded, protected,
or prioritized because public representations affect stakeholder perception, institutional
attention, and political legitimacy [15].
Branding as a planning and governance tool
Branding is not politically neutral. Institutions, residents, visitors, businesses, tourists,
and digital publics may construct different and sometimes conflicting meanings around
the same location [ 16]. Decisions about which histories, users, activities, and images
represent a place may include some groups while marginalizing others [ 17]. Eshuis,
Klijn, and Braun [ 14] show that place branding can address the emotional dimension of
public policy but also note that branding processes are often top-down and provide lim -
ited influence for citizens.
The role of governance in public space branding therefore extends beyond produc -
ing promotional material. It includes maintaining the physical environment, protecting
accessibility, coordinating stakeholders, programming activities, managing crowding
and tourism, preserving cultural identity, and monitoring public perception [1, 2, 12, 14].
A credible public space brand depends on correspondence between public representa -
tion and lived experience.
This distinction has practical consequences. A highly visible public space may still suf -
fer from overcrowding, environmental discomfort, poor maintenance, insufficient seat -
ing, weak greenery, exclusion, or conflicts between tourists and everyday users [ 18].
In such cases, further promotion may intensify existing pressures rather than improve
public value. Conversely, a space may perform well physically and socially but remain
digitally under-recognized because of fragmented naming, linguistic differences, weak
discoverability, or limited communication [19].

Social media branding for hybrid public space
Digital platforms have become part of this governance process because they enable insti-
tutions and users to represent public spaces continuously (see Fig.  1). Platform content
may reinforce official narratives, introduce alternative meanings, reveal dissatisfaction,
or expose differences between promoted images and everyday conditions [ 11, 19, 20].
Social media analysis can therefore help public authorities identify under-recognized
spaces, detect emerging narratives, monitor recurring concerns, and assess whether dig -
ital representation corresponds with physical quality [ 19, 20]. It should, however, com -
plement rather than replace direct participation and field-based evidence.
Social media platforms generate user-produced evidence about how places are visited,
represented, interpreted, and emotionally evaluated [ 20, 21]. Posts, photographs, reels,
captions, comments, hashtags, and geotags can reveal aspects of activity and perception
that may be difficult to capture through short observation periods or one-time surveys
[19, 22, 23, 24]. Facebook and Instagram are especially relevant because their visual and
hashtag-based content circulates architecture, landscape, events, heritage, and everyday
use, shaping how places are recognized beyond their immediate location [25, 26].
Hybrid space refers to the co-constitution of physical settings and digital information.
A hybrid public space is therefore not simply a physical site with a separate online image;
its anticipation, use, memory, and recognition are shaped through interaction between
on-site conditions and digitally mediated representations [ 27]. This relationship reflects
the broader impact of digitalization on social interaction and public space [ 28]. Digi -
tal placemaking is defined here as the collaborative production and circulation of place
meaning through user-generated digital content [ 29]. It extends beyond destination
marketing to include everyday documentation, discussion, and reinterpretation of public
space [29, 30]. In this study, the digital dimension is operationalized through four ana -
lytically distinct indicators—presence, engagement, reach, and sentiment—derived from
established social media functions and urban social media research [ 19, 23, 30].
Social media digital indicators
Hashtags are especially important because they aggregate posts into searchable streams
and contribute to the visibility and recognizability of recurring narratives. Loukianov,
Burningham, and Jackson [ 26] showed that hashtag co-occurrence influences which
interpretations become prominent within Instagram discourse. Hashtags should

This study uses the most specific primary hashtag as an indicator of presence, not as a
direct measure of physical attendance or population-level awareness, because hashtags
can contain unrelated content [ 26, 31]. Variants and broader proxies are reported con -
textually but should be excluded from the principal presence score where attribution is
uncertain.
Digital engagement and content circulation also require conceptual separation. Visible
likes and comments indicate interaction with sampled content, whereas shares indicate
redistribution or circulation. Hashtag volume captures accumulated place-specific visi -
bility but does not necessarily indicate current activity. Presence, engagement, and reach
are therefore related but distinct dimensions [ 19, 23]. Sentiment provides an additional
interpretive dimension by indicating whether sampled posts communicate predomi -
nantly positive, neutral/mixed, or negative evaluations [20, 21].
Accordingly, this study adopts four quantitative social media dimensions: presence,
engagement, reach, and sentiment. Presence represents the accumulated visibility asso -
ciated with the selected place-specific hashtag. Engagement represents visible interac -
tion with sampled content through likes and comments. Reach is operationalized as
content circulation through recorded shares, while sentiment represents the evaluative
orientation of the sampled content.
Research gap: toward a planning-oriented integration of physical and digital dimensions
Previous research has examined the relationship between digital platforms, social
interaction, and spatial experience from multiple perspectives, including engagement
measurement [ 32–34], mediated identity and impression formation [ 35, 36], and the
transformation of public space through digital and networked practices [ 28, 37, 38,
39, 40]. Chen, Parkins, and Sherren [ 25] demonstrated that geotagged Instagram pho -
tographs and captions can reveal social and cultural landscape values and their spatial
concentration. Their findings also showed a strong emphasis on aesthetic value, reflect -
ing Instagram’s visually oriented culture. Instagram data can therefore reveal meaningful
place-related patterns, but these patterns are partly shaped by what users consider visu -
ally attractive and worth sharing.
Other research has supported combining Instagram content with conventional meth -
ods. Krajewski et al. [ 41] found that Instagram effectively revealed visual, spatial, and
seasonal patterns, while surveys identified a broader range of concerns, including envi -
ronmental deterioration and infrastructure problems.
Moreover, social media data has also been applied directly to public space evaluation.
Zhang et al. [ 20] developed a post-occupancy evaluation of Bryant Park based on plat -
form content, activities, spatial settings, relationships, and sentiment. Li et al. [ 21] sim-
ilarly used social media big data to examine public space perception, activities, visual
preferences, and emotional responses. These studies show that user-generated content
can extend public space evaluation beyond isolated field visits and reveal recurring pat -
terns over broader temporal periods. While these studies provide important insights
into digitally mediated interaction, they rarely translate such insights into planning-ori -
ented evaluation or governance tools.

This fragmentation reveals a critical gap in the literature: the absence of a structured,
planning-oriented framework that explicitly links physical public space attributes with
social media-based indicators in a replicable, and governance-relevant manner. Address-
ing this gap requires moving beyond conceptual discussions of hybrid space and brand -
ing toward methodologies that support comparative evaluation and differentiated
intervention across both physical and digital domains. The present study addresses this
gap through an exploratory mixed-methods comparison of fifteen international public
spaces. Its contribution lies not in treating social media metrics as objective measures of
quality, but in integrating physical and digital evidence to diagnose patterns of alignment
and misalignment.
Social media is therefore treated as a supplementary operational input that provides
signals about how public spaces are represented and perceived. The study uses these
signals alongside physical assessment to identify gaps between spatial performance and
mediated representation and proposes a five-phase diagnostic framework for selecting
planning and governance responses according to the observed physical-digital align -
ment condition.
Methodology
This study used an exploratory mixed-methods design to compare physical public space
quality with social media visibility and mediated perception across fifteen international
cases. The design was comparative rather than causal and did not treat digital activity
as an objective measure of public space success. Digital evidence was used as a comple -
mentary diagnostic layer alongside desk-based physical assessment and supplementary
survey evidence.
The methodology is organized into three connected stages across all fifteen cases.
First, an exploratory online visual survey examined recognition, prior social media expo-
sure, perceived spatial qualities, and visitation interest. Second, physical and digital indi -
cators were collected, coded, standardized, and compared. Third, the resulting patterns
were translated into a physical-digital alignment matrix and a five-phase adaptive gover -
nance workflow.
Case study selection
Fifteen internationally recognized public spaces were selected through purposive theo -
retical sampling for methodological calibration rather than statistical representation (see
Fig. 2). Ten cases were drawn from Project for Public spaces (PPS) to provide a physi -
cal-quality benchmark, while five complementary cases were included to expand varia -
tion in digital visibility, geography, and contemporary urban use. Selection followed five
criteria: (1) sufficient documentation in urban-planning, public space, or urban-design
literature; (2) an identifiable digital presence on Facebook and Instagram; (3) representa-
tion of different public space typologies; (4) geographic and cultural variation; and (5)
sufficient recognizability to support the visual survey. The selection logic is summarized
in Appendix B, Table B2.
Comparison is justified because all cases were examined using the same physical attri -
butes, digital indicators, scoring procedures, and interpretative criteria. The sample is
not claimed to represent public spaces globally; instead, its typological and geographical
diversity is used to test whether the framework can identify recurring physical–digital

Exploratory online survey
An online visual survey was administered from 4 to 16 September 2024 and presented
material relating to the fifteen selected public spaces (see Appendix A). It examined
prior recognition, previous social media exposure, perceived spatial qualities, symbolic
identity, and visitation interest. The survey was completed by 200 participants recruited
through online convenience sampling, mainly via Facebook. For each case, a representa -
tive image was followed by 10 standardized questions (Table A1).
The survey sample should not be interpreted as statistically representative of the
general urban population. It provides exploratory evidence from respondents reached
through a digitally connected convenience-sampling network. This is relevant to the
study because participants were likely to encounter social media representations of pub -
lic spaces, but it also introduces sampling bias and may underrepresent people with lim -
ited digital access or those outside the recruitment network [42].
The survey was not used to calculate the physical or digital composite scores and was
not treated as a direct validation of later platform metrics. Instead, it provided supple -
mentary perception evidence for triangulation. Q1 and Q2 contextualized place recog -
nition and prior social media exposure; Q3-Q7 provided image-based perceptions of
sociability, comfort, activity, accessibility, and identity; Q8 measured visitation interest;
and Q9-Q10 provided qualitative context on recognizable features and perceived place
orientation. Because the survey preceded the 2025–2026 social media sampling period,
these measures are interpreted as complementary rather than temporally matched vali -
dation data.

Physical public space assessment
Physical quality was assessed through five categories derived from established public
space literature: sociability, accessibility, comfort, activity, and identity [ 1, 2, 9]. These
categories were operationalized through 21 observable sub-indicators presented in
Appendix B. Sociability covered diversity of users, harmony, interaction, informal
encounters, and opportunities for staying. Accessibility addressed walkability, connec -
tivity, ease of arrival, inclusivity, and integration with the surrounding urban fabric.
Comfort included seating, shade, lighting, perceived safety, environmental conditions,
cleanliness, maintenance, and evidence of management. Activity addressed the diversity
of uses, functionality, density, and bustle. Identity covered reputation, cultural meaning,
symbolic value, recognizability, and collective memory.
Each sub-indicator was scored on a five-point ordinal scale, where 1 represented very
weak performance and 5 represented very strong performance. Because the cases were
geographically dispersed, the assessment was desk-based and drew on published studies,
official documents, maps, photographs, and other available visual evidence. The result -
ing values were treated as comparative expert scores rather than direct field measure -
ments or absolute measures of quality.
Equal weighting was used across the five main physical categories to prioritize trans -
parency and interpretability and to avoid giving additional weight to categories that con-
tain more sub-indicators. This is a methodological choice rather than a claim that all
dimensions are universally equal in importance. The physical composite score for public

Digital public space assessment
The social media assessment combined primary hashtag analytics with post-level con -
tent analysis. Data were assembled from Meta Content Library API in a synchronized
retrieval session on 1 August 2026 using an eligible publication window from 1 January
2025 to 31 July 2026. Post-level public Facebook and Instagram records were treated as
a fixed historical sample rather than a live feed. Primary-hashtag volume values were
obtained from Meta Content Library API. Post-level interaction measures were recorded
from the retained public posts and analysed separately from the hashtag-volume proxy.
One place-specific primary hashtag was selected for each public space (see Appen -
dix C). Alternative spellings, misspellings, generic hashtags, local-language variants,
and broader geographic proxies were documented contextually but excluded from the
principal presence calculation when attribution was uncertain. Approximately 50 high-
engagement public posts were screened for each case using the place name, primary
hashtag, and available place-specific location cues. After eligibility screening, the final
analytical sample contained 750 records across 15 cases, with 49–51 retained posts per
case (373 Facebook and 377 Instagram posts). Because posts were selected from high-
engagement results, the sample is intentionally analytical rather than statistically repre -
sentative of all platform content.

Records were retained when they were publicly visible, confidently attributable to the
selected space, within the defined publication period, non-duplicated, and sufficiently
informative for visual or textual interpretation. Inaccessible, duplicate, ambiguous, or
unrelated records were excluded before the analytical workbook was finalized. The post-
level dataset recorded the public space, post ID, URL or search reference where avail -
able, platform, posting date, account information, follower count where available, likes,
comments, shares, content description, and sentiment category.
Four social media indicators were examined. Presence represented accumulated vis -
ibility using the selected primary-hashtag volume proxy. Engagement represented visible
interaction with the retained posts and was calculated from standardized average likes
and comments. Reach was operationalized as content circulation using the recorded
number of shares. Sentiment represented the evaluative orientation of the retained
posts. These indicators were kept conceptually distinct because they describe different
aspects of mediated performance and are affected by different platforms and sampling
mechanisms.
Comparative analysis
Analysis proceeded in two stages. First, physical and digital composite scores were com -
pared across the fifteen cases to identify broad patterns. Second, qualitative cross-case
analysis was used to interpret the planning significance behind numerical relationships.
Cases were positioned within the physical–digital alignment matrix using study spe -
cific diagnostic thresholds. Strong physical performance was defined as a physical
composite score of 4.0 (80%) or above on the five-point assessment scale, representing
performance between the strong and very strong rubric anchors across the five physical
categories. Digital performance above 3.0 was used as a pragmatic reference point on the
standardized one-to-five composite scale. Because the digital scores are sample-depen -
dent min-max transformations, 3.0 should not be interpreted as a universal or theoreti -
cally neutral benchmark. The thresholds are analytical decision rules used to structure
comparison, and cases close to either threshold are interpreted alongside their disaggre -
gated component scores. The four diagnostic conditions were aligned success, physical
under-recognition, low-profile performance, and digitally amplified experience gap.
Results
The results presented in this section synthesize findings from the physical public space
assessment, social media analysis, and exploratory visual perception survey. The analysis
is comparative and does not establish causality. It focuses on observed case-level pat -
terns and associations across the selected public spaces and uses them as an empirical
basis for the diagnostic framework proposed in the subsequent section.
Survey results
The online visual survey was completed by 200 respondents, who assessed the same
fifteen public spaces. Demographics were aged 25–34 years ( n = 150, 75%), followed by
those aged 35–60 years ( n = 30, 15%) and 18–24 years ( n = 20, 10%). Of the respondents,
55.0% were female (n = 110) and 45.0% were male ( n = 90). Regarding educational attain-
ment, 48.5% were graduates ( n = 97), 47.5% held postgraduate qualifications (MSc or
PhD; n = 95), and 4.0% were undergraduates (n = 8).

Self-reported social media use was high among 55% of respondents ( n = 110), medium
among 35% ( n = 70), and low among 10% ( n = 20). When asked whether social media
affects decisions to visit public spaces, 85% answered “Yes” ( n = 170), 10% selected
“Maybe” (n = 20), and 5% did not provide an answer ( n = 10). Similarly, 75% believed that
social media affects the success of public spaces ( n = 150), 15% selected “Maybe” (n = 30),
and 10% answered “No” (n = 20).
Across the fifteen cases, mean prior awareness was 51.7%, mean previous social media
exposure was 36.0%, and mean visitation interest was 78.7%. The relatively high level
of visitation interest compared with prior awareness indicates that unfamiliarity did not
necessarily prevent respondents from expressing interest after viewing an image of the
place.
Low recognition did not necessarily correspond to low attraction. Rittenhouse Square
generated 65% visitation interest despite prior awareness of only 10%. Granville Island
also generated 65% interest with awareness of 15%, while Union Square produced 75%
interest despite awareness of 20%. Covent Garden similarly achieved 75% visitation
interest with prior awareness of 30%. These results show that prior awareness, previous
social media exposure, and visitation interest represent related but distinct aspects of
public perception. A place may have limited recognition but still generate considerable
attraction when visually presented.
Prior awareness was strongly and positively associated with previous social media
exposure (Spearman’s ρ = 0.910, p < 0.001) and moderately associated with visitation
interest (ρ = 0.583, p = 0.023). However, previous social media exposure was not signifi -
cantly associated with visitation interest (ρ = 0.440, p = 0.100). These findings indicate
that online exposure is closely related to place recognition, while recognition is also
associated with stated interest in visiting. The relationships are exploratory and should
not be interpreted causally.
These findings indicate that visual attractiveness may operate as a pre-visitation fil -
ter, allowing places with limited prior recognition to generate interest when presented
through selected images. However, image-based attractiveness should not be equated
with the quality of direct physical experience because a single image cannot fully com -
municate accessibility, environmental comfort, management conditions, crowding, or
everyday patterns of use.

Social media dataset and digital performance

Proposed diagnostic framework and governance application
The results demonstrate that physical public space quality and digitally mediated perfor-
mance should be considered as related but distinct dimensions. The weak, non-signifi -
cant association between physical and digital composite scores shows that strong spatial
performance does not necessarily produce equivalent digital recognition. Conversely,
high digital visibility does not guarantee strong physical performance. At the same time,
the survey results indicate that previous social media exposure is strongly associated
with prior awareness, demonstrating the relevance of mediated visibility to how public
spaces become recognized.
Building on these findings, this study proposes a Physical–Digital Governance Frame -
work that translates physical–digital alignment and misalignment into differenti -
ated planning responses. The framework is not intended to rank public spaces or treat
social media performance as a measure of spatial quality. Rather, it is designed to iden -
tify whether physical performance and mediated representation reinforce one another,
diverge, or conceal weaknesses that require further investigation. The framework con -
sists of two connected components:
(1) a physical–digital alignment matrix, which diagnoses the relationship between
physical and digital performance; and
(2)  a five-phase governance workflow, which translates that diagnosis into planning
action and subsequent monitoring.

Planning use and governance implications
The framework changes the role assigned to social media within public space planning.
Rather than treating platforms primarily as promotional channels, it uses digitally medi -
ated information as a supplementary source of evidence about visibility, recognition,
perception, and public representation. This distinction is important because greater vis -
ibility is not always beneficial. For a physically under-recognized public space, improved
interpretation and discoverability may be appropriate. For a digitally amplified experi -
ence-gap space, the opposite response may be required: physical and managerial con -
ditions should be improved before further visibility is encouraged. For already highly
visible spaces, governance may instead need to manage demand and protect everyday
public use.
The framework consequently links branding to questions of spatial quality, identity,
inclusivity, management, and public meaning. Digital representation becomes one com -
ponent of adaptive public space governance rather than an independent measure of
success. The integration of digitally mediated perception into planning practice raises
important governance and ethical considerations. Platform algorithms, unequal digital

regeneration prioritization, and adaptive public space governance processes.

Discussion
Physical performance, digital representation, and public perception
The central finding of this study is that physical public space quality and digitally medi -
ated performance are related but distinct dimensions. Across the fifteen cases, strong
physical performance did not consistently correspond with equally strong digital rep -
resentation. Digital visibility therefore cannot be treated as a direct proxy for spatial
quality. It is also shaped by factors such as landmark status, tourism intensity, visual dis -
tinctiveness, events, hashtag specificity, language, account influence, and platform algo -
rithms [19, 25, 26, 31].
The survey results provide a clearer relationship between digital exposure and public
recognition. Previous social media exposure was strongly associated with prior aware -
ness (Spearman’s ρ = 0.910, p < 0.001), while prior awareness showed a moderate associa-
tion with visitation interest (ρ = 0.583, p = 0.023). These findings suggest that mediated
exposure is closely connected to place recognition and that familiarity may contribute to
interest in visiting.
These relationships should not, however, be interpreted causally. Highly recogniz -
able public spaces may attract greater social media coverage precisely because they are
already internationally known, while repeated exposure may simultaneously reinforce
recognition. Tourism, cultural significance, media coverage, major events, and symbolic
identity may influence both variables. Social media exposure is therefore better under -
stood as an indicator of mediated familiarity than as evidence that online visibility inde -
pendently produces awareness or visitation.
The results consequently support the understanding of contemporary public spaces
as hybrid environments in which physical experience and digital representation interact
without necessarily corresponding proportionally [27, 29, 43, 44]. A physically successful
space may remain digitally under-recognized, while a highly visible space may still con -
tain important experiential weaknesses.
Physical–digital misalignment as a planning signal
The physical–digital alignment matrix makes these differences explicit. Using the study-
specific thresholds of ≥ 4.0 for strong physical performance and > 3.0 for above-midpoint
digital performance, six cases were classified as aligned success, five as physically under-
recognized, two as low-profile performance, and two as digitally amplified experience
gaps.
The six aligned-success cases—Millennium Park, Bryant Park, Covent Garden, Marina
Bay Sands Promenade, Times Square, and the National Mall—demonstrate broad cor -
respondence between physical performance and digital recognition. However, align -
ment should not be interpreted as the absence of problems. Times Square, for example,
achieved very high physical and digital composite scores while the disaggregated physi -
cal assessment still identified concerns related to crowding, noise, limited greenery and
shade, thermal comfort, opportunities for rest, and commercialization. In such cases,
the planning priority is not further promotion but the management of visibility, visitor
pressure, environmental comfort, and inclusive everyday use.
Union Square, the High Line, Granville Island, Rittenhouse Square, and Jubilee Gar -
dens South Bank were classified as physically under-recognized. Their physical perfor -
mance exceeded the adopted threshold while their digital performance remained lower.

This suggests that spatial quality is not automatically translated into equivalent digital
recognition. Fragmented naming, weak place-specific hashtags, limited discoverability,
or competition with wider geographical and commercial narratives may contribute to
this condition. The appropriate response is therefore more likely to involve interpreta -

discoverability than major physical reconstruction.
Piazza del Campo and Gyeongui Line Forest Park occupied the low-profile category.

Plaza Mayor and Jemaa el-Fnaa represent the opposite condition: digitally amplified

cultural significance, tourism, symbolic identity, and visual distinctiveness may sustain
strong mediated attraction despite weaknesses in the lived experience. Additional pro -

to comfort, accessibility, environmental quality, commercialization, or management.
The key planning implication is therefore that misalignment is diagnostic rather than
evaluative. The matrix does not identify successful and unsuccessful places in absolute
terms. Instead, it indicates where physical conditions and mediated representation cor -
respond and where further investigation is required.
From public space branding to adaptive governance
These findings support a shift from understanding public space branding as promotion
toward understanding it as a form of governance. Conventional branding approaches
often emphasize visibility, attractiveness, tourism, or competitiveness. The present
results show that maximizing visibility is not an appropriate objective for every public
space.
A credible public space identity depends on the relationship between what a place
offers physically, what it means culturally and socially, and how it is represented pub -
licly. Branding should therefore concern the management of relationships among spatial
performance, symbolic identity, public recognition, and mediated representation rather
than the production of promotional imagery alone [11, 13, 14].
This interpretation leads to differentiated planning responses. Aligned-success spaces
require maintenance, demand management, and protection of inclusive everyday use.
Physically under-recognized spaces may require stronger interpretation and discover -
ability. Low-profile spaces require physical or programmatic improvement before pro -
motional expansion. Digitally amplified experience-gap spaces require physical and
managerial intervention before further amplification.
The proposed five-phase framework operationalizes this logic through physical base -
line assessment, digital diagnosis, alignment analysis, strategic intervention, and moni -
toring. Its purpose is not to prescribe one branding strategy but to help determine
whether a space primarily requires physical improvement, stronger recognition, man -
agement of visibility, or continued monitoring.

Social media as a complementary planning resource
Facebook and Instagram provide useful but partial evidence about how public spaces
are represented and circulated. Presence, engagement, reach, and sentiment reveal dif -
ferent aspects of mediated performance and should remain conceptually distinct. Pres -
ence indicates accumulated place-specific visibility; engagement reflects interaction
with sampled content; reach reflects content circulation through sharing; and sentiment
describes the evaluative orientation of the sampled posts. Combining them facilitates
comparison, but the indicators are not interchangeable.
Several limitations are particularly important for planning. First, social media users
are not representative of all public space users. Participation varies according to age,
income, language, digital access, literacy, platform use, and willingness to publish con -
tent. Private users, non-users, and less digitally active communities leave limited digital
evidence [19, 42].
Second, social media content is affected by visual-selection bias. Landmark architec -
ture, events, attractive landscapes, food, performances, and photogenic activities are
more likely to be shared than inaccessible routes, inadequate seating, poor maintenance,
heat, noise, exclusion, or routine inconvenience [ 25]. A predominantly positive online
representation can therefore coexist with important physical or managerial problems.
Third, platform metrics themselves are ambiguous. Hashtag volume does not repre -
sent unique visitors or population-level awareness, while spelling variations, transla -
tions, abbreviations, and generic hashtags can fragment or inflate digital presence [ 26,
31]. Likes, comments, and shares are similarly affected by account size, influencers, con -
tent format, platform culture, and algorithmic ranking.
These limitations explain why high visibility should not be interpreted as evidence
of spatial success and why low visibility does not imply low public value. Social media
evidence should instead be triangulated with direct observation, behavioural mapping,
accessibility audits, environmental measurements, surveys, interviews, maintenance
records, and participatory engagement.
The appropriate role of social media in planning is therefore as a complementary diag -
nostic layer: useful for identifying emerging narratives, under-recognition, mediated
attraction, changing sentiment, or possible gaps between representation and lived expe -
rience, but insufficient as a stand-alone basis for planning decisions.
Theoretical and practical contribution
The theoretical contribution of this study lies in demonstrating that hybrid public space
performance is characterized by interaction without necessary correspondence. Physi -
cal quality, public recognition, and mediated representation influence one another but
remain analytically distinct. This extends discussions of hybrid space and digital place -
making by positioning digitally mediated perception within public space evaluation
without treating platform visibility as a substitute for spatial quality [29, 43, 44].
The practical contribution lies in translating this distinction into a diagnostic planning
approach. The physical–digital alignment matrix identifies whether spatial performance
and mediated representation broadly correspond, while the governance framework links
different conditions to differentiated planning responses. The purpose is therefore not
to rank public spaces but to determine whether the principal requirement is physical
improvement, stronger recognition, management of visibility, or continued monitoring.

ernance. Its value lies primarily in helping planners ask what type of intervention is
appropriate, rather than simply whether a public space performs well or poorly.
Limitations and future research
Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the fifteen cases were purposively
selected and primarily represent internationally recognized or visually distinctive pub -
lic spaces. Their physical and digital dynamics may differ from those of ordinary public
spaces, which can be locally significant places or spaces with limited tourism exposure.
The sample therefore supports analytical comparison rather than global generalization.
Second, the physical assessment was desk-based. Although the standardized indicator
structure supports comparison across cases, it cannot replace direct observation, behav -
ioural mapping, environmental measurement, or detailed engagement with everyday
users.
Third, the social media analysis was based on 750 publicly visible Facebook and Insta -
gram posts selected from high-engagement results. The sample therefore overrepresents
content that achieved greater platform visibility and should not be interpreted as repre -
sentative of all posts or all public opinion. Private content, non-users, undocumented
experiences, and activity outside these platforms are absent. Platform algorithms,
account characteristics, hashtag practices, content format, and changing interface con -
ditions may also influence what becomes visible and measurable. In addition, sentiment
coding followed a single standardized workflow, but inter-coder reliability was not quan-
tified in the current dataset.
The supplementary survey used convenience sampling through digitally connected
academic networks, and visitation interest represents stated intention rather than
observed behaviour. The survey relationships should therefore be interpreted as explor -
atory case-level associations rather than population-level estimates. Future research
should employ larger-scale and more representative samples, including stratified sam -
pling across relevant demographic and geographic groups where feasible, to reduce
selection bias and improve the generalizability of the survey findings.
Finally, equal weighting, sample-dependent standardization, and the thresholds of 4.0
and 3.0 simplify relationships that are inherently contextual. The digital cut-off is par -
ticularly sensitive for cases close to 3.0. These decisions were adopted to create a trans -
parent and reproducible diagnostic procedure rather than universal standards of public
space performance, and borderline classifications should therefore be interpreted using
disaggregated indicators.
Future research should test alternative weighting and threshold approaches, expand
the framework to neighbourhood-scale and everyday public spaces, and exam -
ine changes longitudinally. Combining platform data with direct environmental

Overall, the findings demonstrate that social media can strengthen public space plan -
ning when it is used to identify questions and diagnostic signals rather than provide
final judgments. Digital representation offers valuable evidence concerning recognition,
attraction, and mediated perception, but assessments of public space quality and deci -
sions about intervention must remain grounded in physical conditions, lived experience,
and public participation.
Conclusion
This study advances public space planning by examining digitally mediated perception
as a complementary component of contemporary public space governance. By com -
paring physical public space attributes with social media visibility, engagement, reach,
sentiment, and survey-based awareness, the research addresses the methodological
separation between conventional spatial evaluation and digital analysis. The findings
show that physical quality and digital performance interact but do not correspond con -
sistently, supporting the use of digital evidence as a diagnostic planning resource rather
than as a promotional measure or direct proxy for spatial quality.
The primary contribution is an exploratory planning-oriented framework that inte -
grates physical public space assessment with social media indicators of presence,
engagement, reach, and sentiment. Rather than treating branding as a marketing activity,
the framework positions public space branding as a governance process concerned with
place identity, recognition, public meaning, and the correspondence between repre -
sented and lived conditions. This approach enables planners to diagnose broad patterns
of alignment or misalignment and to identify differentiated priorities for intervention.
Empirical findings from the fifteen cases show only a weak, statistically non-signifi -
cant association between the physical and digital composite scores (Spearman’s ρ = 0.32,
p = 0.239). This indicates that stronger physical performance is not consistently accom -
panied by stronger digital performance. The alignment matrix therefore functions as a
diagnostic device: it distinguishes cases where physical and digital dimensions broadly
correspond from cases where under-recognition, low-profile performance, or digitally
amplified experience gaps warrant different planning responses.
By reframing social media as an operational planning resource, this research contrib -
utes to ongoing debates on public space, digital placemaking, and urban governance.
The proposed framework offers planners, designers, and policymakers a structured and
transferable method for incorporating digitally mediated perception into public space
evaluation and decision-making processes. For planning practice, this framework can
be used during post-occupancy evaluation, public space regeneration prioritization,
and adaptive management to identify where spatial performance, public perception, and
symbolic identity diverge.
The framework reframes public space branding as a governance process concerned
with the relationship between lived experience, symbolic identity, public recognition,
and mediated representation. Social media evidence should nevertheless remain supple -
mentary to field observation, behavioural analysis, surveys, interviews, environmental
assessment, and public participation because platform data are affected by demographic

further support the development of planning approaches that respond to the increas -
