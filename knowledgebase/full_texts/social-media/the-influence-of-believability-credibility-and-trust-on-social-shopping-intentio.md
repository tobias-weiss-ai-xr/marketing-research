Abstract
This study examines the psychological mechanisms shaping social shopping intention (SSI) by
analysing the roles of believability, credibility and trust while assessing the mediating effect of subjective
norms. Given quantitative approach, measurement scales were adapted from prior research and reﬁned
for the study context. Survey data from 376 Vietnamese Generation Z consumers were analysed using
PLS-SEM with SmartPLS to test the proposed relationships. Credibility inﬂuences SSI only indirectly
through subjective norms, highlighting the importance of perceived social consensus. In contrast, trust
acts as a strong direct antecedent of SSI, independent of social pressure. Believability shows a weaker
yet dual effect, inﬂuencing SSI both directly and indirectly via subjective norms. Marketing executives
should leverage social proof and community endorsement to strengthen credibility while facilitating
platform trust through security and transparency to encourage purchasing. The study clariﬁes the
distinct roles of trust-based constructs and social inﬂuence, thereby advancing understanding of social
commerce behaviour among digitally connected Generation Z consumers.
1. Introduction
The exponential diffusion of the Internet and social media has transformed enormously the retail
industry, accelerating the evolution of online shopping into socially embedded forms of exchange. Social
commerce, which integrates e-commerce functionalities with social media platforms, has redeﬁned how
consumers communicate, share information and make purchasing decisions (Busalim and Hussin, 2016;
Bowen et al., 2021). This transformation is occurring at an unprecedented scale: global e-commerce
sales are projected to exceed USD 4.3 trillion in 2025, with sustained growth expected in subsequent
years (Gelder, 2025). Simultaneously, Generation Z has emerged as the largest and most digitally native
consumer cohort, accounting for 32% of the global population and an expanding share of worldwide
consumption (Spitznagel, 2020; Watty, 2025; Tang et al., 2026). Despite the growing prominence of
social commerce, its behavioural foundations remain insuﬃciently understood. Existing studies report a
positive association between social commerce features and purchase intention; however, empirical
ﬁndings are fragmented and, at times, inconsistent (Chen et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025). While several
scholars denote the persuasive role of CGRs and eWOM, others highlight the contingent nature of their
effects, depending on source characteristics, platform context or consumer traits (Boateng and Okoe,
2015; Rouibah et al., 2021). Thus, there is limited consensus on how information-related cues translate
into social shopping intention (SSI).
Prior research has examined believability, credibility and trust as separate antecedents of online
purchase intention. Although each construct has been shown to matter, treating them in isolation
obscures their joint inﬂuence within socially rich digital environments, where consumers are
simultaneously exposed to multiple information cues (Cassar et al., 2021; Roh et al., 2022). This
fragmented approach mirrors earlier inconsistencies in adjacent literatures and constrains theory
development regarding trust formation in social commerce contexts (Wang et al., 2022; Atito et al.,
2023). Further, although social commerce is shaped by social interaction, the role of subjective norms

remains underexplored. While consumers particularly Generation Z actively seek validation and guidance
from peers, inﬂuencers and online communities, subjective norms are rarely examined as a mediating
mechanism linking CGRs and eWOM to SSI (Francis and Hoefel, 2019; Chen et al., 2022). This gap limits
understanding of the normative processes through which social inﬂuence is internalised and converted
into behavioural intention. Also, empirical evidence from emerging economies remains scarce,
particularly, Vietnam provides an important context due to its rapid digitalisation, high social media
penetration and increasing reliance on key opinion leaders (Nguyen et al., 2024; Rouibah et al., 2021).
Despite these characteristics, limited research has examined the psychological drivers of SSI among
Vietnamese consumers, particularly Generation Z.
Addressing these gaps, our study draws on theory of planned behaviour and information acceptance
model to develop and empirically test an integrated model examining believability, credibility and trust in
CGRs and eWOM as antecedents of subjective norms and SSI among Generation Z in Vietnam. In doing
so, the study advances social commerce concept by clarifying trust-based and normative mechanisms
and extends existing knowledge to an underexplored emerging-market context. As such, the study is
guided by the following research question:
1. How do believability, credibility and trust differentially inﬂuence social shopping intention among
Generation Z?
2. To what extend does subjective norm act as a full versus partial mediator across these relationships?
Our study contributes to social commerce research in several ways. First, by integrating TPB and IAM, it
develops a uniﬁed framework explaining how believability, credibility and trust shape SSI through
subjective norms, thereby addressing the fragmented treatment of these constructs in prior work (Wang
et al., 2022). Second, it advances understanding of normative mechanisms by positioning subjective
norms as a mediating process through which information cues embedded in CGRs and eWOM translate
into behavioural intention (Chen et al., 2022; Francis and Hoefel, 2019). Third, the study extends the
social commerce literature to an emerging Asia Paciﬁc context, providing empirical evidence from
Vietnam where rapid digitalisation and high social media engagement amplify peer inﬂuence and
community validation in online purchasing (Nguyen et al., 2024; Atito et al., 2023).
2. Theoretical background and hypothesis development
2.1. Theory of planned behaviour (TPB)
According to Ajzen (1991), TPB explains individual behaviour as a function of behavioural intention,
which is shaped by attitudes toward the behaviour, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control.
Central to TPB is the premise that intentions emerge from the interaction between external informational
stimuli and internal cognitive evaluations. Accordingly, intention is more likely to translate into action
when individuals evaluate the behaviour positively, perceive supportive social expectations and believe
they possess adequate control over its execution.
Page 3/25

Owing to its parsimony and adaptability, TPB has been applied across behavioural, managerial and
consumer research contexts. Importantly, the framework allows scholars to incorporate additional
antecedents of intention beyond the three core components, provided these extensions enhance
explanatory power (Ajzen, 2020). Nevertheless, this modular approach has been increasingly questioned.
Critics argue that treating TPB constructs as independent may oversimplify decision-making, as
attitudes, norms and control perceptions often co-evolve and interact in practice (Leong et al., 2022). In
response, recent studies have shifted toward examining the antecedents shaping the core TPB
mechanisms rather than adding parallel predictors.
Building on this perspective, the present study focuses on subjective norms as an indispensable
mechanism linking social information to intention formation. This focus is particularly salient in social
commerce, where consumption decisions are socially embedded and visibility heightens sensitivity to
others’ opinions. WOM) and CGR function as salient normative cues, reﬂecting perceived expectations
and behaviours of signiﬁcant others (Yuniarty et al., 2020). Further, emerging extensions of TPB
incorporate trust to capture the credibility and reliability of social information, further reﬁning the model’s
relevance for digital consumption contexts (Hamid et al., 2022).
2.2. Information acceptance model (IAM)
IAM postulates that individuals adopt information when it is perceived as useful, positioning perceived
usefulness as the central mediating mechanism in the acceptance of advice (Sussman and Siegal,
2003). Rather than assuming direct effects, IAM suggests that argument quality and source credibility
inﬂuence behaviour indirectly through perceived usefulness. Thus, individuals evaluate whether
information is relevant and applicable before accepting it. This mechanism is salient in digital
environments characterised by information abundance where consumers rely on diagnostic cues like
credibility, believability and informativeness to ﬁlter content (Chen et al., 2022; Busalim and Hussin,
2016). Another study further indicates that credible eWOM enhances perceived usefulness and purchase
intention, reinforcing the indirect pathway proposed by IAM (Bilgen and Zoghi, 2017).
IAM is especially relevant in social commerce, where user-generated content is embedded in interactive
and socially visible platforms. High-quality arguments and credible sources increase perceived
usefulness, thereby legitimising information and shaping subsequent evaluations (Cassar et al., 2022).
Moreover, social media interactions amplify this process by embedding informational cues within
broader social contexts where visibility and peer engagement inﬂuence interpretation (Boateng and
Okoe, 2015; Bowen et al., 2021). In this regard, IAM complements the TPB by explaining how external
information is cognitively processed before inﬂuencing intention (Ajzen, 1991). While TPB accounts for
attitudinal and normative drivers, IAM clariﬁes how information becomes inﬂuential.
In social shopping contexts, useful information also signals collective approval, thereby shaping
subjective norms. Credibility and believability of CGRs and eWOM enhance trust and reinforce perceived
social expectations (Gautam et al., 2022). Accordingly, this study integrates IAM with TPB by positioning

credibility and believability as antecedents of subjective norms rather than direct predictors of intention,
thereby extending TPB through an explicit informational mechanism linking social inﬂuence on SSI.
2.3. Believability
Believability refers to the extent to which consumers perceive CGRs and eWOM as realistic, plausible and
convincing, reﬂecting an initial and intuitive judgment formed upon ﬁrst exposure. Prior research
operationalises believability through message quality, relevance, clarity and emotional neutrality (Filieri
and McLeay, 2014). In social commerce environments characterised by information overload,
believability thus serves as a crucial screening mechanism that determines whether information is
attended to or dismissed. Empirical evidence indicates that higher believability enhances purchase
intention, engagement and information sharing on social media platforms (Mulcahy et al., 2024). Further,
believability is formed during early encounters with information, when affective responses and heuristic
processing dominate evaluation (Cassar et al., 2021). These early judgments then shape cognitive
evaluations and inﬂuence normative beliefs and behavioural intentions in socially embedded contexts.
CGRs and eWOM also represent key informational cues guiding online purchase decisions (Cheung and
Thadani, 2012). While CGRs provide experiential insights through ratings, reviews and comments, eWOM,
deﬁned as online statements made by current, former or potential customers, ampliﬁes these signals
through social diffusion (Hennig-Thurau et al., 2004). Importantly, such messages also function as social
signals shaping normative perceptions. When perceived as believable, they legitimize others’ opinions
and behaviours, thereby reinforcing subjective norms (Liu and Lapinski, 2024). Believable information
thus facilitates the internalisation and diffusion of favourable norms, enhancing persuasive impact
(Livingston et al., 2024). However, compared with credibility, its normative role remains underexplored.
The following hypothesis is thus put forward:
H1
Believability of CGRs and eWOM positively inﬂuences subjective norms.
2.4. Credibility
Credibility refers to the perceived trustworthiness and expertise of an information source and reﬂects a
more deliberate evaluation than believability, which focuses on message plausibility (Cheung et al.,
2012). Whereas believability arises from intuitive and affective judgments, credibility involves reﬂective
processing, including comparisons with prior knowledge and social expectations (Cassar et al., 2021).
This distinction is critical, as information may initially appear believable yet be discounted if the source
lacks expertise or integrity. Empirical evidence shows that credible sources strengthen favourable
psychological outcomes, including perceived norms and behavioural intentions (Dang, 2022). In social
commerce, credibility is particularly salient because transactions occur in trust-based digital
environments where uncertainty is high and source reliability is inevitable (Tseng, 2022).

Moreover, credibility functions as a conduit through which informational content is transformed into
normative inﬂuence. Beyond assessing message quality, it signals social proof and collective
endorsement, thereby legitimising consumption choices and reinforcing normative expectations
(Cheung et al., 2009; Cheung and Thadani, 2012). In socially visible digital marketplaces, credible CGRs
and eWOM not convey accuracy but also indicate shared acceptance. Consequently, they strengthen
subjective norms by shaping perceptions of what others deem appropriate. This effect is ampliﬁed in
interactive formats such as livestreaming, where perceived authenticity and professionalism enhance
credibility and reinforce shared beliefs (Wang et al., 2025). Likewise, credibility aligns with descriptive
norms and increases sensitivity to social pressure (Wang and Brown-Devlin, 2022; Cheng et al., 2021),
shaping SSI. Following such insights, we propose:
H2
Credibility of CGRs and eWOM has a positive inﬂuence on subjective norms.
2.5. Trust
Trust reﬂects consumers’ willingness to rely on information and recommendations based on
expectations of honesty, competence and benevolence. Unlike believability and credibility, which are tied
to speciﬁc messages or sources, trust is relational and cumulative, developing through repeated
interactions and consistent experiences (Sung et al., 2021). In online contexts, this is often
conceptualized as e-trust, capturing conﬁdence in digital platforms, peer contributors and social media
environments (Atito et al., 2023). Similarly, trust enhances key behavioural determinants, including
attitudes, perceived control and purchase intention. In social commerce, trust consolidates evaluations
of believability and credibility into a stable expectation of reliability, thereby reducing perceived risk and
facilitating SSI (Aslan, 2023; Roh et al., 2022).
Moreover, trust represents a broader institutional belief in the reliability of online environments and
serves as a foundation for transactional willingness (McKnight et al., 2002). While recent studies
incorporate trust within TPB frameworks, they highlight its direct effects on intention, often neglecting its
interaction with normative processes (Aslan, 2023; Nguyen et al., 2024). Notably, prior research models
subjective norms as antecedents of trust (Kashif et al., 2018). However, in social commerce, trust in
CGRs and eWOM also operates as a salient social cue that signals collective reliability and legitimacy.
Accordingly, trust is likely to shape subjective norms by reinforcing perceptions of socially endorsed
behaviour. Based on this reasoning, the following hypothesis is proposed:
H3
Trust in CGRs and eWOM positively inﬂuences on subjective norms.
2.6. Subjective norms
Page 6/25

Subjective norms capture individuals’ perceptions of social pressure from salient referents involving
friends, family, inﬂuencers and online communities to engage in a behaviour (Putri et al., 2024; Chhabra
et al., 2026). In social commerce, these norms are particularly salient due to the visibility of peer
evaluations and the centrality of social media in shaping consumption discourse. Consumers actively
rely on reviews, ratings and endorsements to infer collective approval and legitimacy (Sintia et al., 2023).
Accordingly, subjective norms function as a key mechanism translating social information into
behavioural intention, especially among digitally native consumers who are highly responsive to peer
validation. Prior studies recognise their mediating role across contexts (McKnight et al., 2002; Parveen et
al., 2025; Putri et al., 2024). Building on IAM and TPB, this study conceptualises subjective norms as a
central mechanism through which informational cues shape SSI. Both frameworks highlight that
decision-making is socially embedded; thus, subjective norms act as an intermediary linking information
evaluation to intention formation. Empirical evidence supports this view, showing that subjective norms
mediate relationships between eWOM-related constructs such as usefulness, trust and credibility as well
as purchase intention (Parveen et al., 2025). Moreover, subjective norms are constructed through
ongoing social interaction rather than ﬁxed individual beliefs (Chhabra et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2023).
Within this framework, believability represents an important antecedent of subjective norms. As an early-
stage, intuitive judgment, believability shapes how consumers engage with and interpret social
information (Jiang et al., 2024). When CGRs and eWOM are perceived as believable, they legitimise
others’ opinions and behaviours, thereby reinforcing perceptions of social approval. Conversely, low
believability or misinformation may distort normative perceptions and trigger resistance (Oktavianus and
Bautista, 2023). Despite its relevance, believability has rarely been examined in conjunction with
subjective norms within mediation models. Addressing this gap, the present study posits that subjective
norms transmit the inﬂuence of believability on SSI.
Credibility, in contrast, reﬂects a more deliberate evaluation of information sources and encompasses
perceptions of trustworthiness, expertise and integrity (Chih et al., 2013; Metzger and Flanagin, 2013).
Unlike believability, credibility involves reﬂective processing and provides a more stable basis for
decision-making in social commerce (Ngo et al., 2024). However, prior research has largely treated
credibility as a direct predictor of intention, overlooking its role in shaping normative perceptions.
Emerging evidence suggests that credibility can act as an antecedent of normative beliefs within
extended TPB frameworks (Aisha et al., 2024). Although ﬁndings remain mixed, credible CGRs and
eWOM are likely to signal socially endorsed choices, thereby strengthening subjective norms and, in turn,
SSI (Maleknia et al., 2025).
Trust represents a broader, relational construct reﬂecting consumers’ willingness to rely on online
information under uncertainty (McKnight et al., 2002). It develops through repeated interactions and
consistent informational quality, fostering favourable cognitive and emotional responses (Putri et al.,
2024). Prior studies consistently link trust to positive shopping outcomes, including intention and
habitual engagement (Hsu et al., 2024). Importantly, trust also operates through social inﬂuence
mechanisms, with evidence indicating that its effects on behaviour are mediated by subjective norms
Page 7/25

and related constructs (Zhao et al., 2023; Parveen et al., 2025). In social commerce, trust in CGRs and
eWOM enhances the perceived legitimacy of collective opinions, thereby reinforcing normative pressures
that encourage purchasing (Xie and Madni, 2023).
Given these arguments, subjective norms function as a crucial bridge between informational cues and
behavioural intention. They generate social proof, enabling individuals to infer appropriate behaviour
from others’ experiences and collective endorsement (Masur et al., 2021; Leong et al., 2021). This role is
particularly pronounced in high-uncertainty contexts and early-stage interactions, where consumers
depend on social cues to guide decisions (Long et al., 2024). Moreover, subjective norms reﬂect both
interpersonal inﬂuence and broader awareness of prevailing trends, further shaping consumer intentions
(Pradana et al., 2024). Their inﬂuence has intensiﬁed in digitally mediated environments, especially
during periods such as the COVID-19 pandemic, when reliance on online information increased (Nguyen
et al., 2022). As such, we advance a uniﬁed mediation perspective in which subjective norms transmit
the effects of believability, credibility and trust on SSI. The following hypotheses are proposed:
H4
Subjective norms mediate the relationship between the believability of CGRs and eWOM and SSI.
H5
Subjective norms have a mediating effect in the relationship between credibility of CGRs and EWOM and
SSI.
H6
Trust in CGRs and eWOM inﬂuences SSI via the mediating role of subjective norms.
H7
Subjective norms have a positive inﬂuence on SSI.

3. Methodology
This study adopts a quantitative approach to empirically test the proposed relationships. Such a design
is appropriate for social commerce and consumer behaviour research as it enables systematic
measurement of latent constructs and examination of causal relationships within a theoretical
framework (Hair et al., 2022). Measurement scales were adopted and adapted from prior validated
studies, ensuring conceptual consistency, contextual relevance and linguistic clarity. The use of
established scales enhances reliability, supports cumulative knowledge development and enables
comparability across studies (Churchill, 1979; Hinkin, 1998). Minor wording adjustments were introduced
Page 8/25

to align with the research context while preserving construct validity following established scale
adaptation guidelines (DeVellis, 2017; Hair et al., 2019). The ﬁnal instrument comprised 19 observed
variables across ﬁve constructs: believability, credibility, trust, subjective norms and SSI (see Appendix
1).
Data was collected through an online questionnaire between November and December 2025 using a
cross-sectional design. All items were measured on a ﬁve-point Likert scale ranging from strongly
disagree (1) to strongly agree (5), a format widely used for capturing attitudinal variation with reliability
(Hair et al., 2019). Prior to full deployment, the questionnaire was pre-tested with 10 participants,
including ﬁve Generation Z consumers and ﬁve academic experts, to ensure clarity and conceptual
accuracy. Minor reﬁnements were made based on their feedback.
The study focuses on Generation Z consumers in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam, a context characterized by
rapid social commerce growth. A total of 925 survey invitations were distributed via Qualtrics, yielding
490 responses. After data screening, 376 valid responses were retained (response rate: 40.65%). The
dataset showed no missing values or data quality issues. Non-response bias was assessed using wave
analysis (Armstrong and Overton, 1977). Independent-sample t-tests between early (n = 191) and late
respondents (n = 185) revealed no signiﬁcant differences, supporting data robustness for the analysis.
4. Findings
Our study adopts an empirical research design and employs Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) to test
the proposed relationships. Following data collection, non-compliant responses were excluded and
Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM) was applied for analysis. PLS-SEM is
widely used in management and behavioural research due to its suitability for prediction-oriented studies
and complex research models, particularly when strict distributional assumptions are relaxed (Hair et al.,
2022). Compared with covariance-based SEM, PLS-SEM offers greater ﬂexibility in applied research
contexts. The analysis was conducted using SmartPLS to evaluate both the measurement and structural
models.

The ﬁnal sample comprised 376 respondents (see Table 1), predominantly women (79.79%, n = 300),
followed by men (20.21%, n = 76) and other genders (0%, n = 0). Although female respondents are

4.3.2. Assessment of coeﬃcient of determination R²
The coeﬃcient of determination (R²) serves as a crucial indicator in regression analysis, reﬂecting the
extent to which independent variables can explain the variance in the dependent variable. A value of R²
approaching 1 indicates a high level of model ﬁt and strong explanatory power. In this study, the adjusted
R² values for the two variables, SN and SSI, range from 0.25 to 0.5, suggesting a relatively limited
explanatory capacity. An R² value below 0.5 implies that the model accounts for less than half of the
variance in the dependent variable. However, this is relatively common in research ﬁelds such as social
sciences, consumer behaviour and other complex human phenomena and does not necessarily
undermine the validity of the model. According to Hair et al. (2019), in behavioural research, particularly

The data illustrates that the f² effect sizes of the independent variables on the dependent variables are

5. Discussion
It is feasible to state that the results underscore the pivotal role of credibility (CR) in shaping subjective
norms (SN) and indirectly social shopping intention (SSI). Credibility exhibits a strong and signiﬁcant
effect on subjective norms and its indirect effect on SSI via SN is also substantial. This ﬁnding indicates
that credibility functions as a foundational social mechanism rather than a purely individual-level cue. In
social shopping environments, credible information such as user reviews and peer comments gains
inﬂuence by signalling collective approval, thereby strengthening perceived social pressure. This pattern
aligns with the TPB, which posits that subjective norms are reinforced by socially endorsed and reliable
information sources.
Notably, the direct effect of credibility on SSI is not statistically supported, which contrasts with prior
studies reporting a direct credibility-intention link (Cheung and Thadani, 2012; Bilgen and Zoghi, 2017;
Amarullah et al., 2022). This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in sample composition and
research context. More importantly, it suggests that credibility affects SSI only through its capacity to
shape group-level normative perceptions. Thus, credibility should be understood as operating through
collective validation rather than individual persuasion.
Believability (BE) also demonstrates a signiﬁcant role in the model. It exerts both direct effects on SSI (β 
= 0.331, p = 0.000) and indirect effects via subjective norms (β = 0.045, p = 0.008), while also positively
inﬂuencing SN (β = 0.166, p = 0.002). Although its effect sizes are smaller than those of credibility, these
results are consistent with prior research identifying believability as a determinant of purchase intention
(Cassar et al., 2021). Therefore, the ﬁndings indicate that information persuasion operates through dual
Page 14/25

pathways, particularly believability shapes immediate evaluative judgments, whereas credibility ampliﬁes
social validation and normative pressure.
In contrast, trust (TR) exhibits a strong direct effect on SSI (β = 0.319, p = 0.000) but no signiﬁcant
indirect effect through subjective norms. This pattern suggests that trust functions as an individual-level
driver of purchase intention, independent of perceived social pressure. In e-commerce contexts, trust in
the platform or seller can directly reduce perceived risk and motivate purchase decisions without
reliance on collective endorsement. The null relationship between trust and subjective norms further
implies that trust and norms operate in parallel, occupying distinct but complementary positions within
the decision-making process.
Finally, the model explains 46.6% of the variance in SSI, indicating substantial explanatory power.
Collectively, credibility, believability, trust and subjective norms play important roles in predicting SSI,
with subjective norms and credibility emerging as particularly inﬂuential. Consistent with prior studies
(Noor et al., 2020), the ﬁndings reaﬃrm that social pressure and community inﬂuence are central drivers
of participation in social commerce.
5.1. Theoretical implications
This study advances theory by clarifying the role of credibility in SSI through integrating information
perception with social normative perspectives. Contrary to prior work that treats credibility as a direct
antecedent of purchase intention (Amarullah et al., 2022; Bilgen and Zoghi, 2017), the ﬁndings show that
credibility does not directly inﬂuence SSI. Instead, its effect is fully mediated by subjective norms. Thus,
credibility functions not as a cognitive cue but also as a normative mechanism that shapes perceptions
of socially endorsed behaviour. In turn, this extends TPB research by showing that subjective norms are
not merely predictors of intention but key mediating mechanisms through which information evaluations
become socially embedded (Ajzen, 2020; Leong et al., 2022).
The study also sharpens the distinction between trust and believability, which prior literature often
conﬂates (Metzger and Flanagin, 2013; Mulcahy et al., 2024). Trust emerges as a direct, individual-level
risk-reduction mechanism that strongly predicts SSI but does not shape subjective norms. This supports
prior work suggesting that trust reﬂects personal assurance grounded in platform reliability, consumer
protection and system quality rather than social expectations (Aslan, 2023; Zhao et al., 2023). By
contrast, believability exerts both a direct and an indirect effect through subjective norms. Therefore,
believable information informs individual judgment while also reinforcing perceptions of collective
acceptance. Compared with trust, believability is more socially embedded and better captures the bridge
between information quality and normative inﬂuence (Berry et al., 2018; Cassar et al., 2021; Mulcahy et
al., 2024).
Furthermore, the study advances theory by integrating IAM with TPB to explain how information
perceptions translate into behavioural intentions in social commerce. IAM highlights information quality
and source credibility in information adoption (Sussman and Siegal, 2003) whereas TPB explains
Page 15/25

behavioural intention through attitudinal and normative processes (Ajzen, 1991). By linking believability
and credibility with subjective norms, our study proposes a more complete framework of social
commerce intention formation. In doing so, it challenges the common assumption that trust and
credibility are interchangeable predictors (Elmousa et al., 2024; Tseng, 2022). Instead, credibility
operates mainly through a normative pathway, trust acts as a direct behavioural driver, and believability
connects informational evaluation with social inﬂuence.
Such contributions are especially relevant to the Asia-Paciﬁc context. Social commerce across Asia-
Paciﬁc markets is shaped by dense digital interaction, peer visibility and relatively strong collectivist
orientations, which amplify the salience of social approval and conformity in online decision-making (Liu
and Lapinski, 2024; Pradana et al., 2024). In markets namely Vietnam, Indonesia, China and Thailand,
consumers often rely on peer endorsement, eWOM and community validation when evaluating sellers,
platforms and products (Rouibah et al., 2021; Yuniarty et al., 2020). Accordingly, the strong mediating
role of subjective norms found here suggests that credibility-based information in Asia-Paciﬁc social
commerce is unlikely to inﬂuence intention unless it ﬁrst acquires social legitimacy. This insight extends
regional social commerce scholarship by explaining why normative endorsement may be more
consequential than credibility alone in socially networked consumption environments (Guo and Li, 2022;
Putri et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2024).
5.2. Managerial implications
The ﬁndings offer several practical implications for managers operating in social commerce. First,
credibility does not directly inﬂuence SSI, contradicting prior studies (Amarullah et al., 2022; Bilgen and
Zoghi, 2017). Instead, its effect is fully mediated by subjective norms. Thus, credibility functions
primarily at the collective level. Accordingly, ﬁrms should shift from targeting isolated consumers to
cultivating community-wide legitimacy. Managers should therefore encourage authentic user-generated
content, promote credible peer reviews and collaborate with trusted inﬂuencers. Such practices generate
visible social endorsement and strengthen normative pressure, thereby enhancing purchase intention.
Second, the distinct roles of believability and trust require differentiated managerial strategies.
Believability inﬂuences SSI both directly and indirectly through subjective norms, implying that marketing
communication should be persuasive while also aligned with shared community expectations (Cassar et
al., 2021). In contrast, trust exerts a strong direct effect without normative mediation. Therefore, ﬁrms
should invest in mechanisms that reduce perceived risk, including secure payment systems, transparent
policies, responsive customer service and reliable platforms. Finally, Vietnam’s rapidly expanding digital
economy provides favourable conditions for social commerce. Firms should in turn combine community-
based reputation strategies with trust-building investments while leveraging inﬂuencers, livestreaming
and short-form video marketing.
6. Conclusions
This study advances understanding of social commerce by examining how credibility, believability, trust
and subjective norms jointly shape SSI among Generation Z consumers. The ﬁndings demonstrate that
Page 16/25

credibility inﬂuences purchase intention only indirectly through subjective norms, highlighting the crucial
role of social approval and collective endorsement in digital purchasing decisions. In contrast, trust
operates as a direct driver of intention, underscoring its importance as an individual-level mechanism for
reducing perceived risk, while believability exerts both direct and normative effects. These results reﬁne
existing behavioural theories by clarifying the distinct yet complementary pathways through which
information perceptions and social pressures affect consumer decision-making. Despite these
contributions, several limitations remain. First, the reliance on convenience sampling and a cross-
sectional design constrains causal inference and generalizability. Future research could adopt probability
sampling and longitudinal designs to capture dynamic changes in social shopping behaviour. Second,
the empirical focus on Generation Z consumers in Ho Chi Minh City limits the geographical and
demographic scope. Extending the analysis to other regions and age cohorts in Vietnam would
strengthen external validity. Finally, future studies using QCA may deepen theoretical insights by
modelling trust as a multidimensional construct across platforms, vendors and communities, examining
interactional dynamics in social commerce or exploring credibility across diverse contextual settings.
Such extensions would further enrich the evolving literature on social shopping behaviour.
