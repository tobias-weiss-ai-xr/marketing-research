Purpose: Generation Z cohort is often more susceptible to social media
influencers, as they are more involved with and engaged in social media
compared to other cohorts. This paper explores the effect of social media
influencer marketing on young consumers and how it shapes their decision-
making and purchase behaviour.
Design/methodology/approach: The paper used SPAR-4 and the TCCM
framework to systematically synthesise the literature from 60 studies focused
on young consumer behaviour and influencer marketing. The study aims to
identify influencer attributes that shape young consumers’ trust, brand
perception, brand attitude, brand image, and overall purchase behaviour,
including their purchase intent and actual purchase decisions.
Findings: It offers a comprehensive understanding of the elements that impact
youth trust, purchase behaviour and psychological barriers surrounding the
influencer marketing phenomenon.
Research limitations/implications: While the study adopts a multi-disciplinary
approach to review the existing literature and systematically synthesise and
summarise the findings, the articles were retrieved from a selected database,
and any study falling beyond those search criteria was excluded.
Social implications: This paper draws attention to the role of social media
influencer marketing on the holistic well-being of young adults.
Practical Implications: The study provides meaningful guidance for brand
marketers on effectively selecting influencers and formulating marketing
strategies for younger audiences.
Originality: The paper proposes future propositions and a nomological
framework to further inform research in this direction. This study elaborates
on the current body of literature, helping brand marketers develop and adopt
influencer-led strategies, particularly for younger audiences.

1 Introduction
Social media has transformed the digital landscape through UGC (user-generated
content), likes, comments, and shares, making it an interactive medium for
communication and engagement (Alves et al., 2016). Social Media platforms have
become the primary arenas for brand communication, product awareness, discovery
and identity creation. The social media influencers thus act as mediators, narrowing

the gap between brands and consumers and profoundly shaping
their trust and buying behaviour (Leung et al., 2022; Vrontis
et al., 2021). Djafarova and Foots (2022) describe Generation
Z as “digital natives” born between 1995 and 2010 who rely
heavily on information from digital creators in their purchasing
decisions. This generation is characterised by a profound
dependency on social media for identity construction and
decision-making, coupled with an innate “persuasion
knowledge” that makes them highly resistant to traditional, top-
down advertising (Friestad and Wright, 1994). For Gen Z,
consumer behaviour is no longer a direct path from awareness
to purchase; it is a complex, socially mediated process shaped by
the influencers they follow. Research indicates that Gen
Z consumers view influencer endorsements not as commercial
intrusions but as peer-led advice, provided the influencer
maintains a high degree of perceived fit and authenticity
(Belanche et al., 2021; Schouten et al., 2020).
Due to high perceived similarity and trustworthiness, younger
generations are more likely to associate with social media
influencers than with traditional figures (De Veirman et al.,
2017; Jin et al., 2019). They admire influencers who demonstrate
transparency, especially in disclosing sponsored content, and
who align with their ethical and social values (Han and
Balabanis, 2024). Conversely, trust is rapidly eroded by “over-
commercialisation”, deceptive claims or a product-influencer
“mis-fit” (Kim and Kim, 2021; Weismueller et al., 2020). De
Veirman et al.’s (2017) study emphasised that the popularity of
influencers with huge follower counts alone does not influence
consumer behaviour. Other significant factors in influencer
campaigns, such as credibility, authenticity, parasocial
relationships, and sustainability, draw consumer attention and
influence purchase decisions (Jin et al., 2019; Kumar and Jibril,
2025).
Platforms like Instagram and TikTok help influencers reach
younger audiences more rapidly, fostering trust and authenticity
through constant engagement, encouraging them to try trending
products, and swaying impulsive buying (Soni and Bhukya,
2025; Vrontis et al., 2021). On the contrary, lack of authenticity,
genuineness, and non-disclosure lowers their trust and leads to
scepticism and diminished brand loyalty (De Veirman et al.,
2017; Kumar and Jibril, 2025).
By synthesising evidence from Scopus-indexed journals, this
study aims to classify the underlying mechanisms of influence
and highlight critical gaps in current theories. For brand
marketers, it offers deep insights underscoring a shift from
“reach-based” metrics to “resonance-based” strategies. This
research examines the role of SMI-driven marketing in the
purchase behaviour of young consumers (i.e., Generation Z) to
achieve positive marketing outcomes for this cohort. Based on
this, the following research questions have been developed:
RQ1: What are the publication characteristics, content
structure and implications of SMI marketing on the purchase
behaviour of young consumers?
RQ2: How is our understanding of consumer behaviour with
regard to the Gen Z population and influencer marketing
advancing within the academic arena?
RQ3: What are the prospective research avenues for exploring
the interplay between influencer marketing and young
consumers in the future?
Attempts have been made in the past to synthesise literature
concerning Generation Z and influencer marketing (Table 1).
Ridwan et al. (2025) explore the decision-making behaviour
of Gen Z in online buying. It highlighted the collective role
of social media, user-gener ated content (UGC), peer
influence, sustainable practices, and reliance on social proof,
emotional and moral factors, interactive marketing strategies,
and personalizati on in the decision-making choices of the
younger generation. The study used the PRISMA framework
to identify relevant articles, and 10 were selected for review.
Another study by Barrueta-Pinto et al. (2025) also synthesised
the literature on social media influencers and young
purchasing behaviour, examining the driving factors that
encourage youth purchase, the perception of credibility,
authenticity, and influencer-bran d collaboration. It deployed
the PRISMA framework, and 29 articles were included in the
study review.
While the existing literature, as exemplified by Ridwan et al.
(2025) and Barrueta-Pinto et al. (2025), has made significant
strides in understanding the various dimensions of young
consumer behaviour involving social media influencers, there
remains a visible gap when it comes to examining the overall
impact of social media influencer marketing, such as the type of
influencers, type of industry, type of platforms, and the
influencer’s attributes that affect the young consumers’ trust and
purchase behaviour. Although the cited studies discuss social
media influencers (SMIs) and Generation Z (Gen Z) or youth,
the number of articles reviewed was very limited, which does
not truly reflect the scope of the review. The current review
study extends beyond just the review of all the studies; it
significantly contributes to the current state of the literature by
providing future propositions through careful examination of
the findings and research gaps in the documented studies. It

2 Methodology used
Another justification for conducting a systematic literature
review is that it provides the state of the art through
comprehensive mapping and offers a clear perspective for
future research to advance knowledge (Paul et al., 2021). It
helps synthesise fragmented literature to advance research and
develop theory. The study adopted the Scientific Procedur es
and Rationales for a Systematic Literature Review (SPAR-
4-SLR) protocol, rather than the PRISMA framework used in
previous reviews, because PRISMA provides little rationale for
review decisions (Paul et al., 2021). In this study, 60 articles
were included in the final analysis, which is consider ed
sufficient for the review, as it exceeds the threshold of 40
articles suggested by Paul and Criado (2020) . To synthesise
the findings, the TCCM framework by Paul and Rosado-
Serrano (2019) was deployed for its ability to deliver the
highest level of clarity and coverage to the review (Paul et al.,
2021).

2.2 Identification of studies
2.2.1 Assembling
This stage involves identifying and acquiring articles from
the database. The decisions regarding identification were based
on the research domain, research objectives, and source quality
and type. The database used in the review study was Scopus,
as it is considered the most popular and relevant database for
SLR. The main component of acquisition is the search string
of the keywords. In our review, the keyword search string was
examined using the PEO framework to acquire the articles and
achieve the desired results. It also includes the search timeline,
which in this case was from 2019 to 2026 (Jan), since the first
article was produced in 2019 for that particular search string.
In this stage, all documents published between 2019 and 2026
(Jan) were included, and 87 articles were initially reported.
The first screening was conducted based on the subject
criteria, resulting in 73 documents being identified. The
subject areas that were included during the filtering were a)
business, management, and accounting; b) social sciences; c)
economics, econometrics, and finance; d) arts and
humanities; e) multidisciplinary; f) psychology; and g) decision
sciences, as these subjects fully cover the scope of the
review study.
2.2.2 Arranging
The arranging stage includes the organisation and
purification of documents from the first stage. Next, the de-
duplication protocol was implemented through automated de-
duplication in Zotero, as well as manual audit, to prevent false-
positive exclusion through software. The next step includes the
study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria. In our study, we
included peer-reviewed publications and review papers. Only
articles written in English were included, which served as
another inclusion criterion. To ensure study quality, we
excluded publications in other languages, conference
proceedings, and book chapters. Therefore, of the 73
documents in the first stage, after filtering by document type,
64 were articles, of which 61 were articles and 3 were review
papers. One article was excluded due to language, as only
English-language articles were included in the study, leaving 63
for further analysis.
2.2.3 Assessing
The documents were further evaluated based on title,
abstract, and full content screening. Three studies were
excluded at this stage as they did not align with the scope of
the study, thus making a final study count of 60. To minimise
methodological bias for the heterogeneous study designs, a
QQM checklist (Quantitative, Qualitative and Mixed-method)
was applied. All 60 studies underwent a quality check under 8
indicators, which are: setting, recruitment, administration,

2.2.4 Data coding
After the documents were extracted from the database, the
complete documents were carefully read, and the data coding
procedure was deployed to systematically conduct the review
process under the TCCM framework, enabling transparency
and reproducibility (Vrontis et al., 2021). Each document was
coded for 1) authors 2) countries 3) platform 4) industry
type 5) year 6) variables studied 7) research framework
(methodology, data collection, sampling technique, and
statistical methods) 8) underpinning theories 9) journals 10)
conclusion 11) gaps and future research directions. Figure 2

3 Finding and analysis
The articles extracted from the SPAR-4 process were then
categorised and examined using the TCCM framework by Paul
and Rosado-Serrano (2019) to address the study’s first research
question (RQ1).

Figure 3 shows the yearly developments in studies

shifted to resonance-based metrics. Influencer marketing has
now evolved from a mere tactical novelty to a global business
research domain. The study area has a footprint in all the
major fields, such as marketing, psychology, business,
communication, and sustainable consumption.
Figure 4 shows the density visualisation of the keywords in

clustering of keywords like “social media influencer”, “consumer
behaviour”, “structural equation modelling”, and “purchase

qualitative research paradigms to highly rigorous, variance-based
causal modelling. This structural clustering maps closely to
highly cited conceptual paradigms, showing the digital media
endorsers act as primary modern vectors for values, norms, and
social expectations, transforming the traditional paths of
youth socialisation.

4.1.1 Ethical and privacy concerns
For Gen Z, trust has been the primary indicat or of their
engagement with social media influencers. However, trust
takes considerable time and effort to build, yet it erodes
quickly if not managed properly . Common reasons include
non-disclosure, false claims, and unethical practices (Lou and
Yuan, 2019; Weismueller et al., 2020). Gen Z, the most
vulnera ble cohort, considers it an ethical obligation for
influencers to deliver authentic, trustworthy content to build
long-term brand loyalty (Djafarova and Foots, 2022; Han and
Balabanis, 2024). If they fail to do so, it not only lowers
their expecta tions but also leads to a negativ e relationship
with the brands and the SMIs, significantly reducing their
credibility and heightening communica tion anxieties (De
Keyzer, 2023; Sesar et al., 2023) (refer Table 10 for
conceptual indicat ors).
Proposition 1 (a) Consumer’s data privacy concerns weaken
the relationship between perceived trustworthiness of SMIs and
young consumers’ purchase behaviour.
Proposition 1 (b) Explicit sponsorship disclosure by SMIs
increases perceived authenticity of endorsements and reduces
deceptive communication anxieties.
Proposition 1 (c) The influencer’s social responsibility
behaviour and long- term brand loyalty are positively moderated
by ethical consumption values in Gen Z consumers.

4.1.3 Human vs. virtual influencers concerns
Marketers are now moving beyond the concept of human-touch
to test the “ease of accepting unrealism”. The choice between a
human and a virtual influencer also depends on the product-
influencer fit and brand congruence (Nallasivam et al., 2025;
Orelskaia et al., 2025). The replication of human persona through
storytelling, growing-up narratives, and attributes such as
credibility and interactivity can boost the self-alignment concept
among consumers through highly immersive technologies (Eastin
et al., 2025; Sinha and Srivastava, 2025). Although studies have
measured the impact of influencers’ attributes, both human and
virtual. Future studies should carefully examine their comparisons
and assess whether young adults exhibit similar consumer
behaviour with human and virtual influencers. Also, how do
young adults perceive realism in human and virtual influencers?
Technological advancement through AR-induced content, VIs’
informativeness, narrative styles and entertainment enhance
consumers’ engagement and purchase intention (Cao et al., 2025;
Wang et al., 2025b) (refer Table 12 for conceptual indicators).
Proposition 3 (a) Human influencers exhibit high emotional
trust in consumers through genuine parasocial interaction (PSI)
while virtual influencers draw high engagement through AR-
induced content vividness.
Proposition 3 (b) The type of influencer (human vs. virtual)
moderates the relationship between brand anthropomorphism
and consumers’ purchase intent.
4.1.4 Sustainable marketing concerns
The studies have highlighted that sustainable choices are more
prevalent among specific genders, i.e., females, and in specific
industries such as fashion, food, FMCG, and self-care, where they
successfully drive consumer engagement and purchase intention
(De Keyzer, 2023; Ge, 2024; Pham et al., 2026). Sustainable
marketing practices, such as eco-labelling, are likely to serve a
positive public image, thereby strengthening brand loyalty
(Panopoulos et al., 2023). Some studies argue that due to Gen Z’s
desire to mimic influencer behaviour, it might lead to
unsustainable consumption patterns too (Soni and Bhukya, 2025).
Therefore, there is a need to further explore different
demographics and cultural setups to study the sustainability impact
on consumption patterns (refer Table 13 for conceptual indicators).
Proposition 4 Consumer’s perceived product value positively
affects green consumption intention through sustainable
influencer marketing practices.

pandemic and post-pandemic sustainable shift in fashion
consumption (Pham et al., 2026). Similarly, political changes, war
crises, medical emergencies, and scandals can affect public trust
and opinions (refer Table 14 for conceptual indicators).

5 Implications
Addressing the interplay between young consumers and
influencer marketing has several theoretical implications. First,
it follows a multidisciplinary approach that integrates
influencer marketing with marketing, psychology, technology,
and media communication, emphasising the need to delve
deeper into this area in future research. Second, it advances
our understanding of young consumer behaviour by
underpinning a wide array of theories and communication
frameworks, such as the Stimulus-Organism-Res ponse (SOR)
model, the Theory of Planned Behaviour, the Source
Credibility theory, and the Parasocial Interaction (PSI) Theory.
Third, the study helps identify significant attributes and
influencer characteristics, such as perceived credibility,
authenticity, similarity, persuasion knowledge, familiarity, and
ethical dimensions that capture youth trust and subsequently
affect their purchase decisions. Fourth, the nomological
framework provides a strategic roadmap for future studies by
categorising the antecedents, mediators, and moderators that
generate the desired outcomes.
Alongside the theoretical implications, the study also provides
actionable strategies for marketers, ranging from “reach-based” to
“resonance-based” metrics. The study underscores the significance
of brand partnerships with influencers and regulatory bodies in
strengthening consumers’ trust and fostering a positive social
environment. It highlights that to achieve brand loyalty and
successful marketing outcomes, popularity and attractiveness are
not the sole drivers; rather, factors such as cultural sensitivity,

5 Conclusion and limitations
The emergence of social media influencer (SMI) marketing
has transformed the global marketing arena, shifting from
traditional, one-way advertis ing toward a more interactive
and immersive communica tion model. This systematic review
focuses on a highly crucial generation cohort, i.e., Generation
Z—the first generation of “digital natives”—that is identified
as a dominant economic force. This review study is crucial, as
it primarily targets young consumers’ highly complex
purchase behaviour and views SMI-led marketing as a tool to
influence and dominat e their decision-making (Alves de
Castro and Sousa, 2025; De Veirman et al., 2017; Vrontis
et al., 2021).
By synthesising 60 Scopus-indexed studies using
the SPAR-4-SLR protocol and the TCCM framework, the
review extends the “state-of-the-art” mapping of a
fragmented field and offers a comprehensive understanding
of the factors that affect youth trust, purchase behaviour, and
their physical and mental well-being in relation to the
IM phenomenon.
Despite its comprehe nsive nature, the study has
many constraints. First, the findings are limited to the
literature retrieved from the Scopus database using a well-
defined search string, and any study outside the scope may be
excluded. Future research should include other databases to
cross-check and supplement the existing findings. Second, as
influencer marketing is an emerging field, rapid advances may
not be fully captured. To understand the complexities of
influencer marketing, future studies should adopt more
nuanced approaches that draw on empirical data and
case studies. Third, the existing review is entirely focused
