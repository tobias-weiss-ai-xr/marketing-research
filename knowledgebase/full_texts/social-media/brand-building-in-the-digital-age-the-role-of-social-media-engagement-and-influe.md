Abstract
The rapid evolution of digital platforms has transformed consumer–brand relationships, positioning social media
engagement and influencer marketing as central to modern brand-building strategies. This study examines how distinct
types of consumer engagement—consumption, contribution, and creation—impact two critical brand outcomes: brand
trust and purchase intention. Drawing on the COBRA (Consumers’ Online Brand-Related Activities) framework, a
quantitative survey was administered to a sample of 212 Lebanese social media users aged 18 to 34 who follow at least
one influencer and interact with branded content. Descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and multiple regression were
used to evaluate the relationships between engagement types and brand outcomes. Results indicate that passive
engagement (consumption) does not significantly predict brand trust or purchase intention. In contrast, active behaviors—
particularly contribution and creation—are positively associated with brand trust, with creation emerging as the strongest
predictor of purchase intention. The regression models explained 42% of the variance in brand trust and 46% in purchase
intention. These findings affirm the theoretical relevance of the COBRA model and emphasize the importance of depth
in consumer engagement. Practically, they suggest that marketers and influencers should prioritize interactive and co-
creative campaign strategies to foster deeper brand connections and drive consumer action. The study contributes to a
more nuanced understanding of social media engagement and offers actionable insights for enhancing the effectiveness
of influencer marketing in the digital age.

Keywords: social media engagement, influencer marketing, brand trust, purchase intention, COBRA model

1. Introduction
The digital age has brought radical transformation to
brand building, whereby the two-way advertising has
been replaced by the interactive and consumer-centred
brand building processes 1. In large part, this shift is
associated with the popularity of social media, such as
Instagram, Tik Tok, and YouTube, which have turned
out to be instrumental as spaces of brand
communication and identity formation 2,3. These
platforms allow not only consumers to be satisfied with
what they are being fed but also to be participative in
what they are receiving- to see, like, comment, share,
and even create their own brand-related content 4.
Therefore, branding has ceased being a top-down
procedure that is driven by the company and instead, is
a co-construction procedure of an ever-present
engagement of brands, influencers, and online users 5.
The aspect of influencer marketing is one of the most
effective tools that has emerged as a part of marketing
in terms of social media 6. One of the most significant
forces in the process of forming the perceptions and
behaviors of consumers is the role played by
influencers, individuals who seem to possess credibility,
authenticity, and high-level digital presence 7. Their
messages are more likely to be personal, and they foster
parasocial relationships that enhance the effectiveness
of the message and lead to emotional relationships
between the followers and the brands they are
promoting8. This is a much more human-centered
approach to marketing and finds popularity much more
rapidly with younger, more digitally native audiences as
compared to more impersonal, more traditional
advertisements9. However, not all forms of consumer
engagement with influencer content yield the same
marketing outcomes 10. Though passive involvement,
which may come as the simple viewing of a post, may
lead to brand awareness, the more active ones are the
actions associated with more pronounced psychological
involvement and stronger consumer-brand relationships
(liking, commenting, or reposting) 11,12. The most

2. Literature Review and Theoretical Framework
To comprehend consumer activities with brands in
social media, it is important to have a systematic and
organised classification of behaviours on the internet.
The Consumers Online Brand-Related Activities
(COBRA) framework by Papasolomou et al. (2023) is
one of the best-known and empirically proven models in
this respect 14. The model is based on a continuum of
consumer involvement and differentiates between three
fundamental levels of consumer activity, such as
consumption, contribution, and creation. All levels are a
gradually increasing psychological and behavioral
association between the consumer and the brand 15–17.
Consumption can be described as passive engagement,
where the consumer merely watches branded content,
even though there is no interaction 18. This could be the
view of ads, browsing through influencer posts, or
scrolling through brand posts. This activity provides
more exposure, but not active interest or commitment19.
Contribution, by contrast, has moderate amounts of
interaction, including liking, commenting, or re-reacting
to content. Such actions are indicators of the readiness
to discuss the brand publicly and are characterized by
even more significant cognitive and emotional
interest20. They can be used as early warning signs of
brand loyalty and fledgling trust21.
There is creation, the most engaged level of
engagement, which is based on proactive and
participatory activities, whereby consumers create and
share information about a brand 22. Examples may be to
repost content of influencers, to make original product
posts, or to write a branded challenge. These behaviors
are commonly linked to a high degree of brand loyalty
and advocacy and have proven to bring about high brand
exposure and trustworthiness in the circle of the
consumer19,23. Simultaneously with the COBRA model,
influencer marketing sources emphasize the critical
importance of perceived credibility and perceived
authenticity in influencing campaign performance. Lou
and Yuan (2019) suggest that the consumer trust of an
influencer is usually applied to the brand endorsed by
the influencer- a phenomenon known as source
credibility transfer 24,25. Often unfilled by traditional
advertising, influencers perceived as relatable,
knowledgeable, and authentic can build powerful
emotional connections between the audience and the
brand3. Additionally, not every type of engagement has
the same contribution to brand results. Empirical studies
performed by Gross and Von Wangeheim (2022) prove
that active engagement behaviors, in particular,
contribution and creation, are better related to positive
brand attitudes, stronger trust, and better purchase
intentions compared to passive behaviors, such as
consumption26. The results bring out the significance of
making a difference between engagement levels to
determine the effectiveness of influencer marketing
strategies27.
It is based on these conceptual foundations that the
current study uses the COBRA model to examine the
role of various categories of social media engagement in
two vital brand outcomes, brand trust and purchase
intention28. Through an empirical study of these
relationships, the study aims at offering a more
elucidated explanation to how consumer interaction on
social media sites can play a role in brand development
in the online world. These learnings can be applied to
create more precise and powerful digital marketing
tactics to capitalize on the entire range of consumer
interactions29.

3. Methodology
3.1. Research Design and Instrumentation
The research design used here was a cross-sectional
quantitative study meant to investigate the correlation
between various forms of social media use and their
effects on brand credibility and buying intention in
influencer marketing. The self-administered online
survey was used to collect data and was distributed
among the target population on Instagram and
WhatsApp, two popular platforms of the target
population. The effectiveness of these channels in the
recruitment process and the fact that it could capture
participants who are already in touch with influencer
content made it easy. The questionnaire was divided into
three major parts. The initial part determined social
media engagement based on the COBRA (Consumers
Online Brand-Related Activities) framework that
categorizes engagement into three dimensions, namely
consumption, contribution, and creation. Three items
were used to evaluate each dimension, which added to
nine items. It was based on the validated scale created
by Schivinski et al. (2016). Answers were recorded on a
5-point Likert scale of 1(strongly disagree) to 5(strongly
agree).
The second segment assessed brand trust based on three
questions that were modified according to Chaudhuri
and Holbrook (2001), which established how much
consumers believe that the brand promoted by the
influencers is reliable, dependable, and trustworthy. The
third part was devoted to purchase intention, which is
operationalized by three items based on Spears and
Singh (2004), assessing the probability of consumers
buying an item promoted by an influencer.

A non-probability convenience sampling method was
used in the study. The survey was conducted among 212
users of social media in Lebanon aged between 18 and
34 years old and 62 percent of them are female. Each
respondent passed the eligibility threshold: he/she
followed at least one social media influencer and had
engaged with branded content (i.e., views, likes,
comments, or shares) within the past month. The reason
why this demographic was chosen is that they have high
social media usage and are responsive to influence
which is in tandem with the objectives of this research.
A short introduction in the first part of the survey
clarified the purpose of the study to the participants,
ensured anonymity, and assured that they were free to
participate. The process of acquiring informed consent
was done digitally and the questionnaire was filled in.

3.3. Data Analysis
The data were presented in the form of collected data,
which was analyzed by IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0. The
analysis was initiated by descriptive statistics that
summarize central tendencies and variability of
responses. The analysis of reliability was done based on
the Cronbach alpha ( α ) to determine the internal
consistency of all the multi-item constructs. It was
found that all the constructs had a good reliability with
a conventional threshold of 0.70. Pearson correlation
coefficients were then calculated to test the strength and
direction of the correlation between the most important
variables of the study, the three dimensions of
engagement, brand trust, and purchase intention. Lastly,
two multiple linear regressions were conducted to
examine the predictive effect of consumption,
contribution and creation behavior on the brand trust
and purchase intention, respectively. All inferential tests
were determined to be significant with a level of p < .05.
4. Results
The analysis started with the descriptive statistics and
the test of reliability to all multi-item constructs. Internal
consistency of both scales was also established, and the
value of Cronbach alpha exceeded the acceptable value
of .70: Consumption (=.79), Contribution (=.82),
Creation (=.81), Brand Trust (=.84), Purchase Intention
(=.87). These findings imply that each and every
construct was found to be highly internally reliable.
Contribution behaviors had the largest mean score (M =
3.82, SD = 0.71), and the second rank was consumption
(M = 3.67, SD = 0.75) and creation (M = 3.29, SD =
0.68). The outcome variables, brand trust, and purchase
intention, also demonstrated moderatly high means,
indicating that the respondents have had a rather
positive attitude towards brands promoted by
influencers.

5. Discussion
The results of this paper support and elaborate on the
theoretical premise of the COBRA (Consumers’ Online
Brand-Related Activities) model by showing how
consumer attitude toward social media content is not
one-dimensional in its impact 30. Instead, various
engagement behaviors have a different degree of impact
on brand-related results, especially when engaging a
group of influencers 31. In particular, it was found that,
operationalized as consumption (i.e., simple viewing of
content), passive engagement showed weak and
statistically non-significant correlations with brand trust
and purchase intention 32. This implies that, despite the
fact that consumption can be used to increase the initial
exposure to the brand, it cannot be used to create
cognitive or emotional depth that would form a strong
consumer-brand relationship 33. Exposure, as a form of
brand impact, is not enough in the attention economy
anymore, especially when overwhelmed by the content
on overcrowded platforms 34. Conversely, both
contribution and creation behaviors, with increased
amounts of psychological engagement and user agency,
had a significant connection with brand trust, with
creation making the strongest correlation with purchase
intention35,36. The results here back the notion that active
involvement is a better measure of brand commitment.
Consumers become participants in the story of a brand
when they like, comment, share, or co-create content
about the brand 37. This follows the engagement theory,
according to which the more participatory and
interactive experience a consumer goes through, the
higher the probability that such an experience will lead
to significant attitudinal and behavioral results 38. The
findings also confirm earlier studies on co-creation by
consumers and the transfer of credibility of the source.
According to Basile et al. (2024), user-generated
content does not only enhance reach but also enhances
the credibility and authenticity of a brand since it
exploits peer endorsement processes 39. The findings of
the study suggest that earned influence will always be
more powerful than paid media because the creation
behaviors were found to have the strongest beta
coefficient in predicting both brand trust and purchase
intention40. Campaigns where the content is co-created
with influencers rely not only on the credibility of the
influencer but also on the social capital and network of
the consumer. The other important implication of this
study is the depth of engagement 41. Although many
influencer campaigns continue to be supported with
superficial metrics such as reach, views, or follower
counts, these findings are a strong argument in changing
the focus to behavioral engagement metrics, especially
creation- and contribution-related metrics. These
actions are proxies of deeper emotional investment,
which is a vital antecedent of brand loyalty and
purchasing decision42,43.
Based on the managerial view, the results imply a series
of strategic directions. To start with, brands and
influencer partners must co-create campaigns, which
should not just present products unidirectionally but
rather invite engagement 44. This may be the interactive
challenges, UGC competition, customization product
activities, or sharing testimonials 45. Second, the choice
of the influencers must be based on quality rather than
on quantity. Influencers with a steady stream of
engagement, measured in comments, sharing, and
creative responses, will tend to produce brand trust and
conversion compared to influencers with high but
uninterested followers 46. Third, both platform-specific
strategies need to be maximized to make use of
engagement mechanics 47. As an example, Instagram
polls, TikTok duets, and YouTube community features
are all distinct areas of contribution and co-creation that
marketers can leverage 48. In addition, the contribution
behaviors are a subtle factor, although not as predictive
as creation, leading to the conclusion that even mediocre
degrees of engagement may become effective
touchpoints in the customer experience 49. Familiarity
and brand salience can be better built through
continuous engagement, through posting comments or
replying to stories, which can build familiarity and
brand salience over time, especially when accompanied
by prompt and authentic replies on the part of the
influencer or brand account50,51.
Lastly, the results have wider implications in the study
of digital brand building in a participatory culture. To
the extent that consumers are increasingly gaining more
power to influence brand meaning, marketers need to
respond by creating participatory structures, which are
not just technologically possible but also
psychologically compelling 52. This involves an
understanding that trust is not a broadcast but a co-
product of the interaction, and that conversion is more

6. Conclusion
Social media is one of the pillars of brand
communication, consumer engagement, and loyalty
development in the contemporary digitally-oriented
marketing climate. The current work is part of a growing
literature because it empirically tests the differentiated
effect of consumer engagement behaviors, which are
classified according to the COBRA framework, on
brand trust and purchase intention in the context of
influencer marketing. The findings indicate clearly that
not every form of engagement has the same brand-
related results. Passive consumption can create a sense
of visibility, but it lacks the convincing influence to
create any meaningful consumer-brand relationships.
Instead, active behaviors—particularly content creation
and contribution—emerge as the most impactful drivers
of brand trust and consumer intention to purchase. The
theoretical and practical implications of these findings
are both practical and theoretical. Theoretically, this
research confirms the usefulness of the COBRA model
to the study of digital engagement and expands it to the
sphere of influencer marketing. In practice, it gives
practical information to marketers and brand strategists.
Brands that want to improve their visibility and
credibility by using influencers need to do more than
secure visibility: they need to build interactive, co-
creative campaigns that engage and encourage
participation among readers. Strategies that focus on
user-created content, interactive narrating, and
community-like interaction have a higher probability to
result in enduring brand affinity and consumer action.
In spite of these worthy contributions, this research also
paves way to explore more. Further studies may take a
longitudinal design to determine the long-term effect of
various forms of engagement, and also examine
platform-specific dynamics, such as whether the
algorithms of TikTok or the visual culture of Instagram
mediate the effects of engagement. Also, broadening the
demographic range and experimenting with diverse
product figures may potentially increase
generalizability and offer a more detailed sense of
digital brand-building approaches in contexts.
