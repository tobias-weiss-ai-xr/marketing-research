Abstract
Influencer marketing is widely used in social commerce, but the
process through which influencer attributes translate into brand
equity remains poorly explained. Drawing on source credibility theory,
self-congruity theory, and parasocial interaction theory, this study
tests whether brand awareness mediates the relationships between
four influencer attributes, namely content quality, credibility, personal
identity, and social interaction, and brand equity among social
commerce consumers in Indonesia. Data were collected through a
structured online questionnaire from 200 Indonesian social commerce
consumers aged 18 years and above, recruited through purposive
sampling. The hypothesised relationships were analysed using partial
least squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) with
bootstrapping. All four influencer attributes had significant positive
effects on brand awareness, with social interaction exerting the
strongest effect. Brand awareness, in turn, had a strong positive effect
on brand equity and fully mediated the effects of content quality,
credibility, personal identity, and social interaction on brand equity.
This indicates that influencer attributes do not improve brand equity
directly; they matter because they build brand awareness among
consumers. The study contributes to research on influencer marketing
and social commerce by establishing brand awareness as the key link
between influencer attributes and brand equity, and by showing that
social interaction plays a greater role than content quality or
credibility in building awareness. For practice, brands, especially small

Introduction
The way brands reach consumers has changed considerably. On social commerce platforms, influencers have become the
connective tissue between products and people, shaping how audiences perceive, recall, and ultimately choose brands. 1
Scholars have responded with a growing body of research, but much of it stops short of explaining what actually matters
for building long-term brand value. Studies tend to focus on likes, clicks, and purchase intentions, leaving the deeper
question of how influencer behaviour generates durable brand equity largely unaddressed.1,2 This study sets out to fill that
gap by asking not just whether influencers affect brand outcomes, but through what process they do so.
What makes an influencer effective is more nuanced than reach or follower count. Research consistently shows that it
comes down to how consumers process the content they encounter. When influencers communicate with transparency,
whether through honest disclosures, two-sided messaging, or visible personal conviction, audiences are more likely to
engage meaningfully with branded content.3,4 Content that entertains while also informing reinforces this further, shaping
consumer attitudes toward the brands being promoted. 5 These are not passive effects; they point to active psychological
processes at work. What the literature has been slower to address, however, is how these processes accumulate into
something more lasting, specifically, whether they leave cognitive traces that eventually shape brand equity.
Brand awareness is a natural candidate for that missing link. At its core, it captures how readily a brand comes to mind,
which is precisely what repeated, credible influencer exposure is likely to produce. Studies confirm that when influencers
are seen as authentic and trustworthy, followers engage more deeply with brand messages and are more inclined toward
co-creation behaviours.6,7 This suggests a plausible sequence: influencer attributes shape how audiences think about a
brand, and those cognitions in turn build equity. Yet despite this intuition, no study to date has brought these pieces
together into a formal mediated model. That is the theoretical gap this paper addresses.
Indonesia makes for a compelling setting in which to test these ideas. With 212 million internet users and 143 million
active social media identities as of early 2025, a year-on-year rise of 2.9%, the country represents one of the most dynamic
digital economies in Southeast Asia. 8 Influencer-led commerce is not a niche activity here; it sits at the centre of how
many Indonesians discover and buy products.1,2 At the same time, growing public and regulatory attention to sponsorship
transparency means that the relationship between influencer behaviour and consumer perception is under greater scrutiny
than ever, making it an important market to study rigorously.
The tension around sponsorship disclosure illustrates just how delicate the influencer –audience relationship can
be. Being transparent about paid partnerships is necessary for trust, yet flagging a post as sponsored can undercut the
very credibility that made the influencer compelling in the first place. 4 When followers feel deceived, the consequences
go beyond unfollowing; research documents avoidance behaviours and outright brand rejection when psychological
expectations are violated. 4,9 Interestingly, even studies focused on virtual influencers find that authenticity and ethical
transparency are central to sustaining consumer trust across different cultural contexts. 10 Taken together, these findings
reinforce why understanding what drives brand awareness through influencer channels is not just a theoretical exercise,
but a practical necessity.
Four influencer attributes are central to this study. The first is content quality, which concerns how informative,
entertaining, and genuinely useful followers find an influencer ’s posts. When content resonates on both informational
and hedonic levels, it strengthens perceived authenticity and shapes how audiences feel about promoted brands. 5,9 The
second attribute is credibility, built on the pillars of trustworthiness, expertise, and attractiveness as originally con-
ceptualised by Ohanian.11 A credible influencer lends authority to brand messages; audiences are simply more receptive
to recommendations they trust.4,12 The third is personal identity, which concerns how closely an influencer’s lifestyle and
persona match a follower’s own self-concept. When that match is strong, followers do not just watch; they identify, and
that identity-based connection makes brand endorsements feel personally relevant. 13,14 The fourth attribute, and the one
that turns out to matter most, is social interaction. Unlike the others, social interaction is bidirectional. When influencers
reply to comments, host live sessions, and invite audiences into conversations, they cultivate a sense of genuine
relationship. Parasocial interaction theory explains why this works: sustained back-and-forth, even in a digital context,
generates intimacy and trust that make followers far more open to brand-related messages. 15,16 All four attributes are
proposed to build brand awareness, which in turn drives brand equity, as illustrated in Figure 1.
Against this backdrop, the study makes three contributions. It clarifies the mechanism through which influencer attributes
build brand equity, something the literature has largely taken for granted. It brings content quality, credibility, personal
identity, and social interaction together into a single unified model, rather than treating them in isolation as most prior
work has done. And it does so in the context of Indonesian social commerce, extending a body of research that has been
disproportionately shaped by Western and Chinese market data.

Problem statement
The research on influencer marketing has grown quickly, but it has grown unevenly.1,2 Most studies approach the subject
one attribute at a time, asking whether credibility matters, or whether content quality drives engagement, without asking
how these attributes work together or what they ultimately produce. 13,17 The result is a literature rich in fragments but
short on explanation. Brands are told that certain influencer traits correlate with positive consumer responses, but they are
rarely told why, or through what process those responses accumulate into something as durable and commercially
valuable as brand equity. 1,2 That is the gap this study sets out to close.
Brand awareness sits at the heart of this missing explanation. It is the cognitive footprint that influencer exposure leaves
behind, the point at which a brand name moves from unfamiliar to accessible, and from accessible to preferred. Yet in
most influencer marketing studies, brand awareness is treated as just another outcome to be measured, rather than as the
mechanism that connects influencer behaviour to brand equity. 17,18 When it is removed from the picture, the pathway
from what an influencer does to what a brand becomes in a consumer ’s mind is left unexplained. Bringing these pieces
together into a single unified model is precisely what the existing literature has not done, and precisely what this study
does.13,19
The practical stakes are real. Brands that invest in influencer marketing without understanding which attributes drive
lasting brand value are, in effect, navigating without a map. They may generate clicks and short-term engagement while
failing to build the recognition and associations that make a brand worth choosing the next time a consumer opens a
shopping app. 12,20 This problem is especially acute in the Indonesian social commerce market, where the literature
remains thin despite the country’s scale and strategic importance, and where findings from Western or Chinese contexts
cannot simply be assumed to transfer. 1,2 This study is designed to address that need directly.
Literature review
Content quality
Content quality refers to the degree to which influencer-generated posts are perceived as informative, entertaining, and
genuinely valuable to followers.13,21 Within the framework of Source Credibility Theory, content quality functions as a
signal of an influencer’s trustworthiness and expertise.11 When followers perceive an influencer’s content as informative
and well-crafted, they are more likely to attribute expertise and reliability to the source, increasing acceptance of the brand
message being communicated. 7,22 This process of source-attributed credibility strengthens the cognitive encoding of
brand information, producing improved brand recognition and recall, the core dimensions of brand awareness as defined
by Keller.23
Research confirms that content quality serves as a cognitive bridge between influencer exposure and downstream brand
value, with high-quality content shown to elevate brand salience and reinforce brand equity over time. 5,17 In the

Indonesian social commerce context, where consumers weigh informational and entertainment value heavily when

Studies consistently find that higher content quality generates stronger cognitive engagement, with followers more likely
to encode and recall brand information when content is informative and entertaining. 14,17 Content quality also drives
platform stickiness, compounding followers’ exposure to brand-related stimuli and reinforcing recognition-based brand
awareness.5,13
However, when consumers detect commercial sponsorship, they may activate persuasion knowledge, processing brand
messages sceptically regardless of content quality. 4,7 High-quality content can be reinterpreted as polished advertising
rather than genuine recommendation, weakening brand recall and suggesting that authenticity must accompany quality to
reliably produce brand awareness. 21
Credibility
Influencer credibility refers to the extent to which an influencer is perceived as a trustworthy, expert, and attractive source
of brand-related information, operationalised through the three dimensions of Source Credibility Theory. 11 Trustwor-
thiness reflects the perceived honesty and integrity of the influencer; expertise denotes the knowledge and competence
attributed to them within their domain; and attractiveness encompasses both physical appeal and the perceived similarity
between the influencer and their audience.7,24 Together, these dimensions determine the degree to which followers accept
and internalise an influencer ’s brand messages.
When an influencer is perceived as credible across all three dimensions, followers are more receptive to the information
they present, making it more likely that brand-related content will be processed attentively, retained in memory, and
ultimately contribute to brand recognition and recall. 22,25 This cognitive pathway from credibility to brand awareness is
particularly salient in social commerce environments, where consumers rely heavily on influencer cues to navigate brand
choices in the absence of physical product interaction. 12 In the Indonesian market, where consumer scepticism toward
sponsored content remains a documented challenge, influencer credibility becomes especially critical in determining
whether brand messages register with and are retained by audiences. 4
Credible influencers generate stronger cognitive associations with endorsed brands, making them more retrievable in
followers’ minds.12,25 When trustworthiness, expertise, and attractiveness align, a reinforcing halo elevates branded
content memorability. In Indonesian social commerce, where audience attention is scarce, credibility determines whether
brand messages form durable memory traces. 22,24
However, consumers with high persuasion knowledge may discount credible recommendations, recognising commercial
intent and suppressing brand associations.4,7 Over-endorsement further erodes credibility, reducing processing depth for
individual brand messages. 20 Credibility is therefore a dynamic asset whose effect on brand awareness can diminish
under repeated commercial exposure.
Personal identity
Personal identity refers to the perceived congruence between an influencer ’s lifestyle and persona and a follower ’s own
self-concept.13,26 Self-Congruity Theory holds that consumers are drawn to those who reflect their values and aspirations,
and this principle applies directly to influencer marketing. When followers perceive a strong identity match with an
influencer, branded content feels personally relevant rather than commercially driven, making brand messages more
likely to be processed attentively, retained in memory, and ultimately encoded as brand awareness. 14,21
Influencers who sustain a consistent, authentic identity across their content deepen this effect over time, converting audience
identification into durable brand recognition and recall. 13,19 In the Indonesian social commerce context, where cultural
diversity and community values shape consumer responses to influencer content, identity alignment between influencer and
audience plays a particularly decisive role in determining whether brand messages register and are retained.1,2
Followers who identify closely with an influencer’s values and lifestyle are more motivated to process associated brands,
producing stronger recognition and recall. 14,19 In the Indonesian context, where community belonging drives consumer
behaviour, influencer-follower identity congruence is especially likely to strengthen brand information encoding. 1,2
However, self-congruity effects are moderated by involvement: in low-involvement categories, identity alignment may
not elevate processing depth enough for durable brand recall. 13 When an influencer’s identity is aspirational rather than
relatable, followers may admire the persona without internalising brand messages, weakening the awareness outcome. 21

Social interaction
Social interaction refers to the reciprocal communicative exchanges between influencers and their followers, encom-
passing comment replies, live sessions, and community-driven engagement. 15 Grounded in Parasocial Interaction
Theory, these exchanges simulate the closeness of real interpersonal relationships, making followers more receptive
to brand-related messages.27 When an influencer consistently engages with their audience rather than simply broadcast-
ing content, followers develop a sense of relational intimacy that heightens attention to and retention of brand
information, directly strengthening brand awareness. 16
On platforms like TikTok, where interaction is embedded in the content experience, this effect is particularly pro-
nounced.1,28 However, when social interaction becomes excessive or commercially driven, it risks eroding the authen-
ticity that makes these exchanges effective in the first place, potentially diminishing their impact on brand awareness.2,20
Followers who develop relational bonds through bidirectional engagement become more receptive to embedded brand
messages, generating deeper processing and stronger brand recall. 15,16 On platforms like TikTok, where replies and live
sessions are integral, followers who feel acknowledged show heightened attentiveness to brand-related content. 1,28
However, when followers perceive engagement as commercially manufactured, parasocial authenticity is undermined,
causing disengagement from associated brands.2,20 Over-endorsement compounds this, triggering persuasion resistance
where followers mentally filter commercial content. The positive effect of social interaction on brand awareness,
therefore, depends on sustained perceived relational authenticity.
Brand awareness
Brand awareness refers to a consumer’s ability to recognise or recall a brand within a given product category. Within the
Customer-Based Brand Equity (CBBE) framework, it represents the foundational layer upon which all other dimensions
of brand equity are built. 23 A brand that is readily accessible in memory is more likely to be associated with positive
qualities, trusted as a credible option, and preferred when purchase decisions are made. This cognitive accessibility
translates directly into the added value that defines brand equity. 9,18
The pathway from brand awareness to brand equity is not automatic. Awareness must be accompanied by meaningful
brand associations and consistent consumer experiences; without these, recognition alone may fail to generate the loyalty
and perceived quality that underpin lasting equity. 17 In the Indonesian social commerce context, where consumers are
exposed to large volumes of influencer content, brands that achieve genuine recall, rather than mere fleeting exposure, are
significantly better positioned to convert that awareness into durable brand equity. 1,2
Within the CBBE framework, brand awareness is the foundational layer from which equity dimensions are built: a brand
inaccessible in memory cannot generate positive associations or loyalty.9,23 Consumers with higher brand awareness are
more likely to develop favourable attitudes and purchase intentions, core components of brand equity. 17,18
However, high awareness does not guarantee equity gains when a brand lacks positive differentiation from competi-
tors.1,2 In saturated social commerce markets, awareness without meaningful associations produces only superficial
familiarity. Brand awareness is, therefore, a necessary but may not be a sufficient condition for building durable brand
equity.

Research methodology
This study adopts a positivist research philosophy, premised on the assumption that social phenomena can be measured
objectively and that hypothesised relationships can be empirically tested and generalised.29 Consistent with this stance, a
deductive research approach was employed, whereby the conceptual framework and five hypotheses were derived from
established theoretical foundations before being submitted to empirical scrutiny. The study is quantitative in nature, with
data collected through a structured online questionnaire composed of items adapted from validated prior scales. Online
surveys are well-suited to this type of research given their capacity to reach large, geographically dispersed samples
efficiently while maintaining standardisation of measurement. 30
A cross-sectional research design was adopted, meaning all data were collected at a single point in time. This design is
appropriate when the objective is to examine the current state of relationships between variables rather than track changes

over time, and it is consistent with the time and resource constraints of the present study. 29 The target population
comprised Indonesian social commerce consumers aged 18 and above with prior experience with social commerce
platforms. Purposive sampling was employed to ensure that only participants who met these eligibility criteria were
included, which is well-suited to quantitative research requiring a specific respondent profile that cannot be guaranteed

The minimum sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1 software, applying a linear multiple regression framework
with the following parameters: a medium effect size (f 2 = 0.15), a significance level of α = 0.05, a statistical power of
1 /C0 β = 0.95, and five predictors corresponding to the maximum number of paths entering any single dependent variable
in the structural model, yielding a minimum required sample size of 138. Data were subsequently analysed using Partial
Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM) with bootstrapping. PLS-SEM is particularly suited to this
study given its ability to handle non-normal data distributions, test mediated structural models, and deliver reliable
estimates with modest sample sizes. 32 All ethical requirements were observed throughout the data collection process.
Ethical approval was obtained from the Asia Pacific University Research Ethics Committee (Reference No: APU/FLT/
EA/2026/012). Participation was voluntary, respondents were fully informed of the study ’s purpose prior to completing
the questionnaire, and anonymity was maintained throughout. Written informed consent for participation in the research
was obtained electronically from all participants: the first page of the online questionnaire presented a participant
information statement, and respondents were required to indicate their written consent by ticking a consent declaration
before they could proceed to the survey items. Written (electronic) consent was chosen over verbal consent because the
survey was self-administered online, allowing a verifiable record of each participant ’s agreement to be retained. Only
adults aged 18 years and above were eligible to participate; no minors were involved in the study, so parental consent and
assent procedures were not required.
Results
Descriptive findings
A total of 200 valid responses were collected and analysed in this study. The platform breakdown tells an interesting story
about where Indonesian social commerce is actually happening: TikTok dominated, chosen by 53% of respondents, with
Facebook accounting for 30% and Instagram for the remaining 17%. This concentration on TikTok is consistent with the
study’s theoretical framing around social interaction and parasocial engagement, given that TikTok ’s design makes
bidirectional influencer-follower exchanges a central part of the content experience.
Engagement frequency was notably high. The largest share of respondents (40.5%) reported engaging with influencer
content daily, followed by 34.5% who engage weekly, while 25% reported occasional engagement. Taken together,
three-quarters of the sample engage with influencer content at least once a week, suggesting that exposure to influencer-
driven brand messages is not incidental but forms a regular part of respondents’ digital media consumption. This level of
exposure creates the conditions under which the brand awareness and brand equity effects hypothesised in this study are
most likely to manifest.

and convergent validity. All factor loadings exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.70, confirming that each item
adequately represented its corresponding construct. 32 Cronbach’s alpha values ranged from 0.837 to 0.946, indicating
good internal consistency across all constructs, and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values ranged from 0.605 to
0.822, all exceeding the threshold of 0.50. These results confirm that the constructs captured a sufficient proportion of
variance in their respective indicators and that the measurement model was suitable for structural analysis. The full

Discriminant validity was assessed using the Fornell-Larcker criterion, which requires the square root of each construct’s

constructs, confirming that each variable is empirically distinct from the others. The diagonal values in Table 2 represent
the square root of each construct ’s AVE, all of which exceed the off-diagonal correlations in their respective rows and

The path coefficients tell a clear and consistent story. Every hypothesised path was supported. Among the four influencer
attributes, social interaction emerged as the strongest driver of brand awareness, with a path coefficient of 0.458,
reinforcing the theoretical centrality of parasocial engagement in the model. Content quality ( β = 0.212), credibility
(β = 0.172), and personal identity (β = 0.141) each contributed positively, though with progressively weaker effects. The
path from brand awareness to brand equity was the strongest relationship in the entire model ( β = 0.721), confirming
brand awareness as both a meaningful outcome of influencer behaviour and a powerful antecedent of brand equity. The

Discussion
The acceptance of H1 ( β = 0.212, T = 3.465, p = 0.001) confirms that content quality positively drives brand awareness
among Indonesian social commerce consumers. Source Credibility Theory explains the mechanism that when followers
perceive content as genuinely informative and well-crafted, they attribute expertise to the influencer and become more
open to the brand messages within it, which strengthens recognition and recall.11,13,17 In Indonesia, where consumers are
increasingly selective, quality signals authenticity in a way that reach alone cannot.21 Although sponsorship detection can
activate scepticism and limit how deeply brand messages are processed, H1 confirms that content quality remains a
distinct and meaningful contributor to brand awareness. 4,7
The acceptance of H2 ( β = 0.172, T = 2.448, p = 0.015) confirms that credibility positively influences brand awareness.
Rooted in Source Credibility Theory, when an influencer is seen as trustworthy, expert, and genuinely engaging,
followers are more receptive to the brands they promote and more likely to retain what they hear. 11,22,24 This matters
especially in Indonesia, where around 24% of consumers actively distrust sponsored content, making credibility one of
the few attributes capable of cutting through that resistance.12 The coefficient of 0.172 reflects a real fragility: influencers
who over-endorse too many brands dilute the very authenticity that makes them worth following. 20,25
The acceptance of H3 ( β = 0.141, T = 2.561, p = 0.011) confirms that personal identity positively influences brand
awareness. Grounded in Self-Congruity Theory, when followers perceive an influencer’s lifestyle and values as a genuine
reflection of their own self-concept. 26 Branded content feels personally relevant rather than commercially imposed,
making brand names and associations more likely to stick in memory. 14,19 The smallest coefficient in the model reflects
an important contingency: identity needs to feel relatable rather than aspirational to drive brand attention, since admiring a
persona from a distance does not automatically extend to the brands associated with it. 13,21
The acceptance of H4 (β = 0.458, T = 6.374, p = 0.000) is the most significant finding in this study. Parasocial Interaction
Theory explains that when influencers engage directly through replies, live sessions, and challenges, they create the
feeling of a genuine two-way relationship. 27 Customers are naturally more receptive to brand recommendations from
those they feel they know. 15,16 TikTok’s dominance among respondents (53%) amplifies this further, as the platform
makes interaction an intrinsic part of how content is consumed rather than an optional extra. 1,28
The acceptance of H5 (β = 0.721, T = 12.821, p = 0.000) produced the strongest path in the model. Within the Customer-
Based Brand Equity framework, brand awareness is the starting point from which associations, perceived quality, and
loyalty are built. 23 This result confirms that influencer-driven awareness translates into genuine and lasting brand
value.9,17,18 An R2 of 0.519 for brand equity is a strong result for a five-construct model, and when read alongside H1
through H4, H5 validates the mediated logic at the centre of this study. Influencer attributes reach brand equity not directly
but by first building awareness, which then converts into equity.
Taken together, these results tell a clear story. All four influencer attributes build brand awareness, but social interaction
(β = 0.458) does so at a scale the others do not approach, suggesting that actively engaging with followers matters far more
than simply producing credible or high-quality content. This challenges the long-standing assumption that credibility is
the dominant attribute, pointing instead to TikTok ’s interaction-first design as a structural amplifier of brand awareness
effects.16,28 The strength of H5 confirms that this awareness then becomes equity, addressing the gap identified in
Section 2: influencer attributes do build lasting brand value in Indonesian social commerce, and brand awareness is the
pathway through which this happens. 1,17
Conclusion
This study set out to answer a question the influencer marketing literature has largely sidestepped: how do influencer
attributes actually build brand equity, and through what process does this happen? By testing a mediated structural model
across 200 Indonesian social commerce consumers using PLS-SEM, the study demonstrates that brand awareness is the
cognitive mechanism connecting influencer behaviour to brand equity. 23 All four influencer attributes, namely content
quality, credibility, personal identity, and social interaction positively and significantly predicted brand awareness (H1 to

H4), which in turn drove brand equity (H5). The R 2 values of 0.604 and 0.519 for brand awareness and brand equity,
respectively, confirm that the model accounts for a substantial share of variance in both outcomes, lending strong support
to the idea that brand awareness is the bridge between what influencers do and the brand value consumers eventually
build.17
The most theoretically significant result is the dominance of social interaction ( β = 0.458). This challenges the long-
standing assumption in influencer marketing research that credibility is the primary driver of brand outcomes and instead
points to parasocial engagement as the more powerful mechanism in high-interaction social commerce environments
such as TikTok. 16,28 The study also contributes by treating brand awareness as a mediating process rather than just
another outcome, bringing Source Credibility Theory, Self-Congruity Theory, and Parasocial Interaction Theory together
into a single testable model for the first time in a social commerce context. 1,17
For practitioners, the findings offer clear and actionable direction. Brands operating in Indonesian social commerce
should prioritise influencers who actively engage with their audiences through replies, live sessions, and community-
driven content, since it is that relational quality, not reach or production value, that most reliably builds brand awareness
and, ultimately, brand equity. Credibility remains a meaningful asset. Influencers who over-endorse multiple brands
erode the trust that makes any single recommendation land. 20 Content quality and personal identity each contribute
distinct pathways to brand awareness and should not be overlooked, particularly when targeting audiences where
authenticity and value alignment shape engagement. 13,14
Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design captures associations at a single point in time and
cannot establish causality. The sample was drawn from Indonesian social commerce consumers using purposive
sampling, which, while appropriate for the research objectives, means the findings may not travel well beyond the
Indonesian context. Relying on self-reported measures also introduces the possibility that respondents ’ answers reflect
how they think they behave rather than how they actually do.
Future research should address these limitations by adopting longitudinal designs to track brand awareness and equity
development over time, replicating the model in other Southeast Asian markets to see how well the findings hold across
different cultural settings, and incorporating bootstrapped indirect effect estimates to formally confirm the mediated
pathways identified here. Including objective brand equity measures or examining how variables like product involve-
ment and platform type shape these relationships would give future work a richer and more actionable picture.
