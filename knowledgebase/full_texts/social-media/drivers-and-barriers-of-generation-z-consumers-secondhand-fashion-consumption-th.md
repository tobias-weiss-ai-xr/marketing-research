ABSTRACT
Second-hand (SH) fashion consumption among Generation Z is shaped by both positive and
negative factors. Drawing on Social Proof Theory, the Theory of Consumption Values, and
Perceived Risk Theory, this study examines how social media influencers' social reassurance,
environmental values, perceived social stigma, and perceived low-quality influence Gen Z
consumers' intentions to purchase and recommend SH apparel in an emerging, collectivist
market. Data were collected from 315 Gen Z consumers in Istanbul via a structured online
survey, and the research model was analyzed using partial least squares structural equation
modeling in SmartPLS4. The findings reveal that environmental value is the strongest positive
driver of both purchase and recommendation intentions. Influencer social reassurance
significantly affects the intention to recommend and the purchase intention for SH garments.
Social stigma is a critical social barrier that dominates intention to recommend and purchase
intention, whereas the inhibitory effect of perceived low quality is practically negligible. This
study enhances theoretical knowledge of SH fashion by integrating value-based, social, and
risk-related perspectives and distinguishing between social and functional risks. Managers
should shift from transactional to advocacy-driven peer-sharing marketing that leverages SH-
style digital social proof through collaboration with credible influencers, pro-environmental
messages, and stigma-reduction techniques.

1. Introduction
The global textile industry is widely recognized for its negative ecological impact (Akter et al.,
2022). Second-hand (SH) fashion reduces the textile industry’s ecological and social impacts
while also offering lower prices to customers (Prisco et al., 2025). The global SH garment
market is expected to reach $393 billion by 2030, growing at twice the rate of the overall
apparel market (ThredUp, 2026). In the current fashion re-commerce market, Gen Z and
millennial consumers attempt to sell 52% of their clothes and transact via digital channels,
including social commerce (ThredUp, 2026). As a rapidly growing market, Türkiye’s second-
hand clothing market is estimated at 1,2 billion $ due to economic and ecological factors, where
Gen Z consumers increasingly prefer SH fashion platforms like Dolap, with 15 million users;
Gardrops, with 10 million users (Indetexx, 2025; Baruönü,2025; Avcı, 2026). Moreover, 33
million customers bought SH products in 2020, and 76% of them intended to repurchase them
in Türkiye (Baruönü, 2025). Türkiye has a considerable youth population, with young people
aged 15–24 accounting for around 15% of the national population (TÜİK & UNFPA, 2025).
They are highly digitally embedded, as reflected in the very high rate of social media use among
16–24-year-olds (above 95%) (TÜİK & UNFPA, 2025). Gen Z consumers’ engagement with
SH fashion in Türkiye increases through social media and platforms (Özkan, 2025).
Generation Z shows concern for ecological, economic, and social sustainability issues such as
biodiversity loss, income inequality, and rising carbon emissions, yet they frequently purchase
fast-fashion apparel (Bhutto & Rūtelionė, 2026; Zulu et al., 2026). This green paradox is not
surprising, as sustainable consumption research aims to address the attitude-behavior gap
across diverse consumption contexts (e.g., McNeill & Moore, 2015). Previous studies on Gen
Z’s relationship with SH fashion have shown that hygiene factors (Wang et al., 2022; Herjanto
et al., 2024); environmental attitude (Herjanto et al., 2024); emotional and epistemic value
(Koay & Leong, 2026); green signaling (Huh & Kim, 2024); and environmental concern,
environmental knowledge, and image barriers (Acikgoz et al., 2026) are significant influences.
Meanwhile, while most studies have focused on Gen Z consumers' SH clothing purchase
intention, few (e.g., Koay & Leong, 2026) have examined their intention to recommend SH
fashion as an outcome, given their status as an environmentally sensitive generation. Moreover,
because social media platforms and influencers are an important reference point for consumers
in fashion consumption, little research (e.g., Boyer et al., 2024; Nguyen & Nguyen, 2025a) has
examined their impact on Gen Z consumers’ SH clothing consumption. A few studies (e.g.,
Özkan, 2025; Acikgoz et al., 2026) have also examined the enablers and barriers of SH and
sustainable clothing consumption among Gen Z consumers.
Considering these gaps, this study examines the positive and negative factors that influence
Gen Z consumers' willingness to buy and recommend SH clothing in Türkiye, a highly
promising SH market. This research integrates Social Proof Theory, the Theory of
Consumption Values (TCV), and Perceived Risk Theory (PRT) to propose a distinctive
conceptual model that clarifies influencer social reassurance (ISR) and environmental value
(ENV) as positive motivating factors, while perceived low quality (LOWQUAL) and perceived
social stigma (STIGMA) serve as active inhibiting factors.
This study aims to contribute to existing research by combining theoretical frameworks to
separately analyze the determinants of intentions to purchase and recommend SH fashion
experiences among Turkish Generation Z consumers. Additionally, this research provides
further evidence that influencers' social reassurance serves as a social validation tool for Gen
Z consumers' SH clothing purchases and recommendations in an emerging market like Türkiye.
Furthermore, it seeks to reposition young consumers’ intention to recommend SH clothing as

2. Conceptual Framework and Hypothesis Development
2.1. Social Proof Theory, Theory of Consumption Values, Perceived Risk Theory
In emerging markets like Türkiye, adopting SH clothing is not straightforward due to technical
and social barriers (Calvo-Porral & Lévy-Mangin, 2026). Adoption involves a tension between
environmental values and persistent sociocultural barriers, including perceived social stigma
and hygiene risks (Wang et al., 2022). A study of Gen Z consumers in Istanbul found that
performance or technical risk hindered SH fashion adoption (Onurlubaş & Gümüş, 2022). Ögel
(2022) found that novelty-seeking, frugality, environmental consciousness, and treasure-
hunting influence young consumers’ desire to purchase SH clothing. Özkan (2025) showed
that sustainability, price advantage, and nostalgia positively influence Turkish Gen Z
consumers’ SH fashion purchase intention, whereas social stigma can undermine this
relationship. Furthermore, social media and influencers, which are critical antecedents of
consumer behavior in fashion (e.g., Onurlubaş, 2023) and brand trust (Bora Semiz & Paylan,
2023), remain underexplored in SH clothing consumption research in Türkiye. While Turkish
consumers are interested in the zero-waste movement and influenced by green influencers on
platforms like Instagram (e.g., Tavman & Doğan, 2025), the credibility of an influencer, which
has been shown to be a more powerful antecedent of Turkish consumers' purchase intention
across contexts, seems to be neglected in SH fashion studies in Türkiye.
This study's research model is grounded in the theories of Social Proof, Consumption Values,
and Perceived Risks. It aims to explain how social media influencers legitimize SH fashion
through social reassurance and proof, while aligning with Gen Z consumers' environmental
values, without underestimating the impact of social stigma and the risk of low quality in SH
fashion. The Social Proof Theory suggests that people tend to observe others' actions to guide
their own behavior, particularly in uncertain or doubtful situations (Cialdini, 1993). This
psychological phenomenon indicates that the crowd's perceived wisdom, which can also be
interpreted as social norms in some contexts, acts as a significant heuristic for decision-making,
confirming a choice as a social fact (Cialdini & Goldstein, 2004). Recent studies in young
consumer behavior (e.g., Theocharis & Tsekouropoulos, 2025) indicate that Gen Z is a
validation generation, evaluating brand credibility based on social proof and transparency. In
influencer marketing, Kong and Lou (2026) found that visual social proof enhanced social
validation and customer engagement for the products. Given influencers’ impact on SH fashion
consumption (e.g., Haines et al., 2023; Kaivonen et al., 2024; Nguyen & Nguyen, 2025b; Sekar,
2025), the Social Proof Theory’s principle of social evidence and validation can be examined
through the influencers' social reassurance (ISR) construct, which reduces ambiguity and
technical barriers to SH fashion (Boyer et al., 2024). Influencer Social Reassurance (ISR) is
conceptualized as an active, comparative cognitive mechanism through which consumers
evaluate and validate their SH clothing consumption choices against a preferred social
reference, a trustworthy, legitimate influencer (Londoño-Bedoya et al., 2025). Grounded in the
Social Proof and Social Comparison Theories, ISR reflects an individual's conscious tendency
to compare their evaluations and status concerns with the qualitative narratives of a trusted
influencer to secure normative validation, as subjective norms are critical in secondhand

clothing consumption (Koay et al., 2024; Londoño-Bedoya et al., 2025; De-Juan-Vigaray &
Cocco, 2026). As social reference points with expertise, credibility, and trustworthiness in SH
fashion, influencers’ social reassurance promotes SH clothing consumption from a
sustainability perspective; they provide social validation that influences both the intention to
buy and the intention to recommend luxury or non-luxury sustainable fashion (Shrivastava et
al., 2021; Haines et al., 2023; Koay et al., 2024; Sekar, 2025; Londoño-Bedoya et al., 2025).
The Theory of Consumption Values (TCV) holds that consumer choices are driven by multiple
independent values, including emotional, social, epistemic, and environmental values (Sheth
et al., 1991; Koay & Leong, 2026; Koay et al., 2026). Because SH fashion is associated with
perceived environmental benefits, including extending product lifecycles, reducing waste, and
supporting more sustainable consumption (D’Souza et al., 2026), environmental value, an
important element of the TCV, encompasses both the ecological and economic benefits of SH
fashion and consumers' environmental attitudes. Gen Z consumers who hold strong
environmental attitudes tend to prioritize environmental benefits, purchase SH fashion, and
actively promote it, reflecting their values (Prisco et al., 2025). Moreover, environmental value
is recognized as a key driver of Gen Z consumers’ SH clothing shop visits and WOM intentions
(Koay & Leong, 2026).
Perceived Risk Theory (PRT) is essential for interpreting consumer behavior and holds that
consumers assess potential risks and uncertainties in terms of the utility and value of a good or
service before making purchasing decisions (Mitchell, 1992; Koay et al., 2026). In SH fashion,
Kim et al. (2021) defined financial (e.g., monetary loss), aesthetic (e.g., visual appeal),
functional (e.g., durability, low quality), and hygiene risks. Moreover, SH can be considered a
social risk because it can diminish customers’ self-image. This research model includes
negative factors such as perceived social stigma (STIGMA) and perceived low quality
(LOWQUAL) to evaluate their influence alongside the influencers’ social reassurance (ISR)
and environmental value (ENV). STIGMA and LOWQUAL act as social and technical barriers
to Gen Z consumers’ SH apparel consumption, likely reducing both purchase intention and the
likelihood of recommending it. This research model proposes synthesizing these three
paradigms into an enabler-barrier-legitimizer perspective, in which the TCV explains the
enabler (environmental value), PRT explains the psychological inhibitor (social stigma and
perceived low quality), and the Social Proof Theory, operationalized through influencer social
reassurance, serves as the strategic legitimizer against social and functional risks in SH fashion.
In the research model, purchase intention reflects consumers’ willingness to adopt and use SH
apparel (Aycock et al., 2023), whereas recommendation intention represents an allocentric,
socially visible form of peer advocacy that is conceptually and psychologically distinct from
individual purchase intention (Koay & Leong, 2026). Purchase intention captures a consumer's
private willingness to acquire and wear SH garments (Aycock et al., 2023; Prisco et al., 2025),
whereas recommendation intention reflects the active willingness to spread positive eWOM to
one's social network (Aycock et al., 2023; Koay & Leong, 2026). In SH fashion,
recommendations can be shaped by concerns about social identity, network norms, and
relational risk, rather than by mere transactional utility (Lo et al., 2019). For Gen Z,
recommending secondhand fashion serves as valuable social currency, allowing them to
publicly signal their environmental values, fashion uniqueness, and ethical alignment (Koay &
Leong, 2026). Recommendation also entails significant reputational risk, as endorsing an
unregulated or physically defective product directly threatens the recommender’s interpersonal
credibility and status (Jiang & Veg-Sala, 2026). Consequently, recommendation intention

Cialdini (1993) argued that a person’s social network, particularly those who use a particular
product, serves as social proof and provides social validation of the product's value and
desirability. Consumers often determine their behavior toward a given product by observing
others' behavior in the same context (Cialdini, 1993; Zollo et al., 2021). Through social
comparison with significant others, such as influencers and celebrities, and with other
consumers' comments, likes, and reviews, consumers validate their decision-making processes
(Zollo et al., 2021). Moreover, social proof can directly boost customer engagement by
leveraging social consensus, peer endorsements, and comments (Kong & Lou, 2026). Social
media can be conceptualized as an important source of social proof for fashion consumers, as
Instagram can directly influence impulsive purchase decisions (Djafarova & Bowes, 2021).
Siregar et al. (2023) demonstrated that Gen Z engages in apparel shopping driven by the
fulfillment of experiential needs, which are influenced by their social media networks and
broader connections, thereby also providing social validation. Influencers’ opinion leadership,

2.3. Environmental Value and Gen Z Consumers’ Intention to Purchase and Recommend
Second-hand Clothing
Environmental value is consumers' ecological commitment to sustainability and environmental
concerns (Biswas & Roy, 2015). Environmental values, or ecological consciousness, positively
influence the intention to buy SH clothing (Aggarwal et al., 2026). Environmental value is a
core component of social and green value and drives the adoption of circular models among
Gen Z consumers (Prisco et al., 2025). Eco-conscious consumers aim to reduce textile waste,
lower their carbon footprint, and support circular economy practices. Hur (2020) found that
ecological value was a critical driver of SH clothing purchases, alongside hedonic, economic,
and social values. In research on circular fashion products, including reused, upcycled, and
recycled clothing, Kim et al. (2021) found that environmental value significantly influenced
SH apparel, positively affecting product attitude, which, in turn, influenced word-of-mouth
intention. Koay et al. (2022) showed that environmental values were positively associated with
attitudes, which, in turn, influenced purchase intention for SH clothing. Mathur et al. (2026)
showed that environmental attitude positively influenced green purchase intention among Gen
Z consumers.
Although an attitude-behavior gap exists, internal ecological commitment to sustainability
remains a strong predictor of both the intent to buy SH and the desire to advocate for it within

2.4. Perceived Social Stigma and Gen Z Consumers’ Intention to Purchase and
Recommend Second-hand Clothing
Consumers prioritize perceived risks over a product or service's utility, underscoring the role
of risk perception in buying decisions. Perceived risk is multifaceted and encompasses several
types, including aesthetic risk (e.g., unfashionable clothing), financial risk (e.g., economic
loss), functional risk (e.g., durability of SH clothing), and psychological risk (e.g., social
embarrassment) (Hur et al., 2020; Koay et al., 2026). Perceived Risk Theory highlights social
risk, particularly the fear of stigmatization as financially marginalized, as a key obstacle to SH
consumption (Wang et al., 2022). This social stigma is often linked to the idea that SH clothing
signifies poverty (Valor et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022).
Perceptions of SH clothing as inferior or unclean exacerbate social stigma, discouraging
prospective buyers (Aggarwal et al., 2025; Herjanto et al., 2024). Koay et al. (2023) found that
SH clothing shoppers are less inclined to buy such items when they fear negative judgment
from others. Gen Z is highly sensitive to public self-consciousness and peer-group norms
(McKinsey Explainers, 2024). Social stigma not only deters people from buying SH fashion
but also greatly reduces their willingness to recommend it to peers because they fear negative
social judgment (Boyer et al., 2024). Mobarak et al. (2025) asserted that perceived social
stigma hinders customers' SH garment buying, particularly among new consumers.
Embarrassment is a significant inhibitor that weakens the transition from a positive
environmental attitude to Gen Z's actual SH apparel purchase intention in Türkiye (Özkan,
2025). This hygiene issue has led to hesitation toward SH fashion, a concern worsened by the
lack of transparency about sourcing and prior use. This technical risk heightens consumer
concerns about the cleanliness and safety of SH clothes and also becomes a social risk (Hur,
2020). The enduring social issue of doubts about SH clothing is difficult to address because it
requires a change in social norms around sustainable consumption (Valor et al., 2021). These
longstanding psychosocial barriers can outweigh environmental concerns. Even if pro-
environmental norms grow, purchasing habits do not necessarily align with them (Evans et al.,
2022). This well-known attitude-behavior gap among actual consumers underscores the strong

2.5. Perceived Low Quality and Gen Z Consumers’ Intention to Purchase and
Recommend Second-hand Fashion
The second dimension of Perceived Risk Theory in this research model concerns performance
risk and low quality (Hur, 2020; Wang et al., 2022). Within Perceived Risk Theory, concerns
about the physical and operational integrity of SH garments are a primary barrier to purchase
decisions (Koay & Cheah, 2025). This construct is often operationalized as functional and
performance risk, reflecting uncertainty about an apparel’s ability to meet its intended purpose
(Koay et al., 2026; Koay & Cheah, 2025). In an SH fashion context, performance risk is often
compounded by hygiene risk and concerns about physical integrity, as well as the lack of return
guarantees or warranties in many SH retail settings, heightening the perceived financial risk of
purchasing an item that might fail to meet expectations (Koay et al., 2026; Koay & Cheah,
2025).
Hygiene concerns are a critical dimension of perceived low quality and performance risk,
particularly for Turkish Gen Z consumers (Özkan, 2025). Associating SH clothing with
potential sanitary issues acts as a disgust factor that significantly reduces purchase intention,
regardless of Gen Z consumers’ positive environmental attitudes (Boyer et al., 2024). Mobarak
et al. (2025) and Özkan (2025) suggested that customers may view used clothes as having a
shorter lifespan, being less durable, and having inferior quality due to prior ownership.
Technical risks include concerns about fabric quality, stitch strength, and a worn appearance,
as well as the possibility that the item will deteriorate further after washing (Koay & Cheah,
2025). Onurlubaş and Gümüş (2022) found that perceived risks associated with low-quality
perceptions negatively affected the buying intentions of Turkish Gen Z consumers for SH
clothing. Low quality directly reduces the product's utility and functional value, thereby
lowering purchase intention and making consumers reluctant to risk their reputations by
recommending such items to peers (Wang et al., 2022). Therefore, the following hypotheses
are formulated:
H7: Perceived low quality of second-hand clothes negatively influences Gen Z consumers'
purchase intention of second-hand fashion.
H8: Perceived low quality of second-hand clothes negatively influences Gen Z consumers'
intention to recommend second-hand fashion.
3. Methodology
This paper used a quantitative, cross-sectional design to examine relationships in the research
model. The research information has been provided. To exclude non-Gen Z consumers, we
added an obligatory question to verify age eligibility, and we also collected demographic
information on gender, education, and age.

3.1. Scales Used
The items for Influencers’ Social Reassurance were adapted from Boyer et al. (2024);
environmental values from Koay et al. (2026); social stigma and perceived low quality from
Calvo-Porral et al. (2024a); purchase intention from D'Souza et al. (2026); and the intention to
recommend from Boyer et al. (2024). Each construct in the research model is assessed using a
5-point Likert scale, with 1 indicating “strongly disagree” and 5 indicating “strongly agree”
(cf. Appendix A). The questionnaire was initially developed in English and then adapted into
Turkish using Brislin’s (1970) back-translation method. Two expert linguists first translated
the original items into Turkish. Two marketing professors reviewed the resulting Turkish
version to assess its conceptual equivalence, contextual relevance, and clarity regarding
second-hand apparel consumption. They then back-translated the Turkish version into English
and compared it with the original items. The translators and academic experts addressed and
resolved any minor differences between the original and back-translated versions by consensus.
This procedure ensured linguistic accuracy and conceptual equivalence between the English
and Turkish versions of the questionnaire.
Before the main data collection, we conducted a pilot test with 25 Gen Z consumers to assess
the questionnaire's clarity, comprehensibility, and completion time. Pilot participants indicated
that the items were generally understandable and relevant to the context of second-hand
fashion. No items were removed after the pilot test. However, the researchers made minor
wording adjustments to improve readability and contextual clarity, especially in items related
to second-hand apparel, influencers’ social reassurance, and intention to recommend. These
modifications did not change the original scales' conceptual meaning. The final version of the
questionnaire was then employed for the main survey.
3.2. Data Collection and Research Sample
Consistent with Bhutto & Rūtelionė (2026), participants in this study were assumed to have
been born between 1995 and 2010, and the research population comprised Gen Z individuals
aged 18 to 31. Building on previous research on Gen Z (e.g., Kara & Min, 2024), this study
included individuals in this age group, primarily university students at various levels and recent
graduates. Data collection began after approval from the Istanbul Bilgi University Ethics
Committee on February 05, 2026, and continued until April 15, 2026.
The study used a convenience sampling approach to reach Gen Z consumers in Istanbul. This
approach was considered appropriate because the study aimed to test theory-driven
relationships within a relatively homogeneous and digitally accessible consumer segment,
rather than to estimate population parameters. In consumer behavior research, convenience
samples are often used to test conceptual models among accessible, homogeneous respondent
groups, particularly when a complete sampling frame is unavailable (Peterson & Merunka,
2014). Similarly, recent second-hand fashion research on Gen Z consumers has used
convenience sampling to examine social reassurance and behavioral outcomes related to
recommendations (Boyer et al., 2024). In the present study, convenience sampling also enabled
access to respondents relevant to the research context, namely Gen Z consumers familiar with
digital fashion and second-hand apparel.  To test this research model, Partial Least Squares
Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) is adopted because it is frequently preferred in
recent studies in SH fashion across different countries (e.g., Koay et al., 2024; Özkan, 2025;
Koay & Leong, 2025; and Prisco et al., 2025) for its strength in handling complex structural

4.2. Common Method Bias
To address potential common-method bias, the study implemented both procedural and
statistical measures. Respondents were assured of their anonymity and confidentiality, and the
questionnaire was designed to reduce ambiguity and concerns about evaluation (Podsakoff et
al., 2003). Harman’s single-factor test indicated that the first unrotated factor accounted for
41% of the total variance, below the 50% threshold. Following Kock’s (2015) full collinearity
assessment approach, we further examined common method bias by inspecting the full
collinearity VIF values. The results ranged from 1.184 to 3.426. Although one value marginally
exceeded the conservative 3.3 threshold suggested by Kock (2015), all values remained below
5.0. Together with Harman’s single-factor test result of 41% and the procedural remedies
implemented during data collection, these findings suggest that common method bias is
unlikely to substantially distort the results.
4.3. Testing Structural Model
Before assessing the hypothesized relationships, we examined collinearity among the predictor
constructs using inner VIFs. The results indicated that all inner VIF values were below the
conservative threshold of 3.0, ranging from 1.226 to 2.333. Specifically, the VIF values for
Environmental Value and Influencers’ Social Reassurance were 2.307 and 2.333, respectively.
The VIF values for low Quality and Perceived Social Stigma were 1.226 and 1.509,
respectively. These findings indicate that multicollinearity among the predictor constructs is
not a concern in the structural model, allowing the estimated path coefficients to be interpreted
without substantial concern about collinearity-related distortion.

5. Conclusion and Discussion
This study aims to interpret the main factors affecting SH fashion consumption among Turkish
Gen Z consumers. Accordingly, it explores how influencers’ social reassurance, environmental
values, social stigma, and low quality affect Gen Z consumers’ intentions to purchase and
recommend SH clothing.
The confirmation of H1 and H2, in line with Boyer et al. (2024) and Sekar (2025), proves that
influencer social reassurance (ISR) significantly affects both the intention to recommend and
to purchase SH apparel. These results support recent studies on influencers’ impact in circular
fashion (e.g., Londoño-Bedoya et al., 2025) and clothing consumption in Türkiye (e.g.,
Onurlubaş, 2023). ISR serves as a vital normative buffer, proving significantly more influential
on recommendations (H2: β=0.301, p<0.001) than on individual purchase (H1: β=0.197,
p=0.003). Influencers provide social validation that helps Turkish Gen Z consumers overcome
lingering social self-consciousness and advocate for secondhand fashion among their peers.
Accordingly, influencers appear crucial for providing social proof that supports SH fashion and
reduces social concerns about it. As SH fashion may still carry social uncertainty, influencers
can serve as social validation mechanisms and sources of social proof and legitimization, thus
increasing the social acceptability of SH fashion. In Türkiye, influencer credibility strongly
shapes fashion brand perception; the social assurance it provides bridges the gap and supports
customer decision-making (Onurlubaş, 2023). As digital-native consumers, Gen Z consumers
depend heavily on influencers for social reassurance, expertise, and trustworthiness, which
validates their sustainable choices and normalizes SH shopping as fashionable and acceptable
rather than an indicator of economic difficulty (Shrivastava et al., 2021; Londoño-Bedoya et
al., 2025). Recommending SH fashion to others allows consumers to participate in the social
diffusion of a trend already legitimized by influencers.
The Theory of Consumption Values further clarifies the necessity of environmental values.
Environmental value has a strong, consistent effect on both purchase (H3: β = 0.435, p < 0.001)
and recommendation intentions (H4: β = 0.443, p < 0.001), indicating that Gen Z consumers
respond positively to the ecological benefits of extending product lifecycles and reducing
fashion waste. Environmental value emerged as the strongest antecedent of Turkish Gen Z
consumers’ intentions to recommend and purchase SH clothing, consistent with previous
studies across contexts (Salem & Alanadoly, 2021; Koay et al., 2022; Navarro-Valero et al.,
2026; Kien et al., 2026). Sustainability and environmental values remain central to Gen Z's
consumption values (Prisco et al., 2025). These findings align with local research in Türkiye
(e.g., Ögel, 2022; Özkan, 2025), which shows that sustainability is a key motivator for Gen Z,
particularly among those driven by the zero-waste movement. The consistent and significant
impact of environmental value on both dependent variables suggests that sustainability is more
than just symbolic; it functions as a fundamental motivational factor in SH fashion
consumption (Koay et al., 2024; 2025). Consumers may recommend SH fashion because it lets
them communicate sustainability-oriented values and align with a socially approved circular
consumption practice. Yet, without sufficient product confidence or perceived utility,
influencer reassurance alone may not convert into purchase intention. Thus, a recommendation
may emerge as a lower-cost expression of environmental and social value, whereas a purchase

requires stronger integration of environmental value with personal consumption benefits. This
finding extends the discussion of social proof by showing that influencer reassurance may first
encourage peer-to-peer diffusion and normalization, rather than immediately producing
purchase conversion. The larger effect size of environmental values on recommendation
intention (f² =0.262 vs. 0.193) reflects the social nature of word-of-mouth (WOM). While a
personal purchase can be private and utilitarian, recommending is an allocentric, reputation-
building behavior. As suggested by Jiang & Veg-Sala (2026), Gen Z consumers are not just
sharing information about sustainable consumption; they may also conspicuously display their
ethical and ecological awareness, eco-literacy, and moral character through SH fashion
recommendations.
In line with this result, the negative effect of stigma is particularly pronounced, as it is stronger
for purchase intention (H5: β = -0.222, p < 0.001) than for intention to recommend SH fashion
(H6: β = -0.125, p=0.003). This suggests that stigma becomes more salient when consumers
consider personally buying and wearing second-hand apparel, where identity exposure and fear
of social judgment are more direct. In contrast, recommendations may allow consumers to
support second-hand fashion at a more symbolic or discursive level without necessarily
assuming the same level of personal visibility. This finding enriches Perceived Risk Theory by
showing that social risk is especially relevant to personal adoption in fashion contexts where
clothing is closely linked to identity, status, and self-presentation. The confirmation of the
negative impact of perceived social stigma mirrors the embarrassment factor Özkan (2025)
identified as a primary deterrent for young Turkish consumers. The fear of being associated
with financial hardship or pre-loved items significantly dampens Gen Z's willingness to both
buy and, more importantly, recommend SH items to their peer groups to avoid social judgment
(Boyer et al., 2024; Özkan, 2025; Mobarak et al., 2025; Koay & Leong, 2026). Consumers
appear more concerned about how others perceive them than about the functional attributes of
SH products. The social barrier is especially relevant in fashion consumption, where identity
expression and social image play a central role.
Perceived low quality, whether viewed as a technical or functional risk, has only a small effect
on Gen Z consumers’ willingness to purchase and promote SH fashion (H7: β = -0.095,
p=0.026; H8: β = -0.171, p < 0.001). This distinction refines Perceived Risk Theory, indicating
that social risk outweighs performance risk among Turkish Gen Z consumers for SH clothing.
The negative impact of low quality, a performance and hygiene risk, is consistent with cross-
cultural findings that identify functional risk as a top-tier barrier to SH clothing purchase
intention (Wang et al., 2022; Koay & Cheah, 2025). Although quality concerns negatively
affect both outcomes, the negligible f² value for Purchase Intention suggests that functional
risk has limited practical influence on consumers’ own purchase decisions. The negligible
impact of low-quality SH clothing on purchase and recommendation intentions may reflect
that, as digital natives, Gen Z consumers can manage quality risks privately through mobile
C2C app utilities (reviews, escrow payment protections, and direct messaging) on popular
Turkish platforms. Meanwhile, its stronger effect (f² =0.074) on recommendation implies that
consumers may become more cautious when their advice could affect others’ experiences and
social validation.
6. Theoretical and Practical Implications
This study contributes to research on second-hand fashion consumption by offering a nuanced
explanation of how value-based, social, and risk-related mechanisms shape Gen Z consumers’
purchase and recommendation intentions in Türkiye. Rather than developing a new theory, the
study provides context-specific evidence for applying the Theory of Consumption Values,

Social Proof Theory, and Perceived Risk Theory in an emerging-market SH fashion context.
The findings confirm that environmental value is the most consistent antecedent of both
purchase- and advocacy-oriented behaviors, underscoring the importance of environmental
concerns in SH clothing consumption. The study also provides qualified evidence for the role
of Social Proof Theory. ISR is treated as an adapted, context-specific operationalization of
influencer-mediated social reassurance in young consumers' SH apparel consumption. ISR
significantly affects both recommendation and purchase intentions, but its effect is stronger on
recommendation. This suggests that influencer-based social proof may operate especially
strongly through advocacy and social validation, rather than only through direct purchase
conversion. In addition, the findings refine the application of Perceived Risk Theory by
showing that social and functional risks affect behavioral outcomes differently. Perceived
social stigma and perceived low quality both negatively affect purchase intention and intention
to recommend; however, their relative importance differs across outcomes. Social stigma has
a stronger practical effect on purchase intention, suggesting that identity exposure,
embarrassment, and perceived social judgment are especially relevant when consumers
consider buying and wearing SH apparel themselves. Perceived low quality, by contrast, has a
stronger practical effect on intention to recommend, suggesting that functional risk becomes
particularly salient when consumers consider advising others and potentially risking their own
credibility. The study contributes to the SH fashion literature by treating purchase intention
and intention to recommend as related but theoretically distinct outcomes. The findings show
that the same antecedents do not influence both outcomes equally for Gen Z consumers in
Türkiye.
The findings have practical implications for Türkiye's second-hand fashion platforms, brands,
and social media actors. First, Gen Z consumers value the environment (H3, H4); SH fashion
platforms should communicate sustainability clearly and in personally meaningful ways.
Rather than making broad environmental claims, platforms may emphasize how each purchase
extends garment lifecycles, reduces textile waste, lowers environmental impact, and promotes
circular fashion. Because environmental value strongly influences recommendation intention,
post-purchase impact reviews and shareable sustainability badges should be designed for social
sharing and advocacy. Second, influencers' social reassurance significantly improved
recommendations and purchase intention of Gen Z consumers (H1, H2). This shows that
influencer marketing is effective both as a social normalization agent and as a peer-to-peer
sharing channel among SH fashion Gen Z consumers in Türkiye. Thus, SH platforms’
influencer collaborations should emphasize peer-to-peer sharing, styling inspiration, social
advocacy, and the homogenization of SH fashion. Instead of just promoting products or deals,
influencers may show how they choose, style, and confidently wear SH clothing in daily life,
while delivering messages that highlight SH fashion’s creativity and social acceptance. They
can also encourage followers to publish SH fashion reviews, outfits, and experiences to receive
specific discounts on SH clothing purchases. Influencers can directly engage young consumers
through peer-to-peer interactions to share SH fashion experiences on campuses, in malls, on
livestreams, or in dedicated channels. Third, perceived social stigma was a negative social
barrier to the intention to purchase and recommend SH fashion, with a significantly stronger
effect on purchase intention. To reduce social barriers to Gen Z consumers’ SH clothing
choices, platforms and brands should normalize and promote SH clothing transactions through
influencer-generated content and sales promotions on campuses, on public transportation, in
mobile apps, and in malls to encourage young consumers to purchase SH clothing in Türkiye.
Moreover, platforms and brands should reposition SH fashion as stylish, curated, innovative,
and sustainable, rather than as cheap or used. Visual communication for Turkish Gen Z should

highlight beautiful styling, trend compatibility, distinctiveness, and socially desirable
consumption. Confident young buyers, creators, influencers, micro-influencers, micro-

recommend and purchase SH garments. This implies that if Turkish Gen Z consumers worry
that low product quality will disappoint colleagues or damage their credibility, they may avoid
promoting and buying SH clothing. Accordingly, quality assurance procedures should support
purchasing decisions and boost consumer confidence in promoting SH platforms and products.
Product descriptions, high-quality photos and videos, seller ratings, authenticity checks,
cleaning advice, quick return options, and verified customer reviews may reduce advocacy and
purchase hesitation.
7. Limitations And Future Research Agenda
The data were collected in Istanbul, Türkiye, and may differ from those of consumers in other
cities and regions across the country. Convenience sampling is a methodological limitation of
this study. While this method facilitated access to Gen Z consumers relevant to the research
context, it limits the statistical generalizability of the results to the broader Gen Z consumers
in Türkiye. Future research should consider employing probability-based, stratified, purposive,
or snowball sampling strategies and gather data from multiple cities or regions to improve
sample diversity and enhance the external validity of the findings.
Cultural factors such as fashion preferences, stigma, and social norms, along with analyses of
actual purchasing behavior, can offer valuable insights into SH and sustainable fashion.
Conducting cross-cultural studies can also yield important findings. Because self-reported data
may be biased, especially on sensitive topics such as stigma and environmental concerns, due
to social desirability bias, future research should consider methods such as longitudinal studies,
experiments, or mixed-methods approaches to better understand consumers’ SH fashion
buying patterns and the impact of various predictors. Qualitative techniques, such as in-depth
interviews and focus groups, could further yield new insights by exploring the complex
meanings associated with stigma and SH consumption behaviors.
Even if this study integrates different theoretical perspectives and constructs to explain SH
clothing purchase intention, social (e.g., social recycling, disposal) and economic sustainability
(e.g., perceived financial value, deal proneness) can also be examined with individual traits
(e.g., self-construal), seller-related dimensions (e.g., image of the platform), types and roles of
influencers (Nguyen & Nguyen, 2026), and influencer-related constructs (e.g., greenfluencers
vs. micro celebrities) to interpret SH and circular fashion. Future studies could explore how
immersive technologies that utilize social proof, in partnership with social media influencers,
can help overcome various barriers to SH fashion. The eventual collaboration between green
influencers and both luxury and non-luxury fashion brands can be analyzed to understand its
effect on consumers' perceptions of greenwashing within these brands. Finally, the indicators
LOWQUAL1 and STIGMA3 were excluded from the final measurement model due to their
low outer loadings. Although this exclusion improved reliability and convergent validity,
future research should further validate the revised measurement structure. Additionally,
consideration should be given to incorporating supplementary items to comprehensively
capture the conceptual scope of perceived low quality and the social stigma associated with SH
fashion.

do.

not the only one with concerns about secondhand fashion.

social media influencers because I am interested in secondhand fashion
trends.
