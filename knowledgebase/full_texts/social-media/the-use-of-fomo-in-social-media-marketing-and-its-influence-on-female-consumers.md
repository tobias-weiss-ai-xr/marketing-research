This study examines how Fear of Missing Out (FoMO) is used as a psychological lever in
social media marketing and how it shapes the purchasing behavior of female consumers.
As platforms such as Instagram and TikTok have grown more central to everyday life,
marketers increasingly rely on urgency- and scarcity-based tactics—limited-time offers,
countdown timers, influencer endorsements, and visible peer participation —to prompt
rapid purchasin g decisions. Using a quantitative, cross -sectional survey design, data
were collected from 302 female respondents aged 18–35 through an online questionnaire
distributed via WhatsApp, Facebook, and Instagram. Responses were analyzed using
descriptive statistics, reliability testing, Pearson correlation, and regression analysis in
SPSS. Results show that exposure to FoMO -based marketing strategies is significantly
and positively associated with feelings of FoMO (r = 0.56, p < .001), which in turn is
strongly associated with impulsive consumer behavior (r = 0.61, p < .001). Regression
analysis confirmed that FoMO fully mediates the relationship between marketing
exposure and purchase behavior, meaning marketing exposure influences buying
decisions primarily by activating emotional discomfort rather than through direct
persuasion. All three hypotheses were supported. The findings extend Self -
Determination Theory, Social Comparison Theory, Uses and Gratifications Theory, and
the Elaboration Likelihood Model into t he domain of digital consumer psychology and
suggest that FoMO functions as a situationally induced emotional state rather than a
fixed personality trait. The study concludes with implications for ethical marketing
practice, consumer media literacy, and di rections for future longitudinal and
experimental research.
1. Introduction
Social media has evolved from a communication channel into a central space where identity construction, social interaction,
and consumer activity intersect. Platforms such as Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, and Snapchat are interactive, personalized,
and socially visible, allowing users to continuously compare their lives with those of others (Ellison et al., 2007). This constant
exposure to curated content has reshaped the psychological context in which consumer decisions are made.
One phenomenon central to this shift is the Fear of Missing Out (FoMO) —a pervasive apprehension that others are having
rewarding experiences from which one is absent, coupled with a desire to stay continuously connected (Przybylski et al., 2013).
Although FoMO originated as a construct linked to social media use and well -being, it has become increasingly relevant to

Female consumers are a particularly important group in this cont ext. Women tend to engage more frequently with visually

2022; Przybylski et al., 2013), which may heighten their emotional responsiveness to FoMO-inducing content. Despite growing

female consumers—remains underexplored. Most existing work treats FoMO either as an outc ome of social media use or a
predictor of problematic behavior (Servidio et al., 2025), rather than as the psychological bridge connecting marketing stimuli
to purchasing action. This gap motivates the present study.
1.1. Problem Statement
As FoMO-based strategies intensify, their psychological consequences—heightened anxiety, impulsive consumption, and post-
purchase dissatisfaction—raise concern, particularly among young female consumers who are highly active on social media.
Existing research often assumes a direct link between marketing exposure and purchasing, overlooking the emotional processes
that intervene between stimulus and response, and rarely isolates how FoMO-driven marketing uniquely affects women.
1.2. Objectives and Research Questions
The study pursues three objectives: (1) to examine the impact of exposure to FoMO -based social media marketing on female
consumers; (2) to investigate the relationship between FoMO and impulsive buying behavior among female consumers; and (3)
to analyze the mediating role of FoMO between marketing exposure and purchase behavior. These translate into three research
questions: RQ1 asks to what extent exposure to FoMO -based marketing influences feelings of FoMO; RQ2 asks how varying
levels of FoMO affect impulsive buying; and RQ3 asks whether FoMO mediates the relationship between marketing exposure
and purchase behavior.
1.3. Significance of the Study
Academically, the study contributes to digital consumer behavior literature by empirically validating FoMO as a mediating
mechanism and by foregrounding gender as an analytic lens. Practically, it offers marketers and brand managers insight into
the psychological pathways through which FoMO -based strategies operate, informing more ethical and sustainable practice.
Societally, it speaks to growing concern about the psychological costs of digital marketing and the need for media literacy.
2. Literature Review
FoMO is broadly defined as the anxiety that others are living more rewarding lives than oneself (Przybylski et al., 2013); the
term entered popular use through a 2014 Forbes article invoking the idea that "the grass is greener" elsewhere (Hedges, 2014).
Since its establishment as a psychometric construct in the early 2010s, researchers have repeatedly linked FoMO to heightened
social media engagement, emotional strain, and behaviors ranging from excessive use to impulsive buying (Servidio et al.,
2025). Brands exploit this by framing delayed purchase as a lost opportunity, using influencer endorsements, limited -time
discounts, and "selling out soon" messaging (Mandas & Silfiyah, 2022).
Przybylski et al.'s (2013) foundational work linked FoMO to Self-Determination Theory, arguing that FoMO emerges when basic
psychological needs for connection, competence, and autonomy go unmet; social media offers a partial, ultimately unsatisfying,
fix. Their study also produced the widely used FoMO scale and found that higher Fo MO scores correlate with heavier social
media use and lower life satisfaction, and that FoMO is somewhat more prevalent among younger users and women. Related
work situates FoMO within Maslow's hierarchy of needs, showing that social media both creates and  worsens FoMO by
distorting perceptions of need fulfillment and encouraging upward comparison (Nesbit & Lole, 2025).
A separate strand of research debates whether FoMO is a stable trait or a fluctuating state. Maxwell et al. (2021), studying
reactions to a major television event, found trait and state FoMO to be distinct constructs, with trait FoMO predicting behaviors
such as staying current with a show. Rozgonjuk et al. (2020), surveying over 3,000 German respondents, found FoMO more
common among younger people and strongly linked to Neuroticism, but reported no significant gender difference—an outcome
that complicates, without negating, other findings of gendered susceptibility. Alfina et al. (2023) connect FoMO to bandwagon
consumption, in which individuals feel compelled to participate in every emerging trend to "keep pace" with peers (Kang & Ma,
2019), while other work links FoMO to anxiety, loneliness, and problematic smartphone use, generally more pronounced among
younger and, in several studies, female populations (Fitzgerald et al., 2022; Gul et al., 2022; Sultan Ibrahim et al., 2022; Wang
et al., 2019).

FoMO is also closely tied to social envy. Lin et al. (2018) found that experiential purchases (e.g., travel) generate more envy on
social media than  material goods, because they feel more self -relevant to viewers. Applied research shows FoMO shaping
consumer decisions directly: it drives Generation Z's masstige -apparel purchase motivation (Miguel & Bautista, 2020),
strengthens future brand -engagement intentions after exposure to brand content (Rifkin et al., 2023), and mediates the
relationship between social media marketing and purchase intention among Indian consumers (Habib & Almamy, 2025). At the
same time, FoMO is not uniformly harmful: Roberts and David (2019) found that social media use can strengthen relationship
ties and trend awareness, with negative outcomes emerging mainly among users who are over -engaged. Mediating and
moderating factors —brand credibility, boredom proneness, and anxious att achment—further shape how strongly FoMO
translates into behavior (Holte & Ferraro, 2020).
Taken together, the literature establishes FoMO as a robust construct linking social media exposure to engagement, mental -
health outcomes, and consumption—but it largely treats consumers as a homogenous group and rarely isolates the mediating
pathway from marketing exposure to purchase behavior among female consumers specifically. This is the gap the present study
addresses.
2.1. Theoretical Framework
The study draws on four complementary theories to explain how FoMO-based marketing operates: Self-Determination Theory
(SDT), Uses and Gratifications Theory (UGT), Social Comparison Theory (SCT), and the Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM).
2.2. Self-Determination Theory
SDT (Deci & Ryan, 1985) holds that humans are intrinsically motivated by three needs—autonomy, competence, and relatedness.
Marketers exploit these needs by manufacturing artificial urgency (undermining genuine autonomy), framing time -limited
deals as rewarding "smart shopper" competence, and using belonging-based appeals that pressure consumers to keep pace with
trends to preserve relatedness.
2.3. Uses and Gratifications Theory
UGT (Katz et al., 1973) reframes media users as active agents seeking to gratify needs for knowledge, entertainment, social
interaction, identity, and escapism. Social media gratifies these needs but simultaneously exposes users to curated portrayal s
of others' lives, producing FoMO as a byproduct of otherwise voluntary, need-driven engagement—useful to marketers seeking
to frame campaigns around trending hashtags and influencer content.
2.4. Social Comparison Theory
Festinger's (1954) Social Comparison Theory proposes that individuals evaluate themselves by comparing their circumstances
to others, particularly through upward comparison with those perceived as more successful. Social media intensifies this
process by continuously surfacing idealized, curated content, deepening feelings of inadequacy and exclusion. Because women
tend to be more relationally oriented, this theory helps explain heightened female susceptibility to comparison -driven FoMO
and subsequent impulsive buying.
2.5. Elaboration Likelihood Model
The ELM (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986) distinguishes a central, deliberative persu asion route from a peripheral route driven by
superficial cues. FoMO-based marketing predominantly targets the peripheral route—leveraging urgency, scarcity, and social
proof—bypassing careful evaluation and prompting immediate, emotionally driven purchase decisions.
Together, these four theories provide a layered explanation of FoMO's cognitive, emotional, motivational, and social
dimensions, forming the conceptual basis for the hypotheses tested below.
3. Methodology
3.1. Research Design and Approach
The study used a quantitative, cross-sectional survey design, appropriate for drawing statistical inferences about measurable
relationships among variables while maintaining objectivity (Creswell, 2014). A deductive approach was adopted: established
frameworks (SDT, UGT, SCT, ELM) and prior findings were used to derive specific, testable hypotheses rather than to generate
new theory.
3.2. Variables and Hypotheses
Three constructs were operationalized: Exposure to FoMO Marketing Strategies (independent variable) as encounter ing
campaigns using scarcity cues and urgency framing; Fear of Missing Out (mediator) as anxiety or stress triggered by seeing

others acquire trending products; and Female Consumer Behavior (dependent variable) as FoMO -influenced purchasing
decisions. Based on these constructs, three hypotheses were tested:
H1: Exposure to FoMO-based social media marketing strategies significantly increases feelings of FoMO among female
consumers.
H2: Higher levels of FoMO are positively associated with impulsive buying behavior among female consumers.
H3: FoMO mediates the relationship between exposure to social media marketing and female consumers' purchase
behavior.
3.3. Population, Sampling, and Data Collection
The target population comprised women aged 18–35, selected because younger women are more active social media users and
more susceptible to FoMO (Przybylski et al., 2013). A non-probability purposive sample of active female social media users was
recruited via an online, self-administered questionnaire built on a five-point Likert scale, distributed through WhatsApp groups,
Facebook communities, and Instagram to maximize reach among the target demographic. Participation was voluntary and
anonymous, with no incentives offered to minimize response bias; 302 valid responses were collected.
3.4. Data Analysis
Responses were coded numerically (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree) and analyzed in SPSS using a three -stage
approach: (1) descriptive analysis of general trends; (2) reliability testing (Cronbach's alpha) and Pearson correlation to confirm
internal consistency and initial relationships; and (3) regression analysis to test the three hypotheses, including a mediati on
test for H3.

4.2. Reliability, Correlation, and Regression Analysis
The Exposure to FoMO Marketing scale showed excellent internal reliability (Cronbach's α = 0.81), and the FoMO scale showed
acceptable reliability (α = 0.70). Pearson correlations confirmed the hypothesized pat tern: Exposure to FoMO Marketing
correlated moderately with FoMO (r = 0.56, p < .001) and with Consumer Behavior (r = 0.39, p < .001), while FoMO correlated
strongly with Consumer Behavior (r = 0.61, p < .001).
Regression analysis testing H1 showed that ex posure to FoMO-based marketing significantly predicted FoMO (β = 0.51, p <
.001), explaining approximately 31% of variance. Testing H2, FoMO significantly predicted consumer behavior, explaining
approximately 37% of variance. When both exposure and FoMO we re entered into a single regression model predicting
consumer behavior, the direct effect of marketing exposure became statistically insignificant while FoMO remained a strong,
significant predictor—indicating full mediation and supporting H3.

5. Discussion
The findings indicate that FoMO-based marketing strategies do not influence female consumers' purchasing decisions directly;
rather, their effect operates almost entirely through the emotional mechanism of FoMO. This pattern is consistent with Self -
Determination Theory: marketing content implying tha t others are enjoying an exclusive experience appears to threaten
consumers' sense of social belonging, triggering the anxiety captured by the FoMO measure. It is likewise consistent with the
Elaboration Likelihood Model—female consumers in this sample app ear to process FoMO cues through the peripheral route,
responding to urgency and social proof rather than deliberative evaluation of product information, and with Uses and
Gratifications Theory, insofar as purchasing appears partly aimed at resolving the emotional discomfort FoMO produces.
The strong correlation between FoMO and consumer behavior (r = 0.61) reinforces the view that FoMO functions as an active
motivational force rather than a passive emotional state, echoing prior work linking emotional vuln erability to impulsive
purchasing (Kang & Ma, 2019). The full-mediation result adds precision to this literature: it suggests that marketing exposure
alone is a comparatively weak direct predictor of purchase behavior once FoMO is accounted for, meaning th at campaigns
succeed largely to the extent that they successfully induce the feeling of missing out, not merely by increasing message exposure.
This reframes FoMO from an incidental byproduct of heavy social media use into a deliberately activated psycholo gical lever
within marketing communication.
The demographic profile of the sample—young, highly educated, heavy Instagram and TikTok users—also situates these findings
within platforms whose visual, comparison-heavy formats are theorized to amplify Social Comparison Theory dynamics. That
a meaningful minority, rather than a majority, reported unplanned purchases and regret suggests individual variation in

6. Practical and Theoretical Implications
Theoretically, the study strengthens the case for treating FoMO as a situationally induced state that can be externally triggered
by marketing cues, rather than solely a stable personality trait, and demonstrates the value of integrating SDT, UGT, SCT, and
ELM within a single explanatory model of digital consumer behavior. Practically, the results carry ethical implications: because
FoMO-based tactics wor k by exploiting unmet psychological needs and comparison anxiety rather than by informing
consumers, heavy reliance on them raises questions about long-term consumer trust and well-being. Marketers are encouraged
to balance urgency -based persuasion with tr ansparency, while policymakers and platform designers may consider limiting
deceptive scarcity messaging, particularly where vulnerable audiences are concerned. Media literacy initiatives that help
consumers recognize FoMO-inducing cues could mitigate downstream anxiety and impulsive spending.
7. Conclusion
This study demonstrates that FoMO is not a peripheral or incidental outcome of social media use but a central psychological
mechanism through which marketing strategies are translated into behavioral responses among female consumers. Exposure
to FoMO -based marketing strategies —limited-time offers, scarcity cues, countdowns, and peer -comparison signals —
significantly increases feelings of anxiety related to exclusion, and this emotional activation, rather than exposure itself, is what
drives impulsive purchasing, heightened purchase intention, and reduced post-decision deliberation. All three hypotheses were
supported, and FoMO was confirmed as a full mediator between marketing exposure and consumer behavior, c hallenging
models of consumer decision-making that assume predominantly rational evaluation.
