Purpose: This study examines how social media users discursively construct
“critical” or “conscious” consumer identities within sustainability fashion
discourse on TikTok, and explores the implications for marketing
communication, particularly the risk of being perceived as engaging in
greenwashing or purpose-washing. Methodology: Drawing on Fairclough’s
Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA) and multimodal analytical frameworks, this
study analyzes thirty TikTok videos across three genres: anti-haul, conscious
haul/thrift haul, and ethical fashion advocacy. Data were collected from
content published between January 2025 and April 2026, with analysis
conducted at three interconnected levels: textual (micro), discursive practice
(meso), and sociocultural practice (macro). The corpus includes both English
and Indonesian language content, reflecting the study’s commitment to
capturing local contexts.
Findings: The analysis reveals four identity typologies: (1) The Moral Bargainer,
who simultaneously emphasizes economic and ethical value through thrift
consumption; (2) The System Critic, who structurally challenges fast fashion
and overconsumption as manifestations of exploitative capitalism; (3) The
Reluctant Consumer, who expresses guilt and self-critique while still
participating in consumption practices; and (4) The Curator-Advocate, who
focuses on education, provides alternatives, and builds communities around
ethical consumption. Each typology employs distinct discursive strategies,
including lexical choices, multimodal framing, and intertextual references. The
study further identifies four boundary markers that distinguish authentic
advocacy from accusations of greenwashing, as constructed by audiences:
material evidence, consistency, self-critique, and stakeholder orientation.
Originality/value: This research bridges critical discourse studies and marketing
communication by demonstrating how brands and creators navigate the tension
between supporting conscious consumption and avoiding accusations of co-
optation. It offers a discursive framework for ethical brand activism in the
digital age, highlighting that accusations of greenwashing are themselves
discursive achievements constructed by consumers through multimodal
analysis of communications.

Introduction
The global fashion industry stands at a critical juncture, where
heightened consumer awareness of environmental degradation,
labor exploitation, and the ethical implications of fast fashion
has catalyzed a significant shift in consumption patterns.
McKinsey’s 2025 sustainability surveys indicate that Generation
Z consumers are willing to pay premium prices for products
positioned as sustainable, reflecting what scholars term the
“sustainability paradox”—consumers express ethical concerns yet
continue to purchase from fast-fashion brands (Niinimäki et al.,
2020). This tension between ethical aspiration and consumption
behavior creates fertile ground for discursive contestation, in
which social media platforms emerge as key arenas for
negotiating what constitutes “responsible” consumption.
Social media, and TikTok specifically, has become both an
accelerator and an aggregator of sustainability discourse.
Hashtags such as #ZeroWaste, #EthicalFashion, and
#ConsciousConsumer have accumulated millions of posts,
creating networked publics where consumers share practices,
critique brands, and construct identities around ethical
consumption (Zulli and Zulli, 2020). Within this ecosystem, two
distinct but interconnected genres have gained prominence: the
“conscious haul” or “thrift haul,” which displays secondhand or
ethically sourced goods as forms of resistance against new
consumption; and the “anti-haul,” which critiques products or
trends deemed unethical or excessive—a genre popularized by
creators like Kimberly Clark and subsequently adapted across
platforms (Wood, 2020).
Both genres, while ostensibly oriented toward “critical
consciousness,” deploy distinct discursive strategies and
construct identities in fundamentally different ways. The
conscious haul celebrates finding value within existing systems
of consumption—transforming thrift shopping into treasure
hunting, where economic savings and ethical positioning
converge (Keats, 2012). The anti-haul, by contrast, often adopts
more confrontational postures, naming and shaming brands,
exposing labor practices, and calling for systemic change (Wood,
2020). In the Indonesian context, these discourses take on
additional dimensions, as creators frequently reference local
markets such as Pasar Senen and Pasar Jodoh, frame thrifting as
an economic necessity for “anak rantau” (students living away
from home) and connect sustainability discourse with nationalist
economic sentiment through concepts like “kedaulatan sandang”
(fashion sovereignty). Understanding these discursive differences
is not merely an academic exercise; it has profound implications
for how brands navigate the increasingly treacherous waters of
sustainability marketing, where well-intentioned
communications can rapidly be reinterpreted as greenwashing or
purpose-washing.
Research on haul videos has predominantly focused on
identity construction among general consumers rather than
critically conscious identities (Keats, 2012; Abid et al., 2026).
Meanwhile, studies on greenwashing on social media have
largely adopted an organizational perspective, examining
corporate communication strategies rather than consumer
discursive responses (Lyon and Montgomery, 2015; Torelli et al.,
2020). What remains underexplored is a systematic comparison
of the discursive strategies deployed across conscious haul and
anti-haul genres, and the implications of these strategies for how
audiences distinguish authentic advocacy from co-optation.
Have demonstrated that narratives and symbols on social media
shape public opinion through identity negotiation and meaning
construction, yet their work does not specifically address critical
discourses that resist dominant consumerist narratives.
This study addresses this gap by investigating how critical
consumer identities are discursively and multimodally
constructed on social media, and by identifying the boundary
markers through which audiences distinguish authentic
advocacy from greenwashing accusations. Drawing on
Fairclough’s three-dimensional model of Critical Discourse
Analysis (CDA) and multimodal analytical frameworks, we
examine thirty TikTok videos across three genres to uncover the
discursive strategies through which creators position themselves
as “conscious consumers.” The significance of this research is
threefold: theoretically, it enriches CDA and consumer culture
theory by demonstrating how resistant consumption remains
embedded within market logics; practically, it offers ethical
guidelines for marketing communication; and methodologically,
it integrates multimodal analysis into greenwashing studies while
incorporating local contexts that extend beyond Western-centric
sustainability discourse.
Theoretical framework
Fairclough’s (2013) model of Critical Discourse Analysis
provides a foundational framework for examining how language,
both verbal and visual, constructs social realities and power
relations. CDA proceeds from the premise that discourse is not
merely a reflection of social reality but a constitutive force that
shapes identities, relationships, and systems of knowledge.
Fairclough’s three-dimensional model—textual analysis (micro),
discursive practice (meso), and sociocultural practice (macro)—
offers a systematic approach to unpacking how meanings are
produced, distributed, and consumed within specific
social contexts.
In the context of social media sustainability discourse, textual
analysis examines the lexical choices, metaphors, and modes of
address through which creators construct moral positions.
Discursive practice analysis investigates how texts are produced
and consumed—intertextual references to other content,
responses to brands or other creators, and the narrative
structures through which stories of transformation unfold.
Sociocultural practice analysis situates these discourses within
broader ideological formations, interrogating whether content
challenges capitalism itself or merely promotes “better
consumption” within existing systems (Fairclough, 2013).
This tripartite framework is particularly apt for analyzing
sustainability discourse on platforms like TikTok, where
meaning emerges through the interplay of multiple semiotic
modes. The brevity and multimodal richness of short-form
video demand analytical approaches capable of capturing how
visual, auditory, and textual elements converge to construct
persuasive messages (Machin and Mayr, 2012).

Kress and van Leeuwen’s (2006). grammar of visual design,
extended by Machin and Mayr (2012), provides tools for
analyzing how meaning is constructed through visual elements
including facial expression, gesture, camera angle, and property
use. In short-form video, the multimodal orchestration of these
elements is particularly significant because viewers process visual
and auditory information simultaneously and meaning often
emerges through the interplay between modes rather than from
any single element in isolation (Jewitt et al., 2016).
Recent applications of multimodal CDA to social media
content have demonstrated the analytical power of this approach
for examining how identities are performed and contested.
Kenalemang-Palma and Eriksson’s (2025) analysis of
#beautyfilters on TikTok reveals how multimodal strategies—
facial expression, editing techniques, and on-screen text—
construct relationships between creators, viewers, and standards
of beauty. Similarly, cross-platform analyses of greenwashing
narratives have shown how visual form (talking-head versus
montage), expressive tone (humor versus serious exposition),
and cultural orientation (individual versus collective value
appeals) shape how audiences interpret and engage with
environmental claims (Salimi et al., 2025).
For sustainability discourse, multimodal analysis is essential
because ethical claims are often performed rather than stated: a
creator’s facial expression of disgust when showing a fast
fashion garment, the use of low-key lighting to suggest
seriousness, the display of thrifted items as material evidence
of ethical practice—all these constitute a multimodal rhetoric
that may be more persuasive than verbal argument alone
(Machin and Mayr, 2012).
Digital identity construction and the
performance of consciousness
The construction of identity in digital environments has been
theorized as an ongoing, performative process in which
individuals use semiotic resources to position themselves within
social categories (Hall, 1996; van Zoonen, 2013). In the context
of conscious consumption, identity is constructed through three
interconnected dimensions: moral positioning (who I am versus
the consumptive other), material practices (thrift shopping
versus fast fashion purchasing), and affective stances (feelings of
pride, guilt, or anger) (Cherrier, 2009; Giesler and Veresiu, 2014).
Have demonstrated the role of digital storytelling in shaping
collective sentiment, yet their analysis does not specifically
address how these narratives construct identities that explicitly
resist dominant consumerist norms. The present study extends
this line of inquiry by examining how creators use discursive
resources to position themselves as “critical” or “conscious”—
identities that are inherently relational, defined in opposition to
those perceived as complicit in unsustainable consumption.
Uses and Gratifications Theory (UGT) provides a
complementary lens for understanding why audiences engage
with sustainability content. Audiences approach fashion and
sustainability content to satisfy cognitive needs (learning about
ethical alternatives), affective needs (enjoying aesthetically
pleasing content), identity needs (expressing individuality
through hashtag communities), social needs (connecting with
like-minded others), and escapist needs (using fashion content
as diversion) (Ruggiero, 2000; Sundar and Limperos, 2013). This
framework helps explain not only why critical consumer content
resonates with audiences, but also why these audiences actively
participate in constructing accusations of greenwashing—a form
of digital activism through which they reinforce their own moral
Identities (Salimi et al., 2025).
Greenwashing, purpose-washing, and
the question of authenticity
Greenwashing, originally coined by environmentalis t Jay
Westervelt in 1986, refers to the practice of making misleading
environmental claims—creating a gap between symbol and
substance, rhetoric and reality (Lyon and Montgomery, 2015).
Lyon and Montgomery (2015) define greenwashing as selective
disclosure of favorable environmental information while
concealing negative impacts, creating an overly positive
corporate image. Purpose-washing extends this concept to
social issues, describing the appropriation of social causes for
branding purposes without substantive action (Vredenburg
et al., 2020).
Recent research has demonstrated that greenwashing
accusations are not merely responses to corporate claims, but
discursive achievements constructed by consumers through
multimodal analysis (Salimi et al., 2025). Salimi et al. (2025)
found that social media users, particularly on platforms like X/
Twitter, actively construct greenwashing accusations through
emotional responses (fear, sadness, skepticism), information
sharing, and demands for corrective action. These accusations
function as identity work, through which consumers position
themselves as discerning, morally committed citizens rather than
duped consumers
Beverland’s (2005). work on brand authenticity identifies
consistency as central to perceptions of genuine commitment:
authentic brands align their communications with internal
practices and values. Vredenburg et al. (2020) extend this
framework to purpose-washing, arguing that accusations arise
when audiences perceive a disconnect between a brand’s stated
purpose and its actual practices. In the context of sustainability
marketing, authenticity is not an inherent property of brands,
but a perception constructed by audiences through analysis of
multimodal communications.
The present study builds on these insights by examining
how audiences construct distinctions betwee n authentic
advoca cy and purpose-washing. Drawing on analysis of
comment sections, we identify four boundary markers
through which viewers evaluate brand and creator
communications: material evidence (whether brands or
creators provide concrete proof or vague language),
consistency (whether advoca cy aligns with core practices),
self-critique (whether limita tions are acknowledged or
critique is avoided), and stakeholder orienta tion (whether
focus is on actual impacts or image and sales). These
markers operate not as objective criteria but as discursive
resour ces through which audiences perform their own
critical identities.

Methodology
This study employs a purposive sampling strategy, which is the
most appropriate and widely accepted approach for qualitative
discourse-analytic research, particularly studies employing
Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA) and multimodal analysis.
Unlike probability sampling, which aims for statistical
representativeness, purposive sampling is designed to select
information-rich cases that are most relevant to the research
questions and theoretical framework (Patton, 2015; Palinkas
et al., 2015).
The rationale for employing purposive sampling in this study
rests on several methodological considerations. First, Fairclough’s
(2013) three-dimensional model of CDA does not require
statistical representativeness but rather demands that selected
texts are “particularly salient” or “consequential” for
understanding the discursive practices under investigation. As
Fairclough (2013) argues, “the selection of texts for analysis in
CDA is not a matter of random or representative sampling, but
of selecting texts which are particularly revealing of the
discursive practices and social processes being studied.” Second,
purposive sampling prioritizes cases with high informational
power—those that contain the most relevant and rich data for
addressing the research questions (Malterud et al., 2016). Third,
our stratified purposive approach (Patton, 2015) ensures
balanced representation across three distinct but interconnected
genres, enabling systematic comparative analysis of discursive
strategies across genres.
The research team conducted systematic searches on TikTok
using predetermined keywords and hashtags across the three
genre categories. A total of 187 videos were initially identified
through these searches using the following search terms: anti-
haul (“anti haul”, “anti haul 2025”, “why I’m not buying”,
“#antihaul”), conscious haul (“thrift haul”, “conscious haul”,
“second hand haul”, “#thrifthaul”), and ethical fashion advocacy
(“fast fashion exposed”, “greenwashing example”,
“#ethicalfashion”, “#fastfashionexposed”).
Following the application of inclusion criteria, which
encompassed genre categorization (anti-haul, conscious haul, or
ethical fashion advocacy), hashtag presence, duration (≤90 s),
view count (minimum 50,000 as an indicator of public
resonance), language (English or Indonesian), and publication
period (January 2025–April 2026), 157 videos were excluded.
From the remaining corpus, 30 videos were purposively selected
—10 from each genre—based on thematic richness, content
relevance, and audience engagement metrics.
This sample size of 30 videos was deemed sufficient for
achieving thematic saturation, consistent with established
practices in multimodal CDA research on social media
platforms (Kenalemang-Palma and Eriksson, 2025; Salimi et al.,
2025; Zulli and Zulli, 2020; Guest et al., 2006). Saturation was
achieved after approximately 25 videos, as additional videos no
longer produced new insights about identity typologies or
discursive strategies; the remaining 5 videos confirmed existing
patterns rather than revealing new ones. The inclusion of 20
English-language and 10 Indonesian-language videos reflects the
study’s commitment to capturing both international discourse
and local Indonesian contexts.
For each of the 30 videos, comment sections were
systematically reviewed. The top 20 most-liked comments were
analyzed alongside all comments directly addressing
sustainability or ethical issues (n = 247 total comments
reviewed). This purposive approach ensured both the inclusion
of highly visible commentary and the capture of analytically
significant but less prominent discussions. From this corpus, 82
comments were retained for detailed analysis based on their
relevance to the four boundary markers identified through
iterative coding: material evidence, consistency, self-critique, and
stakeholder orientation.
The identification of boundary markers followed Braun and
Clarke’s (2006) six-step thematic analysis procedure. Two
researchers independently coded the comment data to ensure
analytical rigor, achieving inter-coder reliability. Disagreements
were resolved through consensus discussion. The boundary
markers thus represent inductively derived analytical categories
that emerged from systematic engagement with audience
evaluations of brand and creator communications.
Analysis proceeded through a structured, multi-phase process.
First, each video was viewed a minimum of three times, with each
viewing focusing on different aspects of the multimodal text.
A comprehensive coding table was completed for each video,
documenting lexical choices, metaphors, modes of address,
visual strategies (facial expression, gesture, properties), audio
strategies (music, intonation), textual elements (hashtags, on-
screen text), intertextual references, narrative structure,
ideological positioning, and power relations. These dimensions
operationalized Fairclough’s three-dimensional model at the
analytical level. Two researchers independently conducted
multimodal coding of all 30 videos using the analytical
framework. Coding was performed at three interconnected
levels: textual (micro), discursive practice (meso), and
sociocultural practice (macro). Researchers possessed expertise
in CDA and multimodal analysis, ensuring consistent
application of the framework.
Second, videos were categorized according to emerging
identity typologies. This involved identifying patterns in
discursive strategies across videos and grouping them by shared
characteristics. Initial patterns were identified after the first
coding round and refined through two subsequent rounds of
analysis. Patterns were identified at the textual level (recurring
lexical choices, metaphors, multimodal configurations), at the
discursive level (narrative structures, intertextual references,
responsive strategies), and at the sociocultural level (ideological
stances, power relations constructed). Videos exhibiting
characteristics of multiple typologies were discussed between
coders until consensus was reached; where consensus could not
be achieved, videos were categorized based on dominant
discursive patterns. The typologies identified function as ideal
types—analytical constructs that capture dominant patterns
without claiming that individual creators perfectly embody any
single type.
Third, cross-genre analysis compared anti-haul, conscious
haul, and advocacy content to identify similarities and
differences in discursive strategies. This phase focused on how
genres construct critical consumer identity through distinctive
combinations of lexical, multimodal, and narrative resources.

Fourth, boundary markers distinguishing authentic advocacy
from greenwashing accusations were identified through analysis
of comment sections. Comments were selected purposively
based on relevance to ethical or sustainability issues, rather than
simply the top five most-liked comments, ensuring that
analytically significant but less visible commentary was not
excluded. This purposive selection, while not generalizable,
provides rich insight into how audiences construct distinctions
between authentic and inauthentic advocacy.
Methodological triangulation was achieved through
integration of textual, visual, and auditory analysis. Researcher
triangulation (two independent coders) and data triangulation
(multiple video genres and language sources) strengthened the
validity of our findings. Negative case analysis—identifying and
discussing videos that did not fit emerging patterns—further
enhanced analytical rigor.
This study analyzed publicly available TikTok content, a
platform where videos are intended for public consumption.
Following established guidelines for social media research, we
treated public content as appropriate for analysis without
requiring individual consent. However, we have taken several
steps to protect user privacy. All user identifiers have been
anonymized. Verbatim comments are included only when
analytically significant and do not contain personally identifiable
information. Raw data (video screenshots, transcripts,
codebooks) are stored securely on institutional servers and will
be made available upon reasonable request to the corresponding
author. The study was conducted in accordance with Bunda
Mulia University’s research ethics guidelines. These practices
align with recommendations for ethical qualitative research in
digital and social media contexts (Kozinets, 2020).
Findings
Analysis of the thirty TikTok videos reveals four distinct
identity typologies, each characterized by particular discursive
strategies and multimodal configurations. These typologies are
not hermetically sealed; individual creators may shift between
positions across different videos or even within a single piece of
content. However, each represents a recognizable pattern of
identity construction that emerged consistently across the corpus.
The moral bargainer
The Moral Bargainer construct’s identity through an emphasis
on simultaneous economic and ethical value. This typology
dominates conscious haul and thrift haul content, where
creators celebrate finding high-quality branded goods at low
prices, positioning thrift shopping as both financially prudent
and environmentally responsible.
Lexically, Moral Bargainers deploy vocabulary that frames
consumption as a form of treasure hunting or strategic mastery.
Words such as “score,” “steal,” “rare find,” “jackpot,” and
“gong” (a term used in Indonesian thrift content to denote the
best find) transform thrift shopping from a necessity into an
achievement. The emphasis is on economic efficiency—“murah”
(cheap) and “branded” appear frequently—but also on the
moral satisfaction of rejecting fast fashion. As one Indonesian
creator explains, “Kalau kalian pintar carinya” (if you are smart
in searching), suggesting that thrift shopping rewards skill and
knowledge. Another creator frames thrift shopping as a “life
hack buat anak rantau” (life hack for students living away from
home), positioning it as practical wisdom for navigating urban
life with limited resources.
The moral framing in Moral Bargainer discourse is often
implicit rather than explicit. Rather than directly condemning
fast fashion, creators contrast the quality and uniqueness of
thrifted items with the perceived inferiority of new mass-
produced goods. The opposition is between “branded” and
“biasa” (ordinary), between “vintage” and “trendy,” between
“unique” and “generic.” The implicit argument is that choosing
secondhand is not only economically rational but also
aesthetically superior—a form of distinction that positions the
thrift shopper as discerning and cultured.
Visually, Moral Bargainers employ bright, warm lighting and
display goods in aesthetically arranged settings. Close-up shots
of branded labels serve as visual evidence of value. Transitions
between items are smooth and upbeat, often accompanied by a
“clean girl aesthetic” palette of soft tones. Facial expressions are
joyful, proud, and celebratory—the emotional register of
triumphant discovery. Gestures include holding items up to the
camera, modeling clothing, and pointing to details, creating a
sense of sharing secrets with the viewer. One Indonesian
creator, while thrifting at a market, frames the camera with a
handheld, dynamic POV style that creates a sense of realistic
immersion—as if the viewer is physically present for the hunt.
The visual aesthetic deliberately contrasts the perceived grime of
traditional markets with the refined quality of the finds, creating
a visual argument about hidden value.
The narrative structure follows a “discovery narrative”: a
journey from initial search to triumphant find, often including
moments of suspense or doubt before the final reveal.
Indonesian thrift content frequently opens with an establishing
shot of the market location, moves through the process of rifling
through racks, pauses for moments of decision or hesitation,
and culminates in the revelation of the “gong” or “jackpot” find.
This structure mirrors the suspense of treasure hunting,
maintaining audience engagement through uncertainty about
what will be discovered.
Auditorily, Moral Bargainers uses upbeat, pleasant music that
supports the celebratory tone. Indonesian content often employs
popular TikTok sounds or upbeat instrumentals that signal fun
and excitement. Voice-over intonation is enthusiastic and
conversational, often using informal language that builds
intimacy with the viewer. The overall auditory experience is one
of sharing an enjoyable experience rather than delivering a
serious lecture.
The Ideology underpinning Moral Bargaining is what might
be termed “smart consumerism”—a form of consumption that
remains within capitalist frameworks but insists on extracting
maximum value while minimizing cost. This does not challenge
capitalism itself but rather celebrates individual strategic
consumption within market logics. Power relations position the
creator as an expert who empowers audiences to consume more
intelligently, creating a relationship of educational exchange
rather than structural critique.

The system critic
The System Critic constructs identity through structural
critique of fast fashion, overconsumption, and exploitative
capitalism. This typology predominates in anti-haul and
advocacy content, where creators expose labor practices,
environmental destruction, and the manipulative marketing
strategies of global fashion brands.
Lexically, System Critics deploy vocabulary of moral outrage
and systemic analysis. Words such as “exploitation,” “waste,”
“pollution,” “unfair wages,” “greenwashing,” and “scam”
construct fast fashion as an abusive system. The moral
opposition is stark: “responsible” versus “irresponsible,”
“transparent” versus “hidden,” “sustainable” versus “wasteful.”
As one creator explains, fast fashion is “limbah polutan
terbesar ke-2 di dunia” (the second-larges t pollutant in the
world), while another emphasizes that products come with
hidden costs of “sampah yang tidak akan terurai selama 200
juta tahun” (waste that will not decompose for 200 million
years). These lexical choices do not merely inform—they
perform a moral judgment that positions the speaker as
ethically superior and the audience as potentially complicit
unless they change their behavior.
Metaphors of war, disease, and poison are frequent. Fast
fashion is described as “racun” (poison), “cortisol tinggi” (high
cortisol), or “membunuh” (killing). These metaphors dramatize
the threat, constructing consumption not as a pleasure but as
participation in harm. One creator frames the issue through a
“high cortisol” versus “low cortisol” opposition, associating fast
fashion with stress and environmental degradation while
thrifting is associated with calm and restoration. This
metaphorical framing goes beyond description to create an
affective orientation—the viewer is not just informed but made
to feel the danger.
Visually, System Critics employ stark contrasts. Anti-haul
content often alternates between close-ups of products (to be
criticized) and images of environmental damage or labor
exploi tation. Display s of fast fashion products may be
accompanied by facial expressions of disgust or anger.
Advoca cy content frequently uses inserts—news footage,
documentary clips, images of pollution—as evidence. One
Indonesian creator incorpor ates images of polluted rivers
and textile waste alongside branded logos, constructing a
visual argument about hidden costs. Another combines
footage of factory workers with images of CEO wealth,
visually establishing exploita tion as the relationship
between producers and beneficiaries. The visual aesthetic
tends toward the serious and urgent rather than the playful,
with darker palettes, more rapid editing, and less
aesthetic polish.
Audio strategies include serious or urgent intonation,
sometimes without music to emphasize gravity. When music is
used, it tends toward the cinematic or even ominous. The
System Critic’s tone is often confrontational, addressing brands
directly or calling out specific practices. As one creator states,
“Jangan bohongi publik tentang fast fashion” (Don’t lie to the
public about fast fashion), a direct accusation that positions the
creator as a truth-teller against corporate deception. Another
creator, addressing an influencer audience, speaks with raised
intonation and rapid articulation, performing righteous anger as
a marker of authentic moral commitment.
Intertextuality is a hallmark of System Critic discourse.
Creators reference investigative journalism, scientific reports,
and activist campaigns. One video cites “Liputan6” coverage of
the Citarum River’s pollution; another references “Vice”
reporting on textile waste. This intertextual anchoring provides
evidentiary weight, positioning the creator’s critique within
established knowledge networks. The System Critic often targets
specific brands—H&M, Zara, Shein—or broader industry
practices, constructing a responsive discourse that engages with
corporate communications and industry trends.
Narratively, System Critic content often follows a structure of
revelation: beginning with a hook or provocative claim, moving
through evidence and analysis, and concluding with a call to
action. The story is one of awakening—the creator as an
enlightened revealer who helps audiences see through marketing
to the hidden realities beneath. This narrative structure positions
the viewer as someone who has been deceived and is now being
liberated through knowledge.
Ideologically, System Critics challenge capitalism itself, or at
least its current form. Content critiques not just individual
brands but the system that drives them: relentless growth,
planned obsolescence, and the externalization of environmental
and social costs. One creator frame this explicitly, calling for
“kedaulatan sandang” (fashion sovereignty) and criticizing what
they term “kapitalisme sirkular” (circular capitalism). Power
relations are stark: corporations and irresponsible influencers are
positioned as perpetrators, while workers, the environment, and
local economies are cast as victims. The call is for consumers to
take power by refusing participation.
The reluctant consumer
The Reluctant Consumer construct’s identity through
expressions of guilt, self-critique, and inconsistency. This
typology appears across genres but is particularly visible in anti-
haul content with confessional elements—creators who advocate
against overconsumption while admitting their own
participation in it.
Lexically, Reluctant Consumers deploy vocabulary of
hesitation, confession, and self-awareness. Phrases such as “I
know I shouldn’t, but..” “I’m guilty of this too,” and “I’m
trying” signal an honest acknowledgment of inconsistency. The
tone is often confessional rather than didactic, positioning the
creator not as a righteous critic but as a flawed individual also
struggling with consumption pressures. As one creator reflected,
“gue akan jujur aja .. menurut gue ini gak worth it” (I’ll be
honest .. in my opinion this isn’t worth it), while still
acknowledging previous purchases. The Reluctant Consumer
frequently uses self-deprecating humor as a strategy to preempt
criticism—if they confess their failures first, audiences have less
power to expose them.
Narratively, Reluctant Consumer content often follows a
structure of struggle: starting with an admission of past
behavior, moving through current efforts to change, and
sometimes ending with a commitment to future improvement.
The transformation narrative is present but incomplete—the

rather than judgment. The Reluctant Consumer is not the hero

Visually, Reluctant Consumers may appear more casual and

performative, more natural, creating a sense of
intimate conversation.

subdued soundtracks. Voice-over intonation is conversational,
sometimes hesitant, marked by pauses and self-correction. The
overall auditory experience is one of authentic sharing rather
than polished performance.
The ideology of Reluctant Consumer discourse is ambiguous.
On one hand, it expresses critical awareness and aspiration toward
conscious consumption. On the other, it often remains within the
logic of individual consumer choice without challenging systemic
structures. The Reluctant Consumer may express guilt about fast
fashion while continuing to purchase, revealing the tensions of
consumption in a society where ethical alternatives are not
always available or affordable. Power relations are also
ambiguous: the creator identifies structural pressures but may
not name specific perpetrators, directing critique inward rather
than outward.
The curator-advocate
The fourth typology identified in the analysis, the Curator-
Advocate, does not appear in the original theoretical framework
but emerged prominently from the data. This typology focuses
on education, provides alternatives to problematic consumption,
and builds communities around ethical fashion practices.
Lexically, Curator-Advocates deploy vocabulary of education,
recommendation, and collective action. They present themselves
as researchers, educators, or activists who have investigated
issues and can offer evidence-based guidance. Words such as
“transparency,” “education,” “alternatives,” and “petition” appear
frequently. Unlike System Critics, they do not primarily focus
on condemnation; unlike Moral Bargainers, they do not
celebrate individual consumption victories. Instead, they
position themselves as knowledgeable guides who can help
audiences navigate a complex landscape.
One striking example is the video created by a group of
students who identify themselves as “5 pelajar yang mau
membuat perubahan” (5 students who want to make change).
They propose a “mandatory clothing score on every garment”
modeled on the Nutri-Score system for food, creating a simple,
actionable solution to the problem of hidden information. The
proposal is presented with confidence and clarity, positioning
the creators as young innovators who understand the problem
and have developed a practical response.
Visually, Curator-Advocates often employ clearer, more
structured visual strategies. On-screen text, evidence inserts, and
clear organizational structures guide viewer understanding. One
creator creates a video that begins by posing a question, displays
logos of fast fashion brands, and directs viewers to a longer
YouTube video for education. The visual strategy is designed
not primarily for entertainment but for information transfer.
Intertextuality is prominent in Curator-Advocate discourse, as
creators frequently reference other media, research, and activist
campaigns. The student group’s reference to Nutri-Score, for
instance, creates a bridge between fashion discourse and
established food labeling systems, lending credibility to their
proposal. Creators reference news articles, scientific studies, and
government reports, constructing their authority through
external validation.
The narrative structure of Curator-Advocate content often
follows a problem-solution structure: identifying an issue,
explaining its significance, and offering practical responses. The
story is one of collective action rather than individual
transformation—the creator is not saving themselves but helping
others save themselves and the planet.
Ideologically, Curator-Advocates occupy an interesting
position. They are not anti-capitalist in the same way as System
Critics, but they are more structurally oriented than Moral
Bargainers. They propose systemic solutions (like mandatory
labeling, petitions, collective action) while remaining within
frameworks of policy change and consumer education. Their
critique is of industry practices rather than capitalism itself, but
it is more substantial than merely promoting “better
consumption.”
Discursive strategies across typologies
Each identity typology deploys distinctive but overlapping
discursive strategies. These strategies operate at the textual,
discursive, and sociocultural levels, and they shape how content
positions both creators and audiences within
sustainability discourse.
At the textual level, lexical choices, metaphors, and modes of
address construct particular subject positions. The Moral
Bargainer’s “score” and “jackpot” construct thrift as exciting and
strategic, while the System Critic’s “exploitation” and “waste”
frame fast fashion as destructive. The Reluctant Consumer’s “I
know I shouldn’t” signals self-awareness and moral aspiration,
while the Curator-Advocate’s “transparency” and “education”
position knowledge as the key to change.
Metaphors similarly vary across typologies. The Moral
Bargainer’s “hunting” and “buru” (hunting) positions thrift as
adventure; the System Critic’s “racun” (poison) and
“membunuh” (killing) positions it as threat; the Reluctant
Consumer may use no strong metaphors at all, avoiding
dramatization in favor of honest self-reflection; the Curator-
Advocate may use metaphors of opening and uncovering, such
as “unfold” to suggest revealing hidden truths.
Modes of address also differ. Moral Bargainers use “you” and
“guys” to create a sense of sharing secrets with friends, often using
informal language and direct address. System Critics employ more
confrontational address—“you” may be accusatory, while “we” is

Visually, each typology employs characteristic strategies. The

market logics, not a challenge to those logics. The System
Critic’s anti-capitalist discourse, by contrast, challenges the
market itself and implies that genuine solutions require systemic
change. The Reluctant Consumer’s confessional discourse reveals
the tensions of living within unsustainable systems while
aspiring to better alternatives. The Curator-Advocate’s
educational orientation positions knowledge and collective
action as the path forward.
Boundary markers: authenticity and
greenwashing accusations
Analysis of comment sections reveals four boundary markers
through which audiences construct distinctions between
authentic advocacy and greenwashing or purpose-washing.
These markers emerge from audience evaluations of both brand
and creator communications, demonstrating that accusations of
inauthenticity are not reserved solely for corporations but
extend to individual creators who are perceived as co-opting
sustainability discourse for personal gain.
Material Evidence concerns whether a brand or creator
provides concrete proof of claims or relies on vague language.
Audiences frequently request or demand evidence: photos of
sustainable practices, certifications, or detailed explanations.
Conversely, vague language—“eco-friendly” without
specification, “sustainable” without elaboration—triggers
skepticism. In one case, commenters noted that a “conscious
haul” creator’s thrift finds might be counterfeit, challenging the
evidential basis of the “branded” claim. The marker operates
across contexts: brands and creators who provide visible,
detailed evidence are more likely to be perceived as authentic;
those relying on vague claims invite accusations of purpose-
washing. One Indonesian commenter observed, “Gak menjamin
bro yang full tag itu ori..” (can’t guarantee that the full tag is
original), directly questioning the material basis of the
creator’s claims.
Consistency concerns whether advocacy aligns with core
practices. Audiences are attuned to contradictions: a brand that
campaigns for thrift but produces vast quantities of fast fashion,
or a creator who promotes conscious consumption while
accumulating excessive goods. In one case, commenters noted
the contradiction in promoting thrift while still featuring fast
fashion brands. Another commenter observed the structural
contradiction at the heart of sustainable fashion claims: “brand
lokal pun seperti omosando .. msh sedikit itu ya Krn pasarnya
msh br.. klo ternyata booming sprti shein.. pasti akan produksi
ton2-an jg” (local brands are still small now because the market
is still small .. if they become as big as Shein, they’ll also produce
tons). This comment identifies consistency not merely as
individual practice but as a structural problem—even well-
meaning brands may be unable to maintain sustainable practices
at scale.
Consistency also functions as a marker of personal credibility.
Commenters evaluate creators not just on what they say but on
whether their lifestyle matches their message. One creator who
promotes conscious consumption while posting “massive haul”
content was criticized implicitly by commenters who noted the
contradiction between advocacy and accumulation. Another
creator’s comment that she has “to be selective otherwise my
tiny closet will literally break” was interpreted as genuine self-
reflection and consistency between values and limitations.
Self-Critique concerns whether a brand or creator
acknowledges limitations and demonstrates a learning
orientation. Audiences respond positively to honest admissions
of imperfection, viewing them as markers of authenticity. One
creator’s admission that she must be selective because of
physical limitations was validated by commenters as “finally a
conscious and meaningful Christmas haul.” Conversely, creators
who ignore criticism or delete comments—“ghosting” critique—
are perceived as defensive and inauthentic. As one commenter
noted, a creator who actively responded to praise but ignored
boycott criticism demonstrated passive avoidance that
undermined perceived authenticity.
Self-critique can also be performed through openness to
audience feedback. Creators who engage with critical comments,
acknowledge mistakes, and demonstrate learning are perceived
as more authentic than those who delete critical comments or
block dissenters. The willingness to expose oneself to critique
signals genuine commitment rather than image management.
Stakeholder Orientation concerns whether advocacy focuses
on genuine impacts or on brand image and sales. Authentic
advocacy emphasizes consequences for workers, communities,
and the environment—what is being done and how it benefits
affected stakeholders. Purpose-washing, by contrast, focuses on
“buy our sustainable collection” calls to action that center brand
benefit rather than stakeholder welfare. Commenters in one case
observed how thrift vendors are increasingly “pada tau brand”
(knowing about brands), shifting orientation from sustainability
to profit extraction. Another noted how brand sustainability
claims were merely “strategi pemasaran” (marketing strategies)
rather than genuine commitments. The marker reveals
audiences’ ability to distinguish between rhetoric that centers
brand image and rhetoric that centers substantive impact.

marker through a detailed critique: “masalahnya yg jual tau

critics rather than passive consumers.
Discussion
This study adopts the position that accusations of
greenwashing are not merely responses to corporate claims, but
discursive achievements constructed by consumers through
multimodal analysis of communications (Salimi et al., 2025).
Our analytical focus is on how audiences construct distinctions
between authentic advocacy and inauthentic co-optation, rather
than on determining whether brands or creators objectively
engage in greenwashing. This distinction is methodologically
important: our research design examines discursive strategies
and audience interpretations, not corporate practices or supply
chain audits.
This study makes several contributions to the literature on
critical consumer identity, sustainability discourse, and
marketing communication. First, it extends Critical Discourse
Analysis into the study of sustainable consumption by
demonstrating how identities are constructed through
multimodal strategies on social media (Tarigan et al., 2026;
Robin et al., 2020). The four typologies identified—Moral
Bargainer, System Critic, Reluctant Consumer, and Curator-
Advocate—represent distinct discursive positions within
sustainability discourse, each with characteristic lexical, visual,
auditory, and narrative features.
The identification of the Curator-Advocate typology, not
anticipated in the original theoretical framework, is a significant
empirical contribution. It demonstrates that sustainability
discourse on social media is not simply a binary between
celebratory consumption and condemning critique, but includes
a third position oriented toward education, structural solutions,
and collective action. This position may be particularly
important for understanding how audiences navigate
sustainability discourse, as it offers a way forward rather than
merely identifying problems.
Second, the study demonstrates that “critical” or “conscious”
consumer identity is not a single, unitary position but
encompasses multiple, sometimes contradictory, stances. This
finding complicates simplistic distinctions between “responsible”
and “irresponsible” consumers, revealing the tensions and
contradictions that characterize consumption in late capitalism.
The Reluctant Consumer embodies these tensions—a figure who
aspires to ethical consumption while continuing to participate in
the very systems they critique. The presence of this typology in
the data suggests that sustainability discourse is not merely a
matter of identity construction but a genuine struggle with the
practical constraints of consumption in societies where ethical
alternatives are not always available or affordable.
Third, the study identifies the boundary markers through
which audiences construct distinctions between authentic
advocacy and greenwashing or purpose-washing. These
markers—material evidence, consistency, self-critique, and
stakeholder orientation—ar e not inherent properties of
communications but discursive achievements constructed
through audience interpretation. Accusations of greenwashing,
therefore, are themselves discursive practices through which
audiences position themselves as discerning critics and
reinforce their own moral identities (Salimi et al., 2025). The
finding that these markers apply to both brands and creators is
significant, suggesting that audiences do not make a categorical
distinction between corporate and individual
sustainability discourse.
Fourth, the study extends the application of multimodal CDA
to short-form video content, demonstrating the analytical value of
examining visual, auditory, and textual elements in combination.
The finding that identity typologies are characterized by
distinctive multimodal configurations—not just verbal content—
underscores the importance of this approach for analyzing social
media discourse. The Moral Bargainer’s visual celebration, the
System Critic’s serious montages, the Reluctant Consumer’s
unpolished aesthetic, and the Curator-Advocate’s structured
information presentation are not incidental features but
constitutive elements of identity construction.
Fifth, the study contributes to cross-cultural understanding of
sustainability discourse by including Indonesian-language
content. The incorporation of local contexts—Pasar Senen,
Pasar Jodoh, Sungai Citarum, concepts like “kedaulatan
sandang”—demonstrates that critical consumer identity is not a
Western import but a discourse that adapts to local conditions
and concerns. The Indonesian context adds dimensions of
economic nationalism and post-colonial critique that are less
prominent in Western sustainability discourse, enriching our
understanding of how sustainability is framed in different
cultural settings.
This study examines the discursive construction of
greenwashing accusations rather than making objective
determinations of whether brands or creators engage in
greenwashing practices. Our findings reveal how audiences
interpret, evaluate, and categorize sustainability
communications, but they do not speak to the factual accuracy
of corporate sustainability claims. Future research might
combine discursive analysis with supply chain audits or
certification verification to examine the relationship between
greenwashing perceptions and objective sustainability practices.
The inclusion of Indonesian-language content (n = 10, 33.3%
of the corpus) enriches our understanding of sustainability
discourse beyond Western-centric models. Indonesian content
was transcribed and translated into English by a bilingual
researcher with expertise in Indonesian fashion discourse;
translation accuracy was verified through back-translation by a
second independent translator.

Implications
The findings have significant implications for brands and
marketers seeking to engage with sustainability discourse
without triggering accusations of greenwashing or purpose-
washing. These implications are grounded not in normative
prescriptions but in empirical observation of what audiences
respond to.
Engaging with system critics
The data suggests that System Critics have substantial
influence over sustainability discourse, and their critiques can
significantly damage brand credibility. Brands would be well-
advised to engage with System Critics not as adversaries to be
managed but as consultants whose critique can improve
practice. System Critics possess deep knowledge of
sustainability issues, labor practices, and environmental
impacts, as well as the discursive strategies that resonate with
environmentally conscious audiences. Collaborating with such
creators—rather than merely endorsing them—signals genuine
commitment to addressing the structural issues they identify.
However, engagement must be substantive: performative
partnerships without real change will be quickly identified
and condemned.
Transparency and material evidence
The material evidence marker demonstr ates that audiences
are increasingly sophistica ted at distinguishing between
substanti ated claims and vague language. Providing
detailed informat ion about practices, certifications, and
impacts —and making this informat ion accessibl e and visible
—builds credibility. Conversely , “eco-friendly” claims
without specifica tion trigger skepticism and invite
accusations. Brands should therefor e prioritize evidence-
based communica tion, making their sustainability practices
visible and verifiable.
Consistency across communication and
practice
Consistency between advocacy and practice is essential.
Brands that campaign for sustainability while maintaining fast
fashion production models are vulnerable to attack. Consistency
does not require perfection but does require honest
acknowledgment of contradictions and progress toward
improvement. As one commenter noted, the structural
contradiction of fast fashion brands promoting sustainability is
immediately transparent to sophisticated audiences. Brands
should therefore be honest about where they are in their
sustainability journey, acknowledging the gap between aspiration
and reality while demonstrating genuine movement.
Embracing self-critique
Brands and creators who acknowledge limitations, respond to
criticism constructively, and demonstrate a learning orientation
are perceived as more authentic than those who avoid or
suppress critique. Defensiveness signals dishonesty; humility
signals integrity. Brands should therefore develop processes for
receiving and responding to critique that demonstrate genuine
openness to improvement rather than reputation management.
The data suggests that audiences respond positively to
demonstrated self-critique, viewing it as a sign of authenticity.
Centering stakeholder impact
Communication should center on genuine impacts for
stakeholders—workers, communities, and the environment—
rather than brand image or sales. Calls to action that prioritize
“buy our sustainable collection” signal purpose-washing, while
calls that emphasize substantive change signal authentic
commitment. This does not preclude commercial activity but
requires that commercial messaging is embedded within broader
sustainability commitments. Brands should ask themselves:
“Does this communication primarily serve our brand, or does it
primarily serve stakeholders?”
Avoiding greenhushing
The opposite of greenwashing—greenhushing, or
downplaying sustainability achievements for fear of accusations
—also undermines trust (Font et al., 2017). Brands with genuine
sustainability practices should communicate them clearly and
confidently while acknowledging remaining challenges.
Transparency, not silence, builds credibility.
The Curator-Advocate typology offers insights for brands
seeking to engage with sustainability discourse. Curator-
Advocates occupy a middle ground between celebration and
condemnation—they are critical but constructive, oriented
toward solutions rather than merely exposing problems. Brands
might productively engage with such creators in ways that build
credibility while avoiding co-optation accusations. Genuinely

Conclusion
This study has examined how critical consumer identities are
discursively constructed on social media, identifying four
typologies: (1) Moral Bargainer, (2) System Critic, (3)
Reluctant Consumer, and (4) Curator-Advocate. It suggests
four boundary markers- material evidence, consistency, self-
critique, and stakeholder orientation- that distinguish authentic
advocacy from greenwashing or purpose-washing in
audience interpretation.
The research demonstrates that “critical” consumer identity is
not a unified position but a contested, multivalent discourse
within which creators position themselves through distinctive
combinations of lexical, visual, auditory, and narrative strategies.
These discursive positions have implications for how
sustainability communication is produced and perceived, and
for how brands may engage with sustainability discourse
without triggering accusations of co-optation. The inclusion of
Indonesian-language content has enriched this understanding by
revealing how local contexts, economic nationalism, and post-
colonial critiques shape sustainability discourse in ways that
differ from Western-centric models.
At a broader level, the study reveals the paradoxical nature of
resistant consumption: even as creators critique fast fashion,
overconsumption, and exploitative capitalism, their content
remains embedded within market logics. The Moral Bargainer
celebrates finding value within existing systems; the System
Critic challenges those systems but often calls for consumption
changes that remain individual responses to systemic problems;
the Reluctant Consumer expresses guilt while continuing to
participate in consumption; the Curator-Advocate proposes
solutions that remain within policy and consumer education
frameworks. This paradox is not a failure of critical discourse
but a reflection of consumption’s inescapability—we are all, to
varying degrees, Reluctant Consumers, aspiring to better while
navigating systems that constrain our choices.
For marketing communication, the findings suggest that
authenticity is not a fixed property, but a discursive achievement
constructed through ongoing dialogue between brands, creators,
and audiences. Brands that engage substantively with
sustainability—providing evidence, maintaining consistency,
welcoming critique, and centering stakeholder impact—can
build credibility and trust. Those that treat sustainability as
image management will find their claims increasingly
scrutinized and increasingly likely to trigger accusations.
In an era of heightened environmental awareness and
widespread skepticism of corporate claims, the capacity to
communicate authenticity is not merely a marketing advantage
but a condition of continued social license to operate. This
study offers a framework for understanding and navigating that
challenge, grounded in the discursive practices through which
authenticity—and its absence—are constructed.
