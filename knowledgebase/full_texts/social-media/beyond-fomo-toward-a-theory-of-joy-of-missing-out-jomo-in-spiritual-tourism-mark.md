INTRODUCTION
Sacred	Places	in	the	Attention	Economy
Destinations are now seen before they are visited. Posts, revie ws, travel blogs and inϐluencer content give prospective
tourists a steady view of what others have done and enjoyed and  the visual consumption of places long predates the
smartphone [1]. Destination marketing has met this attention wi th an experience-economy logic: stage memorable
experiences and multiply the occasions to have them [2,3]. Fear  of Missing Out (FOMO), the apprehension that others
are having rewarding experiences from which one is absent [4], supplies the psychological fuel. FOMO appeals use
scarcity, popularity and urgency to convert visibility into participation [5].
Spiritual tourism sits uneasily with this logic. Journeys to pi lgrimage towns, sacred landscapes, yoga centers and
meditation retreats are driven by religious observance, cultural participation, reϐlection and the search for meaning [6].
Many are organized around a few experiences that carry personal  or religious weight and have no ready substitute: a
ritual, a darshan, an evening aarti. For such visitors the value of a journey does not rise with the number of attractions
completed. A marketing logic that rewards completeness and a vi sitor for whom one experience is enough may pull in
opposite directions.
Consider a visitor who spends an extra day at one sacred site instead of visiting three others promoted online. Her
behaviour shows what she did. It does not show how she feels about what she left. She may be at peace with the choice,

What	Existing	JOMO	Research	Leaves	Open
JOMO is not new to tourism research. Putra [7], set JOMO agains t the pull of iconic landmarks and tied it to relaxation
motives. Fusté-Forné and Hussain [8], approached it as an escape from technology an d a slow, digital-free way of
experiencing food and rural place. Wojcieszak-Zbierska [9], compared JOMO and FOMO among university students, who
leaned towards quiet, ofϐline holidays. Steils and Toti [10], s howed experimentally that inϐluencer discourse about
overtourism changes FOMO, JOMO and intentions to visit over tou risted destinations, with both feelings acting as
mediators.
Across these studies JOMO is deϐined by what the tourist escapes (technology, crowds, famous landmarks) or by the
message that prompts it. Three questions remain open. First, th e construct is not separated from digital detox, quiet-
seeking and general contentment, so it is unclear what JOMO adds. Second, no account explains the appraisal that makes
a foregone option a source of satisfaction instead of regret. Third, JOMO has not been developed as a basis for destination
positioning and sacred settings, where some foregone options ar e matters of duty, have not been examined. To our
knowledge no study addresses all three.
We argue that JOMO is best understood as an appraisal of foregone options and that spiritual tourism is where this
appraisal meets its sharpest boundary. In leisure travel, foregone options are mostly interchangeable. In sacred settings
some are not, because skipping them means leaving a duty undone.

Research	Questions,	Contributions	and	Structure
The paper addresses four research questions

 RQ1:	How can JOMO be deϐined and separated from related constructs in spiritual tourism
 RQ2:	What explains when a tourist is satisϐied, instead of regretful, about foregone experiences
 RQ3: How does sacred signiϐicance shape this appraisal and what outcomes follow for tourists

The paper makes three contributions. First, it redefines JOMO in terms of two components, intentional non-pursuit

The	Gap
Three gaps follow from this review. The conceptual gap is that JOMO in tourism has no defined components and no clear
separation from its neighbors. The mechanism gap is that no account explains how foregone options come to be evaluated
with satisfaction. The positioning gap is that JOMO has not been developed as a basis for destination communication and the
sacred context, where some foregone options are obligatory, has not been examined. The sections that follow address each in
turn.

Self‐Determination	Theory:	Choice	That	Feels	One’s	Own
Self-determination theory distinguishes behaviour experienced as volitional from behaviour experienced as controlled and
names autonomy, competence and relatedness as basic psychological needs [17]. Social contexts can support or undermine
autonomy . Language that pressures, such as “only a few places left” or “you cannot leave without seeing this”, tends to be
experienced as controlling, while language that explains and offers choice tends to be experienced as supportive. Scarcity
and urgency appeals belong to th e first group. Presence-oriented communication, defined in S e c t i o n  5 ,  b e l o n g s  t o  t h e
second.
Autonomy is necessary for JOMO but not sufficient. A visitor can freely decide to skip a ghat and still regret it. The
theory tells us when a choice is felt as one’s own. It does not tell us how the visitor feels about what the choice excludes.
That requires an account of appraisal, which Section 3.4 provides.
The other two needs also bear on spiritual travel. Relatedness is often met through shared ritual, which is why many
visitors want group participation and would not call it a loss of autonomy. Competence matters when a visitor feels
equipped to take part in a rite. Presence-oriented communication that explains what each experience involves and how to
take part, can therefore support all three needs, not autonomy alone.

Pilgrimage scholarship has long stressed that sacred journeys are organized around transformation and particular rites,
not the accumulation of sites [6,25]. We separate two forms of sacred significance that act in opposite directions.
The first is the significance of the chosen experience. When a visitor treats a chosen ritual, place or period of reflection

The second is the obligation attached to the foregone experience. Some experiences are felt as duties: a rite of the
route, a vow, a custom that family or community expects. Skipping them is not a choice among attractions but the omission
of something owed. Satisfaction is unlikely here however autonomous the decision. The same act of non-pursuit that is

This asymmetry makes spiritual tourism more than a convenient setting. In leisure travel, foregone options are mostly
interchangeable. In sacred settings they are not and JOMO has a boundary that general theory does not predict. We expect
the same boundary wherever foregone options carry duty, such as family rituals or heritage observances, so the account

Conceptualizing	JOMO	in	Spiritual	Tourism
Deϐinition:	We define JOMO in spiritual tourism as a tourist’s satisfaction with deliberately choosing not to pursue one or
more available experiences, while recognizing that they may be desirable.
Three features follow. First, JOMO concerns specific foregone options, not a general disposition. A tourist can feel it
toward one excursion and not toward another . Second, it requires both intentional non-pursuit and satisfaction. Missing
an attraction through cost, access or illness is not JOMO and a deliberate skip followed by regret is not JOMO either. Third,
it is defined by how the tourist evaluates the choice, not by what she does. The same itinerary can reflect JOMO, regret or
indifference in different people, so observed behaviour cannot stand in for the construct.
The definition does not require silence, solitude, offline travel or a slow pace. Spiritual tourists differ: some attend
many rituals and visit several sites and may still feel JOMO toward options they decline. Sufficiency appraisal is deliberately
kept out of the definition. It is the mechanism proposed to explain JOMO and defining the construct through its cause would
make the explanation circular.

Deϐining	Components	and	Correlates
The two defining components are intentional non-pursuit, a deliber at e decision not to undertak e an option the tourist
knows is available and satisfaction with that decision, a positive evaluation that persists despite recognizing the option’s
appeal. They are separable in practice, since deliberate non-pursuit often ends in regret, which is why the construct cannot
be read from behaviour.
Three characteristics often accompany JOMO but do not define it: intentional disconnection from digital platforms,
present-moment engagement and reflective receptivity, an openness to letting an experience unfold instead of completing
a set list. Each can occur without JOMO. A visitor can switch off her phone and still regret the missed ritual, or attend fully

separation, not as dimensions of the construct.

JOMO	and	FOMO
FOMO and JOMO are often treated as opposite ends of one scale. W e argue they are not. Both attach to specific foregone
options, so one tourist can feel FOMO about a viewpoint promoted by an influencer and JOMO about an excursion she chose
to skip, within the same journey. Low FOMO is also not JOMO: indifference to an option produces no apprehension and no
satisfaction in having left it. Table 2 compares the two.
Because both attach to options, not persons, we state as a conceptual claim, not a proposition, that FOMO and JOMO
can coexist within one journey. Empirical work can test this by measuring both toward the same set of options.

The core mechanism is sufficiency appraisal. When a visitor judges the chosen experience adequate for the purpose of her
journey, foregone options lose their force as a standard of loss. Reflective spiritual motivation, the wish for contemplation
and personal meaning, should make such a judgement more likely, because the purpose it sets is depth in a few experiences.
This is not a claim that spiritual visitors prefer less. A visitor whose purpose is a full pilgrimage circuit has a purpose that
requires many experiences and sufficiency for her means completing them.
Sacred obligation marks the limit. Where a foregone experience is felt as owed, as a rite of the route or a vow, sufficiency
of the chosen experience cannot remove the sense of omission.
T ake two visitors in the same town who both skip an evening aarti. The first has come to sit by the river and judges
that enough. For her the aarti is an optional attraction, so sufficiency is high and obligation is low. The second is on a vow-
bound route of which the aarti is a stage and the sufficiency of her riverside time cannot offset the omission. The same
behaviour is JOMO for one and loss for the other and the framework locates the difference in the two appraisals.

DISCUSSION
Theoretical	Contributions
First, the paper reconceptualizes JOMO. Prior tourism work treats JOMO as a travel trend, an escape from technology or a
preference for quiet [7-9] or as a response to messages [10]. We define it by two components, intentional non-pursuit and
satisfaction and explain it through sufficiency appraisal. This gi ves researchers a construct that is separ ate from deto x,
mindfulness, slow tourism and contentment and a mechanism that can be measured and manipulated. It also shows why
behaviour alone, such as a shorter itinerary, cannot identify JOMO.
Second, the paper identifies a boundary specific to sacred settings. The distinction between the sacred significance of
the chosen experience and the sacred obligation of the foregone one predicts when JOMO is likely and when it is not. This
addresses a criticism that applying a psychological concept to a new context adds only a label. Here the context changes
the theory: where foregone options carry duty, the appraisal that produces JOMO does not hold. The idea may extend to
other obligation-laden settings, such as family rituals and heritage observances.
Third, the paper develops a positioning logic and states its cost. FOMO appeals link visibility to urgency [5]. We argue
that the two can be separated and that presence-oriented communication offers visibility without scarcity cues. By stating
the effect on itinerary breadth as a proposition (P10), the framework treats the economic trade-off as part of the theory .
Whether presence-oriented positioning is distinct in effect from existing practice in destination branding and responsible
marketing is an empirical question the propositions make testable.
The paper also offers a general move for marketing theory. It shifts attention from the options a consumer chooses to
the options a consumer leaves and asks when leaving is felt as loss and when as satisfaction. Sufficiency appraisal and
obligation are not specific to tourism. They can be examined wherever consumers face dense option sets, such as festival
programmers, heritage sites and wellness retreats and spiritual tourism is an unusually clear case because the status of
foregone options runs from trivial to obligatory.

Managerial	and	Policy	Implications
These implications follow from the framework and have not been tested. They are offered as hypotheses for practice, not
as findings.
Destination marketing organisations can separate experiences that are obligatory within a tradition from those that
are optional and describe each honestly. Presence-oriented communication then removes completion cues from optional
experiences while giving obligatory ones their due weight. This  classification should be d eveloped with religious
institutions and local communities, not imposed on them.
Digital content can explain the significance of a site, outline different ways to visit it and give practical information on
access, timing and conduct, without ranking attractions or implying that a visit is incomplete without a particular activity.
Sacred symbols and practices need respectful treatment and should not be used as attention devices.
Four design principles follow . Explain significance before popularity. Say what each option involves and who it suits.
State plainly which experiences are customary or obligatory within a tradition and which are optional. Avoid wording that
makes completeness a measure of a good visit. These are hypotheses for testing, not tested guidance.
Destination managers and policymakers should not treat presence -oriented communication as a substitute for
capacity management. Crowding, infrastructure and site protection depend on transport, regulation and visitor flow and
the effect of messaging on any of these is unproven. They should also weigh the cost in P10: if visitors plan fewer activities,
vendors and peripheral attractions may lose income and mitigation, such as supporting a wider range of local offers, should
be considered.

Illustrative	Application:	Uttarakhand
Uttarakhand offers an illustrative setting, with no data claimed. Its destinations, including Haridwar, Rishikesh, Kedarnath
and Badrinath, differ in sacred significance and in the obligations attached to them. F or a visitor on a pilgrimage circuit
such as the Char Dham yatra, the stops are not interchangeable and the framework predicts little JOMO toward skipping
one (P4). F or a visitor to Rishikesh seeking yoga or reflection, excursions and cafés are optional and presence-oriented
communication that explains what each offers without ranking them is where P1 to P3 apply. A destination could present
obligatory and optional experiences differently and a study could test whether visitors respond as the framework predicts.
The setting also shows why the account is not a recommendation to do less: for many visitors, doing everything is the
purpose.

CONCLUSION
Digital platforms have made sacred places visible before visitors arrive and destination marketing has often treated that
visibility as a reason to promote more: more attractions, more activities, more urgency. This paper asked whether that logic
must hold in spiritual tourism, where many journeys rest on a few experiences that matter deeply and cannot be replaced
by others.
Our answer starts from what existing research on Joy of Missing Out leaves open. Tourism studies have treated JOMO
as a trend, an escape from technology, a preference for quiet places or a response to messages. None of these explains why
a tourist can feel satisfied and not regretful, about what she l e a v e s  b e h i n d .  W e  defined JOMO as sa tisfaction with
deliberately unpursued experiences. Two things must be present: the tourist chose not to pursue the experience and she
is content with that choice. Behaviour alone, such as a shorter itinerary, cannot show it.
We then explained how this satisfaction arises. When a tourist experiences her choices as her own and judges the
chosen experience sufficient for the purpose of her journey, foregone options stop serving as a standard of loss. We called
this judgement sufficiency appraisal. It separates JOMO from satisficing, which describes when a search ends and from
general contentment, which is not tied to any option left behind.
The paper’s central argument concerns the limit of this explanation. In sacred settings, foregone experiences are not
equal. Some are optional. Others are owed: a rite of the route, a vow , a custom the family expects. Skipping an optional
experience can be JOMO. Skipping an obligatory one is an omission, however freely it was decided. This is why the sacred
context changes the theory and does not merely supply an example. It also gives the framework a boundary that general
leisure theory would not predict.
From this we derived ten propositions. They link presence-oriented communication to perceived autonomy, autonomy
and sufficiency to JOMO and JOMO to authenticity, wellbeing, attachment and recommendation. They also state conditions
(digital documentation orientation, sacred significance) and a cost: if visitors feel sufficient in fewer experiences, they will
plan fewer, which matters for local vendors and peripheral attractions.
