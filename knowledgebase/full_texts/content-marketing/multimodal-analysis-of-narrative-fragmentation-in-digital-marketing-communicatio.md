MULTIMODAL ANALYSIS OF NARRATIVE
FRAGMENTATION IN DIGITAL MARKETING COMMUNICATION

Abstract. In the contemporary digital landscape, brand storytelling is
increasingly defined by narrative fragmentation, which is a strategy that
challenges traditional expectations of linearity. This article presents a multimodal
analysis of how  digital storytelling reconfigures narrative structures to increase
engagement within the environments with limited attention span. By adopting a
multidisciplinary approach, the study examines the transition from linear plot
models to dynamic multimodal networks connected throughout various platforms.
The methodological framework is based on three foundational pillars:
Freytag’s Pyramid, adapted as a measure of semiotic tension management;
Halliday’s textual metafunction, employed to identify how thematic p rogression
and cohesive ties maintain discursive flow; and van Leeuwen’s theory of text -
image relations, utilised to analyse how verbal elements provide anchorage in
otherwise disorganised visual streams. Through a comparative analysis of case
studies (spe cifically the utility -based fragmentation of educational platform
Duolingo and the identity-driven fragmentation of beverage brand Liquid Death)
the research demonstrates that fragmentation is not an accidental byproduct of
digital media, but a deliberate rhetorical strategy.
Findings indicate that digital advertisements replace traditional exposition
and resolution with rhythmic modal shifts, dissonant anchorage, and high-salience
hooks. The study establishes that what appears as chaotic content is, in fact, a
controlled selection of modes intended to navigate platform -specific algorithmic
pressures.
By identifying the semiotic markers, this research provides an applicable
framework for scholars to map the evolution of brand communication. These
insights contribute to the broa der fields of multimodal discourse analysis and
digital marketing communication, offering a diagnostic tool for understanding the
future of narrative architecture in modern media.
Keywords: multimodality, storytelling, digital communication, multimodal
discourse analysis, digital marketing

Statement of the problem.  Constant development of digital storytelling
has fundamentally changed the relationship between brands and their audiences,
marked by a shift from the broadcast of monologic narratives to engaging
multimodal experiences. In the contemporary marketing landscape, the objective
of communication has moved away from the sequential development of a brand
story and towards the creation of high-impact short-format moments that capture
attention within the first few seconds of interaction. This fragmentation of
narrative is not merely a consequence of platform limitations, but a strategic
adaptation to the attention economy, where traditional plot stages — such as
exposition or sustained rising action — are frequently compressed or omitted
entirely.
Practically speaking, the modern digital environment such as social media
triggers the move towards narratives that are not contained within a single post or
video, but are instead distributed across an ecosystem of interactive content. For
marketing professionals, this c reates a significant challenge: how to maintain
narrative consistency and emotional resonance when the audience dictates the
pace and sequence of engagement. From a scientific perspective, this
phenomenon raises urgent questions regarding how non -verbal semiotic modes
(ranging from the rhythm of a scroll to the choice of a specific interface icon)
function as narrative elements.
Understanding these mechanisms is of critical importance, as the efficacy
of modern marketing now depends less on the classic per suasive argument and
more on the ability to synthesise disparate signs into a cohesive brand identity that
feels both intuitive and authentic to the user.
In the context of the analysis of recent studies and publications, we can
without hesitation claim that in the modern scholar community multimodality as
a subject of research has gained enormous popularity. Many local and foreign
researchers are engaged in various aspects of studies on multimodality. Particular
mention should be made of the works of G. Kr ess and T. van Leeuwen [1],
G. Lakoff and M. Johnson [2], and M. Halliday [3], which form the theoretical
basis of this study. Authors such as R. Page have effectively demonstrated how
storytelling in social media has transitioned into a co-constructed, dialogic process
[4]. This shift represents a departure from the traditional narratives of the past.
Similarly, E. Djonov and T. van Leeuwen have provided a basis for analysing
software as a semiotic technology, highlighting how interface design inherently
restricts and guides human interaction [5]. These studies establish a foundation
for understanding how platform architecture functions as a narrative constraint.

Furthermore, local scholars I. Voloshchuk and N. Glinka have bridged the
gap between cognitive  linguistics and commercial strategy by examining how
neurocopywriting employs multimodal triggers to bypass traditional analytical
processing [6]. Such research highlights a move away from logocentric persuasion
toward more intuitive, sensory-driven engagement. Despite these comprehensive
contributions, the unified methodology for evaluating the structural compression
of the classical narrative arc is still varying. While existing studies acknowledge
that narratives are changing, they often stop short of explaining how the functional
stages of the dramatic pyramid are reassembled within the spatial and kinetic
limits of modern feeds.
The primary aim of this research is to analyse the process of transforming
linear plot models into multimodal narrative networks and to identify the semiotic
markers that replace traditional elements of exposition and plot development in
brand publications. In addition, the research aims to develop a methodological
framework for examining the multimodal transformation of narrati ve structures
within contemporary digital marketing. By integrating M. Halliday’s systemic
functional theory, specifically focusing on the textual metafunction, with T. van
Leeuwen’s principles of text -image relations, this study seeks to uncover how
narrative coherence is achieved in the absence of traditional linearity. The textual
metafunction allows us to analyse how digital content is organised into a coherent,
persuasive whole through spatial layout and visual hierarchy. Simultaneously,
T. van Leeuwe n’s framework provides the necessary lens to evaluate the
integration of verbal and non -verbal semiotic resources. We assume that the
narrative arc in digital discourse is maintained through these complex multimodal
arrangements rather than through chronological development. Consequently, this
study aims to reveal how the strategic interplay between text and image
reconstructs the classic dramatic plot to satisfy the requirements of digital
platforms.
Presentation of the main research findings. Digital platforms’ algorithms
are becoming more sophisticated, therefore, marketing communication has
evolved into a multimodal set where meanings are constructed through the
strategic interplay of linguistic, visual, auditory, and kinetic semiotic resources.
This fr agmentation is characterised by disrupted temporalities and non -linear
consumption patterns and functions as a deliberate aesthetic and rhetorical
strategy designed to foster engagement in an attention -constrained environment.
According to the survey, cond ucted by A. Poles “excessive use, especially of
stimulating content, disrupts ongoing attention and our cognitive functioning.” [7,
p. 770] The author recommends consuming such media in moderation. However,
the continuous nature of the short -form narratives, by design, is created to form
habits, making consumers return for more information. Abbas et al. conclude that

“short videos may contribute to reduced attention span and increased
distractibility, potentially influencing academic and daily life.” [8, p. 1881] As a
result, marketing strategies are being adapted to the consumer behaviour outlined

In the current climate of users’ attention deficit, the linear representation of
time is losing its effectiveness. The relationship between products and us ers’
media has changed. According to G. Kress, passive consumption has given way
to “relationships of production and engagement.” [9, p.22] The rapid shift from
linear, text-heavy storytelling to fragmented, multimodal digital content calls for
a re-evaluation of how meaning is constructed.
To examine these transformation processes in practice, we have selected
two case studies that illustrate how different digital platforms and brands are
reshaping traditional narrative models.
Scholars have explored diverse frameworks, ranging from Campbel l’s
“Hero’s Journey,” [10] which focuses on archetypal character transformation, to
postmodern models that embrace decentredness and disorientation [11]. However,
Freytag’s Pyramid [12], which was originally conceptualised for dramatic
composition, offers a distinct advantage for this research due to its structural
clarity. By categorising narrative progression into five discrete stages, such as
exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, and resolution, Freytag’s model
provides an analytical baselin e to measure deviations in fragmented digital
communication. In their study Kühn & Boshoff state that “the intensity of
subconscious emotions elicited by the advertisement resemble a pyramid -like
structure similar to Freytag’s structure.” [13, p. 471] Thus , its application in this
study is justified by its allowance for the empirical mapping of how multimodal
elements (visual, textual, and kinetic) are strategically orchestrated to manipulate
viewer tension and emotional resolution within an attention -constrained digital
environment. Consequently, the pyramid acts as an “ideal type” against which
changes in modern digital marketing narratives can be systematically observed
and analysed.
When analysing digital narratives, the Freytag’s model of Pyramid was
mapped though the specific semiotic markers [14, p. 1216]:
1. Exposition (hook): In digital environments, this is often compressed into
the first 1.5 seconds. It is characterised by a high -salience visual (e.g., bright
colour, sudden movement) or an incongruous  audio cue that establishes the
communicative context immediately.
2. Rising action (escalation of modes): This phase is identified by the
layering of semiotic resources. We can observe the increase in modal density (e.g.,
the rapid switching between text overlays, background music changes, and rapid-
cut imagery) that serves to build cognitive tension or curiosity.

3. Climax (the pivotal point): This is the moment of peak semiotic impact,
often where the “brand reveal” or the “call-to-action” occurs. It is usually marked
by a synchronisation of modes (e.g., a musical drop coinciding with a clear
product shot), creating a moment of maximum coherence before potential further
fragmentation.
4. Falling action and resolution (the residual brand impression): In a
fragmented ad, the resolution is often not a traditional textual conclusion, but an
“emotional aftertaste.” The persistence of branding elements, such as a recurring
logo, a consistent sound bite, or a lingering colour palette, anchors the viewer
despite the preceding disruptions.
This study further integrates Halliday’s systemic functional linguistic
framework, specifically focusing on the textual metafunction. While Freytag’s
Pyramid provides the structural scaffold, Halliday’s textual metafunction allows
for an i nvestigation into how marketing discourse manages the distribution of
information to ensure cohesion within a splintered aesthetic. In a digital context,
this involves analysing how thematic progression, information structure, and
cohesive ties are managed across different semiotic modes to guide the viewer’s
focus. According to J. Bakuuro interpretation of Halliday’s textual metafunction,
it “underscores the fact that language is used to organise discourse and create
continuity and flow in our texts or in conversations.” [15, p. 211] By applying this
lens, we can demonstrate that digital marketing is not merely a series of disjointed
assets; rather, it uses the textual metafunction to manufacture a sense of discursive
continuity, thereby bridging the gaps b etween rapid -fire visual edits.
Consequently, this metafunctional perspective allows us to observe how brands
build their digital presence, using subtle linguistic and semiotic cues to create a
coherent narrative thread that persists despite the inherent d isruptions of
contemporary media platforms.
In addition, van Leeuwen’s theory of text -image relations is essential for
interpreting the interplay between verbal and visual components. By examining
how linguistic elements (such as captions or voiceovers) an chor or relay the
meaning of visual segments, this research can delineate how marketing
communication compensates for fragmented visual streams by providing semiotic
stability. As van Leeuwen articulates, text-image relations are critical because the
relationship between the two can be one of enhancement, where one element
elaborates on the meaning of the other, or one of extension, where they
complement each other to create a broader narrative. This is especially pertinent
in the context of digital marketi ng, where, as Barthes famously noted regarding
anchorage, the linguistic message serves to “direct the reader through the
signifieds of the image, causing him to avoid some and receive others.” [16, p.
40] By synthesising these perspectives, this research effectively maps how

As a part of the research, we analysed Duolingo’s marketing strategy, often
described as “unhinged” and meme-centric against three chosen analytical pillars
throughout three sources (Instagram, TikTok and application). Duolingo serves as
an exemplary case study because they often deliberately fragment their narrative
flow (e.g., rapid -cut TikTok skits) while maintaining a highly coherent brand
identity across platforms.
The significant part of Duolingo’s operational strategy is compressed
narrative arcs represented through the “threat” of the mascot ( Duo), which uses
the rising action, a chaotic or ab surdist climax, and a resolution that often loops
back to the app’s “streak” feature, anchoring the viewer in the product cycle.
Despite visual chaos, linguistic markers (e.g., persistent “Duolingo Green” colour
palette, recurring font choices, and consist ent sarcastic tone) function as the
thematic anchoring that provides stability across fragmented clips. Meanwhile,
text overlays in TikToks often anchor ambiguous visual skits (e.g., a person hiding
from an owl), clarifying the narrative by linking the vis ual absurdity to the
functional utility of the app (the “study now” call-to-action).
In a typical TikTok, the exposition is an immediate, high-energy visual (Duo
doing a dance). The rising action involves a sudden shift to “threatening”
behaviour regarding  a missed lesson. The climax is the peak of the joke (the
absurdity of the threat), and the resolution is the subtle, often visual, return to the
brand logo or a call to “keep the streak alive.” By treating the streak as the
resolution, the narrative itsel f becomes gamified, which builds a better rapport
with consumers.
Relying on Halliday’s textual metafunction theory, we can identify that
Duolingo uses modal density to manage viewer attention. In their Instagram reels
or TikToks, they use rapidly changing  scenes (fragmentation). However, the
textual metafunction is upheld through: 1. cohesive ties, such as consistent use of
the Duo mascot acts as a “constant” in the thematic structure; 2. information
structure, as the most salient, brand-defining information is placed in the “New”
slot of the information structure (e.g., the punchline or the product call -to-action
at the end of a chaotic video), ensuring the viewer absorbs the marketing message
despite the disruptive visual rhythm.
Furthermore, Duolingo’s content frequently employs relaying and
anchorage. A video might feature a person in distress, and by adding a text overlay

(e.g., “ When you miss your Spanish lesson ”), the brand anchors the visual
meaning. The text restricts the possible interpretations of  the scene, tying the
“chaotic” visual to the specific functional purpose of the app. As a result, the text
moderates the fragmentation of the image, making it digestible for the platform’s
algorithm.
To further this analysis, we contrasted how anchorage (in the sense of van
Leeuwen’s text -image relations) functions differently when the user is in a
“discovery” phase ( TikTok or Instagram used as marketing tool to help users
discover the service) versus an “operational” phase (the Duolingo app is an actual
service provided by the company).
On TikTok and Instagram, Duolingo’s strategy relies on a relay
relationship. The visual content is intentionally fragmented (e.g., rapid cuts,
erratic movements by the mascot, and non-sequitur humour). The text overlay acts
as the crucial anchor that translates the visual absurdity into a coherent brand
narrative.
The text provides the missing piece of the logical puzzle: “ When you miss
your lesson. ” Without this anchor, the visual fragmentation would lack
communicative intent.
In contrast, within the Duolingo app, the environment is designed for
cognitive flow and task completion. The text -image relation shifts to deictic
anchorage. When a user completes a lesson, the “ Perfect Lesson” badge (image)
is immediately anchored by text such as “Great job!” (text). Here, the purpose is
to provide immediate, unambiguous positive reinforcement. Unlike the social
media platforms where the text creates the narrative, in the app, the text validates
the user’s interaction with the system.
The shift in anchorage represents a transition from rhetorical persuasion to
functional guidance. While on social media, Duolingo uses text to specify the
fragmentation of their video content to maintain brand identity, within the app,
they use text to simplify the complexity of the learning process to ensure user
retention. This duality suggests that Duolingo’s narrative fragmentation is not a
uniform strategy; it is a context -aware semiotic adaptation. They purposefully
fragment the narrative on social media to disrupt the scrolling rhythm, but restore
semiotic stability within the app to ensure the utility of the product is never in
doubt.
In contrast to Duolingo’s gamified utility-based fragmentation, beverage
brand Liquid Death relies on aesthetic-based fragmentation. While Duolingo uses
fragmentation to reinforce a “streak” (a functional habit), Liquid Death uses it to
reinforce a “vibe” (a subcultural identity).
Liquid Death’s content uses non-sequitur arcs, so the climax is represented
as a deliber ate anticlimax or an absurd visual gag that denies the viewer a

conventional brand promise resolution, instead resolving into a subversive
lifestyle statement.
In addition, we observed the usage of consistent heavy metal semiotic
markers (black -and-white palettes, gothic typography, horror motifs) to link
seemingly unrelated, fragmented clips into a singular brand theme. While text
frequently contradicts the visual imagery (e.g., horrific visuals anchored by
mundane, polite marketing copy), creating a diss onance that demands active
audience interpretation.
Unlike Duolingo, which leads the viewer toward a goal (the streak), Liquid
Death often disrupts the pyramid by stripping away the rising action entirely.
Their social media skits frequently begin in the media with a bizarre scenario. The
climax is the moment of maximum absurdity, and the resolution is not a product
benefit, but a bran d signature (the logo appearing over a heavy metal riff). The
fragmentation here is a subversion of narrative expectation, signalling to the
viewer that this is not standard advertising.
Liquid Death  maintains cohesion across highly fragmented content by
relying on a specific visual theme. Even when the videos shift from horror-movie
parodies to fake infomercials, the textual metafunction remains stable. The heavy
metal font and the “skull motif” act as a recurring semiotic anchor. The viewer
experiences these fragmented bursts of content as part of a coherent subcultural
narrative because the textual and visual cues remain consistent even when the
content (the plot of the ad) is erratic.
Moreover, the consistent use of dissonant anchorage makes Liquid Death’s
strategy the most distinct. While in Duolingo’s case, text is used to clarify, in
Liquid Death’s case, text is often used to ironise or complicate. For example, the
marketing video features a gruesome, horror-movie-style visual sequence, while
the text overlay or voiceover maintains a polite, corporate “customer service”
tone. This dissonance creates a meta -commentary on the nature of marketing
itself. The text does not limit the image; it highlights the absurdity of selling plain
water with aggressive imagery.
Thus, Duolingo uses narrative fragmentation as an engagement tactic (to
keep users hooked on the app), meanwhile, Liquid Death  uses narrative
fragmentation as an identity tactic (to establish  reputation and repel mainstream
corporate tropes).
Conclusion. This study has demonstrated that the contemporary digital
storytelling in the marketing landscape is characterised by a deliberate shift from
traditional linear to dynamic multimodal narrative  models. Through the
application of the methodological framework, which relies on Freytag’s Pyramid
system as a measure of tension, Halliday’s textual metafunction as a marker of
thematic cohesion, and van Leeuwen’s text -image relations theory as a

mechanism for semantic anchorage, we have identified the mechanisms behind
this transformation.
Our analysis reveals that narrative fragmentation in digital marketing is far
from arbitrary; rather, it is created as a highly orchestrated semiotic strategy. In
environments where attention is a scarce commodity, brand communication
replaces traditional exposition and plot development with high -salience hooks,
rhythmic modal shifts, and dissonant or supportive anchorage. We have shown
that these semiotic markers function as the scaffolding of brand stories, enabling
marketers to maintain coherence and emotional resonance within splintered, non-
linear communicative architectures. By using this framework, this research
provides an analytical tool for mapping how disparat e multimodal elements are
strategically reconfigured to manage consumer tension, brand identity, and
narrative flow in the attention economy. While this study establishes a
foundational typology of digital fragmentation, it also opens several avenues for
further linguistic and semiotic inquiry in the area such as cross -cultural
multimodal analysis to explore whether the semiotic markers identified here are
universal or culturally contingent. As a practical application, the future research
will be able to in vestigate how recommendation algorithms influence narrative
construction and how narrative fragmentation can mediate commercial content.
