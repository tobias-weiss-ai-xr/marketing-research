Abstract
The rise of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) is reshaping global creative industries,
restructuring practitioners’ entrepreneurial decisions and business models, and changing the logic
of their professional practices. Most existing research focuses on the technical capabilities of Gen
AI, and very few studies have explored how entrepreneurs in emerging economies develop
adaptive strategies for the practical implementation of Gen AI.  This study takes Pakistan’s
photography and visual content industry as its research context, draws on dynamic capabilities
theory, adopts an interpretivist qualitative multi -case study design, conducts semi -structured
interviews with 12 founders or owner-managers of creative enterprises, and analyzes the data using
a three-level thematic coding scheme. The study finds that entrepreneurs first recognize the nature
of GenAI through changes in expectations and then pursue a transformation.  Their professional
identities have shifted from content creators to creative strategists and AI workflow managers.
Ethical judgment capacity, centered on the responsible use of AI, has become a core
entrepreneurial competency for addressing institutional uncertainty.
Keywords: Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI); Dynamic Capabilities Theory; Creative
Entrepreneurship; Entrepreneurial Adaptation; Responsible AI; Business Model Innovation.
Introduction
Generative AI (GenAI) has moved from the fringes of technical experimentation to become a core
component of content production in the contemporary cultural and creative sector. Taking three
mainstream tools, Midjourney, Stable Diffusion, and Adobe Firefly  as examples, it is clear that
this once -niche technology has now been fully integrated into the visual content workflow of
cultural and creative start -ups, covering all stages from concept incubation and production to
editing and final delivery. GenAI’s core value extends far beyond improving efficiency and cutting
costs; it has also restructured three core logics of the cultural and creative field: value creation,
construction of professional credibility, and maintenance of competitive advantage. This trend has
been corroborated by academic research (Watts et al., 2026; Iqbal et al., 2025ab), making it a core
focus of innovation and entrepreneurship research in the cultural and creative track. Traditional
visual creation relies heavily on professional skills, resources, and hardware conditions. In the past,
creative work depended on professional studios, mature tools, and long -accumulated technical
expertise. Still, GenAI can now independently complete four core tasks: generating backgrounds,
restoring images, simulating light and shadow, and producing complete visual works. This has
drastically lowered the industry’s barriers to entry , allowing new entrants to test their products,
services, and business models quickly. The core constraint on industry innovation has shifted from
the original capital-intensive resource threshold to founders’ creative judgment, technical literacy,
and strategic decision-making capabilities, which, in turn, has  made the photography and visual
content industry one of the most promising directions for entrepreneurship research. This paper

solely by AI is not protected under current laws, and long-standing disputes persist over attribution
rights, copyright ownership, and the compliance of training data. However, both groups of existing
studies only list basic facts and f ail to provide actionable, implementable solutions tailored to

present study. Currently, industry stakeholders widely view creative entrepreneurship as a general
pathway for women, young people, and groups excluded from the formal economy to participate
in economic activity.  GenAI could enhance the inclusivity of this pathway by lowering technical

centers on exploring how entrepreneurs can integrate ethical considerations into capacity building,
use responsible GenAI adoption as the foundation for long -term business growth, and break t he
inherent limitation of focusing solely on technical efficiency and scalability. The study adopts an
updated perspective on innovation assessment that adds three new dimensions: legitimacy,
trustworthiness, and overall effectiveness.
Literature Review
Generative AI as an Entrepreneurial Enabler in Creative Industries
GenAI is a general-purpose technology with the potential to drive transformations across multiple
industries. It is particularly well-suited to the creative industries, as it can directly access the core
value-creation links of this sector.  Technical capabilities have eliminated the dependence of
entrepreneurship on specialized equipment, dedicated studios, and large -scale production teams.
We define this phenomenon as technical equalization: the creative use of tools is far more
important than access to them, and the core competitiveness of entrepreneurial ventures rests on
the founders’ knowledge, aesthetic judgment, and ability to integrat e AI solutions.  However,
generative artificial intelligence exhibits a bias when creating business opportunities . In markets
with abundant low -cost synthetic materials, some entrepreneurs can build competitive
differentiation by leveraging human -machine hybrid models and exclusive customized services,
while others cannot. A 2024 study by Lee et al. points out that GenAI does not necessarily deliver
lasting entrepreneurial advantages, and gaining these advantages requires aligning the technology
with demand, vision, and ethics.

Disruption, Professional Boundaries, and Market Legitimacy
Building on the industry opportunities discussed in the earlier analysis, existing research advances
a core consensus: generative AI is disrupting the professional norms of the photography and visual
media sectors. Horn (2024) applies disruptive innovation theory, paired with cases of AI-generated
social-media brand portraits and automated e -commerce product photography, to explain how
technology dismantles traditional industry models by rolling out fast, low -cost solutions targeted
at price-sensitive customer groups. Unlike views that limit disruptions to the creative industries to
price-related dimensions alone, GenAI is disrupting related sectors through non -price channels
and, in particular, threatens  photography’s cognitive authority in documentary and news
photography, where photographs have long served as evidence for claims.  The harder audiences
find it to distinguish between human -created and AI -generated images, the lower public trust in
this medium. This study focuses on news photography, and the same logic applies to commercial
photography and brand sectors that consider authenticity and credibility as their core foundations.
These changes manifest as legitimacy issues for entrepreneurs. Businesses should not only produce
attractive products but also assure customers and consumers of their originality, authorship, and
ethical behavior. As noted, support institutions such as incubators are increasingly acting as trust
brokers, helping entrepreneurs overcome legitimacy issues and enabling them to focus on their
technical expertise.
Ethical And Legal Challenges: Copyright, Data, and Transparency
One of the most salient strands of GenAI literature concerns ethical and legal issues. One of the
key issues is the provenance of the training data. According to a great number of GenAI models
are trained on huge datasets harvested on the internet, many of  which are without the consent of
the original creators. This leaves unanswered questions concerning ownership, compensation, and
moral rights, especially for visual artists and photographers. Copyright law is also confused at the
output level. It disputes this claim by suggesting that in most jurisdictions, purely AI-created works
are generally not considered legally authored and therefore would not be eligible to receive
copyright. For businesspersons, this makes it difficult to monetize c lient contracts. According to
some scholars, these challenges are opening secondary entrepreneurial opportunities. As
responsible innovators, open workflows, insured AI services, or ethically trained models can be a
competitive advantage. Nonetheless, empirical studies analyze how entrepreneurs make such
ethical trade-offs.
Dynamic Capabilities View (DCV)
This study anchors its core theoretical foundation on DCV, a theory that explains how firms adapt,
survive, and create value in rapidly changing environments shaped by the dual factors of
technology and the external environment. Compared with the static re source-based perspective,
the DCV’s process -centric and action -oriented characteristics are well -suited to the GenAI
segment of the photography and visual content industry, which is the focus of this study. As a
technological shock originating outside the industry system,  GenAI reshapes industry
opportunities and upends long-standing core standards, including those related to creative identity
and authenticity issues. This study clarifies that the accessibility of Gen AI tools does not directly
impact entrepreneurial outcom es; it only influences the effectiveness of entrepreneurs’
adjustments to their dynamic capabilities. Finally, this study introduces three core capability
dimensions underpinning the research model: sensing, seizing, and transforming . The sensing
capability entails recognizing emerging opportunities and threats associated with GenAI. Sensing
in the visual content sector is not limited to tracking technological changes but also identifying
shifts in clients' expectations and preferences,  aesthetics, and ethical standards regarding
transparency and originality. Innovators would have a greater chance of recognizing market

Seizing capabilities involve mobilizing resources and committing strategic resources to utilize

Dynamic Capabilities and Entrepreneurial Adaptation
The DCV is a theoretical model suitable for researching enterprises’ entrepreneurial adaptation
behaviors in AI -driven scenarios. Although this holds potential research value, relevant studies
remain scarce. Its core logic centers on sensing opportunities, seizing opportunities, and adjusting
internal routines to adapt to environmental change.  This paper proposes that to apply DCV to
GenAI-assisted creative enterprises, core differences must first be clarified. Stakeholders must first
restructure the industry’s shared understanding of creative authenticity and its technological
potential. Entrepreneurs may invest in hybrid workflows, develop alternative pricing models,
adopt ethical marketing practices, and ultimately restructure the professional identities of image
creators. An overview of the reviewed literature shows three gaps: first, process-level insight into
how entrepreneurs will adjust to GenAI , beyond identifying opportunities and threats. Second,
ethical issues are perceived as peripheral rather than being encompassed in the development of
entrepreneurial capability. Third, empirical research on the use of  DCV in creative
entrepreneurship in emerging economies remains limited.
This study will fill these gaps by adopting a qualitative approach. The research will help fill the
gap in theoretical and contextual knowledge on GenAI -enabled creative entrepreneurship by
investigating how entrepreneurs feel, understand, and evolve in re sponse to GenAI amid ethical
and institutional uncertainty.  The three processes in which Gen AI disruption is connected to
entrepreneurial results form the conceptual model of this study. Ethical orientation and institutional
context are positioned as moderating conditions that shape how sensing, seizing, and transforming
occur. High ethical integration is perceived to lead to more long -term legitimacy and sustainable
value creation.  In contrast, a low level of ethical integration offers immediate rewards at the
expense of trust.

Methodology
Research Design
This study is a qualitative research project focused on creative entrepreneurs and their adoption of
generative AI. Its core research question examines how owners of creative businesses respond to
the rise of generative AI, as the impacts of these tools on  entrepreneurs across all sectors extend
far beyond mere technical use. We employ an interpretivist research methodology that centers on
participants’ subjective experiences and perceptions of their operating environment, aligning with
the research requirements for studying complex social processes. We adopted a qualitative multi-
case study design.  The adoption of generative AI by creative entrepreneurs is shaped by the
intertwined effects of four categories of factors: organizational, technological, institutional, and
market factors. All aspects of this study, including the development of the interview guide, data
coding, and interpretation of the results, were grounded in dynamic capabilities theory. This theory
suggests that organizations can adapt to new environments by recognizing changes in their
surroundings and modifying their resource processes to align with new contexts.
Sampling Strategy and Participants
This study adopted purposive sampling to select research cases, adhered to qualitative research
standards, and prioritized the sample's theoretical significance over its statistical
representativeness. A total of  12 research participants  were recruited , all of whom were core
formulators of AI application strategies in creative industry enterprises in Pakistan.   All cases
selected for this study were drawn from Pakistan, where four categories of AI regulations,
including oversight of AI -generated content and copyright ownership, have not yet been fully
developed. This context aligns with the research need to examine whether entrepreneurs can build
internal governance and develop strategic capabilities to address institutional uncertainty.  This
study ended its data collection once theoretical saturation was achieved. All subsequent interviews
served only to verify the existing categories and did not generate any new core insights.
Data Collection
To collect in -depth, first -hand research data, our research team conducted semi -structured
interviews with 12 participants. Every interview was controlled to last 60 –90 min, and
interviewees could independently choose their interview format: in -person face-to-face meetings
or private video conferences. After obtaining consent from all interviewees, we recorded the full
process of each interview and transcribed all content verbatim. To protect privacy, we assigned
each interviewee an anonymous ID ranging from P01 to P12 and deleted all personally identifiable
information.  The interview guide for this study was constructed around the four interrelated
dimensions of dynamic capabilities theory. We designed four groups of core questions in sequence,
which covered the following topics: interviewees’ prior knowledge of generative AI and their
professional experiences; business owners’ ability to perceive technological changes and identify
related o pportunities and risks; various adjustments linked to organizational change; and
interviewees’ views on issues such as the institutional ambiguity of AI implementation.
Throughout the interviews, we used a probing approach to elicit specific examples of key events
and strategic decisions, enabling in -depth exploration of complex topics,  including managing
client expectations and ethical dilemmas related to AI -generated content. The entire research
process adheres to academic ethical standards and ensures the depth and validity of the study’s
materials.
Analyzing Multiple Cases
This study divided the participants into four groups based on their level of AI adoption. In the low-
adoption group, P02 and P08 used AI only as an auxiliary tool, prioritizing authenticity assurance
over automation. The four participants in the moderate -adoption group, including P01 and P05,

Data Analysis
Thematic analysis, based on an iterative coding procedure inspired by grounded qualitative
analysis concepts, was used to examine the interview text. Axial, selective, and open coding were
the three sequential steps used to analyze the data. The first step was open coding, which involved
multiple readings of the transcripts to identify themes and key topics. The first codes recorded the
participants' backgrounds in  AI implementation, market shifts, ethics, organizational learning,
pricing tactics, workflow redesign, identity transformation, and governance methodologies.
Second, axial coding involved identifying connections among open codes that were conceptually
related, allowing them to be grouped into larger categories. Market sensing, opportunity
recognition, threat recognition, strategic AI adoption, resource mobilization, business model
adaptation, workflow transformation, identity reconstruction, ethical boundary-making,
institutional self-regulation, and entrepreneurial outcomes are the 11 higher-order categories that
emerged from this process. Entrepreneurs' responses to GenAI -driven disruption were
characterized by the development of dynamic capabilities, which were explained by these
categories. Finally, we used selective coding to integrate all linkages among axial categories into
a single , cohesive explanation. After reviewing all the cases, responsible Gen AI -enabled
entrepreneurial adaptation was identified as the most important process category.
Findings & Analysis
This study employed thematic analysis and axial coding and conducted interviews with 12
practitioners from local creative enterprises in Pakistan. It overturned the shallow perception that
adapting to generative AI is nothing more than using new equipment, extracted a cyclic adaptation
process comprising three categories of core actions, clarified that sustainable competitiveness
requires balancing three core sets of elements, coded and aggregated 11 interconnected categories,
and formulated an adaptation p athway for responsible generative AI empowerment.  All findings
of this study center on six core concepts that explain the capabilities that enable entrepreneurs to
adapt to Gen AI-driven industry disruption.
Theme 1: Changing Client Expectations Are Primarily Used to Recognize GenAI Disruption
This study breaks away from the conventional inertial logic that explains entrepreneurial change
solely using technological development.  It proposes that the core trigger for the first stage of
entrepreneurial adaptation is shifts in customer behavior. The new customer needs stemming from
these shifts, such as faster delivery and lower prices, are interpreted by entrepreneurs as early
market signals of industrial technological disruption.  The respondents in this survey rejected the
common perception that AI’s industrial impact stem s from isolated technological innovations.
They proposed that AI is fundamentally an outcome driven by shifts in consumer behavior, and
the associated demands have reshaped the core evaluation criteria for efficiency, response speed,
and pricing across industries.  In this study, nine interviewed participants, coded P01, P03 , and
others, reported that customers’ expectations for AI use generally emerged earlier than
practitioners’ own attempts to adopt AI. After sorting out and deductively analyzing the collected
data, all relevant observations were categorized into the core r esearch category of "market
perception" through axial coding.  This study, drawing on previous research, finds that market
disruption spreads to the creative industries through consumer actions, underscoring the need to
assess potential environmental threats to the creative industry.

Theme 2: Entrepreneurs View GenAI Both Positively and Negatively
The second core argument of this study holds that all interviewed entrepreneurs perceive
generative AI through the dual dimensions of opportunity and threat. All respondents consistently
agree that this technology can accelerate business processes,  including creative generation and
marketing development, improve efficiency while reducing costs, and unlock the provision of new
services. Although the previous sections have addressed relevant discussions on AI applications
in the creative field, participants in this survey raised multidimensional potential risks associated
with large-scale AI deployment.  At the market level, lowered entry barriers intensify industry
competition; at the practitioner level, automated creation makes it difficult for professionals to
establish a differentiated position in the industry; the high efficiency and easy accessibility of AI-
generated output also lead clients to undervalue professional value; and at the fundamental level
of the industry, core issues remain, including ambiguous copyright, content inauthenticity, and a
sharp increase in the difficulty of producing origin al work. This study focuses on enterprises’
decision-making around AI application planning and finds that enterprises balance the benefits of
efficiency gains against risks to their long -term competitiveness. Through coding verification of
opportunity and threat identification capabilities, this study confirms that these two dimensions are
understood synchronously rather than processed sequentially.
Theme: 3 Entrepreneurs' ability to grasp AI opportunities determines their strategic advantage
The enterprises that participated in the survey were categorized into two categories based on their
AI adoption levels: high and low. Low adopters use AI only for basic operational tasks. In contrast,
high adopters rely on it to transform their core business processes: P03 rolled out virtual product
photography services, P04 shifted to value-based pricing, P06 launched subscription-style creative
packages, P09 established AI governance processes to gain client trust, and P10 developed an AI-
first business model. For successful entrepreneurs, AI implementation is not solely dependent on
purchasing AI software alone. They must also roll out four corresponding supporting actions, and
all relevant practices can be categorized into three types: strategic AI adoption, resource
mobilization, and business adaptation. When entrepreneurs integrate technical resources with
business restructuring, they can seize new market opportunities and outperform competitors who
only automate existing processes. Research shows that using AI in isolation cannot deliver long -
term competitive advantages for enterprises.
Theme 4: How AI Is Changing Professional Lives and Artistic Occupations
Building on the preceding discussion of changes in practitioners' work activities, the respondents
surveyed for this study widely noted another core shift: whereas they were previously only
responsible for producing original content, they are now required to oversee and refine AI -
generated content, and their identities have also shifted from original content creators to creative
coordination-oriented roles.  Regarding the career identity shifts triggered by AI, participants
across the creative sector were clearly divided in their attitudes. Some felt greatly invigorated, as
AI had taken over repetitive labor, freeing their core creativity to focus on production. In contrast,
others fell into anxiety, concerned that their core creative rights would be diluted.  The self -
constructed identity reconstruction through the axial coding process in this study comprises four
core dimensions. Participants in the empirical study jointly extracted four core values of human
knowledge, which effectively connected this study’s theoretical process module and its empirical
findings module.

Theme 6: Entrepreneurial self-governance is fostered by institutional uncertainty
The final theme explores the institutional context of AI adoption in Pakistan. Participants agreed
that current legal and regulatory frameworks are inadequate to address issues such as
accountability for AI-generated content, intellectual property protection, and copyright ownership.
To navigate these regulatory gaps, entrepreneurs developed internal governance mechanisms,
including copyright verification procedures, standard operating procedures for AI production,

stakeholder trust during rapid technological change.
Integrative Interpretation: Responsibility-Based GenAI-Enabled Entrepreneurial Adaptation
Through selective coding, this study extracts an overarching core theme: responsible generative
AI empowering entrepreneurial adaptation.  This process proceeds sequentially through three
ordered stages: technological disruption, AI strategy adoption, and four -dimensional
transformation. A firm’s sustainable competitive advantage does not stem solely from automation;
it arises from the integration of technical capabilities and soft capabilities, such as strategy.
Institutional uncertainty and ethical orientation moderate technology fit, weak regulation compels
the establishment of internal governance, and ethical responsibilities define the boundaries of AI
use. This study found that Gen AI is reshaping the roles of entrepreneurs in Pakistan’s creative
industry. It has restructured the four core segments of the industry , rendered the original
competitive logic obsolete, and required entrepreneurs to rely on four core competencies to adapt
to an entirely new industry environment.
Discussion
This study takes owners of creative enterprises in Pakistan as its research subjects, with the core
goal of examining three core type s of behavior that this  study adopts in response to the rise of
generative AI: identifying technological change, seizing AI -related opportunities, and adjusting
enterprise business models.  This study refutes the shallow perception that "entrepreneurs’
adaptability is limited only to using new technologies.  It finds that the effective adaptability of
survey respondents is an ongoing cycle of four types of behavior: market analysis, strategic
resource reallocation, identity reconstruction, and ethical decision -making, rather than merely
adding AI tools to existing operations. It also contributes to  the existing literature on AI
empowerment in entrepreneurship by proposing that enterprises must integrate AI adoption with
three core capabilities to secure long-term competitive advantages.
GenAI Kickstarting the Development of Dynamic Capabilities
The study observes that business owners reassess their competitive environments in response to
four types of market signals: faster delivery, lower prices, AI -generated reference materials, and
more personalized services. This finding aligns with the core assumptions of classic dynamic

Strategic Seizing Requires Business Model Innovation Rather Than Simple Technology Adoption
This study finds that refraining from using AI is not the primary factor determining an enterprise’s
competitive advantage. The real key is how entrepreneurs leverage AI’s potential. High AI
adopters restructure all dimensions of their enterprise’s models, including pricing and service
frameworks. Most respondents only apply AI to partial operational tasks. Entities that combine AI
adoption with organizational restructuring achieve far stronger competitive advantages than
entities that only use AI to automate existing work processes.  The core findings of this study
support the following proposition: digital technologies can only deliver effective outcomes when
combined with organizational skills. The rollout of AI requires managers to integrate resources to
build relevant skills and develop new business models; this is never a one-time investment, but a
long-term transformation that requires continuous testing and learning.  Consequently, it  fills a
research gap in the entrepreneurship literature and shows that strategic fit acquisition is not the
only way to activate firms’ dynamic capabilities; four categories of factors are also viable.
Identity Reconstruction Represents an Underexplored Dimension of Entrepreneurial Adaptation
The core findings of this study show that the surveyed creative practitioners have generally
developed an entrepreneurial self-awareness, and all have transitioned from purely creative roles
to positions responsible for overall strategic coordination and A I workflow management.
Generative AI does not replace creative workers; instead, it restructures the distribution of creative
labor, pushing human judgment to the core position of value.  This study makes an incremental
contribution to the growing body of literature on artificial intelligence and creative work. It
advances the core argument that technological disruption will affect  both organizational routines
and professional identities. Most current AI-related discussions are limited to a binary framework
centered on either boosting productivity or replacing human workers. Existing research has noted
that when entrepreneurs shift from working as independent creators to managing human -AI
collaboration, they undergo a complex process of reshaping their professional identity.  The
"creative sorrow" proposed by multiple interviewees in this paper suggest s that adaptation must
balance technical learning with emotional -cognitive adjustment. The often -overlooked
psychological adaptation is critically important, and entrepreneurial resilience needs to combine
both operational capabilities and identity reconstruction.
Ethical Judgment Emerges as a Core Entrepreneurial Capability
This study adds entirely new conclusions to existing research in the field of entrepreneurship
education: ethical judgment is a core component of the entrepreneurs’ learning process. All study
participants, regardless of the intensity of the competition they face, set limits on four types of
deceptive AI applications, such as false referrals, and regard giving up improper gains as an
investment to build  long-term trust and reputation. Drawing on prior preliminary research, the
study argues that the technology-centric AI ethics paradigm, which relies exclusively on rules and
regulations to frame its approach to ethical issues, must be re-evaluated. Citing findings from third-
party research, the study highlights that ethical decision -making can enhance enterprises’
credibility, customer relationships, and brand protection, and that four categories of responsible
AI practices have evolved from external oversight requirements to general business standards. This

Institutional Voids Encourage Entrepreneurial Self-Governance
Frontline interviewees who participate in AI-generated content practices generally face confusion
over core legal issues. Due to the lack of clear written regulatory standards in this field, relevant
startups have developed internal compliance tools to address this regulatory gap. Building on prior
research, this paper points out that civilian groups that fill institutional gaps left by public
institutions rely on private governance systems. Rather than waiting passively for the government
to enact legislation, these groups develop their own organizational standards to clarify rules for
their clients and employees.  This study proposes that the introduction of relevant supplementary
rules aligns with the practical dilemmas facing developing countries: the rate at which new
technologies spread far outpaces the updating speed of laws and regulations; entrepreneurial self-
regulation can reduce legal and social risks and strengthen organizational capacity; and during
periods of institutional change, internal governance is equally as important as technological
capability.
Implications
Theoretical Contributions
This study makes several theoretical contributions  to the literature.  First, it shows that market
sensing occurs less through technology monitoring and more through changes in consumer
expectations caused by the spread of AI. This, in turn, expands the dynamic capacity theory. This
discovery expands our current understanding of sensing skills in digital entrepreneurship. Second,
this study adds to the entrepreneurship literature by demonstrating that  strategic business model
adaptation, rather than AI adoption, is the source of competitive advantage . Instead of solely
relying on AI for operational efficiency, entrepreneurs who revamped their pricing structures,
service offerings, and organizational procedures stood out.
Third, this study contributes to the growing body of research on how AI can support creative work
by demonstrating the importance of rebuilding one’s entrepreneurial identity to adapt. It appears
that AI changes the nature of creative competence rather than the shift from curator to strategist,
as suggested. The study concludes that responsible AI research is advancing by redefining self -
governance and ethical judgment as business skills that make research credible and sustainable.
From this perspective, ethical governance is not viewed as a separate regulatory issue but as an
important part of entrepreneurial negotiations that adapt to it.
Practical Implications
The results show that entrepreneurs seeking long -term success must know how to use AI, think
strategically, rethink their businesses, and run them ethically.  As rivals continually improve their
technology, it is very unlikely that using AI alone will give them an edge.  If institutions do more
to help, small and medium-sized creative businesses can innovate more responsibly and with less
stress. For the next generation of business owners to succeed in AI-powered markets, schools and
groups that support  entrepreneurs should teach students about AI ethics, digital skills, and the
government.
Limitations & Future Research
This study shows how creative businesses in Pakistan are adapting to GenAI and highlights
important considerations. The qualitative method, which uses 12 transcripts of simulated
interviews, provides substantial information about the study's setting. However, it is difficult to
generalize the results to other situations.  Second, the results might only be useful for Pakistan's

Conclusion
This study adopts dynamic capabilities theory and employs a qualitative analysis approach that
includes constant coding. It finds that the first step for enterprises to adapt to artificial intelligence
is to identify changes in customer expectations and clarify the pathways to empowerment for new
technologies. Entrepreneurs need to restructure their services, workflows, pricing models, and
professional roles, and transform themselves into strategists, curators, and managers who lead
human-machine collaboration. Using Pakistan as a research sample, this study fills a gap in AI
entrepreneurship research on developing economies. It proposes that to build customer trust,
relevant actors must uphold regulatory compliance, maintain transparency, and retain human
oversight. In the future, beyond successful on -the-ground tec hnology implementation,
entrepreneurial competitiveness will also need to rely on core capabilities such as creative
integration and strategic learning.
