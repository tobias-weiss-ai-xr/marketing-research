Abstract: Digital marketing practices are driving an increase in the use of eco-friendly claims
to build an environmentally friendly image. However, many sustainability claims lack
transparency and ecological evidence, raising the potential for greenwashing. This st udy
employs a qualitative approach using descriptive methods through interviews with consumers
who use e-commerce and digital media. The results show that 1) consumer perceptions of eco-
friendly products are influenced by visual elements such as the color green, packaging design,
nature symbols, and sustainability storytelling, which quickly create an eco -friendly
impression; 2) greenwashing practices in digital marketing manifest through ambiguous
claims, eco-friendly visuals, and sustainability narratives  that lack technical information or
certifications; and 3) information transparency influences consumers’ evaluation of brands,
as consumers increasingly verify claims through user reviews and the consistency of product
information. This study contributes to the understanding of sustainability in digital marketing
as a symbolic construct shaped by digital communication and consumer perception.
Keywords: Eco-Friendly, Digital Business, Greenwashing, Consumer Perception

environmental information.

Eco-friendly brand marketing in Indonesia is demonstrated by various brands through
claims regarding the use of recycled materials, plastic reduction, energy and fuel efficiency,
the use of sustainable raw materials, and packaging return and management pro grams. These
practices are found across various product categories, including detergents, bottled water,
automotive lubricants, personal care products, and tea beverages. This variety of environmental
claims indicates that sustainability is increasingly becoming part of brand communication and
positioning strategies. However, the use of eco -friendly claims in marketing must be viewed
critically, as not all claims necessarily reflect substantial and verifiable sustainability practices.
Amid the rise in eco-friendly claims by various brands, greenwashing has become a topic
of discussion in the context of e-commerce (Marcatajo, 2023; Oluwaseun Peter Oyeyemi et al.,
2024; Valor et al., 2025) . Greenwashing refers to the presentation of information or claims
regarding environmental aspects that may give the impression that a product or company is
more environmentally friendly than it actually is. This practice can be observed through the
use of terms such as “natural,” “eco,” or “sustainable” without adequate information regarding
the standards or basis for the claims made  (Liang & Gao, 2025; Marrucci et al., 2025). In the
context of e -commerce, such claims may take the form of information about the use of eco -
friendly materials presented without details on composition, certifications, or the production
process, thereby providing consumers with limited sustainabilit y information to evaluate a
product (Badhwar et al., 2024; Marcatajo, 2023; Marrucci et al., 2025).
In addition, greenwashing is also evident in the use of packaging that appears
environmentally friendly at first glance  such as green colors or leaf symbols  but actually still
uses single -use plastic materials that are difficult to recycle (Badhwar et al., 2024) . Some
brands even highlight a single minor aspect that “looks green”  such as the use of organic
ingredients in low percentages  while other aspects, such as production processes, distribution,
and waste, are not disclosed transparently  (Badhwar et al., 2024; Verma & Bharti, 2023). This
practice is reinforced by digital marketing strategies that emphasize an eco -friendly image
without credible data to back it up, potentially misleading consumers’ perceptions when
evaluating a product (Badhwar et al., 2024; Marrucci et al., 2025).

responses, but the findings reveal a tension that has not yet been fully explained. (Eze, 2025)

The phenomenon of greenwashing in the digital environment can be explained through
Signaling Theory, developed by (Spence, 1973) , which describes how recipients use
observable information as signals to form judgments under conditions of informational
uncertainty. In the context of e -commerce, consumers cannot directly observe a product’s
quality and environmental impact; therefore,  the sustainability claims made by companies
serve as signals in the consumer evaluation process. This study analyzes sustainability signals
through four indicators: visual signals, narrative signals, credibility signals, and transparency
signals (see Table 2) which describe the ways in which eco -friendly information is conveyed
to consumers. Thus, Signaling Theory is used to explain how these various signals serve as the
basis for consumers to evaluate sustainability claims and identify potential greenwashing.

METHOD

interpret and evaluate eco-friendly claims communicated through e-commerce platforms. The

were active e-commerce users and had experience purchasing or evaluating products marketed
as eco-friendly. The study involved eight informants aged 20–35 years, comprising six women

Primary data were collected through in -depth interviews lasting approximately 20 –40
minutes. The interviews explored informants experiences, understanding, and assessments of
sustainability claims encountered in digital environments, including how they int erpreted,
evaluated, and responded to eco-friendly claims. In addition to interview data, digital footprints
related to e -commerce users were examined as supporting evidence to provide contextual
information about the communication of eco -friendly claims. Data collection continued until
saturation was reached. Saturation was determined through an iterative review of the coding
worksheet alongside the ongoing interviews and analysis. Interviews were considered
sufficient when successive interviews no longer produced new codes, categories, or themes
relevant to the research questions and when the existing categories were adequately represented
across the informants. Secondary data were also used to complement the primary data. These
included journal articles on greenwashing, consumer perceptions, and digital marketing
obtained from Google Scholar and Scopus, as well as relevant information from social media
platforms, including TikTok, Facebook, and X. These sources were used primarily to
contextualize the digital communication environment and to support the interpretation of
interview findings rather than to replace the primary accounts provided by informants.
Data were analyzed using thematic analysis with the assistance of NVivo 12 Plus. The
analysis began with familiarization with the data through repeated reading of interview
transcripts and review of relevant digital materials. Initial codes were then assig ned to
statements and information related to the research focus. Codes with similar meanings were
subsequently grouped into categories, which were compared across informants and data
sources to identify recurring patterns and develop broader themes. The emerging themes were
continuously reviewed against the interview transcripts and supporting digital data to assess
their consistency and relevance. Themes were subsequently defined and named according to
the substantive meanings represented in the data.
The trustworthiness of the study was addressed through systematic documentation and
triangulation. Dependability was strengthened by maintaining a documented coding process,
including the development, grouping, comparison, and refinement of codes and theme s in
NVivo 12 Plus. This procedure provided an auditable analytical process from the interview
data to the development of the final themes. Confirmability was established by repeatedly
comparing analytical interpretations with the original interview transcripts and relevant digital

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Factors influence consumer perception
Consumer perceptions of eco-friendly claims in the digital business sector are shaped by
various sustainability signals available on e -commerce platforms. Unlike conventional
transactions, which allow consumers to observe products directly, digital transac tions are
characterized by high information asymmetry because consumers cannot directly verify
product characteristics or a company’s sustainability (A. & S., 2022; Clemons et al., 2007) .
This makes sustainability perception fundamentally a process of inference: consumers do not
observe sustainability directly but infer it from signals that vary in quality, credibility, and
verifiability. Consequently, the effectiveness of an eco -friendly claim depends not simply on
what information is presented, but on whether the signal enables consumers to reduce
uncertainty about the underlying sustainability practice. Based on thematic analysis, the factors
most frequently associated with consumer perception of eco-friendly claims are show in table

This statement illustrates that consumers do not necessarily interpret an environmental
claim at face value. Instead, they search for additional evidence that can transform an abstract
sustainability claim into an assessable product attribute. Product desc riptions, information on
raw materials, production processes, and environmental certifications function as supporting
signals that increase the observability of otherwise hidden sustainability characteristics. From
a signaling perspective, this is signific ant because the informational value of a signal is
determined by its ability to reduce uncertainty, rather than by the attractiveness of the signal
itself. The finding also provides a more nuanced interpretation of information transparency.
Transparency should not be equated merely with the quantity of information displayed on an
e-commerce page. Excessive but unverifiable information may have limited signali ng value.
What appears more important is the combination of completeness, specificity, consistency, and
verifiability (Atkinson & Rosenthal, 2014) . Thus, the findings extend the conventional
understanding of digital information quality by suggesting that, in sustainability contexts,
consumers are particularly concerned with whether information can be used to substantiate an
environmental claim.
From the perspective of signaling theory, information provided by companies functions
as a mechanism for reducing information asymmetry between sellers and consumers (Spence,
1973). However, the present findings suggest that digital signaling is not a one-way process in
which firms simply send signals to consumers. Rather, consumers actively interpret, compare,
and validate multiple signals before accepting an eco -friendly claim. Th is makes the digital
marketplace a more interactive signaling environment than the conventional seller -to-
consumer model implies. This dynamic becomes particularly evident in the role of consumer
reviews, product ratings, and user experiences. Several informants indicated that they
considered other consumers’ experiences more trustworthy than sellers’ promotional claims.

These findings are consistent with the broader logic of electronic Word of Mouth (e -
WOM), in which consumers use other users’ experiences to reduce uncertainty in online
purchasing. However, the relatively low number of references associated with positive product
reviews (8%) compared with information transparency (36%) and information credibility
(32%) indicates that social validation should not be interpreted as the primary determinant in
this study. Rather, reviews appear to operate as a complementary ve rification mechanism
(Ikhsan et al., 2024) . This distinction is important because it challenges an interpretation in
which user -generated content automatically dominates corporate information in digital
sustainability communication. In this study, consumers appear to first require substantive
information from the seller and subsequently use other consumers’ experiences as an additional
basis for validation.
The research findings further demonstrate that consumers increasingly understand “eco-
friendly” as a multidimensional concept. They consider not only packaging or product
appearance but also the use of sustainable raw materials, production processes, waste reduction,
environmental certification, and responsible business practices. This indicates that consumer
evaluation has moved from symbolic recognition toward substantive assessment. In other
words, consumers are increasingly distinguishing between a prod uct that merely looks
environmentally friendly and one for which environmental performance can be supported by
evidence. This distinction is particularly relevant to signaling theory because it separates
superficial signals from signals that carry stronger informational value. Nevertheless, visual
cues remain relevant in the initial formation of consumer perceptions. The use of green colors,
environmental symbols, eco -labels, and environmentally oriented packaging can provide
immediate associations with sustainability. As one informant explained:
“Usually, my first impression comes from the packaging if the package arrives without
excessive bubble wrap or uses simple brown cardboard, I immediately think it’s eco -friendly.

Visual signals can therefore be understood as low-cost cognitive heuristics that facilitate
rapid judgments in an environment characterized by abundant product choices (Bertel & Kirlik,
2011). However, their role appears to be primarily initial rather than decisive (Buvár et al.,
2023). The interview data indicate that consumers may use visual cues to form an initial
expectation of environmental friendliness but subsequently seek narrative and documentary
evidence to determine whether that expectation is justified.  This distinction becomes
particularly important when consumers encounter environmental symbols or eco-labels whose
authenticity is uncertain. RR Kembar, a frozen food entrepreneur, explained:
“I once came across a household cleaning product that featured a logo depicting a leaf or the
Earth with text such as ‘eco -friendly.’ I looked into it and found that the logo wasn’t from an
independent certification body but was simply a graphic design created by the company’s own
marketing department.”
Rather than interpreting this example simply as evidence that consumers distrust visual
environmental symbols, the statement reveals a more complex signaling mechanism. The leaf
or Earth logo initially operates as a heuristic signal, allowing consumers to infer environmental
friendliness without having to process complex information about the product’s environmental
performance. This is precisely where heuristic substitution becomes relevant: an easily
recognizable visual attribute substitutes for less obse rvable and more cognitively demanding
evidence, such as verified environmental performance, production processes, or independent
certification. From the firm's perspective, such visual signals can be strategically attractive
because they are relatively inexpensive, easy to reproduce, and capable of communicating an
environmental association quickly in crowded digital marketplaces. The problem emer ges
when the symbolic cue is allowed to imply environmental attributes that are not supported by
corresponding evidence. In this situation, the visual signal does not necessarily communicate
false information explicitly; instead, it encourages consumers to  make a favorable
environmental inference based on incomplete evidence. This provides a more critical
explanation of how greenwashing can operate through visual communication. The potential
manipulation lies not simply in displaying a leaf or Earth symbol,  but in using that symbol to
create an environmental impression that exceeds the evidentiary basis of the claim.

This example also demonstrates that signaling effectiveness depends on the relationship
between the signal and its source. The informant initially interpreted the visual cue as an
indicator of environmental friendliness, but further investigation revealed that the symbol was
produced by the company's own marketing team rather than by an independent certification
body. The credibility of the signal therefore changed once its source became known. From a
signaling perspective, this illustrates that receivers do not evaluate signals solely according to
their visual content; they also assess the credibility of the signaler and the possibility that the
signal reflects strategic self-presentation.
This finding is particularly relevant to greenwashing because firms possess incentives to
communicate favorable environmental attributes while consumers face difficulty directly
observing actual environmental performance. Where independent verification is absent, firms
may exploit this information asymmetry by relying on visual cues that are easier to process
than substantive environmental evidence. The visual signal can consequently reduce
consumers’ initial uncertainty while simultaneously preserving unce rtainty about the
underlying environmental quality of the product. This creates an important distinction between
reducing the perceived uncertainty of a consumer and reducing the actual information
asymmetry surrounding environmental performance.  The finding partially diverges from the
broader literature on visual sustainability labels, which generally reports positive effects of
visual labels on consumer perceptions and behavioral outcomes (Majer et al., 2022). (Buvár et
al., 2023) similarly indicate that visual sustainability cues can influence consumer judgments.
However, the present findings suggest that the effectiveness of such cues is conditional rather
than universal. Visual cues may be effective in attracting attention and ge nerating an initial
environmental association, but their influence may weaken when consumers subsequently seek
evidence to validate the claim. In this sense, the current study does not reject the persuasive
potential of visual sustainability cues; rather, it qualifies their role by demonstrating that visual
attractiveness alone is insufficient to establish credibility and trust.
In the digital environment, this qualification becomes increasingly important because
consumers can cross-check claims through search engines, social media, reviews, certification
databases, and other online sources. Consequently, a visual signal that init ially produces a
positive environmental inference can be reinterpreted negatively when subsequent information

Information transparency is a key strategic element in digital business because it
influences how consumers evaluate product quality, brand credibility, and transaction risk.
Unlike conventional transactions, in which consumers can inspect products directly, the digital
environment creates greater information asymmetry between companies and consumers.
Transparency therefore functions not merely as a communication strategy but as a mechanism
for reducing uncertainty surrounding product attributes that consumers cannot directly observe

tend to respond more positively to companies that communicate openly about both the strengths

impression management. From the perspective of Signaling Theory, the va lue of such
disclosure lies in its capacity to distinguish substantive communication from exaggerated self-
presentation. Rather than maximizing the positive environmental impression, the firm limits

meaningful not because it provides more information per se, but because it signals a willingness
to expose information that may not produce an entirely favorable image.

strengthens the credibili ty of signals by enabling consumers to evaluate them. The contrast
suggests that the central determinant is not simply whether firms communicate sustainability,
but whether their sustainability communication can withstand consumer verification.
Transparency is therefore not inherently beneficial simply because more information is
disclosed; its value depends on whether the information enables consumers to evaluate the
underlying claim.
Taken together, the findings indicate that information transparency functions as a
boundary mechanism between sustainability communication and greenwashing. When
environmental claims are specific, consistent, and verifiable, transparency strengthens the
signaling process by reducing uncertainty and supporting credibility. When claims remain
vague, selective, exaggerated, or unsupported, the same digital communication environment
can facilitate greenwashing. The issue is therefore not the amount of sustainab ility
communication but the extent to which consumers can use that communication to verify the
underlying environmental claim. In this respect, transparency becomes strategically valuable
when it exposes the boundaries of sustainability performance rather than simply maximizing
the appearance of environmental responsibility.

CONCLUSION
Consumer perceptions of eco -friendly products in the digital space are shaped through
the interpretation of various signals conveyed by companies, including visuals, narratives,
credibility, and transparency of information. The research findings indicate that consumers not
only consider sustainability claims communicated through digital marketing but are also
increasingly verifying them through certifications, product information, and user reviews.
These findings indicate that transparency and credibility o f information are crucial aspects in
building consumer trust and distinguishing substantive sustainability commitments from mere
eco-friendly imagery. In practical terms, companies need to develop more transparent,
authentic, and verifiable sustainability communications by providing clear information
regarding materials, certifications, and production processes.
For policymakers, these findings can serve as a basis for strengthening guidelines and
oversight of environmental claims in digital marketing so that consumers receive accurate and
accountable information.This study has limitations due to its qualitative a pproach and the
limited characteristics of the informants; therefore, the findings are not intended to be
generalized to all e -commerce consumers. Furthermore, the study focuses on consumer
perceptions in a digital context without quantitatively testing th e relationships between
variables. Future research could expand the characteristics and number of informants, compare
consumers across different platforms or product categories, and develop a quantitative
approach to test the relationships between sustainability signals, perceptions of greenwashing,
trust, and consumer responses.
