ABSTRACT
In the post-pandemic era, public utilities and transportation services have faced challenges in
rebuilding customer trust. PT Kereta Api Indonesia (Persero) addressed this by creating a
branded short film, "Pulang" (2023), for its digital marketing and Eid homecoming initiative.
This research identifies and analyzes how implicit soft-selling marketing messages are
integrated into the film's storytelling and cinematography. Using a descriptive qualitative
method and content analysis, 22 key scenes were examined across six aspects: station setting,
corporate services, physical features, digital application usage, train amenities, and ambiance.
Findings reveal that PT KAI utilized a branded entertainment strategy, featuring mostly
soft-selling techniques, with 21 scenes focused on this approach and only 1 scene using
hard-selling. The film incorporates heartwarming family reunion themes and appealing visuals,
promoting "narrative transportation" to enhance brand memory and reduce ad resistance. This
study highlights how public utility organizations can leverage cinematic storytelling as a
non-intrusive marketing strategy.
Keywords: Branded Entertainment, Soft-selling, Qualitative Content Analysis, Short Film
"Pulang", PT KAI.

INTRODUCTION
 The COVID-19 pandemic has led to massive
disruption of human activities throughout the world
(Alizadeh et al.). For almost three years, strict social
restrictions, regional lockdowns, and quarantine
measures temporarily halted mudik, the cherished
annual Eid al-Fitr homecoming (Veruswati et al.;
Alamsyah et al.; Nurmandi et al.). Within
Indonesia's socio-cultural context, this activity
extends beyond mere seasonal mass migration as it
embodies a deeply ingrained communal ritual, a
spiritual tradition for family reconciliation, and an
essential psychological coping mechanism for urban
migrants seeking to reconnect with their heritage
(Azhari and Sahputri; Alamsyah et al.). When the
government eventually lifted travel restrictions,
public transportation providers faced an
extraordinary challenge in both operations and
communication. They not only had to restore
physical services and scheduling but also needed to
rebuild psychological reassurance, trust, and
emotional ties with a cautious public that had
become accustomed to isolation and digital
disconnection (Andari et al.).
 PT Kereta Api Indonesia (Persero), better
known as KAI, acknowledged that train travel in
Indonesia had evolved beyond its basic utility.
Railway journeys in Indonesia, as correlated with
mudik, are viewed as an emotional ecosystem
characterized by personal memories, joyous
reunions, and deeply rooted family connections
(Herdianti et al.). This emotional bond became
particularly crucial due to KAI's historical
background. Before its extensive corporate
modernization initiated by Ignasius Jonan in 2009,
Indonesian railways were largely regarded as
chaotic, overcrowded, unsafe, and inefficient
(Herdianti et al.). Over more than a decade of
rigorous modernization, KAI transformed into a
highly efficient, clean, and customer-focused entity
(Herdianti et al.; Rahman). However, the
post-pandemic environment required a new

LITERATURE REVIEW
Marketing Messages and The
Soft-Selling Approach
 A marketing message is a structured
representation of a communicator's ideas, which can
be encoded into visual and/or verbal symbols,
designed to influence the attitudes, beliefs, and
subsequent behaviors of target consumers (Hamizar
et al.; Liu et al.). In marketing theory, these
messages are executed through two primary
communication styles: hard-selling and soft-selling
(Muttaqin et al.). The hard-selling approach relies on
direct, explicit, and aggressive product promotions,
focusing primarily on functional attributes, pricing
advantages, and immediate calls-to-action (Muttaqin
et al.; Desmarais et al.). In contrast, the soft-selling
approach is characterized by an indirect, subtle focus
on brand building, emotional stimulation, and the
cultivation of long-term psychological connections
(Muttaqin et al.; Desmarais et al.). This is achieved
through narrative storytelling, education, and
aesthetic engagement. By avoiding explicit sales
pitches, soft-selling minimizes consumer skepticism
and builds a more organic relationship between the
brand and the audience. This makes it an ideal
strategy for public service providers seeking to
improve their reputation rather than just push
transactional sales.
Branded Entertainment and Narrative
Transportation Theory
 Branded entertainment signifies an advanced
form of content marketing, characterized as original
entertainment media that a brand produces to convey
its fundamental values in a subtle, non-intrusive way
(Rodríguez-Rabadán Benito et al.). In contrast to
typical product placement, where a brand's item
merely serves as a passive object, branded
entertainment integrates the brand's identity and
offerings directly into the story's framework

(Loggerenberg et al.; Rodríguez-Rabadán Benito et
al.). The psychological basis for this approach is
elucidated by Narrative Transportation Theory,
which was initially developed by Green and Brock
(2000) (Green and Brock; Green and Appel). This
theory suggests that when individuals engage with a
compelling and emotionally impactful story, they
undergo a mental "transportation" into that narrative
world. This immersive cognitive and emotional
experience temporarily halts the viewer's critical
thinking, skepticism, and usual resistance to
advertising. Consequently, audiences become highly
receptive to the implicit commercial messages,
corporate values, and brand associations that are
seamlessly incorporated into the plot. This
phenomenon is strongly linked to the psychological
idea of identification, wherein viewers embrace the
perspectives and emotions of the characters. When a
character within a film finds comfort or relief
through using a brand's service, the viewer who is
transported likewise feels these positive emotions
indirectly. This leads to a highly favorable and
enduring attitude toward the brand that traditional
advertisements struggle to achieve.
Cinematography in Marketing
Communications
 Cinematography involves the artistry and
techniques of capturing moving images and
organizing them into a sequence of scenes to express
distinct dramatic meanings, thematic ideas, and
emotional atmospheres (Eenoo). In the realm of
marketing communications, cinema functions
through two main aspects, which are the visual
aspect and the linguistic aspect (Hamizar et al.; Liu
et al.). Visual aspect includes elements such as
camera angles, frame size, shot composition (like the
rule of thirds), camera movements, lighting, and
color grading to create particular psychological
states and brand perceptions (Diya Mirzha Rusyanto
et al.; Kinanti et al.; Kashaka; Dibda and Ahmad).
For instance, warm color temperatures
(approximately 3200 Kelvin) are frequently utilized
to evoke sensations of nostalgia, security, and homey
comfort. Likewise, low-angle shots can depict
corporate settings as stable and trustworthy. In
addition to the visual aspect is the linguistic or
verbal component, which comprises the dialect
spoken through words that establish the dramatic and
cognitive context for brand integration (Al-Badawi;
Hashem et al.; Nugroho et al.). When these two
aspects are synchronized, they enable a corporate
brand to convey its identity and services as natural,
integral elements of a shared human experience,
rather than as unwelcome interruptions.
METHOD
Research Design and Data Collection
 This research utilizes a descriptive qualitative
research framework to examine the structure of PT
KAI’s marketing and service communications within
the short film "Pulang." Descriptive qualitative
research is particularly effective for investigating
the "how" and "why" of communication phenomena
(Lim), enabling researchers to uncover underlying
meanings and processes in media texts. Situated
within a post-positivist paradigm, this study regards
the researchers as the main analytical tool. This
method necessitates a thorough engagement with the
media text to extract, interpret, and synthesize
qualitative information in a systematic and
comprehensive way, acknowledging that media
messages are inherently socially and culturally
constructed. The focus of this study is on PT Kereta
Api Indonesia (Persero), which serves as the brand
owner, principal funder, and corporate
communicator aiming to influence its public
perception. The subject of this research is the
branded short film "Pulang" (2023), which was
sourced from PT KAI’s official YouTube channel,
"Kereta Api Kita."

 Data collection was carried out through
comprehensive participant observation and
documentary review. This involved repeatedly
watching the film, performing detailed video frame

Data Analysis
 To maintain analytical rigor, the collected data
were evaluated using Qualitative Content Analysis
(Assarroudi et al.). This method is particularly
effective for exploratory studies because it allows
coding categories and theoretical insights to emerge
directly and inductively from the textual and visual
data, rather than forcing the data into pre-existing
rigid frameworks.

 The analysis proceeded through three main
stages. First, the verbal dialogues and visual actions
from the 22 selected scenes were fully transcribed
and logged in chronological order. Second, open
coding was performed to identify specific
cinematography techniques (such as shot types and
lighting) and their corresponding marketing
implications. Third, these codes were clustered into
six functional categories that represent the specific
service dimensions promoted by PT KAI. This
systematic categorization allows the research to
transition from simple visual observation to a deeper
understanding of the corporate communication
strategy. To ensure the reliability of this qualitative
process, the generated codes were cross-verified
through peer debriefing and continuous triangulation
with corporate policy documents.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Categorical Dimensions of Soft-Selling
Messages
 PT KAI's soft-selling marketing messages are
methodically arranged into six fundamental
categorical dimensions, according to a conventional
content analysis of the 22 chosen situations. Instead
of functioning as stand-alone commercials, they are
integrated into the movie's storyline, which enables
the company to convey its operational enhancements
and customer-focused principles in a very natural
way. The analysis of these six dimensions is given
below in thorough, narrative-driven paragraphs to
give a thorough qualitative perspective.
Promotion of Semarang Tawang Station
 PT KAI consistently depicts Semarang Tawang
Station (Figure 1) as more than a mere backdrop; it is
illustrated as a crucial character and the primary
narrative guide of the film. The station's historical
and physical characteristics are captured through
high-quality cinematography to boost brand
recognition and establish a sense of place. At the
film's opening, specifically at the 00:18 mark, a
breathtaking aerial drone shot reveals the expansive
facade of Semarang Tawang Station illuminated at
night. While the surroundings appear dark, the bright
white colonial building is framed using the rule of
thirds, underscoring its architectural importance and
modern design. The surrounding darker features,
such as the trees and sky, are deliberately
underexposed to highlight the striking, clean white
structure of the station. Following this visual
portrayal, at the 00:48 mark, an eye-level shot
captures the glowing interior nameplate "Semarang
Tawang."

Promotion of Service Hospitality and
Staff Demeanor
 The film exemplifies outstanding customer
service and staff professionalism through the
everyday behaviors of its characters. This plays a
crucial role in enhancing passenger satisfaction and
fostering brand trust. At the 07:04 mark, when the
main character, Bapak, arrives at the station's
administrative office, a Railway Police officer
(POLSUSKA) warmly welcomes him with a polite
phrase: "Oh, please come in, Sir..." as depicted in
Figure 2.

To understand the communicative significance of
this greeting, it is essential to ground the dialogue in
the firm's strategic core values. PT KAI operates
under the unified state-owned enterprise (BUMN)
code of ethics, known as BerAKHLAK (Amanah,
Kompeten, Harmonis, Loyal, Adaptif, Kolaboratif)
(Nur Kalinggar et al.). The greeting "silakan masuk"
(Oh, please come in, Sir) serves as an empirical
indicator of the Berorientasi Pelayanan (Service
Orientation) and Harmonis (Harmony) dimensions.
Service Orientation is defined corporate-wide as the
commitment to deliver friendly (ramah), agile
(cekatan), and helpful (solutif) public care. Thus, the
courteous invitation reflects a structured corporate
commitment to respect and approachability rather
than a mere casual exchange. In this scene, a medium
shot clearly focuses on both the officer and the
transparent glass doors of the office, conveying
notions of corporate accessibility, safety, and
transparency. The deliberate lack of heavy shadows
or dark color tones in this segment visually
reinforces this sense of systemic openness.
 This representation is further developed at the
07:16 mark (Figure 3), where a POLSUSKA officer
sees an older passenger hurriedly trying to board the
train while leaving his bag behind. The officer takes
the initiative to dash after the passenger and return
the bag, gently advising: "Please don't leave your
belongings behind next time, Sir." (Lain kali jangan
sampai tertinggal ya, pak).

safety and security of KAI's physical environment,
directly addressing historical public concerns about

Promotion of Physical Station Amenities
 To address persistent public views of
disorganized and uncomfortable train stations from
earlier years, the film emphasizes contemporary
station amenities. These facilities are portrayed as
offering exceptional comfort comparable to that of
modern airports. At the 03:55 mark (Figure 4), a
slowly panning camera shot sweeps across the dining
area of the station, illustrating its clean and modern
design.
 The color saturation in this sequence is rendered
with high chrominance and natural, bright high-key
lighting. According to film aesthetics and visual
semiotics (Giannetti; Monaco), high saturation and
balanced high-key illumination visually erase hidden
shadows, dirt, and clutter. In cinema language,
brightness and color clarity are coded indicators of
comfort and spatial safety (Kolokotroni et al.;
Sihombing et al.). By visually eliminating the dark,
grimy aesthetic historically associated with older,
pre-modernized railway platforms, this specific
cinematography primes the viewer to perceive KAI’s
facilities as clean, comfortable, and highly
welcoming. This visual is complemented at the 03:07
mark (Figure 5) by a playful exchange from the
Station Master (Pak KS), who introduces Naila to the
station diner and comments: "Disini saja, bikin
candu" (Eat here, it's highly addictive).

 In consumer behavior literature, the term "bikin
candu" (translated in this retail context as "highly
craving-inducing") represents the consumer
construct of craving. Craving, specifically food
craving, is defined as an intense motivational desire
or psychological longing to re-experience a highly
rewarding sensory stimulus (Meule). Unlike clinical
physical addiction, which carries negative
pathological connotations, the linguistic trigger of
"candu" describes a powerful state of liking and
wanting (incentive motivation). By claiming the
station food is "bikin candu," the dialogue
encourages the audience to view the station platform
not merely as a transitional, unhygienic pass-through
to be avoided, but as a reliable, comforting culinary
destination that invites repeat visitation.
 Additionally, at the 08:43 mark, the film
features the interior of the Station Master's VIP
meeting room, displaying wood-paneled walls,
framed awards, luxurious leather sofas, air
conditioning, and neatly arranged refreshments. In
terms of camera placement, the scene utilizes a
low-angle shot (Figure 6) to capture this VIP
meeting space. A low-angle shot positions the
camera physical lens below the subject's baseline
eye-line, forcing the viewer to look slightly upward
(Baranowski and Hecht). Psychologically, this
composition exaggerates the vertical scale, structural
stability, and structural weight of the environment
(Gruber et al.; Baranowski and Hecht), investing the
corporate setting with authority, prestige, and
institutional credibility. This camera angle
underscores PT KAI's high professional management
standards and superior hospitality.
 Furthermore, the warm, gentle lighting
streaming through the large windows of the office
adds a touch of elegance. In lighting theory
(Matbouly; Shahidi et al.), low-contrast, warm-toned
light softens facial features and diffuses textures,
minimizing the cold, sterile, and institutional
shadows of typical government office blocks. This
lighting choice triggers psychological associations
of coziness, security, and prestige, reassuring the
viewer that KAI's physical environments are crafted
to offer premium comfort and absolute attention to
travelers.

Promotion of Passenger Coach Amenities
 The level of passenger comfort during long
journeys greatly relies on the design of the coach
interiors. This aspect is emphasized in the film
during the journey segment of the story. At the 15:07
mark (Figure 8), there is a scene where Naila assists
Bapak in lifting his heavy baggage into the overhead
compartment in an executive-class coach, saying,
"Let me help you, Sir." (Sini pak, biar saya bantu).
 From a cinematographic standpoint, the camera
uses a medium-wide shot with a deep focal length,
positioned at an eye-level perspective inside the
coach aisle. This spatial composition provides the
audience with a clean, realistic view of the scale and
spatial layout (Sakhaei et al.). This angle effectively
showcases the spaciousness of the overhead storage,
the tidy luggage compartments, and the inviting
amber lighting that conveys safety and comfort. It
also brings attention to the ergonomically designed
plush seats, large clean windows for viewing, and
air-conditioned vents. This shot illustrates that
traveling with PT KAI offers a relaxing and
enjoyable experience.
 Furthermore, by showcasing the organized
layout of the cabin, KAI discreetly primes the
viewer's behavior. Grounded in Nudge Theory,
human behavior is heavily influenced by choice
architecture, the physical environment in which
decisions are made (De Paolis et al.;

visual nudge. Rather than using coercive, hard-sell
rules, this silent environmental cue subconsciously
promotes self-regulated compliance. It primes
passengers to keep things orderly and adhere to
luggage regulations, positioning compliance as a
natural, non-coercive aspect of a pleasant travel
experience. The scene reassures travelers that they
can avoid physical strain during lengthy trips, as the
environment is designed to support their comfort.

Critical Discussion: The Mechanism of
Narrative Transportation in "Pulang"
 The qualitative analysis highlights a distinct
difference in the delivery of marketing messages
within the film. Although there is a single brief
hard-sell moment, a close-up shot of a physical train
ticket at the 01:15 mark (Figure 11) without any
narrative transition, the other 21 scenes exclusively
utilize a sophisticated, indirect soft-selling
approach. This evident disparity signifies a
conscious strategic choice by PT KAI to emphasize
narrative immersion rather than direct product
placement. The psychological effectiveness of this
soft-selling method can be best understood through
Narrative Transportation Theory (Green and Brock).
The engaging and emotionally driven story of a
former convict striving for redemption and
reconciliation effectively immerses the audience into
the narrative environment. As viewers become
engrossed in the characters' challenges, their usual
cognitive defenses and skepticism towards
advertising diminish.
 During the process of narrative transportation,
viewers enter a unique cognitive state where they are
less conscious of their physical environment and
highly empathetic toward the characters’
experiences. This heightened sense of empathy is
essential for the effectiveness of soft-selling. As the
audience observes Bapak overcoming his emotional
guilt and achieving peace within the train’s
supportive, comfortable setting, they subconsciously
link this feeling of comfort and resolution to the
brand itself. The train transitions from being just a
metal vehicle to a safe space where emotional
healing transpires. When consumers encounter overt
hard-sell advertisements, they often perceive a threat
to their freedom of choice, which incites a defensive
reaction and leads to ad avoidance and negative
brand perceptions.

cognitive defenses. The audience does not feel

Conclusion
 This qualitative content analysis of PT KAI's
branded short film "Pulang" (2023) illustrates that
the company effectively utilized a dominant branded
entertainment strategy that focuses on a soft-selling
approach throughout 21 scenes while limiting direct
hard-selling messages to just one scene. The
marketing messages are skillfully distributed across
six key customer experience dimensions: station
aesthetic appeal, staff friendliness, high-quality
physical amenities, user-friendly digital booking,
exceptional coach comfort, and a compassionate
passenger-centric environment. Moreover,
cinematographic techniques such as aerial views,
warm color grading, rule-of-thirds composition, and
shallow depth of field collaborate seamlessly with
thoughtful verbal cues to enable narrative
engagement. This combination successfully
cultivates long-lasting emotional connections and
improves PT KAI’s corporate reputation in the
post-pandemic context. By shifting away from
conventional, functional service announcements and
adopting high-quality cinematic storytelling, PT
KAI has illustrated how public utilities can convey
corporate modernization in an engaging and
human-focused manner. This study is confined to a
qualitative, textual, and visual examination of the
film "Pulang" from the researcher’s analytical
viewpoint. It does not evaluate empirical audience
reactions or quantify shifts in brand perception
among actual passengers. Additionally, since the
focus is strictly on the visual and verbal aspects of
the film, it does not assess the influence of auditory
components, such as the music score and
environmental sound design, in promoting narrative
immersion.
 Future researchers in branded entertainment
should combine content analysis with empirical
audience research methods like reception analysis,
focus groups, or neuromarketing techniques to gauge
emotional engagement and booking intentions
post-viewing. Comparative studies on branded short
films from various state-owned enterprises could
reveal trends in how public utilities convey their
corporate identity through narrative media. For PT
KAI, continuing to use emotional, localized
storytelling in digital marketing is essential. In
future productions, integrating app features into
character actions—such as ordering food or checking
train schedules—can effectively showcase the app's
usefulness. This approach exemplifies how public
utilities can build trust and brand preference without
intrusive advertising, offering a model for
organizations in sectors like utilities, healthcare, and
public welfare to communicate their improvements
respectfully and engagingly.
