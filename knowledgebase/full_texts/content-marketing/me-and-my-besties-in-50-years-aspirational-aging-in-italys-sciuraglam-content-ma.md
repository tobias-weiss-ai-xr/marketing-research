Department of Humanities and Cultural Heritage, University of Udine, Udine, Italy
This article examines representations of older women in Italian fashion branded
content through the case of Instagram project Sciuraglam. Drawing on feminist
age studies and the concepts of technology of age and aspirational aging, it
explores how influencer marketing shapes narratives of gendered aging. The
study combines a qualitative close reading of Sciuraglam’s six most successful
Instagram posts with an analysis of users’ comments. The findings show that
Sciuraglam challenges the historical invisibility of older women by portraying
them as stylish, active, and desirable. However, this visibility links positive
aging to luxury consumption, elegance, and class privilege. Younger
followers’ comments reveal aspirations to emulate these lifestyles, highlighting
their cross-generational appeal. The article concludes that branded content
functions as a technology of age that makes older women more visible whilst
also commodifying later life while reproducing idealized, classed, and
gendered models of successful aging.

1 Introduction
Whether through veteran film stars walking runways and posing for fashion
photographers, the rise of so-called “granfluencers” (Harvey, 2019), or unknown yet
sophisticated women “of a certain age” acting as models, in recent years the
representation of older women has undeniably been changing. The fashion industry is
a paradigmatic case in this sense. After having been long considered the purview of the
under 35, with its “frenetic and frothy discourses, its images glamorous and – above
all – youthful” (Twigg, 2013, 1), in recent years, fashion has started to open up to
more age-diverse faces and bodies: from so-called ’silver models’ walking the runways
of London and Paris fashion weeks (Eredi, 2025; Hill, 2024), to older celebrities
endorsing fashion brands, like French star Isabelle Huppert becoming brand
ambassador for Balenciaga at age 70, or 1937-born Jane Fonda modeling for Gucci’s
eco-friendly collection in 2022. From a marketing perspective, such a shift can be
traced back to the ongoing expansion of the market segment comprising older
consumers. Known as “silver market” or “graying market”, this group comprises
consumers that are: aged 50 or 55 and older (depending on the country’s average life
expectancy); relatively well-off; and, to marketers, potentially “very attractive and
promising, although still very underdeveloped in terms of product and service
offerings” (Kohlbacher and Herstatt, 2011, vii). In turn, the growing importance of the

This article examines how older women are represented in

fashion branded content featuring older women reproduce,
reject, or reshape dominant narratives around gendered aging.
Italy is an interesting case to examine in this regard: with a
median population age of 48.7 years in 2024, and a share of 65+
people nearing one quarter of the total, Italy ranks as the eldest
country in Europe and the second globally, after Japan. This
evolving landscape presents Italian marketers and media
strategists with unprecedented challenges. On the one hand, the
expansion of an affluent – and gradually more digitally-literate
(ISTAT, 2025) – segment of late-middle-aged and third-aged
consumers create new marketing and business opportunities. On
the other hand, the intergenerational imbalances in the
allocation of financial resources call for a reassessment of
communication strategies on the part of the advertisers, after
long focusing on young demographics to the neglect of mature
consumers (Farinosi and Fortunati, 2020). Whilst Italy’s
mainstream fashion industry has so far been more cautious in
embracing this “greynaissance” (Bøilerehauge, 2020, 24),
grassroot practices of digital storytelling have seen mature
women take an increasingly prominent space (Farinosi and
Fortunati, 2020); by becoming fashion influencers, and/or by
collaborating with younger content creators. An exemplary case
is that of 1930-born Licia Fertz (@liciafertz), aka “Buongiorno
nonna”, whose Instagram profile, managed by the woman’s
nephew and primary caregiver, currently has 352’000 followers
and features a mix of candid and ad-funded posts on fashion,
beauty, and leisure content. Another example is that of
Magnifiche Perennial (@magnificheperennial), the joint digital
moniker of Isabella Marconi and Marilli Amari, two friends in
their sixties, self-describing as having “no expiration date”,
whose 344’000 followers across Instagram and TikTok enjoy
regularly posted content sponsored by fashion, beauty, and
hospitality brands. A third example, which will be extensively
discussed later in this article, is that of Sciuraglam
(@sciuraglam), an amateur Instagram page, turned social media
marketing project and cultural phenomenon, which features
photos of elegantly dressed, upper-middle-class, real-life Milan-
based women (known in the Milanese dialect as sciure) enjoying
the best that the city has to offer, in terms of fashion, food, and
cultural hotspots. Among the brands that sponsored contents on
Sciuraglam’s 427’000 followers-Instagram page there are fashion
powerhouses Gucci, Loro Piana, Bottega Veneta, Manolo
Blahnik, as well as luxury e-commerce platforms Mytheresa,
Vestiaire Collective, and online auction platform Catawiki.
The remainder of the article is organized as follows. Section 2
provides a theory- and data-driven overview of the portrayal of
mature women in screen-based media, which, as Woodward
(1999) observed, has traditionally been characterized by the
duality of invisibility and hypervisibility: invisibility, as will be
discussed shortly, results from the marked underrepresentation
of mature women in the media, while hypervisibility arises from
the stereotypical narratives that characterize their existing
representations. To analyze this phenomenon, the article
mobilizes the concept of “technology of age” (Medina Bañón
and Zecchi, 2020), by arguing that whilst branded content
affords older women new forms of visibility, it also confines
them within the framework of “aspirational aging” (Douglas,
2020), which both feeds on and nurtures inter-generational class
disparities. Section 3 of the article applies this theoretical
approach to the analysis of female granfluencers and their
growing cultural and economic relevance within content
marketing. Section 4 presents the case of the Italian Instagram-
based digital marketing project Sciuraglam, whose storytelling
practices, blurring sponsored and editorial content, heavily rely
on an aspirational view of (female) aging. Finally, Section 5
discusses the results of the case study and offers some
concluding remarks, also in light of the newly emerging
research trajectories.
2 Gendered aging in screen-based
media: theories and numbers
Aging is a multifaceted phenomenon shaped not only by
biological, cognitive, and psychological processes, but also by
social and cultural factors. Since the late 1990s, the cultural turn
that revitalised humanities and social-science perspectives on
identity traits such as gender, “race”, and disability extended to
age studies as well (Twigg and Martin, 2015). The contribution
of feminist theory has been essential in this sense. Early feminist
thinkers like de Beauvoir (1972) and Sontag (1972) are widely
credited for opening the conversation and laying the
foundations of subsequent reflections on the intersection of age
and gender. At the turn of 2000s, a number of seminal works
was published that addressed aging as a gendered construct
(Maierhofer 2003, 2004; Morganroth Gullette, 1997, 2004;
Woodward, 1999). Among the epistemological tenets of these
works, and most of later age studies, is the transcending of
biological determinism, to investigate how culture influences our
gendered (self-)understanding of aging. As Margaret
Morganroth Gullette (2011, 5) put it: “Whatever happens in the
body, and even if nothing happens in the body, aging is a
narrative. Each of us tells her own story”. In this sense, screen-
based media play a crucial role in shaping the images and
narratives around aging, and its implications for gender identity.
As Woodward (1999, xvi) observed in her seminal cultural study
of gendered aging: “Given the virtual obsession of western
culture with youth and with the appearance of the body, given
the very real prospect of frailty in older age, mass culture, which
is a consumer culture, seizes on the vast disparities between
what it presents as two extremes, producing the older female
body as both invisible and hypervisible”. Older women are
invisible because of their structural marginalization from mass
media contents, and, at the same time, they are hypervisible
because, in a culture that privileges youth, the body of a mature
woman, with the outward signs of her age, is all that can be

advertising industry, whilst it is particularly difficult to compare
research findings because of the great variety of methodologies
and media involved, a systematic review has confirmed the
underrepresentation of older people in advertising, and also that
this underrepresentation especially penalizes older women,
despite them being more numerous than men in the general
population (Baumann and de Laat, 2015; Eisend, 2022; Lee
et al., 2007; Ramos Soler and Papí Gálvez, 2012).
As to the ways in which older women are represented on
screen, age studies literature identifies two dominant narrative
and ideological frameworks at play: the “decline narrative”
(Morganroth Gullette, 2004), and its opposite framework of
’successful aging’ (Rowe and Kahn, 1997). The decline narrative,
which, according to its theorist, has been long predominant in
Western cinema and cultural products, frames aging as a
process of inevitable loss: loss of reproductive capacity, of sexual
drive and appeal, of physical and cognitive abilities, and loss of
agency (Morganroth Gullette, 2004). Examples of this type of
narrative include films and other screen-based content that
center on the dramatic experiences of characters living with
Alzheimer disease. But the decline narrative fuels shame and
anxiety not only about old age itself, but also about the outward
signs of the aging process. Entire industrial sectors, and related
media discourses, are postulated on making women fearful not
so much of growing old, but of looking old. This is the case for
most of the ads commissioned by what Susan Douglas calls the
“anti-aging industrial complex” (Douglas, 2020), but also “make
over” television shows (Jermyn, 2015), and celebrity culture
(Jermyn and Holmes, 2015). The recent success of a body
horror movie like The Substance (Coralie Fargeat, 2024) speaks
to the ongoing resonance of social anxieties around female aging,
which form a significant part of the decline narrative. The second
dominant framework informing the narratives around old age is
that of “successful aging” (Rowe and Kahn 1997). Born out the
gerontological field, the paradigm of successful aging champions
a multidimensional idea of the aging process, beyond a purely
biomedical approach. According to John Rowe and Robert Kahn,
aging successfully means: to minimize the risk of physical and
cognitive impairments, by adopting responsible and healthy
lifestyles, as well as to remain active, by continuing to sustain
meaningful personal relationships and productive activities,
through work and community engagement. The paradigm of
successful aging encountered significant success among health
institutions and policymakers, as it promoted a shift in attitude
toward old age, away from an approach based on pity and
dependency, toward one based on the empowerment of
individuals2. Whilst welcomed for overcoming long-held view of
old age as frailty, the paradigm of successful aging has also raised
criticism: for promoting a prescriptive, unrealistic notion of aging;
for implicitly marginalizing those who cannot adhere to that
notion, for example because of a disability; and for reflecting a
neoliberal ideology that overemphasizes individual responsibility
over structural and material conditions (Katz and Calasanti,
2015). Scholars have observed how the paradigm of successful
aging informs a large part of the narratives around older
characters, across film (Dolan, 2013; Swinnen, 2015), television
shows (Sako and Oró-Piqueras, 2023), and advertising (Ylänne,
2015). A study conducted on the portrayal of older people in UK
advertising, for example, has shown the recurrence of “positive”
stereotypes attributable to the paradigm of successful aging: older
individuals are represented either as “Golden Agers”, i.e., enjoying
glamorous lifestyle; as “Perfect Grandparents”, i.e., performing
grandparental duties with cheerful attitudes; as “Copers”, i.e.,
overcoming minor problems thorough the use of the advertised
product; or again, in the case of popular individuals, as “Celebrity
Endorsers”, authoritatively vouching for the quality of the
advertised product (Williams et al., 2010)3. As Ylänne (2015, 372)
observes, “Although the imagery of active and healthy ‘golden
agers’ appears positive, it also promotes idealistic notions of
individual agency in the management of the aging process.”
Whilst seemingly opposite, both the decline narrative and the
successful aging frameworks contribute to othering older
individuals and fueling social anxieties toward the aging process.
In the case of advertising and marketing communications, which,
as mentioned, tend to favor positive stereotypes around aging,
this positivity acquires a distinct aspirational tone that is
instrumental in engaging prospective consumers and driving sales.

the digital sector. Starting from 2020, Italian advertisers’
investments in online advertising have, for the first time in

billion Euros in 2024 against 3.8 billion (UPA-Nielsen, 2025).
Online advertising currently makes for 51% of the whole Italian
advertising market, and a growing part of these investments
goes to influencer marketing: estimated 352 million Euros in
2024, with a 9% increase from 2023 (Montolivo, 2024). Among
the reasons that drive older consumers to use social media,
following celebrities and influencers has increased from 5.8% in
2022 to 8.4% in 2025. For those 55 + followers of social media
influencers, know-how, credibility, and authenticity are the three
most important drivers (ONIM, 2025). In this sense, senior
celebrities and creators emerge as a popular demographic
among older audiences, as they appear to be able to speak to
their peers in a more empathetic, authoritative, and credible way
than younger creators (Ibid.) Such dynamic is typical in this
type of marketing, since, as Hardy observed, “The use of third-
party endorsement to inform and persuade (…) has been turbo-
charged as the vehicle for brand communications by social
media influencers” (Hardy, 2021, 24).
However, the appeal of older celebrities and influencers is not
confined solely to consumers in their own age bracket. In
explaining the decision to partner with Sciuraglam for a series
of co-branded social media posts featuring older women,
Vestiaire Collective, French ecommerce platform specialized in
luxury vintage fashion, said: “They’re always elegantly dressed
and recognizable by their effortless, carefree mood, embodying a
timeless approach to Italian fashion that is at the heart of
Vestiaire Collective”.5 Such notion of older endorsers
embodying an “ageless” or “timeless” elegance echoes in several
commentaries tackling the growing salience of mature celebrities
and granfluencers. For example, with regard to American
clothing brand J.Crew’s 2022 campaign featuring older
celebrities like Diane Keaton, the Business of Fashion magazine
wrote: “The hope was (…) that these women would resonate
across age groups because they embodied the idea of being
classic but in this fresh and modern way.” (Pearl, 2022). Again,
in a piece about older women being “back in vogue for designer
brands”, The Telegraph reports that: “Increasingly, brands are
finding timeless icons may be the best ones to sell them – to
both younger and older shoppers.” (Boland, 2023).
This type of marketing strategy aims to bridge inter-
generational differences by tapping into a symbolic repertoire of
“timeless” values associated to older celebrities - such as
elegance, craftmanship, and authenticity - to which consumers
of all ages can aspire. On social media, older influencers do
seem to exert an attraction on younger audiences. Analytics
gathered from social media marketing platform Modash reveal
that both Licia Fertz and Magnifiche Perennials, the Italian
granfluencers previously mentioned, have an audience of
Instagram followers composed by 50.1% of women aged
between 18 and 34; another share between 32% and 39%
respectively is made of women between 35 and 64 years. Such
gender- and age-breakdown, which mirrors that of Instagram
users in general, finds confirmation in personal accounts by
granfluencers themselves. When asked about her popularity
among younger people, Licia Fertz said in a 2025 interview with
Marie Claire: “The extremes of life—the young and the old—
share the same need to be heard, to have their worth
recognized.” (Antista, 2025). Australian jewelry designer and
fashion influencer Sarah Jane Adams suggests that what drives
younger women to her Instagram profile is “my attitude to life
and daily interactions rather than my clothes since I have always
dressed for myself and not to follow a fashion.” (Farinosi and
Fortunati, 2020: 53). When interviewed about their digital
project Magnifiche perennial, Isabella Marconi and Marilli
Amari mention their wish to be a source of inspiration for
“women of all ages” (Flu Agency, 2022). In a 2018 interview
with The Independent, commenting on her followers being
mostly women aged 25 to 35, American fashion influencer Lyn
Slater said: “The people who support me, follow me, hire me –
they’re all young. (…) We [baby boomers] lived in extraordinary
times. These girls know that, they know what we lived through.
They envy us.” (La Ferla, 2018). In this framework, aging is
represented as a process not to be feared or concealed but
rather embraced as part of a continuum of aspirational living, in
which the dimension of age is coalesced with that of an affluent
socioeconomic class. Furthermore, a communication strategy
based on “timeless” values with cross-generational appeal allows
marketers to avoid engaging with the pronounced material
disparities that characterize older and younger generations in
Italy.6

From a socio-cultural perspective, the growing interest of
brands and marketers in the long-neglected silver market and
the related phenomenon of female granfluencers can be
interpreted in a twofold manner. On the one hand, this is a
welcome corrective action, after years of underrepresentation
and misrepresentation of older female identities in the media.
Providing spaces for mature women to be (self-)represented as
fashionable, active, and inspiring counters both the invisibility
bias (Woodward, 1999) and the decline narrative (Morganroth
Gullette, 2004) that have long affected their presence in
mainstream media. Doing so has also to potential to create
positive models to both older and younger female audiences.
On the other hand, though, attaching this visibility to
commercial practices that leverage older women’s identity as
marketing value feeds into the hypervisibility bias (Woodward,
1999)7. Such representative and promotional strategies also
speak to the rise of a specific articulation of the successful
aging paradigm (Rowe and Kahn, 1997), that of “aspirational
aging” (Douglas, 2020). American feminist scholar and cultural
critic Susan Douglas describes aspirational aging as “a media-
crafted, marketing-created zeitgeist (…) [that] draws from a
decade of market research (…) about how baby boomers see
themselves as very different from the retirees of yore and the
stereotypes that encased them.” Douglas acknowledges the
twofold nature of aspirational aging, which “recognizes that
many older people are active in the world, feel younger than
their age, and hate aging stereotypes, and then seeks to exploit
this positive sensibility in ways that can commodify, and
distort, what we need and how we are supposed to see
ourselves.” (Ibid.) In such framework, inspirational content
acquires normative undertones that, because they are
predicated on consumerism, end up undermining the very
message of empowerment they purport to endorse. Moreover,
by showcasing glamorous lifestyles that gloss over
intersectional differences, contents conveyed by female
granfluencers like the ones previously mentioned operate as
powerful technologies of age that affect younger women too.
The next section illustrates and analyzes these dynamics in
greater detail, by looking at a specific Italian Instagram profile,
that of Sciuraglam.
4 Analysis of Sciuraglam’s content
marketing practices
This section provides the case study analysis of the Italian
Instagram-based digital marketing project Sciuraglam. Section
4.1. presents the aim, method, and sample for the analysis are
presented, then Section 4.2. illustrates the main findings. The
selection of Sciuraglam as a case study is motivated by it
being a particularly influential case within Italy’s
bourgeoning landscape of “granfluencer” marketing. Compared
to the average performance of influencers in the same
category8, Sciuraglam displays a below-the-average rate of fake
followers (13,6%) and an above-the-average engagement rate of
3,39%, suggesting a lively and loyal community. Its audience is
mostly made of women (81,6%) in the age segment 25–34
(35,9%); female followers in the 18–24 segment constitute 17,8%
of its audience, tailed by a 16,7% in the 35–44 age group. Female
followers between 45 and 64 years old are 9% of the total.9 From
a geographical point of view, the bulk of the profile’s audience is
located in Italy (74%) and more specifically in the area around
the Northern city of Milan (23%). This is hardly surprising, since
the profile’s posted contents – and its very name – are rooted
in the Milanese culture and vernacular. The term sciura comes
from the Milanese, and more broadly the Lombardy Region’s
dialect, and it means signora, which is the traditional Italian
polite form of address for married women.10 Whilst originally the
term applied to all mature women, over time it has come to
acquire an additional nuance that signifies an older woman of
higher socioeconomic status and refined taste. In contemporary
Italy, the sciura has become a sort of archetypical figure
embodying some of the cultural stereotypes generally associated
to the city of Milan and to its residents: fashionable, affluent, and
a bit aloof. Interestingly, the suffix -glam, which reinforces the
imagery of glamour and elegance associated to the contemporary
Milanese sciura, was chosen as a replacement of the original
name of the profile, Sciuragram, following a cease-and-desist
letter sent in 2018 by the Meta-owned platform, complaining
about the assonance with Instagram (Gandolfi, 2025). Sciuraglam
was started in December 2016 by 1993-born Angelo Petrocelli, a
then-medical student from a small town in the Southern Region
of Basilicata, who had moved to Milan to attend university. The
very first photo posted on Sciuraglam’s profile depicts a white-
haired lady wrapped in a fur coat, photographed from the back
while standing in front of a wall of books11. The capture reads:
“Fur and culture”, followed by the nail polish emoji. Geo-
localization informs the followers that the photo was taken at the
(then-newly inaugurated and architecturally impressive)
headquarters of the Giangiacomo Feltrinelli Foundation, a long-
standing cultural institution in Milan. For two years, Sciuraglam
remained purely a passion project, born out of the desire to
chronicle the experiences of a young student from a provincial
background discovering new urban lifestyles. Even in its initial
phase, though, Sciuraglam carried a distinct aspirational flare, as
it emerges from an interview that its creator gave in 2018 to the
British Vogue magazine: “I used to capture two women at a time,
and say [to my friends], ‘I wish we were them, because they are

4.1 Aim, method, and sample of the analysis
The aim of the analysis presented in this article is to explore
how Sciuraglam’s branded content reproduces, rejects, or
reshapes dominant narratives around older women, particularly
in terms of the implications for the cross-generational
communication that characterizes the Instagram platform. In
this sense, the analysis particularly aimed at illuminating how
Sciuraglam’s posts – which include a mix of paid for and non-
paid for videos and photos – incorporate discursive elements
that can be ascribed to the paradigm of aspirational aging. To
do so, a close reading of a sample of posts was performed that
looked at the content of the posts as well as their visual framing
and, when present, sound elements. In order to better
understand how its followers make sense of and engage with
Sciuraglam’s storytelling practices, a secondary interpretive layer
was considered, by examining a selection of comments posted
by users under each close-read post. Given the exploratory
nature of this study, which was conducted using a qualitative
methodology that prioritizes meaning-making processes over
statistical representativeness, the posts and comments to analyze
were selected based on a combination of top-performance and
meaning saturation criteria (Hennink et al., 2017). A close
reading of Sciuraglam’s six most popular posts was conducted,
based on the analytics gathered from social media marketing
platform Modash.15 For each of them, user comments were
examined, according to the order of publication set by the
platform, until they no longer suggested new interpretive cues,
which happened for all the selected posts between 30 and 40
comments. All the content and posts published on the
Sciuraglam profile are public and visible to both followers and
non-followers. The following pages provide a concise description
of each of the six top-performing posts selected for analysis.
These descriptions highlight key elements that will be referenced
in the following section, devoted to the presentation of the
main findings.

4.2 Findings
The qualitative analysis of Sciuraglam’s most successful posts
revealed that half of the sampled contents explicitly mentions
brands in the fashion and hospitality sectors (Posts #1, #4 and
#5), while the other half does not disclose branded
collaborations, although in two cases (Posts #2 and #3) it does
feature high-fashion products that are easily recognizable to the
profile’s followers. This finding seems to be consistent with
Sciuraglam’s overall editorial strategy that, according to the
analytics retrieved from Modash, includes a 52,6% of posts
labeled with either the #ad or the #adv hashtag. According to

brands, the choice of locations for filming and photoshoots, the
background music, the visual elements associated to the
portrayed women’s stylish and visibly expensive outfits, as well

storytelling that fits perfectly within the paradigm of aspirational
aging described by Douglas (2020).

by Sciuraglam’s followers under the analyzed posts. The average
number of comments left by followers under the posts is 1700:
the post with the most comments is the video we titled “Kiko

sentiment towards the content: hearts and hearty eyes emojis,
fire emojis, clapping and hands-up emojis. The verbal comments
unequivocally show that Sciuraglam’s followers picked up on the
aspirational view of aging that the posts convey. From a
thematic perspective, users express their aspirational drive
primarily in relation to: (a) the age of the women depicted; (b)

those created by Sciuraglam and other popular granfluencers –
adds a distinctly aspirational tone to this identity construction
dynamic, a tone that plays a key role in advancing the
marketing goals behind their creation. The results of the analysis
conducted on the thematic and visual elements present in
Sciuraglam’s six most popular posts, as well as on a selection of
comments posted by its users, revealed representations of female
aging with a distinctly aspirational tone. This finding aligns with
previous research conducted on the representation of aging in
television and print advertising. The recurring practice observed
among younger users to tag other users of their same age to
express admiration for the look of the sciure and the lifestyle
associated with them reinforces the cross-generational
effectiveness of the aspirational aging paradigm. In this sense, in
their comments, the Sciuraglam followers appear to aspire as
much to the economic capital manifested through the high-
fashion outfits depicted, as to the social and affective capital
embedded in the representation of female friendship in later life.
This type of reactions speak to the strong marketing value
carried by branded content on social media, which relies
precisely on “the blurring of advertising with editorial” (Hardy,
2021, 7) and the consumers’ engagement elicited by it. In the
case of Sciuraglam, the class difference embedded in the
aspirational aging narrative is rarely acknowledged in the
followers’ comments. This aspect, we argue, is a direct effect of
the deliberate blurring of branded and unbranded content that
influencer marketing relies on.
In this sense, branded content acts as a powerful “technology
of age” (Medina Bañón and Zecchi, 2020, 252), shaping social
media users’ idea of how (their own) aging should look like.
The notion of technology of age, originally developed by
Medina Bañón and Zecchi24 in relation to cinema and its
traditional catering to the “youthful male gaze” (Ibid. 252) can
be fruitfully extended to other visuality-based media, including
advertising and content marketing. In the case of Sciuraglam
and other Instagram influencers mentioned in this article, the
gendered nature of their aspirational narrative is apparent. As
previously argued, the rise of female granfluencers has been
viewed by some authors and commentators as a positive
phenomenon that counteracts the traditional invisibility bias
affecting older women in mainstream media. However, the
forms of digital storytelling about and from female
granfluencers, and the ways that this is framed in media
interviews, appear to emphasize the age dimension in ways
that, whilst challenging older women’s invisibility, end up fall
into into the hyper-visibility bias described by Woodward
(1999). Celebrating older women because of their age seems
just as othering a trend as celebrating them in spite of their
age. This is especially the case for contents like those published
by the Sciuraglam profile, whose aspirational narratives carry
strong – yet never explicitly acknowledged – class-based
undertones. By playing into the stereotypical traits associated
to the city of Milan, and to its sciure, the profile offers a
representation of older women that glosses over the material
conditions that enable or hinder younger followers from
attaining that model: an above-the-average income, or the
access to generational wealth. In doing so, the narrative offered
by Sciuraglam fulfils the fortunate – yet heavily criticized –
paradigm of successful aging (Rowe and Kahn, 1997), in that it
upholds a prescriptive, mostly unrealistic view of aging, one
that ignores social and individual differences. In the case of
Sciuraglam and other Instagram-based branded content, such
aspirational representations are all the more powerful (hence
potentially persuasive) because they reach a demographic
segment of the Italian population – that of young Millennials
and older “Gen-Zers” – that is particularly affected by the
decline in purchasing power and the unequal concentration of
wealth among generations. Thus, the examples of Italian
branded content presented here appear to reproduce, rather
than oppose dominant narratives about gendered aging. As
Baumann and de Laat wrote: “Advertisers typically employ
aspirational marketing strategies, linking their goods and
services to people consumers are assumed to aspire to be, or to
qualities that consumers are assumed to aspire to possess.”
(Baumann and de Laat, 2015, 14). In the case of Sciuraglam,
the aspirational tone of the communication is particularly
pronounced, as it is also evidenced by the high standing of the
brands featured in it. This aspect is consistent with a more
general tendency of fashion brands to frame older women’s
involvement in marketing communications in terms of
“timelessness” and “agelessness”, thus keeping a distance from
any of possible negative stereotypes associated to aging. The
visibility that branded content grant to older women is to a
large extent contingent on them embodying a mostly
unattainable model of aging. In this sense, it seems that the
binarism between “invisibility” and “hyper-visibility”, and
between a “declining” and a “successful” view of aging is still
affecting mainstream representations of gendered aging. The
case study of Sciuraglam has also shown that it is especially
young women who embrace the aspirational discourse about
aging, projecting themselves 40, 50 years into the future,
without seemingly being mindful of the socioeconomic gap
between their embodied and their desired selves. Whether
these attitudes stem from performative behaviors on the part
of the younger users (common on social media, as discussed,
among other, by Boyd, 2014), or from acritical adherence to
the models proposed, the implications that these attitudes have
for the ways in which female aging is constructed and
perceived cannot be underestimated and call for
additional investigation.
This aimed to illuminating an under-scrutinized area in
Italian media studies, i.e., how the representation of older
women is shaped by branded content and the impact that such
representation has especially on younger women. In this sense,
the exploratory nature of the article, and the case-study
approach it applies, call for further research to be conducted on
this and related topics. For instance, further qualitative analysis
could explore the role that different types of intermediaries may
have in affecting the representation of older women: younger
