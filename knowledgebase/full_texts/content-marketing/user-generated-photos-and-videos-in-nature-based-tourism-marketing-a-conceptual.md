# User-generated photos and videos in nature-based tourism marketing: a conceptual model for harnessing their impact on social media

User-generated photos and videos in nature-based tourism
marketing: a conceptual model for harnessing their impact on
social media
Wiebke Finklera, Lloyd S. Davisb and Lei Zhub
aDepartment of Marketing, University of Otago, Dunedin, New Zealand; bCentre for Science Communication,
University of Otago, Dunedin, New Zealand
ABSTRACT
User-generated photos and videos on social media are changing
the values associated with a tourism destination in ways that do not
necessarily align with those promoted by destination marketing.
Focusing on nature-based tourism, we identify the affective ele-
ments of user-generated photos (colour, charisma, storytelling) and
videos (trust, authenticity, storytelling) that determine their impact
on social media. These elements are derived from a re-analysis of
empirical studies: a photograph-based Q methodology and choice-
based conjoint analysis conducted with visitors to Chinese national
parks, and a survey of viewers responding to a whale-watching
video. We incorporate these findings into a conceptual model of
how social media influences the co-creation of tourism imaginaries,
capturing the interaction between tourist-generated content and
destination marketing in shaping a destination’s collective imagin-
ary. The model extends existing theories of destination image for-
mation, brand co-creation, narrative transportation and customer
engagement, and yields four testable propositions for future
research. We discuss implications for destination marketing, includ-
ing how these affective elements can inform brand positioning,
audience targeting and campaign design, as well as how the
same mechanisms can drive negatively-valenced imaginaries and
divergent imaginaries across different stakeholder groups.

1. Introduction
Traditionally, tourism literature has considered communication mostly as communicating
to tourists or potential tourists (Finkler & L. S. Davis, 2021), typically to facilitate and
encourage tourism. There is also communication from tourists via direct feedback, surveys
and, more recently, online reviews (e.g. text, photographs and videos posted and shared
on platforms such as TripAdvisor). The dissemination of such tourist-generated materials
is vital for nature-based tourism, in particular, which focuses mainly on outdoor recreation
and wildlife viewing in natural areas such as national parks (da Mota & Pickering, 2020).

This emphasis on nature-based settings is not to suggest that user-generated imagery
is unimportant for other forms of tourism. A growing body of work shows that tourist- and
resident-posted photographs also shape the image of urban and cultural destinations,
even informing public space design and urban redevelopment, e.g. Saiz et al. (2018). What
differs for nature-based tourism is that the destination’s value rests overwhelmingly on
non-substitutable natural attributes – a particular landscape, species or ecosystem – that
cannot be relocated or re-staged as an urban streetscape can. Because these attributes
cannot be altered by planners or marketers to fit a desired image, the destination’s image
formation depends disproportionately on how these fixed qualities are captured, framed
and shared by visitors themselves. It is this constraint that underlies our focus on nature-
based tourism.
The tourist destination image refers to how tourists perceive the attributes and
characteristics of a particular place (da Costa Carvalho et al., 2015), and this has been
considered a vital part of tourism planning for potential tourists and destination market-
ing by tourism stakeholders such as tourism marketers, local governments and national
park managers.
However, it is not only the objective attributes that create the image of a tourism
destination, but also the subjective, emotional perceptions that influence it (da Costa
Carvalho et al., 2015) – a largely neglected form of communication between the object of
the tourism activity and the participant. This affective element of the tourism experience
colours the tourist’s perceptions (Finkler & Davis, 2021) and affects them in measurable
ways that determine perceived knowledge, self-worth, satisfaction and identity integral to
tourists’ perceptions of a place and the longevity of that perception (Salazar, 2012;
Youssef et al., 2019).
1.1. Nature-based tourism
Natural areas are increasingly popular destinations, with visits exceeding 8 billion per year
(Balmford et al., 2015). Such nature-based tourism includes wildlife viewing (e.g. bird
watching and whale watching), scenic viewing and outdoor recreation in natural areas,
helping to create a specific destination image distinct from other tourism activities
(Balmford et al., 2009; Tasci et al., 2007). Recent research demonstrates that Instagram
imagery mediates visitors’ affective relationships to protected natural areas (Molin et al.,
2025).
1.2. Destination images
The perceived image of an object is ‘the set of meanings by which an object is known and
through which people describe, remember and relate to it’ (Chon, 1990); in tourism, the
destination may be considered the object. Definitions evolved from a vague list of
attributes toward functional, psychological and holistic characteristics (Echtner &
Ritchie, 1991). Most now acknowledge that a destination image includes tourists’ personal
thoughts, interests, experiences and culture backgrounds, not just objective attributes (da
Costa Carvalho et al., 2015).

The importance of the destination image has been studied for how it affects tourist
behaviour and how it may be influenced by tourism marketing (Moustaka &
Constantoglou, 2021). The destination image is closely related to the image potential
tourists perceive before visiting and is a major factor in destination selection (da Costa
Carvalho et al., 2015).
Jenkins (2003) described the relationship between the destination image and iconic
local attractions using a circle of representation model in which tourists and marketers
photograph icons, the images are disseminated, and then perceived by others who visit
and photograph the attractions afresh. The model reflected the importance of tourist-
generated photographs and foreshadowed the potential of social media to link the
individual level and social levels of communication.
1.3. Tourism imaginaries
The destination image of a place does not necessarily align with what may be called the
tourism imaginary, which is sourced not only from the collective set of values put on
a place by tourists and marketers, but also from non-tourism-related aspects of commu-
nities such as demographics and political considerations (Salazar, 2012; Salazar & Graburn,
2014).
Until recently, the means for co-creating such tourism-based social imaginaries was
limited by expensive channels readily controlled by destination marketers and adver-
tisers, so the ‘collective’ destination image for nature-based tourism was shaped
largely by marketers, forming an associated ‘ideology’ of the place or tourism
experience.
The notion of imaginaries, as constructs that represent a collective set of values and
symbols belonging to a social group, has become a popular focus in anthropological and
sociological studies (Salazar, 2012). Imaginaries are what happens to ideologies as they
become embedded in the dominant common-sense about a place (Steger & James, 2013)
and, thereby, the collective psyche of potential visitors (Gravari-Barbas & Graburn, 2016).
1.4. The construction of tourism imaginaries in the digital age
Traditionally, both the destination image and the collective tourism imaginary were
influenced by available communication channels such as brochures, newspapers, maga-
zines, television and film (Salazar & Graburn, 2014). To date, the concept of a tourism
imaginary has often been applied from an anthropological perspective, such as indigen-
ous impressions of a place displayed in photographs (Kelly, 2020). However, tourism
imaginaries are much broader than that: they are the collective set of values of a place or
activity. They are derived from both collective tourism experiences and social interaction,
although there is no common definition of tourism imaginaries, with the term being
applied to different imaginative phenomena (Leite, 2014).
As an aspect of human imagination, imaginaries are, for all intents and purposes,
invisible; yet they do crystallize in material form (Leite, 2014), with user-generated photos
and videos being the two forms of media that manifest these tourism imaginaries.
Tourism imaginaries derived from such imagery are co-created social representations of
tourism experiences that involve three phases: construction of the imagery, sharing of the

The potential effect of the internet and social media on a destination image and, by

Nixon et al. (2017) found that photos posted on Instagram significantly influenced the
destination image for nature-based tourism, with user-generated photos more influential
than those posted by destination marketing organisations, shaping tourists’ online desti-
nation image and subsequent engagement more than institutional content (Bernkopf &
Nixon, 2019; Guerreiro et al., 2024).
The popularity of user-generated content, therefore, provides an opportunity to
observe its effects in the co-creation of tourism imaginaries and to assess implications
for conventional tourism marketing of nature-based tourism.
1.5. User-generated photos
The advent of social media has transformed the internet over the last decade or two into
a user-generated space. Due to the ease of access to social media platforms such as
Instagram and the ubiquity of smartphones, tourists have become co-creators of tourism
imaginaries through being part of an online community (Buhalis & Foerste, 2015). This is
particularly so for nature-based tourism, whose appealing subjects (e.g. landscapes, fauna,
flora) encourage the generation of imagery reflecting tourists’ emotions, beliefs and
preferences about their travel experiences (Conti & Lexhagen, 2020) that, when shared
online, contribute to the formation of co-created values that make up the tourism
imaginary, which are continuously updated as meanings and emotions attached to
tourism places evolve (Yanchin, 2018).
Instagram has over three billion active monthly users uploading more than 100 million
photos and videos that generate 4.2 billion likes per day (Bazian, 2026). Conti and
Lexhagen (2020) analysed Instagram photos of tourists taken at three national parks in
Sweden. They found that the photos contained multisensory cues and emotions about
the experience of being in the national parks. Furthermore, the act of sharing the photos
was part of the value creation, providing access to ‘value tribes’ – that is, the community
that co-creates the tourism imaginary – through active engagement with an audience.
Kelly (2020) analysed photos taken by indigenous activists and their supporters at
a beach in Broome, Australia and contrasted them with the images used by destination
marketers. Their visual representations reframed the orthodox destination image with an
alternative narrative more representative of the social grouping creating the imaginary.
Indeed, the relevance of photos to the creation of a tourism imaginary will depend upon
the social grouping to which they are communicated. For example, people with only
a moderate or no interest in birds react differently to photographs of birds compared to
specialised bird watchers (Zhu et al., 2021a).

1.6. User-generated videos
Of all the forms of online media, video has experienced the most explosive growth in
recent times, reflecting a dramatic change in the ease and cost of production (especially
through the video capabilities of mobile smartphones) and the ease of distribution by
uploading videos to social media platforms such as YouTube, Facebook, TikTok and Vimeo
(L. S. Davis & León, 2018). On YouTube, alone, over 500 hours of video are uploaded
every minute, with over a billion hours of videos viewed daily by its 2.7 billion users (SQ
Magazine [SQ], 2026). Among people under 30, video on social media has become their
dominant source of information and entertainment, and is increasingly important for
other age groups (L. S. Davis et al., 2020).
An analysis of 826 YouTube videos over 12 months found that the fastest growing,
most popular segment consisted of user-generated content (L. S. Davis & León, 2018).
What makes them resonate with viewers is their focus on having individuals express their
personal narratives, with the emphasis being placed on the personal and emotional
qualities of the video rather than its production values (Finkler, 2017). Short-form videos
have become the dominant format for user-generated travel content, shaping destination
choice and visit intention among travellers (Fang et al., 2023).
This raises RQ2:

ism popular on social media and, therefore, likely to have a disproportionate effect on the

1.7. Anchoring the model in marketing theory
The tourism-imaginary model developed here is not merely descriptive; it extends and in
places challenges four streams of marketing and tourism theory that have so far largely
treated destination image, brand meaning, persuasion and engagement as separate
processes.
First, classical models of destination image formation (Echtner & Ritchie, 1991; Tasci
et al., 2007) treat the image as an accumulation of attributes and impressions held by an
individual tourist, typically formed through marketer-controlled sources. Our model
instead treats the destination’s affective image as an emergent, socially co-created
property arising from the interaction between user-generated and marketer-generated
content, reframing image formation as a co-creation process (Buhalis & Foerste, 2015),
extended here by specifying the affective mechanisms – colour, charisma and storytelling

for photos; trust, authenticity and storytelling for videos – through which co-creation
occurs.
Second, our findings speak directly to brand meaning co-creation and consumer-based
brand equity (Merz et al., 2009), where brand associations are increasingly built through
consumer-generated rather than firm-generated content (Santiago et al., 2022). Where
that literature has generally treated user-generated content as a single, undifferentiated
input, our results decompose it into specific, measurable attributes a destination brand
can encourage, monitor or counter: meaning accrues not from the volume of user-
generated content but from the extent to which it exhibits these affective elements.
Third, storytelling, the affective element common to both photographs and videos,
connects the model to narrative transportation theory (Green, 2004), which holds that
narratively structured content persuades by ‘transporting’ audiences into the story world.
Narrative transportation reliably increases persuasion, identification and behavioural
intention across marketing contexts (Thomas & Grigsby, 2024). Our contribution is to
show the relevance of this in typically unscripted user-generated content for nature-
based tourism specifically.
Fourth, the model bears on customer engagement theory (Hollebeek et al., 2022): the
interactivity of liking, sharing and commenting on user-generated content is
a behavioural manifestation of engagement, while storytelling, trust and authenticity
extend an emotional-engagement account of why some content elicits more of it.
2. Methods and aims
To answer these research questions, we interrogate our own visual research that has
analysed (1) the effects of photos on tourists in national parks, and (2) the effects of whale
watching videos on potential tourists. Detailed methodologies for these two studies are
contained in Zhu (2019) and Finkler et al. (2019), respectively.
Consistent with a conceptual, theory-building contribution (MacInnis, 2011), our
approach re-analyses and integrates findings from completed, published studies to
build a novel theoretical model that can subsequently be tested, establishing well-
specified constructs and propositions prior to a particular empirical test.
We use the findings from these two studies to identify the affective roles that imagery
plays in defining tourism imaginaries, create a conceptual model of how user-generated
photos and videos influence those imaginaries and discussing why tourism marketing
should be influenced by these co-created elements rather than trying to influence them.
Our specific aims are to:
(i) Describe how tourism imaginaries are co-created and manifested through user-
generated photos and videos on social media,
(ii) Develop a conceptual model of how the affective communication from tourism
experiences is manifested in the user-generated photos and videos of tourists and
how these, when seen through the lens of social media, help influence the co-
creation of tourism imaginaries associated with nature-based tourism and
(iii) Provide a practical guide for how destination marketing can leverage the char-
acteristics of user-generated photos and videos to co-create destination images
and, ultimately, the form of tourism imaginaries.

2.1. Method to assess the aesthetic appeal of photos for nature-based tourism
The aesthetics of photos can be evaluated by human observers using attributes such as
sharpness, exposure, composition and colouration, but such assessments lack repeatabil-
ity, being skewed by subjective interpretations (Zhu, 2019). By contrast, computational
assessments rely on computer programmes, such as Acquine, which compare measurable
elements of a photo to a database of characteristics from tens of thousands of crowd-
rated photos, mimicking the act of ‘liking’ that occurs on social media. Given the large
sample size used for ‘training’ the algorithm, it can more accurately predict the aesthetic
appeal of unrated photographs (Datta & Wang, 2010).
Using a photograph-based Q methodology (Dewar et al., 2007), 36 tourists visiting XiXi
National Wetland Park were asked to sort 30 photographs of landscapes and birds based
upon their preferences and give explanations for them (Zhu et al., 2021b). As Q methods
require diverse participants, to avoid bias given 60% of the photographs were of birds
(Maple et al., 2010), similar numbers were recruited from park visitors across three levels of
interest in birds (No Interest, General Interest, Serious Interest), with some participants in
the latter group coming from the Zhejiang Wild Bird Society who had visited the park in
the previous six months. Interviews occurred between May–July 2017; apriori, the aes-
thetics appeal score for the photographs was determined by the computer algorithm
Acquine (Zhu, 2019).
A follow-up study was carried out at China’s Xishuangbanna Rainforest National Park
using choice-based conjoint analysis (Orme, 2010) – used frequently in marketing to test
how product attributes influence customer preference (Jervis et al., 2014) – to test the
influence of specific visual attributes (colour, sharpness, exposure and behaviour) of
photographs of four bird species on the preferences of 303 visitors (Zhu, 2019).
2.2. Method to assess the aesthetic appeal of videos for nature-based tourism
Finkler and León (2019) set out a methodology and visual rhetoric for interrogating the
effect of a video on potential nature-based tourists. Here, a 2 minute 29 second video
about whale watching was constructed using their SciCommercial format, a modified
form of Shimp and Andrews’s (2013) SUCCES model for marketing, showing authentic
human reactions of actual tourists viewing whales. The video was shared via Facebook,
with viewers directed to watch it and answer a survey (1698 respondents) (Finkler et al.,
2019).
While the SciCommercial video format and its underlying visual-rhetoric framework
were developed by Finkler and León (2019), our methodological contribution is to bring
that framework together with the Q methodology photo-based protocol used from the

3. Results and discussion
3.1. User-generated nature-based tourism photos
Images are integral to tourism, but little research has analysed tourism photos using visual
methodologies (Finkler, 2017).
Factor analysis showed that the visual quality of photographs of landscapes and birds
in XiXi National Wetland Park was the most important factor determining tourists’
preferences, and these corresponded closely with the aesthetics appeal scores deter-
mined by the computer algorithm Acquine (Zhu, 2019). This permits us to identify the
attributes of photos that affected their aesthetic appeal.
Factor analysis of the 30-photograph rating exercise yielded four factors accounting for
71% of total variance, with the first factor – loading most strongly on colour saturation and
subject charisma – accounting for the largest share (38%) of explained variance (Zhu et al.,
2021b). Tourists’ rankings closely matched the Acquine-derived aesthetic scores: the six top-
ranked photographs on Factor 1 had a mean score of 8.8 and included Acquine’s top four
photographs; the six bottom-ranked had a mean score of only 4.7, all classified as poor quality
by Acquine (Zhu et al., 2021b). This indicates the computationally derived aesthetic score is
a reasonable, scalable proxy for tourists’ affective responses to an image, therefore, usable as
a low-cost screening tool by marketers seeking content likely to resonate on social media.
Of the 303 visitors surveyed in Xishuangbanna Rainforest National Park, 78.5% were
18–44 years old, 54.1% were females, and 70.3% had a university education. Beyond
image sharpness, the conjoint analysis revealed the attributes determining likeability of
photographs were colour, species (a corollary of charisma) and behaviour (an element of
storytelling) (Zhu, 2019).
The key elements, then, that influenced the aesthetic appeal of the photographs
shown to tourists in XiXi National Wetland Park and Xishuangbanna Rainforest National
Park were colour, charisma and storytelling.
3.1.1. Colour
Interviews revealed that, beyond needing to be in focus, the most important characteristic
determining the visual appeal of photographs was their colour (Zhu, 2019) associated
with colourful subjects, consistent with evidence that Instagram photographs with
brighter and more saturated colours receive more ‘likes’ (Yu et al., 2020). A survey of
1,216 visitors to XiXi National Wetland Park found similarly that saturated colours influ-
enced the attractiveness of signage including such imagery (Zhu, 2019).
3.1.2. Charisma
The other main factor that influenced the appeal of photographs was the subject itself (Zhu,
2019), with visitors preferring specific landscapes or birds – what we call the charismatic
character of the subject. Charismatic landscapes – a term applied by extension from its usual

association with humans and animals – are those that affect the viewer at an emotional
level, the same affective communication that helps fashion the tourism imaginary.
Promoting a romanticized view of charismatic landscapes and animals can be an
effective tactic for nature-based destination marketing, as such photos evoke feelings
and desires for a connectedness to nature, which is doubtlessly rooted in our psychology
(Liu et al., 2019). Photographs of charismatic animals, especially, evoke strong emotions
and appeal to us (Finkler & S. Davis, 2021). This is consistent with content analyses
showing that visual attributes most associated with positive audience responses to wild-
life imagery (colour, charisma) are largely platform-independent (Shaw et al., 2022).
3.1.3. Storytelling
The other aspect that significantly influenced tourists’ preferences for photos was story-
telling: photos of birds showing a behaviour, action or relationship to the landscape were
more affecting than simple portrait photographs (Zhu, 2019).
Although stories are widely accepted as a core part of the tourism experience
and marketing, little attention has been paid to them in tourism research. Yet,
tourism imaginaries are constructed through the ‘intangible practices of storytell -
ing’ as depicted in photos (Hannam & Ryan, 2019, p. 436), which manifest –
through sharing, liking and commenting – as part of the collective co-created
tourism imaginary.
User-generated photography records moments of time and their associated
stories that might otherwise be lost (Hannam & Ryan, 2019), capturing the authen -
ticity of tourism experiences that affords them meaning: we authenticate such
photographic stories through our emotional responses to them, and photographs
that capture authenticity allow others to experience it vicariously, generating
emotional reactions that, through social media, contribute to the co-creation of
the tourism imaginary.
3.2. User-generated nature-based tourism videos
Storytelling and authenticity, influential in user-generated photos, are likely to be
similarly influential in user-generated videos, given that narrative structure and
authenticity are known to influence the appeal of online videos, with elements relating
to trust and authenticity found to elicit favourable audience responses (L. S. Davis &
León, 2018; Finkler et al., 2019). In the video about whale watching, more than half the
1698 respondents said they were ‘likely’ or ‘very likely’ to share the video on social
media (Finkler & León, 2019). It is telling that the video altered tourists’ behavioral
intentions, yet only 35% of respondents considered it ‘authoritative’. By contrast,
approximately two-thirds of the viewers found the whale watching video to be
‘emotional’ (Finkler & León, 2019), which speaks to the affective element of real
tourism experiences.
Videos that elicit emotions and reactions will be those that most influence the co-
creation of the tourism imaginary by disproportionately encouraging more sharing,
hashtagging and commenting on the videos, consistent with recent research on tourist
attitudes and intentions derived from short-form videos (Qiu et al., 2024).

The implications for destination marketing are clear: influencing tourists via
short-form on-demand videos requires attention to the elements that make user-
generated content of authentic tourism experiences so successful and influential.
This contrasts with the traditional videos used in destination marketing, which are
characterized by their slick production values, being authoritative, and their lack of
storytelling (Alegro & Turnšek, 2021). The key features of user-generated videos
that we identified as most effective at influencing potential tourists were trust,
authenticity and storytelling.
3.2.1. Trust
Over 93% of viewers rated the whale watching video as ‘believable’ (i.e. trust-
worthy) (Finkler et al., 2019). Effective communication needs to be believable, fair,
unbiased and truthful (Shimp & Andrews, 2013), but trust is also influenced by
likeability, which is why successful user-generated content depends upon
a presenter’s likeability: popular presenters – Social Media Influencers – act as
‘opinion leaders’ whose influence depends primarily on perceived trustworthiness
(Magno & Cassia, 2018).
3.2.2. Authenticity
The whale watching video showed real emotional responses of actual tourists watching
whales and over 90% of respondents rated it as ‘real’ (i.e. authentic) (Finkler & León, 2019).
Authenticity is related to trust: videos capturing people being themselves are more
authentic and generate more trust (Voltz & Grobe, 2012). This is consistent with findings
that authenticity – rather than production polish – drive viral engagement with short-
form video (Chu et al., 2022).
Authenticity equates to honesty, a crucial element in the viral distribution of online
content and any campaign involving online video should include a strategy to make
people care through trust, authenticity and storytelling.
3.2.3. Storytelling
Nearly two-thirds of those viewing the whale watching video (63.6%) described it as
telling a story (Finkler & León, 2019). Storytelling is even more important for effective
communication when using videos than for photographs. Humans are strongly pre-
disposed for narrative structures, which implicitly make us ‘care’ by showing something
is at stake: viewers identify with characters and feel empathy, increasing their likelihood of
changing their attitudes, beliefs and behaviour (De Graaf et al., 2012). Stories in YouTube
videos also enhance information recall compared to videos devoid of storytelling
(L. S. Davis et al., 2020).
Storytelling, then, has the power to influence beliefs, attitudes and behaviour:
a characteristic termed narrative persuasion (De Graaf et al., 2012). On social media
platforms, user-generated short-form videos that exude trust and authenticity are likely
to be especially effective, therefore, when they also tell a story (Finkler, 2017). This has led
to a resurgence in storytelling as a destination-branding mechanism on social media
(Phung et al., 2025).

3.3. Conceptual model for the co-creation of tourism imaginaries
Sheungting Lo and McKercher (2016, p. 233) presented a conceptual model for how
tourism, photos and social media interact to produce an imaginary, but their model
proposes a deceptive presentation of the tourist’s self, not the co-creation of destination
values, and omits destination marketing entirely.
Iordanova (2015, p. 49) went a step further, defining the destination image as
a construct of impressions, beliefs and feelings shaped by an individual’s sociodemo-
graphic and psychological characteristics, but this too focused on the individual rather
than a collective, socially co-created set of values.
The framework (Figure 2) upon which our model is based has the tourism
imaginary associated with a nature-based tourism destination or activity as being
partly co-created by the interaction that occurs on social media between tourists
and other members of the public (i.e. potential tourists) through the distribution,
liking, sharing and commenting of user-generated photos and videos (s). The
tourism imaginary is also shaped by input from destination marketing, which elicits
interactivity with tourists and other members of the public (e). That is: s + e = t,
where t is the co-created tourism imaginary associated with a tourism destination
or activity.
These variables share similarities with the PESO model of marketing (Luttrell,

3.4. Implications for destination marketing
As the image of a place is now so dominated by the sharing of photographs and videos on
social media, destination marketers should observe how user-generated content contri-
butes to the tourism imaginary, rather than trying to create the destination image in
isolation. Metrics from platforms such as Instagram, Facebook and YouTube can help
identify which type of user-generated content resonate most strongly with viewers and
should be amplified.
However, such metrics provide only after-the-fact insight into the collective tourism
imaginary. Tourism imaginaries are constantly evolving (Leite, 2014), so destination
marketers should try, where possible, to get ahead of trends on social media.
Beyond monitoring and mimicking these affective elements, our model carries impli-
cations for core strategic decisions. For positioning, destination marketing organisations
(DMOs) can use the dominant affective elements in user-generated content as an evi-
dence base for brand positioning, rather than marketer intuition or generic norms such as
‘unspoiled wilderness’. For targeting, disaggregating the audience by constituency (tour-
ists, residents, activists) lets DMOs identify which segment’s imaginary is most aligned
with, or threatening to, the desired brand position, and to target engagement accord-
ingly. For campaign design, our finding that trust and authenticity are generated by
unscripted, non-authoritative presentation suggests a shift away from high-production-
value marketing videos and toward licensing and amplification of existing user-generated
content and recruiting credible micro-influencers. Collectively, these implications reposi-
tion the DMO from primary author of the destination image to curator and amplifier of an
image co-authored by tourists and the public.
Our research provides destination marketers with the tools to monitor tourism-related
user-generated content by analysing photos for colour, charismatic subjects and story-
telling; and videos for the trustworthiness, authentic experiences and storytelling, so as to
mimic the affective elements that make such content successful (Table 1).

A related complication is that the tourism imaginary co-created around a nature-based
destination is unlikely to be singular. Different groups with a stake in a place – tourists,
residents and, sometimes, activists contesting a particular land use – bring different values
and share different affective elements of the same setting (Salazar & Graburn, 2014):
tourists’ photographs might show scenic or wildlife-focused charisma, residents’ imagery
relationships with the land, and activists’ imagery conflict or loss – three different
imaginaries built from the same location using the same affective grammar. Our frame-
work (s + e = t; Figure 2) accommodates this by allowing the ‘public’ to be disaggregated
into these constituencies, each generating its own imaginary as well as contributing to
a broader, contested collective one. Campaigns built around user-generated content
need to identify which constituency’s imaginary they are seeking to align with, or counter,
rather than treating ‘the’ imaginary of a natural site as a single, homogeneous target.
3.6. Testing of the model
From our model, we identify four testable propositions that future survey- or experimen-
tal-based work could test directly:
P1: User-generated photographs exhibiting higher colour saturation, subject charisma
and narrative content will generate more sharing, liking and commenting behaviour (i.e.
higher (s)) than photographs low in these elements, controlling for destination.
P2: User-generated videos exhibiting higher perceived trust, authenticity and narrative
content will produce greater shifts in destination image and behavioural intention (e.g.
intention to visit, willingness to pay, word-of-mouth) than videos low in these elements.
P3: The alignment between destination-marketing content (e) and the affective ele-
ments dominant in user-generated content (s) will be positively associated with the
perceived effectiveness of that marketing content (i.e. a shift toward p rather than i in
Figure 2).
P4: The affective elements that drive negatively valenced nature-based tourism ima-
gery (e.g. depicting environmental harm) will be the same elements (colour, charisma,
storytelling; trust, authenticity) identified here for positively valenced imagery, differing in
valence rather than in kind.

4. Conclusions
Photography and filmmaking carry significant potential for marketing and tourism
research. As we have shown, the affective nature of an image or video is not a random
process but is shaped by certain characteristics of photos and videos. The testable
propositions above offer a starting point for experimental validation of our model, set
out explicitly so that they, in addition to the narrative account, can be part of this paper’s
contribution.
We have identified three main visual elements that affect the reception of tourism-
related user-generated photography (colour, charisma and storytelling) and videography
(trust, authenticity, and storytelling). The technological revolution of ‘take anywhere’
smartphones and ubiquitous social media has turned the public from mere consumers
of tourism media into active creators, critics and commentators of it.
Tourism imaginaries result from the co-creation of meaning and values and are con-
stantly changing and evolving (Leite, 2014): meanings do not exist in the tourists’ user-
generated photos and videos themselves, but are constituted by the interaction of the
imagery and viewers together. Hence, user-generated photos and videos are not just
manifestations of an underlying socially constructed tourism imaginary but are integral to
its construction. Strategically, destination marketing should co-opt their characteristics to
its own ends.
