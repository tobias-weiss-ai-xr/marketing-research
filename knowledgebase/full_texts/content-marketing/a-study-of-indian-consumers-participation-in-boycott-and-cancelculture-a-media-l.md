Introduction
In recent years, the concept of boycotting and cancel
culture have become an important global phenomenon
in reshaping consumer behavior and relationships
between brand and consumer. Boycotts, traditionally
seen as “organized collective refusals to purchase a
particular good or service”, have a long history of being
used as a tool for social and political change. Cancel
culture is a more recent development amplified by
social media which operates at a faster pace, where
individuals or communities cancel and withdraw
support from brands, products or public figures that
conflict with their values or beliefs. (Klein et al., 2004;
Saldanha et al., 2022)
Throughout the globe, consumers worldwide
increasingly used boycotts as channels for ethical
consumption, social movements and political activism.
Whether climate protest campaigns for sustainable
choices or boycotting companies for human or
diversity issu es, consumer power now merges with
social changes. The online sphere also contributed to
accelerating the process by allowing individuals to
collectively make an effect on company behaviors and
public proceedings within fewer hours due to this
there is an increasing need for media literacy.
(Narayanan et al., 2025)
In India, cancel culture and boycott culture are shaped
by numerous sociocultural sensitivities, religious
sentiments and political opinions. When it comes to
films, commercials or brands, consumers frequently
organize protests against  films or commercials and
brands that they feel are inappropriate from a cultural
perspective and politically controversial. It is also vital
to understand Indian consumers' involvement with
boycott and cancel culture because it reveals the active
process of consumer decision making through which a
nation such as India integrates values, identity and
digital connectivity. (Sahu & Gupta, 2024)
The paper tries to delve into the motivators from moral
and emotional drivers through socio -political
indicators that lead to such participation. It also tries to
examine the consequences of all such practices for the
businesses that undertake them within the nation,
where the reputation and trust of consumers are
determined not just by the product but also by what the
brand stands for.

Research Objectives
Primary: - 1. To investigate the influence of social
media platforms in advocating boycott movements in
India.
Secondary: - 2. To understand how consumers react to
social media in the wake of controversy.
3. To analyze the effects of boycotts on changes in
brand communication actions.
Abstract
Boycott and Cancel culture have empowered a new wave of digital consumer activism in the modern social media-
constructed public sphere. In the Indian context, these campaigns and movements arise out of normative ethics,
cultural concerns, political storyte lling, and rapidly producible and distributable content on social media
platforms. This research analyzes the Indian consumer’s involvement in boycott and cancel culture in the context
of media literacy, focusing on the critical evaluation of digital infor mation and outrage in the social media
narrative. With a descriptive research model, the research study relies on primary research using a structured
questionnaire submitted among 105 Indian consumers, supported by secondary research from existing literature.
Chi-square and Spearman’s rank correlation techniques were applied statistically in the analysis of the correlation
of social media influence, the subsequent reaction of the consumer, and the impact on the communication and
marketing activities of the brand. The research results suggest that social media constitutes a critical platform in
the spread of the boycott and cancel culture campaigns and remains unaffected by the demography of the Indian
consumer, namely, the age and gender divide. Indian consu mers boycott a brand majorly due to practices of un -
ethically moral business, misleading advertisement, and politico -cultural disputes, largely due to emotionally
constructed and unascertained digital contents of the social media narrative. The study also suggests that
consumers act responsibly when they critically understand and evaluate the content as compared to people who
lack the ability to evaluate, they act impulsively by immediately boycotting the brand. The study also proposes a
critical need for m edia literacy, educational outreaches and critical digital ethics in the management of critical
awareness and responsibility in the consumption of digital activism.
Keywords: Boycott and Cancel culture, Ethical Consumerism, Media literacy, Digital activism, social media

Chapter 2: Literature Review
The growing prevalence of consumer boycotts and
cancelled culture in India is a dynamic shift in the
consumption-values-socio-political identity nexus.
This literature review searches for existing academic
literature to make sense of consumers' drivers,
triggers, and socio-cultural determinants of behavioral
participation in these activities.

1.Mady, K., et al. (2025).
This paper examines the effects of social media in
increasing consumer involvement in boycotts with a
special consideration to the role played by animosity,
perceived behavioral control and consumer efficacy as
mediators. As the study is based on the Theor y of
Planned Behavior, it concludes that the role of social
media in enhancing the attendance to boycotts is great
in terms of reinforcing the beliefs of consumers that
personal actions will lead to collective results.
These results indicate that resentment or hatred of a
brand or company which is frequently induced and
promoted using social media distribution is a decisive
element that transforms passive online clients into
active boycott supporters. The consumers who
consider themselves as having the power to change the
brand behavior will be more willing to convert their
outrage into actual buying behavior. The study proves
to be very pertinent to the study of the Indian
consumer environment where social media sites li ke
Twitter and Instagram instantly gather social
awareness against a brand.

2.Saldanha, N., Mulye, R., & Rahman, K. (2022).
This paper can analyze cancel culture through the
prism of strategic marketing, proposing it  that the
outcome of the once social phenomenon has
progressed into an important marketing threat that
requires strategic corporate responses. The authors
study the impact of cancellations that are being
initiated by consumers in the digital age on brand
strategy, communications, and reputation
management.
The paper concludes that brands that do not actively
embrace consumer values especially concerning ethics,
diversity, and social responsibility are becoming an
easy target of organized campaigns of cancellation. It
also states that cancel culture is no cri sis management
problem but rather a strategic problem in the long
term. The insights are particularly critical in markets
where social values and cultural sensitivities form part
of consumer identity, as is the case with companies that
conduct business in such markets as India.

3.Demsar, V., Ferraro, C., Nguyen, J., & Sands, S. (2023).
The paper uses the institutional theory perspective to
examine the dynamics of calls to brand cancellation,
which offers a broader understanding of how markets
are-reconfigured to bring back in line with the current
institutional logics concerning politica l ideology, race,
gender, sexuality, abuse, and corporate greed. There
are four consecutive phases that the authors identify
the process through which the cancellation campaigns
are formed, and they include transgression evaluation,
consumer mobilization, brand response, and market
realignment.
The study draws attention to the realization that
someone is not an act of consumer outrage but an
organized action by institutional entrepreneurs who
are usually consumers and social media communities
that question the authenticity of the established
brands. There are three popular constructive elements
of cancellation: the mass shaming of offensive or
offensive statements or actions by organized activist
groups on social media, and trying to fully withdraw
attention and support of a target brand or a soci al
figure. This model applies directly to the Indian
boycotts, as coordinated consumer movements on
Twitter and Instagram often take brands to task on the
issue of cultural and ethically unacceptable actions.

4.Hill Cummings, K., et al. (2024).
This paper examines the phenomenon of cancel culture
as a consumer behavior and comes to the conclusion
that the emotions of anger, moral outrage, disgust, are
the strongest emotion drivers that elicit cancellation
behavior, with Gen Z as one of the most a ctive users.
The study shows that cancelled brands not only suffer
immediate reputational harm but also perception
problems in the long term despite correctional
measures. The peer-influence factor and social media’s

consumers is minimal.
7.Sahu, M. K., & Gupta, T. C. (2024).
The present study examines the role of social media
systems as dominant instruments of organizing and
maintaining political and consumer activism in India.
The results also indicate that campaigns on hashtags
using Twitter and Instagram represent the most
efficient forms of finding a large number of  consumers
within a short period of time, sometimes within hours
reaching national levels. The research also notes that
these movements are often spread faster through the
influence networks of influencers and emotional
amplification of the controversy whi ch may at times
override the facts themselves.

8.Narayanan, S., et al. (2025)
This is a systematic review of 404 studies exploring
consumer activism and identifies factors like human
rights abuses, environmental degradation, politics and
corporate negligence as the main stimuli that prompts
consumer activism. The researchers conclud e that
consumer activism remains a potent force able to bring
changes to the society , making a brand re -adjust its
strategies according to the changing values of
consumers. These findings give a solid theoretical basis
to the reason behind the growing use of boycotting and
cancel culture as instruments of corporate
responsibility by Indian consumers.
Gaps in Literature
While global studies on cancel culture are on the rise,
Indian-specific empirical studies are few. The primary
gaps are:
Quantitative research on how long exactly customers
do boycott.
Understanding of demographic variation (urban and
rural, Gen Z compared to other generations).
How does social media and fake news impact public

Chapter 3: Research Methodology
A. Type of Research-
Descriptive research, aimed at understanding
consumer motives, attitudes and behaviors related to
boycotting and cancel culture.
B. Population under Study -
Indian consumers who are active users of social media
and online platforms, as they are more exposed to
boycott and cancel culture campaigns.
C. Sample location-
The urban region is Mumbai, which has high digital
engagement.
D. Sampling method-
Non-probability, convenience, sampling (easy
accessibility to respondents through digital surveys
and close neighborhood interviews)
E. Sample size-
Minimum hundred responses collected from diverse
demographic groups (age, gender, and occupation)
F. Method of data collection-
Primary data – Questionnaire and Limited structure
interviews
Secondary data – research articles, industry, reports,
social media, and published case study of boycott/
cancel culture in India.
G. Statistical tools used for hypothesis testing-
Chi Square Goodness of Fit Test: This test was used to
check if observed frequency and the expected
frequency are statically identical or different.
Chi Square Test of Independence: This test was used to
identify statistical relations between two categorial
variables (e.g. Age v/s Reactions).
Spearman Correlation Test: This test was conducted to
understand and measure the strength between two
ordinal variables (sentiments and brand marketing
decisions).

Chapter 7: Recommendations & Conclusion
1.Lead with Transparency and Accountability
This point suggests that what audiences seek is not
merely an apology, but rather accountability. A brand’s
first step should always be to own up to the error
publicly rather than attempting to manipulate the
story and obfuscate specifics. This is at the c ore of any
rebuilding of trust.
2. Avoid Silence at All Costs
Silence is one of the worst plays to make in the midst of
a crisis. It gives the community the opportunity to fill
that gap with their own narrative; one that will always
be more negative.
3.Sequence Your Response: Recognize, Justify and Take
Action.
By looking at what consumers consider the best action
to undertake, there is no one action that would win by
a big margin. Rather, the most popular options, which
are, issuing a public apology, Transparency and
explanation, and Corrective actions are clust ered. It
means that the successful reply cannot be considered a
single event but a three-step process.
4.Create a Customer Feedback Loop that Permanently
Operates.
The responses provided by the form clearly indicate
that consumers feel that their sentiment should be
relevant in the brand strategy and that it does not go
away easily. A proactive request to get opinions about
campaigns before the launch or about socially sensitive
issues can be used as an early warning system.
5.How to Humanize Your Brand, particularly in the
Time of Crisis.
In all the open -ended questions, the fundamental
theme is that the person desired authenticity, sincerity,
and empathy. Customers can easily identify and
dismiss a manufactured, robotic, or proven apology by
the company. This is a strong appeal to brands to speak
more in human terms.  The best -recovered brands are
the ones that shed the corporate veil and react with the
true human feeling.
