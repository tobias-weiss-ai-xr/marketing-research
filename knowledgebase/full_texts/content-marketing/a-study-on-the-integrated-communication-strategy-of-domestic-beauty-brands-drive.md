Abstract
Competition among domestic beauty brands in China has moved beyond product
function and price advantage. Brand communication now increasingly depends on cultural
meaning, platform coordination, visual experience, consumer participation, and long -term
trust. Taking Huaxizi as a case, this paper re-examines the integrated communication strategy
of a domestic beauty brand under the framework of Oriental aesthetic symbols. Different
from a strategy -listing approach that simply describes brand positioning, influencer
cooperation, content marketing, cultural collaboration, private -domain operation, and
overseas communication, this paper reconstructs the case through four analytical
dimensions: cultural symbol translation, platform -context adaptation, trust -evidence
construction, and relationship maintenance. Based on literature analysis, case analysis, and
online observation, the study argues that Huaxizi’s strength lies in transforming Ori ental
aesthetics into visible brand language and shareable consumer experience. However, the
brand still faces challenges such as insufficient cultural explanation, fragmented platform
content, weak product evidence, dependence on traffic, and the need for  more concrete
cross-cultural translation. The paper proposes optimization paths including symbol archives,
platform-specific content matrices, product evidence chains, user co -creation mechanisms,
and localized cultural interpretation.
Keywords: Oriental Aesthetics , Cultural Symbol Translation , Integrated Communication ,
Brand Trust; Huaxizi

The research value of Huaxizi does not only lie in the fact that it uses Chinese-style visual

products, content, platforms, influencers, communities, and overseas mark ets. A lipstick,
eyebrow pencil, powder box, or gift set is not only a product. It can also become a
photographed object, a topic for social media sharing, a sign of cultural taste, and a medium
through which consumers participate in brand meaning. This makes Huaxizi a useful case for
discussing the relationship between cultural branding and integrated communication.

However, the study of this case must avoid repeating the structure of earlier descriptive
work. If a new paper simply follows the order of brand positioning, influencer marketing,
content marketing, cultural co -branding, private-domain traffic, and oversea s expansion, it
may remain too close to the original case narrative. Therefore, this paper does not ask only
what communication actions Huaxizi has taken. Instead, it asks how Oriental aesthetic
symbols are encoded, how they are adapted to different platform contexts, how they become
credible product and brand evidence, and how they support long-term user relationships.

The purpose of this paper is to turn shared case materials into a new analytical object. It
acknowledges that the case facts come from the same brand, but it reorganizes them through
a different research question, theoretical entry point, and chapter structure. The paper does
not fabricate sales data, survey samples, or traffic figures. It uses literature analysis, the
original case manuscript, public brand information, and online observation to conduct a
qualitative study of Huaxizi's integrated communication logic.

This restructuring is also necessary because the same brand case can support different
levels of academic inquiry. A descriptive paper may record what the brand has done, while a
deeper communication study should explain how those actions create meaning, how they are
interpreted by users, and where their limitations appear. In this sense, Huaxizi is not treated
as a simple success story. It is treated as a case through which the opportunities and risks of
cultural branding, platform marketing, and consumer trust can be examined together.

Study Objectives
To analyze how Huaxizi translates Oriental aesthetic resources into recognizable brand
symbols and consumer experiences.
To examine how these symbols are adapted and coordinated across social media platforms,
e-commerce environments, influencer communication, and consumer relationship
touchpoints.
To identify tensions among cultural narration, traffic dependence, product evidence, and
long-term brand trust, and to propose context -sensitive optimization paths for Huaxizi and
comparable domestic beauty brands.

Literature Review and Theoretical Basis
Integrated Communication: From Touchpoint Coverage to Meaning Consistency
Integrated marketing communication emphasizes the coordination of advertising, public
relations, social media, sales conversion, and user service around consumer cognition. Schultz
et al. (1993) regarded IMC as a way to integrate communication activities around the
consumer, while Kliatchko (2008) further emphasized the interaction among st akeholders,
content, channels, and results. For beauty brands, consumers may first encounter a product
through a Xiaohongshu review, then see a short video on Douyin, enter a live -streaming
room, read e-commerce comments, and finally join a community for usage advice. Integrated
communication is therefore not simpl y the expansion of media channels, but the
coordination of touchpoints around a coherent brand meaning.

In the case of Huaxizi, Oriental aesthetics can be understood as the central meaning
thread. If packaging design, influencer explanation, platform content, and membership
operation all point to the same cultural and aesthetic value, consumers are more like ly to
form stable brand memory. If different channels separately emphasize low price, national -
style packaging, celebrity recommendation, or limited -edition gifts, the brand image may
become fragmented. The key issue is whether the bra nd can maintain a consistent cultural
logic while adapting to different communication contexts, since integrated communication
strengthens brand knowledge when contact points contribute to compatible associations
(Keller, 2009).

Duncan and Moriarty's (1998) communication-based marketing model is also useful here
because it treats communication as the basis of relationship management. Beauty brands do
not complete communication when a product is sold. Communication continues in after-sales
service, user reviews, community interaction, and product iteration. From this perspec tive,
Huaxizi's communication should be evaluated not only by exposure or conversion, but also
by whether it supports continuous dialogue and trust between the brand and consumers.

For a culturally positioned brand, meaning consistency is especially important. If a brand
claims to represent Oriental aesthetics, every major touchpoint should help consumers
understand what this claim means. Product design should not be disconnected from content
explanation; influencer scripts should not reduce the brand to price comparison only; private-
domain service should not ignore the cultural and aesthetic promise made in public
campaigns. Integrated communication is therefore both a media -management issue and a
meaning-management issue.

Cultural Symbol Translation: From Visual Elements to Consumer Experience
Cultural symbols are not equal to the simple accumulation of traditional elements. Hall's
(1997) theory of representation reminds us that meaning is constructed through symbol use
and social interpretation. Names, patterns, colors, objects, and intangible heritage crafts
must be translated before they can enter modern consumption. In this paper, translation
refers to the process through which a brand turns cultural resources into produc t
appearance, content stories, usage scenarios, and user interaction. Consumers do not merely
watch symbols; they reinterpret them through purchase, use, photography, sharing, and
comments.

The value of Oriental aesthetics in domestic beauty communication is twofold. First, it
helps the brand differentiate itself from international beauty brands that often emphasize
technology, luxury, fashion, or professional authority. Second, it provides c onsumers with a
sense of local cultural identity and aesthetic participation. However, if the brand cannot
explain the source, logic, and product relevance of these symbols, cultural expression may
decline into replaceable decoration.

Holt's (2004) theory of iconic brands also suggests that cultural meaning can become a
source of brand value when it responds to social desires and identity tensions. In the context
of Chinese beauty consumption, young consumers may want products that are functional,
visually attractive, and culturally meaningful. Huaxizi's use of Oriental aesthetics addresses
this desire, but the cultural meaning must be continuously m anaged rather than repeatedly
stated as a slogan.

The process of translation can be divided into several stages. The first stage is selection,
in which the brand chooses particular cultural materials, such as a poetic image, a regional
association, or a craft tradition. The second stage is design transfor mation, in which the
material becomes packaging, product texture, naming, or visual style. The third stage is
narrative explanation, in which the brand tells consumers why the symbol matters. The fourth
stage is user reproduction, in which consumers re -create the symbol through photos,
reviews, tutorials, and discussions. Huaxizi’s communication can be evaluated according to
how complete this chain is.

Platform Context and Brand Trust
Social media platforms have different communication grammars. Kaplan and Ha enlein
(2010) define social media as Internet -based applications that support user -generated
content, while Kietzmann et al. (2011) show that social media involves multiple functions
such as identity, sharing, relationship, reputation, and conversation. Xiaohongshu is suitable
for experiential notes and lifestyle narratives; Douyin emphasizes short -video rhythm and
immediate conversion; Weibo supports public issue diffusion; Bilibili is more suitable for long
reviews and knowledge explanation. When a brand communicates the same core symbol
across platforms, it must adapt content forms.

At the same time, beauty consumption depends heavily on trust. Consumers care not
only about whether packaging looks attractive, but also about texture, shade, makeup effect,
ingredients, safety, price, and after-sales service. Therefore, cultural narratives need product
evidence. A brand that communicates aesthetics without credible product explanation may
gain short -term attention but fail to generate long -term repurchase and word of mouth.
Research on brand equity, electronic word of mouth, and influencer credibility shows that
consistent knowledge structures and credible user experience sh ape consumer perceptions
(Hennig-Thurau et al., 2004; Keller, 1993; Lou & Yuan, 2019).

Platform context also affects how consumers judge brand content. On seeding platforms,
consumers expect detailed and authentic user experience. In live -streaming rooms,
consumers pay more attention to immediate benefits and the credibility of the host. On
public discussion platforms, price, attitude, and brand values can quickly become
controversial issues. Because social media combines organizational promotion with

This also means that the same communication message may have different effects in

criticism in a public discussion if users feel that the value is not clearly justified. The brand
must therefore understand not only where consumers are, but also what kind of evidence
consumers expect in each platform situation.

Research Design
Research Questions
This paper is organized around three research questions. First, how does Huaxizi
translate Oriental aesthetic symbols into brand recognition and consumer experience?
Second, how does the brand re -express these symbols in different media platforms? Third,
what risks exist between cultural communication, platform traffic, and user trust, and how
can the brand optimize its integrated communication strategy?

Research Methods
This paper adopts literature analysis, case analysis, and online observation. Following the
logic of qualitative case study research (Yin, 2018), literature analysis is used to build the
theoretical basis of integrated communication, cultural symbols, soci al media, and brand
trust. Case analysis is used to reorganize Huaxizi's comm unication practices. Online
observation is used to examine public brand content, user interaction, and controversy -
related issues. The paper does not conduct questionnaires or statistical modeling, and it does
not invent sales figures, sample sizes, or traffic numbers.

These methods are appropriate because the purpose of the paper is not to measure
consumer attitudes quantitatively, but to explain the communication mechanism behind a
cultural brand case. The analysis therefore focuses on how communication materials are
organized, how different platforms function, and how brand meaning is produced between
the brand and consumers.

The limitation of this approach is that it cannot produce statistically generalizable
findings. However, it is suitable for identifying communication structures and conceptual
problems. The paper therefore treats case materials as evidence for interpretati on rather
than as numerical proof. When discussing brand practices, it avoids unverifiable claims about
sales volume, market ranking, or exact traffic performance. This is consistent with the
requirement that the study should not invent data.

Case Analysis: Cultural Symbol Translation and Integrated Communication of Huaxizi
Symbol Translation in Brand Naming and Visual Design
Huaxizi's brand recognition first comes from its name and visual system. The Chinese
character "Hua" is associated with flowers, nature, and the idea of nourishing makeup, while
"Xizi" evokes Hangzhou, West Lake, and the classical image of feminine beauty (East Garden
Group, n.d.; Kang, 2020). This naming strategy does not simply create an antique atmosphere.
It establishes a cultural semantic space that can be extended into product packaging, gift sets,
carved patterns, and Oriental makeup content.

From the perspective of communication mechanism, packaging design functions as a
medium. When consumers post unboxing photos, swatches, or makeup images, the product
appearance becomes material for secondary communication. Huaxizi’s carved surfaces,
embossed cases, and traditional patterns make products easier to photograph and share,
giving the brand strong recognition in information flows. Nevertheless, visual recognition
cannot replace product explanation. The brand still needs to explain how design rela tes to
product function, usage scenarios, and cultural sources.

This means that Huaxizi’s symbol communication must move from being attractive to
being explainable. For example, the brand should clarify where a certain pattern comes from,
why it is suitable for a certain product, whether the transformation respects the  original
cultural meaning, and how consumers can perceive its value in use. If these questions are not
answered, consumers only see refined appearance. If the explanation is sufficient, packaging
can become an entrance to brand education and cultural communication.

The symbolic value of product design is therefore double -sided. On the positive side, it
creates immediate recognition and encourages consumers to share visual content

Re-Encoding Content in Platform Contexts
The same Oriental aesthetic symbol takes different forms after entering different
platforms. On short -video platforms, it may appear as makeup atmosphere, rhythm, and
visual impact. In image-text communities, it is more likely to become swatch notes, unboxing
experience, and daily usage advice. In live -streaming rooms, it is often combined with price,
efficacy, and host explanation. In long videos or official account articles, it is more suitable
for craft stories, cultural sources, and brand philosophy.

This platform adaptation is more important than the simple distribution of advertising
materials. If a brand sends the same slogan to all platforms, consumers may feel that the
content is repetitive and lacks relevance. Huaxizi’s communication value lies partly in its early
understanding of the visual and social nature of beauty consumption. However, it still needs
clearer division of content functions: short videos attract attention, image -text notes
accumulate experience, long-form content explains culture, and private communities provide
service and feedback.

Platform content should also form a progressive path. The first layer solves the problem
of being seen, the second layer helps users understand the product, the third layer builds
trust, and the fourth layer encourages continued attention. When users move from
accidentally watching a short video to actively searching for reviews and then entering e -
commerce or community spaces, they need continuous information support. If platform
content is disconnected, user interest may remain superficial.

A clearer platform matrix can also reduce the risk of overusing the same cultural image.
If every channel repeats similar national -style visuals, the brand may look consistent at first
but become monotonous over time. Instead, each platform should deepen a different part of
the brand meaning. Short videos may dramatize the aesthetic atmosphere, review platforms
may explain product suitability, brand -owned accounts may tell cultural stories, and
communities may collect user feedback. Such division allows the  same symbol to remain
coherent without becoming repetitive.

From Traffic Exposure to Trust Evidence
The original case material pays attention to influencer cooperation and live -streaming
communication. This paper focuses more on the trust mechanism behind them. Top live -
streamers can quickly enlarge visibility, but visibility does not automatically becom e stable
trust. After purchasing in a live-streaming room, consumers still judge the brand according to
actual use, platform comments, and feedback from peers.

Therefore, Huaxizi needs to shift the communication focus from who recommends the
product to why the product is trustworthy. For beauty products, credible information
includes suitable skin types, shade selection, makeup durability, ingredient explanation,

application methods, and after -sales response. The brand can continue to use influencer
cooperation, but different communicators should perform different tasks: top influencers
provide reach, professional creators provide explanation, ordinary users provid e real
experience, and brand -owned channels provide long -term answe rs. This differentiation
reflects evidence that influencer reach and message credibility shape brand responses in
distinct ways (De Veirman et al., 2017; Lou & Yuan, 2019).

Trust evidence should also be consiste nt. If live rooms emphasize price, product pages
emphasize culture, reviews emphasize packaging, and after -sales channels cannot answer
usage questions, consumers receive fragmented information. A better approach is to build a
unified product knowledge base and translate it into platform-specific content. This reduces
inconsistent communication and connects cultural narrative with concrete consumer
experience.

In beauty communication, evidence does not have to be purely technical. It can be
presented through comparison photos, usage demonstrations, long -term wear tests, shade
guides, ingredient explanations, and user questions. What matters is that consumers can
connect the brand promise with observable experience. For Huaxizi, the ideal structure is not
culture first and product second, but culture and product evidence presented together. This
would allow Oriental aesthetics to function as a value enhancer rathe r than a substitute for
product credibility.

Legitimacy and Depth in Cultural Collaboration
Huaxizi’s collaboration with intangible cultural heritage and cultural IP is an important
support for its Oriental aesthetic communication. Cultural resources such as Miao silver
ornaments and Song brocade can enrich product stories and help the brand move  beyond
generic national -style visuals. However, the core of cultural collaboration should not be
limited to co -branding or scarcity. It should explain cultural sources, partners, craft
transformation, and contemporary use value.

If consumers only see an attractive gift box without understanding the source of the
pattern or craft, intangible heritage collaboration may be interpreted as marketing packaging.
In contrast, when the brand shows the craft background, design translation, and product
experience, cultural collaboration becomes part of brand credibility. The more frequently
cultural symbols are used, the more responsibility the brand has to explain them.

Cultural collaboration also involves the relationship between the brand and cultural
subjects. When a brand uses intangible heritage, museums, or regional cultural resources, it
should avoid hiding the cultural subject behind commercial packaging. A more r esponsible
communication approach presents the partner, production process, and authorization
relationship, so that consumers know that cultural elements are not randomly appropriated
but selected and redesigned through cooperation.

This point is related to cultural legitimacy. When a brand borrows cultural resources,
consumers may ask whether the brand understands and respects the culture or merely uses
it for commercial attraction. Transparent explanation can reduce this doubt. For example,
showing the craft process, introducing the cultural partner, and explaining the design choices

User Participation, Private-Domain Operation, and Relationship Maintenance
User-generated content is an important p art of Huaxizi's communication. Unboxing,
swatches, imitation makeup, and usage feedback turn consumers from information receivers
into communication participants. Compared with advertising, ordinary user content is closer
to everyday use and can supplemen t the limitations of brand self -description, while also
shaping brand equity and brand attitude (Schivinski & Dabrowski, 2016).

Private-domain operation should not be understood only as a repurchase tool. Official
accounts, communities, membership systems, and experience -officer programs can easily
cause user fatigue if they only send promotional information. If they provide makeup
tutorials, shade advice, product trials, feedback channels, and after -sales explanations, one
transaction can become continuous service. For a brand emphasizing Oriental aesthetics,
private-domain spaces can also carry aesthetic education and cultural content.

Relationship maintenance should distinguish different user stages. New users need basic
brand introduction and product instructions; repeat users pay more attention to new product
information and benefits; highly engaged users may be willing to participate  in trials,
feedback, and content co -creation. If the brand sends the same information to all users,
private-domain operation becomes a promotion list. If the brand provides differentiated
service according to relationship depth, users are more likely to form long-term belonging.

Brand communities also create a space in which consumers can interact with one another
rather than only with the brand. This is important because peer experience often feels more
credible than official claims and can motivate electronic word of mouth (Hennig -Thurau et
al., 2004). If Huaxizi can encourage users to discuss makeup methods, shade matching, gift -
giving experience, and cultural stories, the community may become a site of shared meaning.
In tha t case, private -domain operation is not only a sales tool but also a mechanism for
strengthening brand identity and shared community consciousness (Muñiz & O'Guinn, 2001).

Cultural Translation in Overseas Communication
The difficulty of Huaxizi’s overseas communication is not whether it should continue to
emphasize Oriental aesthetics, but how overseas consumers can understand this aesthetic.
Domestic consumers may more easily understand images such as West Lake, Song br ocade,
Miao silver ornaments, or classical makeup, but overseas markets need clearer cultural
explanation and usage scenarios.

Therefore, overseas communication cannot simply copy domestic national -style
narratives. The brand should adjust expression according to skin tone, climate, makeup
habits, and aesthetic preferences in different markets. In European and American markets,
design stories, artistic value, and gift attributes may be emphasized. In Southeast Asian
markets, sweat resistance, shade suitability, and everyday makeup needs may be more
relevant. Effective cultural export is not the mystification of the East, but the tr anslation of
concrete cultural details into product value that consumers can understand.

Cross-cultural communication also needs to avoid treating Oriental aesthetics as a single,
mysterious, and static image. More effective communication turns abstract culture into
concrete details, such as the origin of a pattern, the modern transformation o f a makeup
style, or the way a craft affects packaging texture. Specific details are easier for consumers
from different cultural backgrounds to understand and can reduce misunderstanding.

Moreover, overseas communication should not assume that all foreign consumers
respond to Oriental aesthetics in the same way. Different markets have different beauty
routines, climate conditions, purchasing channels, and cultural expectations. A story that
works in the domestic market may need to be shortened, explained, or connected with
practical benefits abroad. The challenge is to maintain cultural uniqueness while avoiding
incomprehensibility. This requires localization without losing the brand’s core identity.

Existing Problems
First, cultural symbol explanation is still insufficient. Oriental aesthetics has become
Huaxizi’s core label, but some communication content emphasizes visual effects more than
the sources, craft logic, and contemporary transformation of symbols. If the e xplanatory
chain is incomplete, consumers may understand cultural expression as packaging decoration.

Second, platform content still tends to be fragmented. Different platforms have different
functions, but brand communication sometimes repeats the same visuals and slogans across
platforms. Such repetition can increase exposure but may not improve understa nding. The
brand needs to design a more coherent information path.

Third, the product evidence chain needs to be strengthened. Consumers ultimately
evaluate beauty products through makeup effect, texture, shade, wear time, price, and
service. If communication overemphasizes cultural narrative while lacking verifiable
information about product function and real experience, brand trust may be weakened.

Fourth, traffic cooperation carries external risks. Live -streaming rooms and leading
influencers can attract attention, but they may also make the brand dependent on platform
rules, influencer speech, and price controversies. The brand should reduce dependence on a
single traffic entrance.

Fifth, cross-cultural communication needs more specificity. Overseas consumers do not
naturally understand Chinese traditional aesthetics. The brand needs to translate cultural
symbols into explainable stories, usable scenarios, and perceptible product value.

Sixth, public -opinion response needs to be more proactive. The price, efficacy, and
service of beauty brands can easily enter public discussi on, as demonstrated by the public
controversy surrounding Huaxizi's 79 -yuan eyebrow pencil (Liang & Han, 2023). Once
controversy occurs, passive explanation may quickly consume previously accumulated
cultural goodwill. The brand needs transparent product explanation and response
mechanisms in daily communication.

Optimization Paths
Establishing Cultural Symbol Archives
Huaxizi should build archives for core patterns, colors, crafts, and naming imagery. These
archives should explain the source, meaning, design transformation, and product relevance
of each symbol. This can improve the academic quality and credibility of communication and
prevent cultural expression from being understood as arbitrary collage.

A symbol archive can also support consistent communication across platforms. Short
videos can use simplified visual stories, product pages can provide detailed explanations, and
long-form content can present cultural context. When all materials come from t he same

Reconstructing the Platform Content Matrix
Different platforms should undertake different tasks. Short videos attract attention,
image-text communities accumulate real experience, live -streaming rooms provide
immediate explanation and conversion, long -form content interprets culture, and private
communities provide service and feedback. Through this division, the brand can reduce
repetitive communication and increase overall understanding.

The platform matrix should also guide users from shallow contact to deeper engagement.
A consumer may first see an attractive visual, then search for reviews, then compare product
evidence, and finally join a community or repurchase. The brand should desig n content for
each step rather than treating all platforms as traffic outlets.

Strengthening the Product Evidence Chain
Huaxizi needs to present aesthetic narrative and product evidence together. Each key
product should provide clear information on suitable skin types, shade suggestions,
application methods, makeup effects, and after -sales response. Cultural value attracts
attention, while product evidence supports trust.

Evidence-based communication does not mean abandoning aesthetics. It means
explaining the product in a way that is concrete and easy to verify. For example, shade
demonstrations on different skin tones, tutorials for different makeup styles, and
comparisons of usage scenarios can all make product value more credible.

Improving User Co-Creation and Public-Opinion Response
Experience-officer programs, membership communities, and UGC activities should move
from content collection to feedback governance. The brand can incorporate user opinions
into product iteration and content correction, and respond to issues related to price, efficacy,
service, and cultural expression when controversy appears.

Specifically, user feedback can be divided into product experience, content
understanding, service process, and price perception. Product experience issues require
usage guidance; content understanding issues require cultural explanation; service issues
require process optimization; price controversies require clear explanation of value
composition.

Promoting Cross-Cultural Semantic Translation
Overseas communication should avoid exporting only an abstract Oriental image. The
brand needs to explain cultural details in language that overseas consumers can understand
and localize content according to makeup habits, climate, and consumption scenarios.

A more effective international strategy is to combine cultural explanation with product
usefulness. If consumers understand both the origin of a design and the reason why the
product fits their daily needs, Oriental aesthetics can move from visual curiosit y to
meaningful brand recognition.

Conclusion
This paper re-analyzes the integrated communication strategy of domestic beauty brands
driven by Oriental aesthetic symbols through the case of Huaxizi. Instead of simply listing
communication practices, it examines Huaxizi within the framework of cultural  symbol
translation, platform-context adaptation, trust-evidence construction, and user relationship
maintenance. The study argues that Huaxizi’s core advantage lies in turning Oriental
aesthetics into a recognizable brand entrance and expanding its commun ication through
product visuals, social content, cultural collaboration, and user participation.

At the same time, the case shows that cultural symbols cannot replace product
experience, and traffic exposure cannot replace long -term trust. Domestic beauty brands
must connect cultural explanation, platform content, product evidence, and user
relationships. Only when consumers can perceive aesthetic value and believe in product value
can Oriental aesthetics become a stable brand asset rather than a temporary communication
label.

Theoretical and Contextual Contributions
Theoretically, this study extends integrated marketing communication beyond the
coordination of channels and messages by conceptualizing culturally positioned brand
communication as a linked process of symbol translation, platform adaptation, trust-evidence
construction, and relationship maintenance. This framework connects representation and
cultural branding with platform -specific communication and consumer trust, showing that
aesthetic symbols create durable brand value only when cultural meaning, product evidence,
and user experience reinforce one another. It therefore adds a process-oriented explanation
to existing IMC knowledge rather than treating cultural elements as isolated creative devices.
Contextually, the Huaxizi case demonstrates how this proc ess operates within China's
domestic beauty market, where guochao consumption, influencer -led commerce, social -
media participation, and public scrutiny intersect. The findings offer domestic beauty brands
a diagnostic lens for balancing Oriental aesthetic distinctiveness with verifiable product value,
responsive user relationships, and culturally intelligible overseas communication. Although
conclusions from a single qualitative case are not statistically generalizable, the framework
provides analytically t ransferable concepts that future comparative studies can examine
across brands, platforms, and cultural markets.

Research Limitations
This paper mainly adopts literature analysis, case analysis, and online observation. It does
not conduct consumer interviews, questionnaire surveys, or platform data crawling, and
therefore cannot quantitatively evaluate consumer attitudes or communication  effects. The
analysis is also based on a single case, so the conclusion cannot be directly generalized to all
domestic beauty brands. Future research may introduce competitor comparison, platform
