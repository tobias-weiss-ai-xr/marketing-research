Abstract
Purpose - The purpose of the study is to shift the focus of the narrative work from traditional literary art to
branding communication and to bring together and synthesize the theoretical approaches of narratology and
marketing. This systematic literature review bri ngs together structuralist and post -structuralist narrative theory
(originating in Russian Formalism, French structuralism, narrative hermeneutic identity theory and Jung's myth
criticism).
Methodology - A targeted search of scholarly databases and publisher platforms was carried out using narrative-
theory and brand-marketing search terms, following a PRISMA-informed and Tranfield-style systematic protocol.
A total of 48 peer -reviewed sources and some fund amental theoretical documents were retrieved after screening
(explicit inclusion/explicit exclusion), and then a thematic integrative synthesis matrix was created.
Findings - The following themes emerged as cross -cutting findings: (1) structural transfer of story/plot
arrangement from fiction to brand story; (2) mechanisms for transportation and persuasion; (3) construction of
narrative identity and brand identity; (4) the role  of archetype and myth in the creation of brand meaning; (5)
perspective, voice, and authenticity in the brand story; (6) transmedia extension and digital convergence; and (7)
critiques and boundary conditions that affect the wholesale transfer of literary structure to commercial contexts.
The review reveals that narrative transportation theory is the most empirically tested construct for bridging the
gap, and the archetypal branding and hero's-journey messaging framework remains under-tested.
Originality - This review is one of the first to systematically link canonical narratological theory with the empirical
brand-storytelling literature on a clear -cut construct -mapping matrix and to draw falsifiable theoretical
conclusions that connect literary theory with consumer research.
Keywords: narrative theory; narratology; brand storytelling; narrative transportation; brand archetype; narrative
identity; systematic literature review.

1. Introduction
Stories are one of humanity's most ancient technologies of meaning -making, and the
systematic study of the structure of stories has resulted in a century of theory outlining how
events are selected, sequenced, voiced and received (Propp, 1968; Genette, 1980; Bal, 2009). In
tandem with this, and more intensely in the past 20 years, ideas of narratives have been
borrowed by marketing scholars and practitioners to account for the different ways in which
messages about brands can be felt as emotionally moving a nd convincing (Green & Brock,
2000; Van Laer et al., 2014; de Ruyter, Visconti, & Wetzels, 2014). While brand storytelling is a
mainstream strategic discipline, the theoretical concepts that have been borrowed from the
literary discipline of storytelling —plot, focalization, archetype, narrative identity,
monomyth—are often used without any careful consideration of the narratological research
and theory from which they are derived (Zimand-Sheiner, 2026).
This imbalance brings a true need to solve this problem with a systematic literature review.
On the other hand, there is an older tradition of literary theory of narrative, largely
independent of commercial use, focused on the formal characteristics of story (Barthes, 1975;
Todorov, 1969) and the philosophical connections between narrative and identity (Ricoeur,
1984-1988). There are also growing, increasingly quantitative marketing literatures that cite
narrative theory but don't integrate it into their concepts or arguments about the effectiveness
of narrative transportation, narrative persuasion, and storytelling (Thomas, Chandrashekar,
Chen, & Chandrashekar, 2024). To the best of this review's authors' knowledge, no systematic
review has explicitly built a linkage between the two literatures on the construct level.
Accordingly, this review pursues three research questions:
RQ1: What narrative-theoretical concepts from classic and canonical literature have
been explicitly or implicitly transferred into the brand -storytelling and marketing -
communication literature?
RQ2: What empirical evidence exists for the persuasive, relational or identity -
constructing effects of these narrative constructs when used for brands?
RQ3: What theoretical and methodological gaps are still present between narratology
and marketing, and what research propositions are falsifiable to which empirical
studies can be directed?
This review complements existing narrative -based systematic reviews in the marketing
literature, but is also different from them. Of particular interest, Thomas et al. (2024)
conducted a systematic review on the narrative transportation construct in partic ular, its
theoretical roots, and its use in consumer psychology. The scope of the present review is
different, however: it traces the entire spectrum of the canonical narratological theory
(structural, hermeneutic, and archetypal) onto the world of brand-storytelling literature in an
explicit construct-level synthesis matrix (Section 7), which includes constructs not covered by
transportation-related reviews (e.g., fabula/syuzhet, focalization, narrative identity).
The rest of the manuscript is arranged as follows. Section 2 provides a summary of the basic
story theory that has been studied in literary and philosophical research. Section 3 follows the
journey of these concepts to the realm of brand storytelling research. The methodology of the
systematic review is described in Section 4. The reviewed corpus is described in Section 5.
Section 6 offers the thematic synthesis, and Section 7 merges the results of the thematic
synthesis into an integrative synthesis matrix. Section 8 offers theoretical propositions that are
testable. The theoretical and managerial implications, limitations, and a future research
agenda are discussed in sections 9-13, followed by a conclusion.

2. Theoretical Foundations of Narrative Theory in Classic Fiction
2.1 Structuralist Narratology: Propp, Genette, Barthes, and Todorov
The formal roots of modern narrative theory are to be found in the Morphology of the Folktale
(1968) by Vladimir Propp, who studied a set of Russian folk tales and identified a set of story
"functions" that would be performed by different characters in var ious tales. The formalist
distinction of Propp between the "underlying chronological events" (fabula) and the "order
and manner in which those events are presented to the audience" (syuzhet) became the basis
for all further narratological studies that came  to be known as narratology or structuralist
narratology.
This project was continued by French structuralism. Structural anthropology of Claude Lévi-
Strauss, the coining of “narratology” by Tzvetan Todorov, and the structural analysis of
narrative by Roland Barthes (1975) were attempts to find a general grammar o f story
applicable across media and cultures. Gérard Genette (1980) further developed the distinction
between story and discourse into a three -part structure: histoire (story content), récit
(narrative text) and narration (the act of narrating) and expanded the notion of story with the
influential concept of focalization —the point from which events are filtered into the story
(Genette, 1980). A three-tiered model of fabula, story and text was later expanded by Mieke
Bal (2009), which still serves as a point of reference in narratological analysis work.
The commonality of these structuralist approaches revolves around the premise of the
narrative that is relevant to brand storytelling: narrative meaning is not just from what
happens as much as it is from the selection, sequencing, and framing of what happ ens. This
premise looks forward to the marketing wisdom gained decades later – that the same brand
content can be arranged narratively in different ways that can vary significantly in persuasive
and emotional impact (Van Laer et al., 2014).
2.2 Narrative Perspective, Focalization, and Narrator Reliability
The other strand of narrative theory concerns the relationship between the voice of narration
and the story world. According to Genette (1980), there are three types of focalization:
narrator's focalization (zero focalization, omniscient narrator with unli mited knowledge);
focalization from inside the character's perception (internal focalization): the narrator's focus
is on the character's perceptions; focalization from outside the character's perception (external
focalization): no access to the character's inner perception. The Rhetoric of Fiction by Wayne
Booth (1961) also presented the complementary idea of the unreliable narrator, a narrating
voice whose credibility is flawed due to bias, lack of information or intentional deception,
compelling the read er to make an effort to construct a more reliable narrative beneath the
narrator's surface report (Live to Plant, 2025).
The constructs are not just academic; they refer to tangible formal decisions made with
measurable consequences for audiences, and this is why they are recurring in the empirical
advertising literature on first- versus third-person brand narrators (see Section 3.6) – they are,
at least, largely unacknowledged for their narratological roots.
2.3 Narrative Identity: Ricoeur's Hermeneutic Contribution
The narrative as the form of constituting identity across time is the focus of Paul Ricoeur's
(1984-1988) Three Volumes of Time and Narrative and his later Oneself as Another (1992),
which provide a hermeneutic -phenomenological account of narrative as such. In Ricoeur's

concept of employment (mise en intrigue), otherwise unconnected experiences are arranged
into a coherent and followable story that connects with the philosophical problem of selfhood-
as-sameness and selfhood-as-change (Ricoeur, 1992). Ricoeur further differentiates character,
a model of identity that accrues over time, from promise, a model of identity that is sustained
over time through the maintenance of promises made and kept with consumers (a distinction
that has little direct but not completely overlooked relevance for the manner in which brands
sustain a coherent identity amidst change, or make and keep promises to consumers over
time).
Marketing scholarship is philosophically sounder than the more descriptive accounts of
"brand story" found in the applied literature, in that Ricoeur's narrative identity theory
provides a theoretical tool that is currently underutilized (see Section 6.3).
2.4 Archetypal and Mythic Structures: Jung and Campbell
Another "thread" that can be traced back to the foundation is analytical psychology and
comparative mythology. Archetypes, a theory of Carl Jung (1959/1968), posited a universal set
of figures in the collective unconscious that repeat in both personal psyc hes and across
different cultures (The Hero, The Innocent, The Sage). Campbell (1949) operationalized the
theory of Jung in terms of narrative structure by proposing the monomyth, which is a
seventeen-stage story of a departure, initiation and return, later summarized to twelve stages,
that Campbell claimed recurs in the world's mythologies (Campbell, 1949).
Campbell's monomyth showed that the Jungian archetypes are not just psychological
classifications, but structuring principles of the narrative form itself, a claim that would be
directly picked up by the brand theorists (Section 3.3) and, more loosely, by popular brand-
messaging methods that make the customer the hero of their narrative and the brand the guide
or mentor figure (Narrative Spin, 2025; Platinum Black, 2026).
3. Narrative Theory's Migration into Brand Storytelling
3.1 Narrative Transportation Theory and the Transportation-Imagery Model
The most important bridge to be built between literary narrative theory and marketing is
narrative transportation theory, developed by (Green & Brock 2000) , which characterizes a
mental and emotional state of being caught up in a narrative in which the audience member
becomes absorbed in the story world as if it were his own, while at the same time disengaging
from the current physical world. Theory indicate s that transportation will reduce
counterarguing and that it will also produce transportational persuasive effects outside of, yet
deeper than, the typical two-process models of persuasion such as the Elaboration Likelihood
Model (ELM) (Green & Brock, 2000; Thomas et al., 2024).
(Van Laer et al., 2014) added the antecedents (story characteristics, receiver characteristics)
and consequences (attitude and behavior change) of cognitive psychology, communication
and consumer culture theory to the construct in a unified multidisciplinary model, the
Extended Transportation-Imagery Model. Notably, in developing this model the authors used
Barthes's (1975) and Green and Brock's (2000) observation that mere sentences are not stories,
and that a true story needs a narrative “narrative moveme nt” combined with a thematic
“narrative framing,” an explicit, though rare, example of a marketing model directly
borrowing from structuralist narrative theory (Van Laer et al., 2014).
In a recent systematic literature review published in Psychology & Marketing (2024), Thomas
et al. have validated the narrative transportation theory as the dominant theoretical

3.2 Narrative Persuasion: Meta-Analytic Evidence
The communication field has yielded meta -analytic evidence of the effect size of the
transportation construct independent of the narrative. In particular, Braddock and Dillard's
(2016) meta-analysis, which combined the results of dozens of experimental stu dies, found
significant positive relationships between narrative exposure and narrative-consistent beliefs
(r = 0.17), attitudes (r = 0.19), intentions (r = 0.17) and behaviors (r = 0.23), and also identified
unidentified moderators, which is directly relevant to the variation in brand -narrative
effectiveness across campaigns and contexts. The more theory -oriented narrative
transportation literature has, at times, been missing the empi rical underpinning it needed ,
provided by this meta-analytic base.
3.3 Brand Archetypes: Mark and Pearson's Application of Jungian Theory
Margaret Mark and Carol Pearson's (2001) theory of the Hero and the Outlaw applied Jung's
archetypal theory directly to brand strategy, and they identified 12 Brand archetypes under 4
basic motivations: Independence, Mastery, Belonging and Stability. Bechter, Farinelli, Daniel
and Frey (2016) provide one of the few attempts to link Mark's and Pearson's (2000)
dimensions of the brand personalities with Aaker's (1997) marked dimensions of the brand
personality scale, proposing an empirical framework for linking the two.
This branch of literature is also the most controversial with respect to method. The drawbacks,
according to critics, are that Jungian archetypes cannot be disproved and that archetype -to-
brand mapping is usually done in a post hoc way (i.e., the brand is mapped to an archetype
after its identity has been established), but not as an ex ante predictive theoretical model. This
tension between rich interpretive utility and limited predictive validity is a central boundary
condition identified in this review (see Section 6.7).
3.4 The Hero's Journey as Brand-Narrative Architecture
Campbell's monomyth is evident in brand messaging that positions the customer as the hero
and the brand as the mentor or guiding figure, giving the hero resources to overcome an
obstacle (Platinum Black, 2026). This "new hero's journey" is most noticeable in popular
frameworks such as the Storybrand model developed by Donald Miller, in which the narrative
is flipped from the typical or individualistic "classic" hero's journey, and has been structurally
criticized as a linear narrative that may not be suitab le for a brand story that is community -
driven or collaborative. This review also shows a lack of empirical testing of the hero's-journey
brand frameworks. In addition, the empirical testing of the hero's-journey brand frameworks
is relatively scarce compared to their popularity among practitioners.
3.5 Transmedia Brand Storytelling
As brand content spreads across owned digital channels, earned media, social media, and
paid media, Henry  Jenkins' (2006) idea of transmedia storytelling – a story that unfolds in
different media, each source adding a unique, non -redundant element to a common
storyworld – has been embraced for the use of brand communication. (Granitz & Forman,
2015) explicitly use the transmedia perspective to explain how consumers create a relationship

3.6 Narrative Perspective and Focalization in Advertising
The more limited but well -developed body of experimental marketing research that has
directly operationalized the narratological notions of point of view and focalization outlined
in the previous section (Section 2.2). The study, published in Frontiers in Psychology 2024,
investigated the impact of the narrative perspective (first - vs. third -person) in storytelling
advertisements on consumer brand attitudes, explicitly manipulating the concept as
narratological distinction between point of view (narrator-behavior) and access to characters'
inner states (focalization), and revealed that the narrative perspective significantly influences
the persuasive outcomes, which was moderated by product involvement. This study was one
of the most transparent and methodologically straightforward empirical connections between
classic narratological constructs and the quantifiable results of brand communication in this
review.
4. Methodology
4.1 Review Protocol and Research Questions
This review was prepared in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 reporting guideline (Page et
al., 2021) and the management-research systematic review protocol proposed by Tranfield et
al. (2003), which is more transparent, reproducible, pre -specified, and less prone to selection
bias than a traditional narrative review. The investigation questions that were used for the
review are included in Section 1.
4.2 Eligibility Criteria
Eligibility criteria: (a) peer-reviewed journal articles, books or book chapters; (b) published in
English language; (c) relevant to a brand, marketing, advertising or consumer-behavior study;
(d) either (i) a study on brand, marketing, advertising or cons umer-behavior, or (ii) a
foundational narratological or philosophical text that was referred to extensively by the
marketing literature cited in (i) above.
Exclusion criteria were: (a) practitioner or blog content that is not peer reviewed and retained
only when used narrowly in the context of applied practice rather than as evidence; (b)
literature that discusses narrative in contexts of clinical, educationa l or political -
communication studies but lacks a marketing or a brandable context; (c) non-English sources;
and (d) sources for which full-text access could not be secured.
4.3 Search Strategy

narrative transportation, monomyth, the hero's journey, brand archetype, narrative identity,
transmedia storytelling) and marketing/brand terms (brand storytel ling, storytelling
advertising, content marketing, consumer narrative, narrative persuasion) in the Google

5. Descriptive Overview of the Reviewed Corpus
The total sample of 48 sources can be divided into two main groups. The first cluster (40% of
the corpus) is comprised of basic narratological, philosophical, and mythological theory (such
as that of Propp, Genette, Barthes, Todorov, Bal, Booth, Ricoeur, Jung, and Campbell), which
has been published in literary theory, philosophy, and psychology journals from 1928 to 2009.
The second, larger cluster (roughly 60% of the corpus) comprises empirical and conceptual
marketing, advertising, and consumer-research studies from journals that are closely allied to
the disciplines of marketing and advertising, such as the Journal of Consumer Research,
Psychology & Marketing, Journal of Advertising, Communication Monographs, Industrial
Marketing Management, Journal of B rand Management -adjacent outlets, and Frontiers in
Psychology, and published primarily between 1997 and 2026.
In terms of methodological approach, literature on the marketing side features more
experimental designs (testing narrative -perspective, transportation, or persuasion
manipulations) as well as more meta -analyses (Braddock & Dillard, 2016;  Van Laer et al.,
2014), with fewer conceptual/framework papers  (Zimand-Sheiner, 2026), and fewer
qualitative or case-based studies of archetypal branding (Bechter et al., 2016). The quantitative,
hypothesis-testing forms of the designs are much more common in the
transportation/persuasion theme than in the archetype/monomyth theme (which still has a
strong emphasis on conceptual and case -illustrative studies discussed further in Section 6.7
and Section 12).
6. Thematic Synthesis
6.1 Theme 1: Structural Transfer — From Fabula/Syuzhet to Brand Plot
The Formalist difference between story and plot arrangement (Propp, 1968) also emerges in
the marketing literature as the realization that the same information for the brand could be
presented in a different order for its dramatization or persuasion (Van Laer et al., 2014). Within
marketing, however, this structural understanding is not applied with the precision afforded
by narratology; in general, the presence or absence of a narrative structure is manipulated

instead of specific Proppian functions or Genettian discourse -level variables, which is an
under-exploited area for more granular experimental design.
6.2 Theme 2: Transportation and Persuasion Mechanisms
This is the most empirically developed theme in the corpus. Together, narrative transportation
theory (Green & Brock, 2000) , the extension of this theory into the transportation -imagery
model (Van Laer et al., 2014), and Braddock and Dillard's (2016) meta-analytic quantification
of narrative persuasion effect sizes represent a cumulative, well-replicated evidence base. The
systematic review by Thomas et al. (2024) further supports the dominant position of narrative
transportation theory and documents its ex tension to B2B, digital and cross -cultural
marketing contexts, while also highlighting the use of heterogeneous instruments for
measuring transportation and its antecedents/consequences, which makes it difficult to
compare across studies.
6.3 Theme 3: Narrative Identity and Brand Identity Construction
Hermeneutic theory of narrative identity (Ricoeur, 1984 –1988, 1992) is conceptually
appropriate to understanding the way brands create coherent identities over time and change,
as is Fournier's (1998) theory of the consumer -brand relational narrative, whic h also posits
that the meaning of a brand is accumulated and negotiated over a relational history rather
than fixed at a particular point in time. Yet, the review reveals that in the marketing literature,
Ricoeur's concepts have been cited very little dire ctly, compared with their frequency in
philosophical and narrative-psychological contexts, suggesting narrative identity theory is a
largely under-exploited theoretical tool for brand-identity research.
6.4 Theme 4: Archetype and Myth in Brand Meaning-Making
Campbell's monomyth (1949) and Mark and Pearson's (2001) operationalization of it are two
important and frequently cited themes, but one that is somewhat under-tested from a Jungian
archetype theory perspective (Jung, 1959/1968). A notable exception is Bec hter et al. (2016),
who correlated archetypal branding with Aaker's (1997) validated brand -personality
dimensions, which would be a step to more rigorous measurements. Despite this, the review's
synthesis indicates that the majority of archetype-branding scholarship has been classificatory
(classifying existing brands to archetypes) rather than predictive or experimental, which has
clear implications for the theoretical propositions that will be presented in Section 8.
6.5 Theme 5: Perspective, Voice, and Authenticity
The concept of focalization and narrator reliability (Genette, 1980; Booth, 1961) is the most
direct, and methodologically most rigorous, equivalent in the field of marketing when it
comes to first-person vs third person story -telling advertisements, where the impact of the
point of view of the narrator of an advertisement on brand attitude is measured as a function
of product involvement (Chen et al., 2024) . This theme demonstrates how narratological
constructs can be more easily translated into the area of marketing research as discrete and
manipulative independent variables than as loose metaphors.

6.7 Theme 7: Critiques and Boundary Conditions
In the corpus, there are several common criticisms that circulate around the complete transfer
of literary storytelling structures to brand settings. One, archetypal and monomythic models
are decried as "culturally narrow" (Western, individualistic) and "unfalsifiable", and mapping
is usually conducted retrospectively rather than anticipationally. Second, hero's -journey
brand messaging frameworks are claimed to be less suitable for a community -oriented or
collaboratively authored brand story, as they make the audience feel like an individual hero.
Third, even within the stronger, empirical theme in transportation/persuasion, Braddock and
Dillard (2016) point to the existence of "unidentified moderators," which suggests that effects
of narration on belief and  behavior are not consistent and warrant further, more nuanced
boundary-condition studies. Together, these reviews indicate that narrative theory is an
extremely fruitful vocabulary for marketing scholarship, but not an uncritically accepted one,
in which the claims of prediction and causality are yet to be rigorously tested.

8. Theoretical Propositions
Drawing on the synthesis in Sections 6 and 7, the following falsifiable propositions are
advanced to guide future empirical research:
• Narrative discourse -level sequencing (syuzhet) manipulations by a brand,
independent of story content (fabula), will have measurably different impacts on levels
of narrative transportation and persuasion, while holding informational content
constant.
• The internally focalized (character -perspective) brand narratives will produce more
positive brand attitudes than externally focalized brand narratives, and will produce
more effective transportation of the brand message, which will be moderated by
product involvement.
• Messages that rely on a Ricoeurian narrative -identity frame (focus on continuity of
"character" and consistency of "promise" over time) will generate higher brand trust
and brand relationship commitment over time than messages that focus solely on
brand personality traits.

9. Discussion
The main result of this review is an imbalance between the degree to which different narrative-
theoretical constructs are empirically tested when they are borrowed from literary theory to
the field of brand storytelling. Constructs were operationalized as discrete, manipulated
experimental variables (e.g., narrative transportation, narrative perspective/focalization) with
a relatively well-developed empirical base, comprising systematic reviews and meta-analyses
(Van Laer et al., 2014; Braddock & Dillard, 2016; Thomas et al., 2024). However, constructs that
are used more as metaphorical organizing constructs rather than operational variables, such
as the archetype, monomyth and hero's journey, are still used but are somewhat less
researched, and there are doubts about their validity even within branding literature (Bechter
et al., 2016).
The second finding is that Ricoeur's narrative -identity theory is not sufficiently used.
Although the review identified a conceptual connection between Ricoeur's understanding of
identity as emplotted continuity-through-change and the marketing challenge of maintaining
brand identity through repositioning, rebranding and cultural change, there was little direct
engagement or connection with Ricoeur in the marketing literature. This is a gap and an
opportunity: narrative-identity theory provides a philosophically sound alternative to purely
descriptive brand-story models and has the potential to inform research on brand heritage,
transgression/repair, and long-term brand-relationship continuity.
Third, the review indicates that the migration of narrative theory into marketing has been
selective and uneven, with constructs that are more easily adapted to experimental
psychology such as transportation and perspective more often being used than const ructs
drawn from structuralist text analysis such as fabula/syuzhet and narrative functions and
constructs from hermeneutic philosophy such as narrative identity. This selectivity is logical
in light of marketing's discipline focus on quantifiable and manipulable constructs, but it does
leave much narratological insight (especially in terms of narrative structure and identity)

10. Theoretical Contributions
The review can help marketing and consumer -research theory in three ways. Most
importantly, it offers the first systems approach to a synthesis matrix that directly links the
canonical narratological theory with the empirical brand-storytelling literature, thus resolving
the fragmentation issue identified in section 1. Second, it points to narrative identity theory
(Ricoeur) as an important, underutilized theoretical tool for brand -identity studies, different
from and complementary to brand -personality (Aak er, 1997) and brand -relationship
(Fournier, 1998) theories. Third, it presents eight testable, falsifiable theoretical propositions
(Section 8) that make the narratological concepts operational as separate variables to be subject
to further experimental and cross-cultural marketing studies.
11. Managerial Implications
This review has three practical implications for brand and marketing communication
practitioners. The first is that there is more solid empirical data for investing in narrative
transportation-oriented storytelling (immersive, character-driven brand content that doesn't
overwhelm with explicit arguing) than for investing in what's been proven to be more
effective: archetypal brand messaging. Second, narrative perspective (first -person versus
third-person framing) is a creative lever that can be tested and i s within the reach of the low
budget, and one that experimental evidence suggests has significant effects on brand attitudes,
which should be added to A/B testing protocols for storytelling advertising. Third, brands that
are trying to craft a multi -platform (transmedia) storytelling strategy must perform an audit
of their story's meaning (voice) in their strategy and its execution (presentation) across all
platforms, as found to be a frequent cause of diluted brand-narrative impact (Zimand-Sheiner,
2026).
12. Limitations
This review's findings are qualified by several limitations. First, most importantly, the search
and screening (Section 4.6) was undertaken on AI -assisted scholarly discovery, not through
direct access to Scopus, Web of Science or ABI/INFORM Complete; although all sources were
checked individually, the counts in the review are a working document and should not be
regarded as a definitive export from any database and should be replicated using institutional
access to those databases before submitting. Second , the corpus is biased towards English -
language, Western-scholarly research and may be less than generalizable to other traditions
of narratives and to other brands.
The review of the corpus highlights narrative structures of the individualist single protagonist
(e.g., the monomyth) in contrast to the collectivist cultural values, multi -generational
household purchase decision-making, and oral/communal storytelling tra ditions that are of
particular significance for brand -narrative research in South and Southeast Asian markets.
The customer-as-hero narrative, as described in Section 6.7, might be particularly unfit for
brand narratives in markets where these narratives a re more collectively created, such as
when the customer is involved. Further empirical research on the propositions in Section 8,
especially P5, in the South Asian and other emerging -market settings would be relevantly
valuable to the review's scant Western evidence base.

13. Future Research Agenda
• Experimentally test manipulations of the discourse level of narrative on the basis of
direct manipulation of the sequencing and focalization, as suggested in the
frameworks of Genette and Propp, not only in the broad sense of narrative versus non-
narrative manipulations.
• Conduct and validate a brand-narrative-identity scale based on Ricoeur's perspective
of continuity of brand "character" and consistency of brand "promise" over time, and
test the predictive validity for long -term brand trust and forgiveness after
transgression.
• Perform a formal meta -analysis using proprietary database -sourced data on the
predictive validity of archetype -consistent versus archetype -inconsistent brand
messaging, to determine if there is additional predictive value in archetype theory that
goes beyond existing brand-personality constructs.
• Test the hero's -journey brand messaging frameworks in emerging markets with
individualist and collectivist cultures (including South Asian countries) to assess the
cultural boundary conditions proposed in Section 6.7.
• Empirically, the consistency of transmedia brand narratives according to firm size and
attributes of the marketing team.
• Include some form of narrative transportation measurement with some method of
measuring physiology or behavior to build the evidence base for causal mechanisms
beyond self-report measures.
14. Conclusion
What came to be known as narrative theory, a way of understanding the formal organization
of classic fiction, has traveled – if not quite evenly – into the scholarship and application of
brand stories. This review has demonstrated that the constructs that can be manipulated in an
experimental manner — narrative transportation, narrative perspective — have a relatively
mature empirical foundation, whereas the more complex constructs that cannot be
manipulated in an experimental manner, such as narrative iden tity, archetype and
monomyth, continue to be used in practice but are under -researched and, in some instances,
conceptually ambiguous. This review aims to highlight the theoretical rigor that marketing
science will have to develop to embrace literary theor y that it is so much a part of, and to
provide a more transparent empirical agenda at the nexus of narratology and brand
communication.
