Abstract. Amid accelerating brand globalization and the rise of visual consumption culture among
Generation Z, questions about how gender identity is encoded into intellectual property (IP) character
design have gained renewed theoretical urgency. This study examines how Pop Mart, a leading Chinese
designer toy brand, deploys gender -fluid and androgynous IP character  design as a strategic mechanism
for cross -cultural branding on Douyin (China) and TikTok (United States). Drawing on 17 semi -
structured in -depth interviews with active consumers from both markets an d a narrative analysis of six
brand advertisements published between 2023 and 2025, this paper investigates how non -binary aesthetic
choices embedded in characters such as Molly, Dimoo, Skullpanda, and Labubu shape brand recognition,
emotional resonance, a nd consumer identity across culturally distinct audiences. Guided by post -gender
brand theory, glocalization theory, and visual narrative analysis, findings reveal that Pop Mart's
androgynous design strategy transcends traditional binary gender marketing b y positioning characters as
identity-neutral projective surfaces that consumers personalise through emotional engagement. Results
further demonstrate that gender fluidity in IP design operates differently across cultural contexts: Chinese
consumers emphasi se cultural belonging and aesthetic tradition alongside gender ambiguity, while
American consumers foreground individual identity expression and stylistic diversity. The study
contributes an empirically grounded framework linking gender -fluid visual design , cross -cultural
consumer engagement, and digital brand storytelling, offering both theoretical insights and practical
implications for global lifestyle brands operating in a post-gender media landscape.

Introduction
Contemporary brand communication has entered a phase defined by the
entanglement of visual culture, identity politics, and platform -mediated interaction. As
global consum er markets become increasingly saturated with experience -driven
products, brands can no longer rely solely on functional differentiation; they must
negotiate symbolic meaning across cultural and demographic divides. Within this
context, the designer toy in dustry, exemplified by Pop Mart , has emerged as a
compelling site for examining how gender aesthetics, character -based intellectual
property (IP) design, and cross -cultural brand storytelling converge. Pop Mart, founded
in Beijing in 2010 by Wang Ning, has  rapidly evolved from a domestic blind box toy
retailer into a globally recognized lifestyle brand with significant traction in Asia, North
America, and Europe. Central to this expansion is the brand's portfolio of IP characters,
including Molly, Dimoo, Sk ullpanda, Labubu, and Crybaby, whose visual identities
intentionally resist binary gender classification. Unlike conventional toy marketing,
which historically assigns products to either a masculine or feminine market segment

post-gender branding strategy (Yin et al., 2025; Holt, 2004), and it raises important
questions about how gendered visual meaning is produced, received, and repurposed in
cross-cultural digital environments.
The rise of short -video platforms , specifically Douyin in China and TikTok in the
United States , has transformed the conditions of brand storytelling. These platforms
function not merely as distribution channels but as cultural systems that shape the very
grammar of visual and emotional communication. Brands operating across Douyin and
TikTok must contend with markedly different algorithmic logics, user participation
cultures, and aesthetic conventions (Zhou, 2024; Zhou and Belk, 2004). For a brand like
Pop Mar t, whose core communicative strategy depends on emotionally resonant
character narratives, these platform differences create both challenge and opportunity:
the same IP character must speak to a user in Chengdu who values collective cultural
memory and to a user in New York who is drawn to stylistic nonconformity.  Despite
growing scholarly interest in brand communication, gender studies, and cross -cultural
marketing, few empirical studies have examined how gender -fluid IP design functions
as a specific stra tegic mechanism within cross -cultural branding. Existing literature
tends to treat gendered design and cross -cultural communication as parallel, non -
intersecting concerns. This gap is particularly acute in the study of emerging Asian
lifestyle brands whose  global ascent challenges the Eurocentric assumptions embedded
in much brand communication theory ( De Mooij, 2019 ). This paper addresses the gap
by examining how Pop Mart encodes gender fluidity into its IP characters and how this
encoding generates differ ential resonance across Chinese and American consumer
audiences on two culturally distinct social media platforms.
The study is guided by three research questions: (1) How is gender fluidity encoded
in Pop Mart's IP character design across Douyin and TikTo k content? (2) How do
Chinese and American consumers perceive and engage with Pop Mart's androgynous IP
characters? (3) How does gender -fluid IP design contribute to cross -cultural brand
recognition and identity construction? By answering these questions t hrough qualitative
interview data and narrative analysis, this paper contributes to theoretical discourse at
the intersection of gender studies, brand communication, and platform media, while
offering actionable insights for practitioners engaged in global  lifestyle brand
management.

Literature review
Gendered design in consumer products and brand communication
Gendered design has long shaped the relationship between consumer products and
identity. Toy marketing has been particularly implicated in the construction and
reproduction of gender norms: research consistently demonstrates that products color -
coded pink reinforce feminine associations while those in blue or neutrals reinforce
masculine ones ( Davis and Hines, 2020; Kollmayer et al., 2018 ). Knoll  et al. (2011 ),
examining gender role portrayals in German television advertising, found that female
characters were systematically associated with aesthetic and domestic roles while male
characters were coded for authority and professional competence. Gra u and Zotos

brand practice. Prompted by shifting consumer demographics, the mainstreaming of
queer aesthetics, and the rise of Generation Z audiences who actively resist identity
categorisation, a growing number of brands have adopted gender-neutral or gender-fluid
design philosophies (Yin et al., 2025). Holt (2004) theorized that brands increasingly
function as cultural intermediaries capable of redistributing symbolic resources; post -
gender branding leverages this capacity by dissolving conventional aesthetic bina ries.
Truong (2025) noted, however, that the academic study of gender -neutral design across
non-Western cultural contexts remains underdeveloped, a gap that the present study
addresses through its focus on a Chinese brand operating in both Eastern and West ern

IP characters, visual narrative, and emotional brand identity
Intellectual property (IP) characters are increasingly recognized as potent vehicles of
brand identity construction. Unlike product logos or slogans, IP characters can carry
multi-layered emotional and narrative meaning, enabling what Aaker and
Joachimsthaler (2000) termed brand association richness. Holt (2002) proposed that
brands become cultural icons through co -construction of meaning with audiences; IP
characters operationalize this dynamic by providing consumers with projective surfaces
onto which personal narratives, aspirations, and identities can be mapped. In the context
of designer toy culture, Zhou (2025) emphasized that characters must possess emotional
resonance and derivative narrative potential to sustain consumer attachment beyond the
initial purchase. Ryan (2004) multi -dimensional narrative model offers a robust
analytical frame for understanding how IP characters generate meaning across media.
Ryan identifies six constitutive dimensions: narrativity, tellability, temporality,
worldmaking, a gency, and moral evaluation. Applied to brand communication, this
framework reveals how a single character can sustain coherent identity across disparate
platforms, genres, and cultural contexts. Van Laer et al. (2019) extended this line of
thinking through the concept of immersive digital narrative experience, demonstrating
that consumers who achieve narrative transportation, whereby they become absorbed in
brand story worlds, exhibit stronger brand attitudes and greater loyalty. Escalas (2004)
provided em pirical grounding for this claim, showing that narrative self -referencing
mediates the relationship between brand storytelling and consumer identification.
Recent scholarship has begun to examine the specific intersection of gender ambiguity
and emotional resonance in visual narrative. Connell and Messerschmidt (2005),
revisiting hegemonic masculinity theory, noted the emergence of alternative
masculinities that privilege emotional expressiveness; characters such as Pop Mart's
Dimoo, whose design features s oft contours, large melancholic eyes, and nurturing
symbolic associations, can be read as instantiating this shift. Bravo Velázquez et al.
(2023) demonstrated that androgynous visual stimuli elicit broader audience
identification than gender-coded stimuli across East Asian consumer samples, providing
empirical support for the commercial logic underlying gender-fluid design.

Cross-cultural brand communication theory has evolved considerably since De Mooij
(2010) foundational articulation of the paradoxes of global marketing. De Mooij argued
that cultural value dimensions fundamentally shape consumer reception of brand
messages, such that strategies effective in individualistic, low -context communication
cultures may fail in collectivist, high -context ones. Hollensen (2019) extended this
insight by theorizing the tension between global brand consistency and local cultural
adaptation, proposing that successful global brands achieve dynamic calibration rather
than static compromise. Robertson (1995) concept of glocalization , the mutual
constitution of global and local forces in brand practice , has been widely applied to
platform media brand strategies. Vo et al. (2026) demonstrated that KOL -mediated
narratives on Chinese social media can accelerate cultural bridging by embedding
global brand values within locally resonant contexts. Kizgin et al. (2018) further showed
that social media platforms enable brands to establish cross -cultural influence precisely
by facilitating consumer co -creation of cultural identity. In the context of short -video
platforms, Rejeb et al. (2024) documented how Douyin a nd TikTok create
algorithmically differentiated visual cultures that require brands to pursue platform -
specific aesthetic localisation while preserving overarching narrative coherence.  What
remains underexplored in this literature is the specific role of g ender-fluid design as a
glocalization mechanism. If gender ambiguity in IP characters functions as a kind of
visual lingua franca , legible across cultural boundaries precisely because it does not
demand culturally specific gender decoding, then it may constitute a distinctive resource
for cross-cultural brand communication. This paper contributes to filling this gap.
Materials and Methods
This study adopts a dual qualitative research design integrating thematic analysis of
semi-structured in-depth interviews with narrative analysis of brand advertising content.
This methodological pairing enables simultaneous access to audience -level meaning -
making processes and brand-level communication strategies, providing a more complete
account of how gender -fluid IP de sign functions in cross -cultural branding than either
approach could yield alone. A total of 17 purposively sampled participants were
recruited from China (n = 10) and the United States (n = 7). Inclusion criteria required
participants to be between 18 and  35 years of age, to be active users of Douyin or
TikTok, and to have regular purchasing or engagement experience with Pop Mart
products. This demographic range was selected because it corresponds to Pop Mart's
core consumer base and because Generation Z a nd young millennial consumers exhibit
the highest self -reported sensitivity to gender representation in brand communication
(Yin et al., 2025). Each semi -structured interview lasted approximately 30 minutes and
was conducted online. Interview topics addres sed IP character preference, gender
perception, brand awareness, and cultural belonging. Data were transcribed and
analysed using thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke (2006) six-phase reflexive
approach. For the narrative analysis component, six br and advertisements were
systematically selected from Pop Mart's official Douyin and TikTok accounts for the
period 2023 –2025: three advertisements from each platform. Selection prioritised
advertisements featuring core IP characters (Molly, Dimoo, Skullpan da) and ensured
temporal distribution across the study period. Advertisements were analysed using Ryan
(2004) six-dimensional narrative model: narrativity, tellability, temporality,
worldmaking, agency, and moral evaluation. Gender coding dimensions, inclu ding

Results and Discussion
Gender fluidity in IP character design: Narrative analysis findings
Results are organized around three intersecting analytical axes: (1) the encoding of
gender fluidity in IP character design, examined through narrative analysis of six
advertisements; (2) audience reception of androgynous characters across Chinese and
American consumer samples, derived from thematic interview analysis; and (3) the
contribution of gender -fluid design to cross -cultural brand recognition.  Across all six
advertisements analysed, Pop Mart's IP characters consistently exhib it visual design
choices that destabilize binary gender codes. Molly, the brand's inaugural character,
combines a rounded childlike silhouette with wardrobe and contextual elements drawn
from Chinese cultural tradition, Hanfu costume, rabbit ears, lantern motifs, that carry
neither masculine nor feminine connotation in their own right. The 2023 Douyin
advertisement "Molly Lantern Show" places her within a collectivized festival space,
positioning her as a cultural everyman figure whose gender is rendered ir relevant by
ritual participation. On TikTok, the 2023 "Molly Imaginary Wandering" advertisement
instead emphasizes imaginative autonomy and aesthetic free play, qualities whose
cultural valence is similarly gender -neutral within a North American visual reg ister.
Dimoo presents a more nuanced case of androgynous design. The character's elongated
proportions, oversized melancholic eyes, and consistent positioning as an emotionally
attuned, quietly heroic figure draw simultaneously on conventions of soft mascu linity
and expressive femininity without fully inhabiting either. The 2024 TikTok
advertisement "Dimoo Weaving Wonders" is particularly instructive: Dimoo is depicted
as collecting star-wishes and confronting cloud -monsters through quiet, non -aggressive
action, evoking what Connell and Messerschmidt (2005) termed an alternative
masculinity while also resonating with broader post -gender identity frameworks. The
absence of verbal dialogue in this advertisement delegates meaning entirely to visual
character cu es, maximizing cross -cultural legibility.Skullpanda occupies the most
radical position within Pop Mart's gender -fluid design spectrum. Both Douyin
advertisements analysed ("Skullpanda The Mirage," 2025) and TikTok advertisements
("Skullpanda Impressionism," 2025) deliberately move the character away from the cute
aesthetic conventions typical of the designer toy genre, a strategy the data supports
reading as "de -cutification", toward a philosophical -aesthetic register in which gender
becomes an irrelevant i nterpretive category. The character's cold color palette, angular
silhouette, and symbolic associations with mirrors, fragments, and temporal dislocation
construct a visual world in which identity is fluid, historical, and metaphysically open.
This strateg y corresponds to Yin et al. (2025) formulation of post -gender brand
communication, in which the dissolution of gender markers amplifies rather than
reduces consumer identification (Table 1).

Audience reception: Thematic analysis of consumer interviews
Thematic analysis of interview data identified gender fluidity as the single most
consistently salient feature of Pop Mart's brand identity across both samples, although
its meaning and function differ con siderably across markets. Among Chinese
consumers, gender ambiguity in character design is frequently interpreted through the
lens of aesthetic freedom, a liberation from the constraining visual logic of traditional
gender-coded toys rather than an explici t political claim about gender identity.
Participant CN -09's description of Dimoo as "like a little boy and a little girl, which
feels very free" exemplifies this interpretive register. For Chinese consumers, this
aesthetic freedom is importantly tethered to cultural symbolism: characters that embed
traditional Chinese visual elements (Hanfu, seasonal motifs, ink -wash aesthetics) within
a gender-neutral design framework enable consumers to experience cultural belonging
and contemporary identity fluidity sim ultaneously. American consumers articulate
gender-fluid design in more explicitly anti-binary terms. Participant US-09's assessment
of Skullpanda as "genderless" reflects an interpretive framework shaped by the visibility
of non-binary and queer identity discourses within North American popular culture. For
this consumer group, the absence of explicit gender coding functions as an invitation to
project individual identity without negotiating cultural specificity. Participant US -04's
observation that "these dolls don't have the traditional 'male' or 'female' look" positions
gender ambiguity as the key value differentiator in a marketplace otherwise dominated
by gender -targeted collector figures. Taken together, these findings support Bravo
Velázquez et al. (2023) demonstration that androgynous visual stimuli generate broader
audience identification than gender -coded stimuli, while extending their analysis by
showing that the mechanisms of identification differ systematically across cultural
contexts. For Chine se consumers, identification is mediated by cultural symbolism; for
American consumers, it is mediated by stylistic nonconformity. This distinction has
significant implications for cross-cultural brand strategy (Table 2).

Gender-fluid design and cross-cultural brand recognition
The findings collectively support a reading of Pop Mart's gender -fluid IP design as a
glocalization mechanism in the sense theorised by Robertson (1995): a design language
that achieves local cultural embedding and global aesthetic coherence simultaneously by
refusing to specify the very identity coordinates, gender, cultural alignment, age , that
typically constrain brand meaning. This refusal functions differently across markets, as
Table 3  demonstrates, but its underlying logic is consistent: by designing characters
whose visual grammar is open rather than closed, Pop Mart creates IP that consumers in
markedly different cultural contexts can use to construct personally meaningful brand
relationships. This finding resonates with Escalas (2004) narrative transportation theory:
the gender ambiguity of Pop Mart characters lowers the threshold f or narrative self -
insertion by removing the identity screening that gender -coded characters impose. A
consumer who does not identify with conventional feminine or masculine aesthetics can
nonetheless fully inhabit the emotional world of Skullpanda precisel y because
Skullpanda makes no such demand. This mechanism is magnified in digital short -video
environments where, as Van Laer et al. (2019) demonstrated, immersive narrative
conditions maximise the brand attitude effects of transportation. The differential
functioning of gender -fluid design across Chinese and American markets also enriches
our understanding of cultural branding theory. De Mooij (2019) cautioned that
overemphasis on cultural adaptability risks fragmenting global brand identity; the
present findings suggest that gender -fluid design resolves this tension by operating as a
culturally translatable visual constant whose interpretation, rather than whose meaning,
varies by market. The characters themselves do not change; what changes is the cultura l
framework consumers use to decode and inhabit them. This constitutes a distinctive
contribution to glocalization theory, particularly in the context of Asian lifestyle brands
entering Western markets.  Finally, the findings speak to the risk of cultural
essentialization noted by Iwabuchi (2010). Pop Mart's deployment of Chinese
traditional symbols (Hanfu, cranes, lanterns) within a gender -neutral design frame
creates opportunities for cultural connection, but the data also suggest that overreliance
on fixe d cultural totems may constrain the brand's capacity for ongoing symbolic
innovation. Brands pursuing gender -fluid cross -cultural strategies must balance the
specificity of cultural reference with the openness that makes androgynous design
strategically valuable.

Conclusion
This study demonstrates that gender -fluid IP character design functions as a
distinctive and strategically consequential mechanism in Pop Mart's cross -cultural
branding. By embedding androgynous visual aesthetics within emotionally resonant
character narratives, Pop Mart constructs IP that s imultaneously satisfies the cultural
specificity demands of Chinese consumers and the identity openness demands of
American consumers. The mechanism operates through four interdependent design
dimensions: androgynous morphology, culturally embedded gender neutrality, non -
verbal emotional narrative, and platform -adapted aesthetic register. Theoretically, the
study advances glocalization theory by demonstrating that gender -fluid design can
function as a culturally translatable visual constant —a design strateg y whose
interpretation, rather than whose meaning, varies by market. It extends post -gender
brand theory by showing that gender ambiguity in IP characters does not merely avoid
exclusion but actively generates broader consumer identification by functioning  as a
projective surface for diverse identity claims. It contributes to narrative transportation
theory by demonstrating that the absence of gendered identity cues lowers the threshold
for narrative self-insertion in digital brand storytelling environments.
Practically, the findings offer guidance for global lifestyle brands seeking to develop
cross-cultural IP portfolios. Designers and brand managers should resist the temptation
to gender -code characters as a targeting mechanism: for Generation Z audiences
operating in platform media environments, androgynous aesthetics function more
effectively than binary gender coding as a driver of emotional engagement and brand
loyalty. Platform localisation should adjust the contextual and cultural frame of
character narratives without altering their fundamental visual identity. Future research
should expand the geographic scope of this inquiry to include Southeast Asian and
European markets, where gender norms and platform media cultures differ significantly
from the Chinese and American contexts studied here. Longitudinal analysis of how
consumer perceptions of Pop Mart's IP characters evolve as the brand's global footprint
expands would further illuminate the dynamics of gender-fluid brand identity over time.
Multimodal discourse analysis approaches that incorporate audio and movement
alongside visual design would offer additional depth of insight into the affective
mechanisms through which androgynous characters generate cross-cultural resonance.
