This secondary research paper systematically explores the role of
storytelling as a strategic driver of luxury brand equity. Physical
scarcity, conspicuous consumption and heritage are no longer the main
tools in luxury brand management. Today, it is more and more about
narrative strategies to communicate authenticity, justify premium
pricing and generate symbolic resonance. This paper combines the core
frameworks such as Keller‟s Customer -Based Brand Equity model,
Aaker‟s Brand Equity framework, Green and Brock's Narrative
Transportation Theory, and Spence‟s Signalling Theory. It utilises
secondary empirical and theoretical literature published in leading
marketing and business research journals from 2000 to 2026. The
synthesis indicates that the narrative dimensions, founder genesis
myths, heritage preservation, artisanal craftsmanship an d cultural
patronage are significant contributors to perceived quality, brand
loyalty, emotional attachment and price premium tolerance. Moreover,
the analysis also considers the paradox of traditional exclusivity versus
modern digital transmedia dissemina tion. The analysis discusses how
luxury brands manage digital platforms without compromising brand
prestige. Finally, new trends including hyper -personalised artificial
intelligence narratives, immersive virtual heritage environments and
ethical provenance  storytelling are reviewed. The paper ends with
action-oriented strategic recommendations for managers of luxury
brands and outlines key directions for future empirical investigation.

Introduction:-
Introduction and Background of the Study:-
Traditionally, the global luxury sector has relied on tangible scarcity, superior material quality, high entry price
points and restricted distribution channels as its value proposition. But for the past 30 years, structural changes in
global wealth, consumer demographics, and digital communications technology have transformed the mechanics of
luxury value creation. Physical scarcity and material craftsmanship, while still necessary baseline conditions, are no
longer sufficient by themselve s to maintain competitive advantage or command premium price margins in saturated
international markets. Today, luxury purchases are increasingly providing symbolic, experiential and psychological
value to modern consumers, shifting luxury consumption from  a status-driven functional transaction to an identity -
expressive narrative experience.Storytelling has become a key pillar of luxury brand strategy in response to this

Research Problem:-
The contemporary luxury brand is infused with a basic tension in its management: the paradox of exclusivity and
digital accessibility. For decades, luxury brand equity has been built on distance, rarity and controlled exposure. The
growth of digital media, social networks and e -commerce platforms demands a co ntinuous dissemination of
narratives and wide engagement with the audience. The dangers of an unfettered digital presence include
overexposure and brand dilution, and being too enthusiastic can make a brand irrelevant to younger consumer
groups such as Mil lennials and Generation Z.Furthermore, although strategic brand storytelling is considered to be
critical in the practitioner literature, academic literature shows disconnected views regarding the direct effects of
specific narrative dimensions (e.g., foun der myths versus current ethical narratives) on various elements of
Customer-Based Brand Equity (CBBE). However, a thorough synthesis of narrative transportation theory, brand
signalling, and equity frameworks is still needed to explain how narrative const ructs can lead to tangible equity
outcomes in contemporary globalised luxury markets.

Research Objectives:-
 The main objective of this secondary research paper is to evaluate the theoretical and practical connections
between strategic storytelling and luxury brand equity. In particular, the research aims to:
 Systematically review the existing secondary literature on narrative antecedents and their influence on
consumer perception of luxury brand equity.
 We investigate the theoretical pathways, i.e., Narrat ive Transportation and Signalling Theory, that enable brand
stories to influence perceived quality, brand authenticity and price premium tolerance.
 Analyse the strategic management of the „luxury paradox‟ in digital transmedia storytelling and platform
expansion.
 Recognise emerging technologies and ethics trends impacting how luxury brands tell their story, such as AI,
immersive virtual environments and sustainability messaging.

Research questions:-
To achieve the research objectives, this study addresses four main research questions:-
 RQ1: How do key dimensions of narratives (founding myths, heritage, craftsmanship, cultural patronage)
influence the main dimensions of luxury brand equity?
 RQ2: What are the theoretical mechanisms that explain the link between brand narrative immersion and
consumer willingness to pay a price premium?
 RQ3: How do luxury brands sustain narrative authenticity and brand exclusivity while using wide -reach digital
and social media channels?
 RQ4: What are the key managerial implications, ethical considerations and technological shifts disrupting
luxury storytelling in current research?

Scope and Importance:-
This study is dedicated to luxury goods in the fashion, leathe r goods, haute horlogerie, high jewellery and premium
automotive segments. The review assesses peer -reviewed literature and high -impact secondary empirical studies
published in top business, marketing and consumer research journals. This paper integrates v arious conceptual
streams of brand equity, consumer psychology, narrative transportation, and digital communication to present an
integrated strategic framework. For academics, it fills important gaps in the literature on narrative -based value
creation. For marketing practitioners, it provides actionable strategies for using storytelling to preserve equity over
the long term.

Literature Review:-
Conceptualising luxury brand equity:-
Brand equity is the incremental value that a brand name, symbol or identity adds to a product or service. In general
consumer markets, brand equity is built primarily on functional utility, competitive pricing, broad awareness and

consistent service delive ry. Conversely, luxury brand equity is built upon perceptions of symbolic value,
psychological resonance, perceived prestige, perceived quality and controlled accessibility. The foundation of luxury
equity is based on the models of Keller (1993) and Aaker (1991).

Keller‟s Customer-Based Brand Equity (CBBE) model proposes that equity is developed sequentially in four steps:
identity of the brand (awareness), brand meaning (performance and imagery), brand responses (judgements and
feelings) and brand relationships (resonance). “In luxury environments, images and emotional responses matter a lot
more than just functional performance. Similarly, Aaker‟s framework organises equity in terms of brand loyalty,
brand awareness, perceived quality, brand associations and proprietary assets. For luxury entities, perceived quality
is strongly associated with perceived craftsmanship and brand associations are rooted in heritage, social status and
artistic narrative.

Storytelling Antecedents:-
The academic literature reco gnises four major storytelling antecedents that influence and sustain luxury
brand equity:-
 Founder Genesis Myths:  Tales of the vision, struggle, ingenuity and original artistry of the brand‟s creator
(Coco Chanel, Louis Vuitton, Enzo Ferrari, etc.) These myths endow the brand with a human quality, a sacred
origin no competitor can replicate.
 Heritage & Longevity: Stories of Historical Endurance, Royal Endorsements, Survival Through Cultural Ages,
and Timeless Continuity. Heritage stories build trust, commu nicate durability and turn commercial products
into timeless heirloom assets.
 Artisanal Craftsmanship and Provenance:  Tales emphasising artisanal techniques, strict material selection,
geographic provenance (e.g., Swiss watchmaking, French haute couture, I talian leatherworking), and labour -
intensive processes. And these are stories that can justify extreme price premiums by displaying objective
mastery.
 Cultural Patronage and Artistic Alliances:  The narratives that link the brand to fine arts, architectural
masterpieces, cinematic heritage and high -culture events. This makes the luxury house not only a commercial
institution but a cultural one.

Storytelling in the Digital Age:-
The shift to digital ecosystems has transformed luxury storytelling from linear,  one-way corporate broadcasts to
multi-directional transmedia experiences. Transmedia storytelling is the process of distributing unique, yet related,
narrative content across multiple digital platforms, including short films, interactive social media camp aigns, mobile
apps, and virtual environments in an effort to create a cohesive narrative universe.

Yet digital storytelling creates a management dilemma known as the 'luxury paradox'. Luxury requires distance,
mystery and exclusivity; digital media requir es immediacy, accessibility and high frequency. Research shows that
luxury brands resolve this paradox through “controlled intimacy”. Instead of disclosing all operational details or
using discount -oriented promotional rhetoric, successful luxury houses us e digital channels to grant behind -the-
scenes access to ateliers, display master artisans, and show artistic cinema. It preserves the brand's lofty positioning,
cultivating emotional intimacy with global digital audiences.

Haute couture and haute horlogerie: omnichannel dissemination and transmedia archetypes :-
Advanced understanding of transmedia architecture is necessary to strategically tell stories across the ecosystem of
today‟s luxury enterprises. Traditional multi-channel marketing is about repeating the same narrative content across
different media touchpoints. Transmedia storytelling is about breaking up a brand‟s overarching mythos into
different, complementary narrative pieces that are delivered through sp ecialised platforms. In high fashion and haute
horlogerie, this architecture allows luxury houses to reconcile the fundamental operational tension between mass
digital visibility and exclusive brand aura. Luxury brands create immersive symbolic universes b y connecting
specific narrative archetypes with targeted digital, physical and virtual dissemination channels to involve various
consumer segments without losing perceived prestige.

In high fashion (haute couture and ready -to-wear), transmedia architectur e often draws on short -form cinematic
films, interactive Web3 environments, spatial media, and virtual flagship spaces that project particular narrative
layers. For example, the linear, highly curated media of documentary films, physical archive exhibition s, and coffee-
table monographs are left for ancestral genesis and artisanal heritage. Conversely, today‟s brand transformations,
artistic collaborations, and seasonal thematic universes are expressed by vibrant digital media such as augmented
reality (AR) filters, interactive virtual catwalks, and spatial computing environments. This channel differentiation
secures the core brand image rooted in historical legitimacy, while interactive digital touchpoints enable current
relevance and affective engagement for younger consumer cohorts.

Transmedia storytelling is heavily reliant on archetypes of technical provenance and temporal lineage within haute
horlogerie, where mechanical complexity and micro -engineering dominate the value proposition. Digital product
passports, interactive 3D visualisations of movements and virtual visits to the manufacture help watchmakers to
demystify complex horological mechanics without losing the mythical status of the master watchmaker. Horological
houses turn technical specificati ons into narrative journeys of human patience, historical continuity, and material
excellence through virtual flagships and Web3 provenance tracking. Spatial media enables collectors to explore
micro-components in virtual environments, reinforcing the narr ative signal of uncompromised precision and
artisanal dedication.

Cross-cultural consumer psychology and regional socio -economic conditions significantly moderate the
effectiveness of luxury brand storytelling. Although the core narrative antecedents of founder genesis and artisanal
craftsmanship have a global appeal, their psychological reception and translation into brand equity differ widely
between mature Western markets and eme rging luxury markets in Asia and the Middle East.In mature Western

sophistication develops in these markets, the leading luxury houses are e xperiencing a convergence of younger,

Theoretical Framework:-
Theory of Narrative Transportation:-
Narrative Transportation Theory (Green & Brock, 2000) proposes that absorption in a narrative leads to an
integrative mental process that involves imagery, affect, and suspension of disbelief. Narrative transportation alters
message processing in luxury  marketing. Unlike the analytical and critical processing of functional product
evaluations, transported consumers experience greater empathy with story protagonists (e.g., master artisans,
visionary founders). This psychological absorption diminishes coun ter-arguing, enhances emotional connection and
establishes strong beliefs about the intrinsic value and prestige of the brand.

Conceptually, three central psychological dimensions can be distinguished that operate within narrative
transportation: Suspension of Disbelief, Affective Identification, and Narrative Schema Consistency. Suspension of
disbelief is the JEDI mind trick that allows the consumer‟s analytical cognitive faculties to be temporarily bypassed,
reducing critical scrutiny of premium pricing or utility claims. Affective identification is the emotional alignment of
the consumer with the brand‟s main protagonists (e.g. historical artisans or visionary founders) and the resulting
intense feelings of admiration, pride and personal resonance. The consistency of the narrative schema is the extent to
which the new brand narratives are consistent with the existing mental models of the consumer on the brand‟s
identity history. Therefore, the new narrative should not create cognitive dissonance w ith the existing mental models
of the consumer and hence enhance brand trust.

Signalling Theory and Brand Authenticity:-
According to Signalling Theory (Spence, 1973), credible signals are used to overcome information asymmetry
between buyers and sellers.  In luxury markets, where it can be difficult for consumers to objectively evaluate the
intrinsic quality of the product prior to purchase, brand storytelling is a costly and difficult -to-counterfeit signal of
authenticity, uncompromised standards and prestige. Becker, Wetzels, van Doorn and Grewal (2019) posit that story
elements like documented archival heritage, certified geographical origin and lineage of master craftsmen act as
reliable signals. These signals mitigate perceived consumer risk, reinforce  brand authenticity and rationalise
structural price premiums.

Costly signalling mechanisms are crucial structural pillars in the framework of artisanal craftsmanship and heritage
management. Real luxury storytelling is based on verifiable, historical and  resource-intensive commitments like
decades of artisanal training, localised production in traditional geographic clusters, and hand -selected rare
materials. These elements of narrative cannot be easily copied or faked by mass -market competitors. The high
capital and temporal investments needed to maintain these operational commitments make the resulting brand
stories exceptionally credible signals of quality, protecting the brand‟s long -term equity and structural price
premiums from dilution.

Customer-Based Brand Equity Model:-
Definition:-
The combination of Keller‟s (1993) CBBE framework with luxury storytelling reveals that narrative inputs are
active at all levels of the equity pyramid. Compelling stories enhance brand salience at the base of top -of-mind
awareness. At the second level, the antecedents of narrative are used to create meaning for the brand, turning the
basic product specifications into images of craftsmanship and heritage. The third level of narrative transportation
evokes strong posi tive responses such as pride, self -enhancement, and emotional attachment. Continuous narrative
engagement at its best results in true brand resonance, which is characterised by active brand communities, repeat
purchases and deep brand loyalty.

Integrated Conceptual Model of:-
The relationships synthesised across these theoretical frameworks are conceptualised in a sequential
structural process:-
1. Narrative Antecedents (Inputs):  Founder Genesis Myths, Heritage and History, Artisanal Craftsmanship and
Provenance, Cultural Patronage.
2. Psychological Processing Mechanisms (Mediators): Narrative Transportation (Absorption), Brand
Authenticity Signalling, Reduced Counter-Arguing, Affective Identification & Empathy
3. Brand Equity Dimensions (Outputs): Enhanced Perceive d Quality, Higher Brand Resonance & Passion,
Greater Brand Loyalty, Price Premium Tolerance.
This integrated flow shows that storytelling is not just a promotional wrap. It is a fundamental value creation engine
that directly translates narrative content into measurable economic and relational equity outcomes.

Role of Brand Authenticity as a Dual-Mediator (Constructive and Existential Authenticity):-
The psychological relationship between narrative inputs and brand equity outcomes can only be fully underst ood
when brand authenticity is conceptualised as a dual -mediator consisting of Constructive Authenticity and Existential
Authenticity. Whereas the conventional marketing literature has treated authenticity as a singular construct, luxury
brand storytelling engages consumers along two distinct psychological dimensions.

Constructive Authenticity is the projection of authenticity from the consumer onto a brand based on verifiable
attributes, historical archiving, documented provenance and traditional producti on methods. Storytelling, using
historical archives, certified geographical origins and genealogies of master craftsmen, satisfies the consumer‟s
cognitive need for objective evidence of quality and heritage. It creates the material legitimacy of the luxur y house,
a cognitive anchor that legitimises the brand‟s positioning in the premium market.

Existential Authenticity, by contrast, is the subjective and emotional experience of self -discovery, expression of
identity and personal truth that is induced in the consumer through his or her interaction with the brand. Storytelling
that emphasises timeless values, artistic liberty, ethical stewardship and personal empowerment enables consumers
to build and project their desired self -identity. Brand story as a br idge between positive historical legitimacy and
existential self -expression can be a strong dual -mediation mechanism of brand authenticity, influencing rational
perceived quality and intense emotional brand passion.

Research Methodology:-
Secondary Research Design & PRISMA Framework:-
The present study adopts a systematic secondary research design based on the Preferred Reporting Items for
Systematic Reviews and Meta -Analyses (PRISMA) statement. A systematic secondary review methodology was
chosen to synthesise various theoretical perspectives, resolve empirical inconsistencies in international studies, and
provide a unified narrative frame of reference for luxury brand equity.

Search Strategy and Database Selection:-
Five major electronic databases and publisher indexes were searched for literature, including Elsevier /
ScienceDirect, Emerald Publishing, Taylor & Francis, Wiley Online Library, and Springer Nature. Keywords were
combined using Boolean operators ( " AND ", " OR " ). The search strings were (“luxury brand equity” OR
“customer-based brand equity”) AND (“storytelling” OR “narrative marketing” OR “brand heritage”) AND (“brand
authenticity” OR “price premium” OR “narrative transportation”). The publication search time frame was set from
2000 to 2026 to embrace the basic frameworks and the current digital transitions.

Results and Discussion:-
Key Findings: Narratives and Equity Dimensions:-
The systematic synthesis provides strong evidence for the positive impact of strategic storytelling on four
major brand equity dimensions, showing significant differences across specific luxury  product categories:-
 Haute Couture and Leather Goods: The narrative is one of artistic direction, seasonal narrative worlds, and
specialised hand-stitching traditions (e.g. saddle stitching) in fashion and leathercraft. In this sector, storytelling
mainly affects brand salience, emotional resonance and seasonal purchase momentum, which leads to high
consumer willingness to pay for symbolic design innovation.
 Fine Jewellery and Haute Horlogerie: In hard luxury, stories tend to be biased toward mechanical pr ecision,
rare gem origins, generational inheritance and historical permanence. Storytelling in this domain acts as a
powerful costly signal that reduces perceived financial risk, resulting in a high price premium tolerance and
turning products into long-term investment assets.
 Premium & Luxury Automotive:  The premium automotive space is a balancing act of storytelling between
heritage motorsport engineering and future -facing technological innovation and sustainable mobility. Founder
racing heritage, hand -built powertrains and leading -edge performance stori es elevate the perception of
engineering excellence and build strong brand community loyalty.
 Price Premium Tolerance: The empirical literature consistently confirms that consumers exposed to rich
brand heritage and craftsmanship stories are significantly more willing to pay price premiums than consumers
exposed only to product features. Storytelling moves cognitive processing from price sensitivity to symbolic
appreciation.
 Perceived Quality and Excellence:  Origin storytelling, rare material sourcing and m aster artisan techniques
raise perceived product quality above objective benchmark testing. The story itself is an implicit guarantee of
quality.
 Brand Loyalty & Advocacy:  High affective commitment through transportive storytelling. Consumers who
have an emotional connection to the brand‟s storytelling journey have higher retention, are more engaged in the
brand community and become organic brand advocates.
 Brand Authenticity and Resilience:  Founder myths and archival heritage safeguard brands in public rel ations
crises. Real historical narrative anchors. Even in the event of adverse market fluctuations or leadership changes,
brand trust is maintained.

Managerial Implications for Luxury:-
Our findings suggest that storytelling should be considered a core o perational strategy rather than a peripheral
marketing tactic for the management of luxury brands. Luxury houses need to be completely consistent across all
narrative touchpoints. Inconsistencies between the historical story and the present business (e.g. outsourcing
manufacturing while promoting local artisanal tradition) generate consumer disbelief, which damages brand
authenticity and drastically reduces equity.

New Trends in Luxury Narratives:-
A synthesis of recent literature reveals three transformat ive trends in the delivery of luxury narratives, as
well as increased operational and strategic risks:-
 Generative AI and Hyper -Personalized Narratives:  Luxury brands are turning to generative AI for hyper -
personalised narrative interactions, customising h ow stories are told to individual consumers based on their
preferences, while staying true to the core of the brand history. However, if human supervision is compromised,
unconstrained algorithmic narrative generation has serious risks of narrative dilutio n, loss of brand voice
consistency and consumer perceptions of commercial manipulation.
 Immersive Virtual Heritage (AR/VR/Spatial Computing):  Global consumers can virtually visit historical
workshops, experience craftsmanship in 3D spatial environments and  engage with the origin stories of founders
in immersive digital museums thanks to extended reality technologies. These spatial experiences maintain
exclusivity by offering high -end digital access in a curated environment without the need to grow the physi cal
store.
 Ethical Provenance, Digital Product Passports and Blockchain  Verification: Today‟s luxury consumer
wants narrative transparency about environmental responsibility, ethical sourcing of labour and circular craft
practices. Luxury houses are adopti ng blockchain technology and Digital Product Passports (DPPs) to create
immutable, tamper -proof digital records of supply chain provenance, transforming ethical commitments into
verifiable narrative assets.
 Generative Artificial Intelligence and Hyper-Personalized Narratives:  Luxury brands are applying
generative AI to craft hyper -personalised narrative interactions, tailoring story delivery to align with individual
consumer preferences while preserving the essence of the brand history.
 Immersive Virtual Heritage (AR/VR/Spatial Computing):  Extended reality tools enable worldwide
audiences to virtually step inside historic ateliers, witness craftsmanship in 3D spatial settings, and interact with
founder origin stories at immersive digital museums.
 Ethical Provenance and Sustainability Narratives: Contemporary luxury consumers, especially younger
consumers, are looking for narrative transparency about environmental responsibility, ethical sourcing of
labour, and circular craft practices. Brands are infusing ethical provenance into their heritage stories.

Critical discussion: Contextual differences and contradictions:-
Cross-cultural literature shows significant differences in narrative reception between mature and emerging luxury
markets. Consumers in  developed markets (e.g. Western Europe, North America) are more receptive to nuanced,
understated narratives around understated elegance, sustainable sourcing and personal self -actualisation. Conversely,
in emerging luxury markets (e.g. select East Asian and Middle Eastern regions) consumers often prefer prominent
status signalling, pronounced founder prestige and explicit displays of historic royal lineage. Therefore, global
luxury houses are required to modify narrative execution across geographic region s, while keeping an unaltered core
brand personality.

Research Gaps:-
Although progress has been made in the academic field, there remain important gaps not addressed in the current
secondary literature. First, there is an absence of long-term longitudinal studies that gauge the precise multi-year rate
of equity dilution when luxury brands oversaturate digital channels. Second, the long -term effects of content
produced by AI on consumer perceptions of the authenticity of human craft need further empirical exploration.

Conclusion:-
Summary of Study:-
This study systematically evaluates the role of strategic storytelling in building, sustaining and boosting luxury
brand equity. The paper integrated Keller‟s Customer -Based Brand Equity framework, Aaker‟s equity dimensions,
Narrative Transportation Theory and Signal ling Theory, and demonstrated that the narrative strategy is a core value
driver in contemporary luxury brand management. Antecedents of the core narrative founder myths, heritage,
artisanal craftsmanship, and cultural patronage generate signals of psychol ogical absorption and authenticity that
lead to perceived quality, brand loyalty, and consumers‟ willingness to pay structural price premiums.

Major Findings:-
The review produces three major findings: First, functional product excellence is a necessary b ut not sufficient
condition for maintaining luxury brand status; symbolic narrative value is the key driver of modern brand equity.
Second, luxury brands can successfully navigate digital channels without diluting exclusivity through “controlled
intimacy” and transmedia storytelling strategies. Third, sustainable brand equity requires rigorous narrative
consistency, linking historical heritage to contemporary ethical provenance.

Managerial Implications for Marketing Managers:-
 Audit and Formalise Brand:  Heritage Archives. Brand managers should develop a multi -step archival
process: First, conduct systematic historical audits to inventory founding records, artisan patents, and key
milestone creations. Second, convert physical archives into digital, searchabl e repositories that can be accessed
by marketing units around the world. Third, create formal brand narrative codices that specify the boundaries
within which stories can be told, to ensure consistent global marketing across various cultural markets.
 Implement Controlled Digital Intimacy: To find the sweet spot between reach and exclusivity, brand teams
need to implement a tiered digital channel framework: Use open digital channels (such as social media
platforms) only for high -concept cinematic storytellin g and artistic brand imagery. Keep the most intimate,

Methodological Limitations:-
This paper is limited by the constraints of secondary research designs. The synthesis is based on secondary data
only, published elsewhere, so there is no possibility to empirically test the proposed structural relationships directly.
Moreover, publication bias  in academic journals may skew available literature toward successful luxury brand case
studies, under-representing instances where narrative strategies failed.

Implications for Future Empirical Research:-
Future research should employ longitudinal empiri cal approaches to monitor consumer brand equity metrics over
extended periods following transmedia digital campaigns. In addition, experimental research designs should
examine consumer psychological reactions to AI -assisted storytelling versus human -created brand stories to
determine boundary conditions for the use of technology in luxury marketing.

Ethical, Cultural, and Regulatory Issues:-
As luxury brands scale up global storytelling, ethical, cultural and regulatory considerations move to the fore. The
regulatory landscape in some of the major international markets, including the soon -to-be-implemented European
Union Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation and Digital Product Passport requirements, is such that luxury
houses will need to provide s ubstantiation for all environmental and craftsmanship claims. Brands that tell
sustainability stories that are not backed up risk severe greenwashing penalties, legal sanctions and catastrophic
reputational erosion.

Furthermore, as luxury houses draw insp iration from stories around the world, cross -cultural ethics demand the
implementation of robust safeguards against cultural appropriation. For regional heritage or indigenous
craftsmanship, brands need to have formal cultural advisory boards and use colla borative co -creation models that
ensure equitable attribution and economic partnership. Finally, the application of personalised artificial intelligence
storytelling must adhere to global data privacy regulations (e.g., GDPR) to guarantee that hyper -personalised
narrative delivery honours consumer consent and safeguards personal data without being intrusive or surveilling.
