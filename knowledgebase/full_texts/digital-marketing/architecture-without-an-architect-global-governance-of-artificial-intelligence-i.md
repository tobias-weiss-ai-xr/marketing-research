# Architecture Without an Architect? Global Governance of Artificial Intelligence in a Divided World

Architecture Without an Architect?
Global Governance of Artificial
Intelligence in a Divided World
Review Essay

MATTHIJS M. MAAS, Architectures of Global AI Governance: From Technological Change to
Human Choice (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2025)

Simon Chesterman, National University of Singapore

Artificial intelligence presents an unusually difficult problem for global
governance. The technology develops rapidly, crosses borders easily, and is
shaped by actors whose resources and capabilities may rival those of states.
Yet international responses remain fragmented, unevenly representative, and
overwhelmingly non-binding. The challenge is therefore not simply to identify
appropriate rules or institutions, but to understand who has the capacity and
incentive to create, enforce, and adapt them.
This review essay examines these questions through Matthijs Maas’s
Architectures of Global AI Governance. Maas offers an ambitious framework
for thinking about AI governance through the lenses of sociotechnical change,
governance disruption, and regime complexity. His account usefully resists
both technological determinism and the search for a single institutional
blueprint, emphasizing instead the possibilities of a fragmented and evolving
governance architecture.
The essay argues, however, that institutional design cannot be separated from
the distribution of power. Maas frequently invokes what “we” should do about
AI, but that collective subject obscures important differences among states,
international institutions, and technology companies. States retain formidable
powers over markets, infrastructure, strategic inputs, and firms themselves. At
the same time, many consequential decisions about frontier AI — what is
built, how quickly, with what safeguards, and when it is released — are
concentrated within a small number of private companies. The central problem
of global AI governance may therefore be less architecture without an
architect than an emerging architecture shaped by multiple actors possessing
different forms of power, divergent incentives, and no common set of plans.

These are boom times for AI governance. An ever -expanding number of companies and
countries have adopted hundreds of guides, frameworks, and principles that purport to
moderate the risks associated with a fast -moving technology that could improve the world or
destroy it — with a surprising number of technopreneurs holding both Panglossian and
catastrophist positions simultaneously.  The patchwork of national initiatives is now
complemented by a growing canvas of regional efforts, notably including the various European
architectures, as well as initiatives in Africa, the Americas, and Asia. Selective groups like the
G7, the G20, the OECD , and the Global Partnership on AI  have also adopted foundational
documents. Private sector bodies such as the World Economic Forum and standards
organizations have been active, as have civil society actors. Even the Pope has entered the fray,
placing AI high on his own agenda.1
Yet the most important documents have hardly been ‘global’. In 2024, the United Nations
estimated that only seven states were party to the seven most important such initiatives — the
wealthy, industrialized members of the G7 — while fully a hundred other states were party to
none of them. 2 To the armchair regulator, the need for a global response is self -evident. The
technology pays no heed to boundaries; to the extent that it does, companies are apt to engage
in regulatory arbitrage to seek more permissive jurisdictions.
This has led to a proliferation of academic proposals for regimes of various kinds in journals
and books. There is no shortage of models to draw upon when seeking to establish some form
of global architecture . Popular candidates include bodies such as the International Atomic
Energy Agency (IAEA),3 the European Organization for Nuclear Research ( CERN),4 and the

Fast-moving technology puts regulators in an unenviable  position. Act too swiftly and they
constrain innovation or drive it to more permissive jurisdictions. Act too slowly and they
expose their citizenry to risk. Recent history is littered with examples of both, from the US
falling behind in stem-cell research after Christian lobbyists secured a moratorium in 20017 to
the global struggle to address social media harms following two decades of inaction. 8 Even
when they do act , the response is often ‘ reactive, fragmented, and grounded in shallow
analogies to past technologies or legacy institutions ’.9 For their part, technology companies
themselves have increasingly accepted the need for some form of regulation in theory — even
as they oppose any specific proposals in practice.10
Into this space, Matthijs M. Maas offers a weighty new tome : Architectures of Global AI
Governance. He argues that we must reject the ‘epistemic and political pessimism’ (594) that
often paralyses debates over AI governance: assuming that the technology’s future
development is too speculative or too inevitable for us to make meaningful decisions today.
Maas, now Senior Research Fellow at the Institute for Law & AI in Boston, argues that global
governance of AI can and should ‘do better’ (2) by drawing on the concepts of sociotechnical
change, governance disruption, and regime complexity.

basis. Maas takes a longer horizon. His book builds on a doctoral thesis at the University of

That ‘we’ is doing some heavy lifting, however. From an analytical shorthand, the term is soon

A quick search finds  the word ‘we’ more than 400 times in the text.  Why ‘we’ need global
governance of AI, however, is not the same as explaining why those who actually wield power
in this space — states, but increasingly technology companies themselves — might actually
create it. Nor is power distributed uniformly between them. States retain formidable coercive
and regulatory capacities, while frontier AI companies increasingly control the technical

1 Lenses
The book is structured in three parts. It begins with a survey of the challenges posed by AI, the
need for a global response, and how initiatives to date have fallen short. Part II seeks to draw
lessons from sociotechnical change, governance disruption, and regime complexity to better
understand the current moment and the limitations of existing approaches. The final part seeks
to set out ‘frameworks for choice’ in order to help shape an emerging AI governance regime
that, at its best, might be effective, resilient, and coherent.
The lens of sociotechnical change offers potential guidance on allocating scarce attention of
regulators to the most socially disruptive technologies  (371). It also suggests past examples
that demonstrate the pros and cons of targeting that attention upstream (for example, to model
developers) or downstream (to deployers, users, and so on). Drawing on Collingridge, it can
also help address the question of when  to act — while acknowledging the dilemma that early
action is easy but uncertain, whereas delay allows greater clarity about risks but increases the
costs of addressing them.12
When turning to the disruptive impact of AI on governance, Maas sensibly does not overplay
his hand. Indeed, unlike technologies that truly opened up terra incognita — space travel, the
early days of cyberspace — AI is accelerating and transforming, for the most part, activities
that map onto traditional jurisdictions (392). That is not to say that AI does not pose regulatory
challenges. Its speed, autonomy, and increasing opacity pose many practical and some
conceptual difficulties for existing norms. There are gaps, to be sure — maritime law long
defined a ship as a ‘manned’ [ sic] vessel;13 traffic law in many states  criminalizes leaving an
automobile unattended.14 These must be amended if autonomous vessels and vehicles are to
ply our waterways and our roads. But it would be too much to say that AI requires a complete

2 Action and Reaction
The harsh truth is that major change in the institutions of global order requires crisis. We would
not have had the League of Nations without the First World War; the United Nations would not
have existed but for the Second. The IAEA emerged from the shadows of Hiroshima and
Nagasaki,21 much as the modern taboo against chemical weapons owes much to the trenches
of Ypres and Passchendaele.22 Swift action is sometimes possible — the Montreal Protocol to
address ozone depletion  is a case in point, arguably the most successful environmental
agreement in history.23 Yet the larger climate crisis offers a stark warning: it is a slow-moving
crisis with fairly clear scientific consensus about the problem, and yet action remains difficult.
AI offers a fast -moving challenge with no consensus and even less willingness to act in a
coordinated manner.
‘We’ are also reactive in inconsistent ways. Despite widespread fears of lethal autonomous
weapon systems (LAWS) being normalized, their use by Ukraine has been quietly tolerated by
many governments because of sympathy for its cause against Russian aggression. 24 Some
international lawyers similarly tied themselves in knots to support NATO’s 1999 intervention
in support of autonomy (and later independence)  for Kosovo, arguing that it was ‘illegal but

At times, Maas’s wariness of offering simplistic solutions makes it hard to see the problem he
is trying to solve. The global deficit in AI governance is the absence of red lines, in particular
with regard to uncontrollable and uncontainable AI, which would threaten to escape human

that AI systems remain attributable for their actions back to human, corporate, or state actors.
These are the structural challenges posed by AI to existing legal orders. Norms against bias,
requirements for safety  and the like can be upheld merely by ensuring that existing human
rights norms and product liability laws are made to apply. More general calls for accountability
are, in essence, demands that AI not be allowed to exist in a normative vac uum. There are, to
be sure, larger questions about how the economic disruption of AI will be navigated, how its
benefits might be distributed, how the consequences of mass disinformation will be navigated.

collective action problem, where cooperation can reduce regulatory uncertainty and arbitrage,
comparing it to governance of the internet and nuclear non- proliferation (162-165). He also
acknowledges the potential for treating AI governance as a global public good, though this is
framed primarily in terms of establishing standards for behaviour and constraining bad actors
(165-168).

3 The Trillion Dollar Question

authority no longer maps neatly onto the sources of power that matter most. States can regulate
markets, control strategic inputs, and impose costs. But many of the most consequential
decisions about frontier AI — what is built, how quickly, with what safeguards, and when it is
released — are made inside private firms.  Tellingly, at its Summit of the Future in 2024,
member states of the United Nations  adopted a Global Digital Compact. Those states
committed to various actions by 2030, including ensuring that legislation relevant to digital

Towards the end of his book is a section entitled ‘strategies’, though Maas is once more at pains
to stress that he is not offering a single roadmap. Indeed, he abjures even discussing
‘substantive values or goals for AI regimes’ as these may change over time (581). Th e
admirable intent is to provide a future-proof toolbox of governance options for the future, along
with a means of evaluating the efficacy, resilience, and coherence of any such regimes.
Maas’s achievement is to show that global AI governance is not a blank page. It is already an
emerging architecture: fragmented, overlapping, and shaped by the interaction of technology,
law, and institutional choice. The harder question is whether anyone is capable of building upon
it. States still speak the language of sovereignty and public authority, but the most consequential
decisions about frontier AI are increasingly made by private firms whose resources, incentives,
and time horizons sit uneasily with the slow rituals o f international  law — and indeed of
domestic law also. The result is not simply architecture without an architect. It may be
architecture in which neither the builders nor anyone else is holding the plans.
