Abstract Islamic educational institutions in Indonesia currently face a complex epistemological and
strategic dilemma, situated at the crossroads between maintaining sacred normative
traditions and responding to technological disruption in the Society 5.0 era. Generation Z,
as the demographic group constituting the largest market share for educational institutions
today, displays information consumption behavioral  patterns that demand authenticity,
transparency, and symmetrical two-way communication interactions. This comprehensive
scientific report explores and deeply analyzes the branding strategies of Islamic educational
institutions, focusing on the utilization  of digital marketing and influencer marketing to
attract prospective students from Generation Z. Using a qualitative approach with a
multicultural case study method and phenomenological analysis of various educational
institutions (such as Aqobah  International School, SMPI Al -Azhaar, IAIN Kerinci, and
Phatnawitya School), this research deconstructs conventional marketing frameworks. The
analysis results indicate that the transformation of the Marketing Mix (7Ps) into the digital
ecosystem is an ab solute necessity. Furthermore, this report finds that the Islamic Brand
Personality model—centered on sharia compliance, sincerity, and transparency —plays a
crucial role in mitigating the risk of the commercialization of religion. Through platform
comparison, it was found that TikTok, with an average engagement rate of 1.42%, is highly
effective for the awareness phase, while Instagram, with an engagement rate of 5.47%, is
superior in building community loyalty. The utilization of moderate Muslim influencers has
proven capable of amplifying Electronic Word of Mouth (e -WOM) and aligning the
institution's image with the halal lifestyle of Generation Z. This article concludes that the
effectiveness of future Islamic education branding strategies requires a synergy between
data-driven technological innovations, including Artificial Intelligence (AI) governan ce,
and the preservation of tarbiyah values and maqasid sharia to ensure institutional sustainability
without compromising the integrity of da'wah.

1. INTRODUCTION
The Islamic education ecosystem in Indonesia, historically represented by boarding
schools (pondok pesantren ), madrasahs, and Integrated Islamic Schools (SIT), is currently
navigating a highly dynamic and turbulent transition period. Modern Islamic education faces
a complex epistemological dilemma; these institutions are caught between the strong pull of
sacred normative traditions and the unstoppable tidal wave of modernity and cultural
globalization ("The Construction of Islamic Boarding School Education Ethics", 2026).
Amidst the acceleration of information technology advancements, a shift toward a
transformative, student-centered pedagogical paradigm is imperative, while simultaneously
bringing forth contemporary challenges such as value secularization, a crisis of religious
identity among students, and the dominance of profane scientific narratives that often do
not align with tawhid values ("Islamic Religious Education Learning", 2026).
This disruption is further accelerated by the generational shift dominating the school-
age demographic. Generation Z (Gen Z), the group of individuals born between the mid -
1990s and early 2010s, is now the primary target audience and essential decision -makers in
selecting educational institutions (Aimah et al., 2024). This generation grows and develops
entirely within a digital ecosystem, granting them limitless accessibility to global information
flows. The Indonesia Millennial and Gen Z Report 2025 highli ghts that this group is not a
passive participant in social change, but rather a driving force actively demanding inclusivity,
institutional transparency, work-life balance, and support for sustainability and mental health
(IDN Research Institute, 2025). A s a generation aware of their political and social rights —
evidenced by high digital literacy coupled with a critical stance toward traditional
institutions—a one-way communication approach is no longer relevant for them ( Sukandar
et al., 2024).
Historically, Islamic educational institutions have often been perceived by some
segments of society as entities that are slow to adapt to changing times, exclusive, and lagging
in the adoption of modern managerial strategies. This assumption poses a serio us threat to
the sustainability of Islamic educational institutions, especially when they have to compete in
a hyper-competitive market (red ocean) against general educational institutions, international
schools, and technology -based education platforms (E dTech). This increasingly sharp
competition forces Islamic educational institutions not merely to be complacent with their

Referring to this phenomenon, educational branding and marketing strategies can no

process of articulating authentic Islamic values through digital mediums, aiming to convert
the academic identity and spiritual charact er of the school into a measurable competitive
advantage and a strong brand personality (Muttaqin, 2021).
This report aims to explore, analyze , and dissect the anatomy of digital marketing
strategies in Islamic educational institutions comprehensively. Through a synthesis of recent
literature, phenomenological analysis, and evaluation of social media metric data using a
multidisciplinary qualitative approach (Creswell & Poth, 2018; Fadjarajani  et al., 2020), this
report dissects how the implementation of the Marketing Mix (7Ps) evolves in the digital era,
how social media algorithms mediate decision -making, the extent to which influencer
marketing can catalyze Electronic Word of Mouth (e -WOM), and how institutions can
maintain the integrity of marketing ethics amidst the threats of religious commercialization
and Artificial Intelligence (AI) advancements.
2. RESEARCH METHODS
The scientific construction in this report was developed through a qualitative approach
with a methodological design combining phenomenological analysis and multicultural case
studies based on literature research (Creswell & Poth, 2018; Fadjarajani  et al., 2020). The
phenomenological approach was crucially selected because this study aims to understand the
profound meaning of how branding strategies are projected as a social and academic process
within Islamic educational institutions. Unlike purely commercial marketing research, which
relies heavily on quantitative probabilities, analyzing  the branding of Islamic education
requires an exploration of managerial processes, cultural contexts, and the ethical
foundations of da'wah that cannot be entirely reduced to a matrix of numbers ("The
Construction of Islamic Boarding School Education Ethics", 2026).
Technically, data collection was conducted by extracting findings from various
reputable national and international journal articles, industry reports (such as data on the
Gen Z consumer landscape in Indonesia) (IDN Research Institute, 2025; Sukandar et al.,
2024), and third-party social media analytic metrics. This literature study not only mapped
conceptual theories but also examined empirical reports using in -depth interviews, Focus
Group Discussions (FGD), and digital footprint observation s applied to various research
loci.
The locus of analysis or case study objects drawn from empirical studies covers a broad
spectrum of the education ecosystem, including: (1) Primary and secondary educational

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1 Paradigm Shift in School Selection and Characteristics of Muslim Generation Z
The success of a branding strategy always begins with precise analytical empathy
toward the target audience. In this context, Muslim Generation Z in Indonesia presents a
sociological and psychological profile fundamentally different from Millennials and
Generation X (IDN Research Institute, 2025). Indonesian Muslim Gen Z integrates a strong
level of religiosity with massive digital literacy; a convergence resulting in a demand for
"globally connected spirituality". They are no longer entities that blindly a ccept traditional
authority without question. Instead, as reflected by their high access to political information
and analytical tendencies toward citizenship, Gen Z processes every institutional claim
through a filter of critical thinking (Sukandar et al., 2024).
In the process of searching for and selecting educational institutions, Generation Z
prioritizes the value of authenticity. Explicit advertising narratives, overselling facts, or hard-
selling styles actually trigger skepticism  and cognitive resistance (Aimah et al., 2024). This
generation evaluates a school not solely from the written promises in a printed brochure, but
from a real digital track record: how the institution responds to audience comments, the
extent to which incl usivity is applied in the campus environment, and what sustainability
values are actively championed. Therefore, brand positioning strategies can no longer focus
on hierarchy and superlative claims but must transform into honest storytelling, showcasing
the real challenges of students (student's journey), emotional dynamics, and communal values
(Steils et al., 2022).
Regarding religious identity, Muslim Gen Z in Indonesia is driven by the Halal
Lifestyle concept (Abuznaid, 2012). An in-depth regression study on the purchasing behavior
of local Muslim fashion products on the TikTok app—which has a highly equivalent pattern
to selecting educational services —found an interesting paradox. The research proved that
Electronic Word of Mouth (e -WOM), the influence of Muslim influencers, and th e Halal
Lifestyle have a positive and significant impact on Generation Z's decisions (Steils et al.,

3.2 Construction of Islamic Brand Personality
Branding is the effort to provide human characteristics to an institution so that it can
build emotional resonance with the public. In the educational domain bearing the name of
Islam, adopting purely Western marketing theories is often incompatible or can  distort the
moral essence. Therefore, Muslim marketing scholars developed the Islamic Brand
Personality framework. This model deconstructs conventional dimensions and incubates
sharia principles as absolute quality indicators (Abuznaid, 2012).
Conceptually, an Islamic Brand Personality is not reduced merely to the use of Arabic
calligraphy or superficial visual attributes. This personality manifests in a series of holistic
dimensions: Shariah Compliance, Trustworthiness, Sincerity, Justice, Open ness,
Competence, Ruggedness, and Emotion. Structural equation modeling  (SEM) statistical
analysis testing the influence of these dimensions proved that Shariah Compliance and
Trustworthiness provide the most significant positive impact on customer satisfaction, far
exceeding superficial promotional attributes (Muttaqin, 2021).
When these dimensions are translated into the management of Islamic educational
institutions, the marketing strategy becomes a manifestation of institutional da'wah. Sincerity
is projected through the pedagogical dedication of educators and manipulation-free customer
service. Openness is applied by providing accurate public information regarding the
educational fee structure, open fund allocation, and a real profile of the institution's
capabilities—instead of trapping prospective students with hidden fees that clearly violate
Islamic business ethics. Competence is represented through student achievements and the
integration of modern academic curricula (such as STEM curricula or talent acceleration)
with the tahfidz curriculum (Mahmud et al., 2022).
A solid Islamic Brand Personality construction becomes a strategic foundation to
ward off the trap of educational commercialization. Madrasahs and Integrated Islamic
Schools (SIT) should not stray from their philosophical roots as spiritual entities but mu st
use marketing management to expand the reach of goodness (maslahah ). By having a
consistent Brand Personality, all built digital initiatives will feel authentic to the Gen Z
audience, minimizing cognitive dissonance between online-built expectations and the reality
of offline academic services (Wardani & Muttaqin, 2022).

Quantitative data shows a surprising phenomenon: although TikTok is glorified for
its viral potential (reach), deep interactions (such as comprehensive comments and saving
information for careful consideration) actually occur much stronger on Instagram. Empirical
research using the ANOVA test on 18,026 posts from various popular Islamic community
accounts in Indonesia proves that, contrary to mainstream assumptions, single-photo and
carousel formats consistently receive significantly higher engagement rates (likes
and comments) compared to video content on Instagram . This can be explained
through a cognitive lens: narratives of Islamic education, Quranic verses, registration fee
details, or school curriculum infographics require a slow and reflective reading process, which
is highly unfacilitated by fast-moving videos.
On the other hand, TikTok dominates the Attention stage. Gen Z views TikTok as
a fun recreational escape medium. A promotional initiative (e.g., "A Day in My Life as a
Santri" peppered with light humor or trendy visual transitions) can explode virally on TikTok
and instantaneously bring tens of thousands of profile visits to the institution's account,
before finally being redirected to Instagram for more substantial academic literacy processes.
An empirical study at UIN Antasari proved that social media activities combining elements
of entertainment, interactivity, and trendiness have a visible impact on the enrollment
intention of high school graduates. Therefore, a hybrid marketing architecture is an absolute
recommendation: TikTok as a mass -catching instrument (top -funnel) and Instagram as a
loyalty incubation instrument (mid-funnel).
3.5 The Strategic Role of Influencer Marketing, Digital Preachers, and e -WOM
Amplification
In a digital economy running on the trust economy principle, credibility can no longer
be bought with advertising budgets; credibility must be borrowed through authoritative
figures loved by the community. Influencer Marketing has revolutionized the landsc ape of
educational promotion. For Islamic educational institutions, influencers are not limited to
lifestyle content creators but also include ustadzs, popular preachers (cool ustadz), academics,
and contemporary hijab fashion figures who actively reproduce religious discourse (Steils et
al., 2022).
The contemporary sociology of religion in Indonesia notes the emergence of the
repackaging of ulama phenomenon to be more aligned with urban youth subcultures. The
most representative case is the emergence of Salafi da'wah communities driven by figures
such as Ustadz Khalid Basalamah and Ustadz  Syafiq Riza Basalamah. Unlike traditional
religious authorities who are rigid or use confrontational diction, their da'wah approach has
been adapted to be highly structured, personal, dialogical, and actively utilizes contemporary
visual trends favored by Generation Z. These da'wah figures and Muslim lifestyle influencers
play a fundamental role in reducing the cognitive barrier between youth and religion; they
foster an attitude of openness, inclusivity, and a strong religious identity among their massive
followers (Akmaliah, 2020).

their brand with relevant moderate da'wah figures, a brand association transfer phenomenon

3.6 Ethical Challenges, Commercialization of Religion, and the Transparency
Paradox
The modernization strategy of marketing management in Islamic educational institutions is
like a double-edged sword. If not based on the epistemology of maqasid sharia and sincerity
of intention (nawaitu), the exploitation of digital marketing tools has the potential to spawn a
highly dangerous ethical reduction: the commercialization of religion.
Commercialization in this educational context refers to the practice of positioning an
institution solely as an economic profit -generating factory, where Islamic symbols (such as
memorization obligations, sharia jargon, or dormitory facilities) are engineered as advertising
gimmicks to justify skyrocketing fees without being balanced by the substance of education
and adequate transfer of adab (manners/ethics). The marketing of educational services
should not distance the institution from its essence as a forum for community building.
Overselling practices on social media —such as claiming manipulated state university
acceptance ratios, or exaggerating dormitory conditions visually to deceive the perception of
reality—are forms of fatal violations against the principle of honesty (Shiddiq) in muamalah.
Furthermore, collaborations with preachers or influencer content creators give rise to what
is known as the Transparency Paradox among Generation Z. Generation Z is highly
responsive and sensitive in distinguishing an honest opinion from an influencer vers us a
scripted endorsement. If a school uses the services of an ustadz or influencer to promote the
institution but hides the financial affiliation behind it, it will actually harm brand authenticity
and trigger a collapse in credibility that is difficult to recover from (cancel culture). Moreover,
institutions must be highl y selective and not get trapped with influencers who have
provocative, emotional, or exclusive track records. Influencers who like to polarize religious
identities will only damage the reputation of schools based on the values of community unity
and Islam wasathiyah (moderation). Communication practices must prioritize the consistency
of institutional behavior as a reflection of pure da'wah manifestation.
