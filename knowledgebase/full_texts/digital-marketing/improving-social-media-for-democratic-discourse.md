Executive

summary

Designing

social

media

platforms

for

democratic

discourse

is

among

the

most

important

challenges

of

our

time.

Meeting

this

challenge

will

require

ideas

from

people

with

a

broad

range

of

perspectives,

including

many

who

are

not

experts

on

social

media.

This

white

paper

makes

a

start

by

assembling

a

wide

range

of

ideas

for

improving

social

media

and

connecting

them

to

relevant

literature

across

multiple

disciplines.

Social

media

are

widely

seen

as

causing

or

enabling

political

polarization,

misinformation,

disinformation,

social

isolation,

and

loss

of

civility.

However,

these

harms

are

not

unavoidable

consequences

of

human

nature

or

of

being

connected

through

technology.

Rather,

they

reﬂect

the

design

choices

and

incentives

of

the

few

platforms

that

dominate

today.

Today’s

platforms

are

optimized

for

engagement

because

it

drives

advertising

revenue,

and

this

objective

is

inherently

in

tension

with

the

goals

of

democratic

discourse.

Beyond

small

experiments,

designing

social

media

for

democratic

discourse

has

not

been

seriously

attempted.

Designed

to

support

democratic

discourse,

social

media

might

yet

deliver

on

their

original

promise

of

connecting

people,

broadening

participation

in

political

discourse,

creating

truly

accessible

national

and

global

public

spheres,

and

enhancing

our

collective

intelligence.

To

expand

our

practical

imagination

beyond

the

engagement

paradigm,

this

white

paper

proposes

a

modular

set

of

“mechanisms”

that

could

be

implemented

on

social

media

platforms.

Each

mechanism

is

presented

as

a

one-page

table,

with

a

short

description,

bullet

lists

for

its

promise

and

challenges,

references

to

related

mechanisms

in

this

white

paper,

and

references

to

the

literature.

The

collection

is

intended

both

as

an

entry

point

for

discussion,

with

pointers

to

relevant

literature,

and

as

a

menu

of

implementable

design

options.

Our

overarching

goal

is

to

catalyze

broad

discussion

among

the

general

public,

journalists,

and

experts

in

multiple

ﬁelds.

The

ideas

may

also

inspire

more

technical,

cross-disciplinary

work

in

technology,

engineering,

the

sciences,

and

the

humanities,

toward

a

digital

public

sphere

that

is

built

for

democratic

discourse,

collective

deliberation,

and

collective

intelligence.

Below

is

a

brief

overview

of

the

proposed

mechanisms

(bold)

for

improving

social

media

for

democratic

discourse.

1

(1)

Shift

feedback

and

incentives

from

emotional

engagement

to

epistemic

value.

Cognicons

are

one-click

cognitive

reactions

(analogous

to

emoticons

but

reﬂective

of

rational

judgment)

meant

to

amplify

thoughtful

and

informative

contributions

rather

than

emotionally

provocative

ones.

A

related

proposal

is

probability

ratings
,

which

let

users

annotate

factual

claims

on

a

probability

scale

from

0

to

1

to

enable

decentralized

fact-checking.

The

number-free

feed

would

hide

public

like

counts,

share

counts,

and

follower

totals,

encouraging

users

to

assess

content

on

its

own

merits

rather

than

following

perceived

majority

opinion.

More

broadly,

the

paper

explores

game-aware

design

for

democratic

discourse
,

where

platforms

can

determine

what

content

should

receive

ampliﬁcation.

Mechanisms

such

as

cognicons,

probability

ratings,

a

discourse

score
,

and

milestone

badges

for

high-quality

participation

could

reward

credible

sourcing,

constructive

argument,

cross-ideological

engagement,

and

public

belief

revision.

(2)

Increase

individual

and

community

control,

transparency,

and

participation.

Natural

language

feed

algorithm

tuning

would

let

users

give

direct

instructions

to

their

recommender

systems

so

that

feeds

reﬂect

people’s

conscious

goals,

not

just

their

habitual

engagement.

Similarly,

open-source

forkable

recommenders

would

allow

users

to

select

among

audited

ranking

algorithms,

modify

their

settings,

program

their

own

algorithms,

and

compare

feeds

from

alternative

algorithms

side

by

side.

At

the

individual

level,

the

content-engagement

summary

and

feed

recalibration

mechanism

would

visualize

what

content

users

have

engaged

with,

and

which

topics

or

perspectives

may

be

missing.

At

the

collective

level,

communal

feed

curation

would

let

communities

design

and

publish

feeds

based

on

shared

values,

while

the

daily

algorithm

ballot

would

allow

users

to

vote

on

temporary

feed

priorities

–

for

example,

boosting

local

news.

Stream

networks

are

decentralized

networks

of

collective

intelligence,

where

streams

of

cultural

items

are

created

and

curated

by

people,

with

each

stream

drawing

selectively

from

multiple

other

streams

using

algorithms

that

are

entirely

controlled

by

the

participants.

(3)

Expose

the

plurality

of

perspectives

and

engage

across

diﬀerences.

Stance-based

search

would

make

it

easier

to

ﬁnd

arguments

for,

against,

or

neutral

toward

a

particular

position.

A

devil’s

advocate

button

would

provide

high-quality

alternative

viewpoints

through

retrieved

posts,

AI-generated

counterarguments,

or

opt-in

human

advocates.

Virtual

travel

through

geographic

location

ﬁltering

would

allow

users

to

explore

local

conversations

in

other

regions,

while

the

cross-lingual

bridge

layer

would

make

posts

in

other

languages

routinely

accessible

through

automatic

translation,

conﬁdence

indicators,

and

community

corrections.

For

developing

events

and

complex

discussions,

live

opinion

mapping

would

organize

posts

into

an

interactive

multi-perspective

visualization.

Interactive

bubble-chart

opinion

clustering

would

use

axes

to

visualize

individual

and

collective

stances

from

social

media

posts,

fostering

discussions

across

ideological

clusters.

Timeline

generation

for

controversial

societal

issues

would

present

the

historical

development

of

events

and

claims,

while

the

conversation

graph

for

meaningful

discourse

would

visually

label

replies

as

supporting

points,

counterarguments,

or

clarifying

questions.

Population-representative

feeds

would

match

the

visibility

of

opinions

to

their

prevalence

among

the

user

base,

amplifying

widely

held

but

under-expressed

opinions

and

exposing

in-groups

to

a

larger

range

of

opinions.

To

facilitate

constructive

interaction

across

ideological

divides,

MatchAcross

would

connect

users

across

ideological

divides,

using

social

proximity

(e.g.,

friends

of

friends)

as

a

bridge.

2

(4)

Strengthen

accuracy,

civility,

fairness,

and

evidentiary

infrastructure.

Collaborative

fact-checking

would

allow

users

to

work

together

to

investigate

claims

and

provide

context

to

content.

AI

fact-checking

could

work

alongside

it

to

ﬂag

claims

and

attach

expert-reviewed

sources

directly

to

posts,

with

transparency

about

source

and

model

selection.

An

AI

civility

toolkit

complements

traditional

moderation

by

providing

opt-in,

user-controlled

support

before

posting

(e.g.,

clearer

framing)

and

ﬁltering

of

incoming

content.

Relatedly,

ad-hominem

detection

could

provide

immediate

feedback

and

ranking

signals

that

help

keep

discussion

focused

on

arguments

by

reducing

personal

attacks.

To

protect

crowd-driven

systems

from

manipulation,

automated

detection

of

ﬁlter

algorithm

manipulation

would

combine

credibility

scoring,

anomaly

detection,

reputation

thresholds,

and

network

analysis

to

identify

artiﬁcial

engagement.

For

courts

and

journalists,

the

crowd

media

collage

synthesizer

would

combine

overlapping

eyewitness

recordings

into

synchronized

accounts

while

preserving

attribution

to

each

original

source.

The

selective-scope

immutable

stream

ledger

would

complement

this

by

authenticating

viral

eyewitness

videos

only

when

risk

signals

trigger

it,

blocking

the

viral

spread

of

tampered

footage.

Finally,

resolving

popularity

and

majority

bias

would

give

users

the

option

to

evaluate

anonymized

content

independently

of

its

creator’s

identity

or

popularity,

helping

new

and

underrepresented

voices

receive

attention

based

on

the

perceived

quality

of

their

contributions.

A

thread-privacy

toggle

would

complement

these

mechanisms

by

allowing

users

to

control

the

privacy

level

of

their

post,

making

higher-risk

conversations

safer

without

requiring

a

private

conversation.

This

white

paper’s

overall

objective

is

to

explore

how

we

can

deliberately

design

an

ecosystem

of

signals,

incentives,

user

controls,

and

integrity

tools

that

make

high-quality

democratic

discourse

easier,

more

rewarding,

and

more

resilient.

The

design

of

social

media

should

ﬂow

from

democratic

values

–

including

pluralism,

free

expression,

equal

participation,

public

justiﬁcation,

transparency,

collective

self-rule,

and

human

authentication

–

and

will

require

contributions

from

the

humanities,

the

sciences,

and

engineering.

We

hope

this

white

paper

provides

a

starting

point

for

a

broader

discussion.

Introduction

Social

media

have

revolutionized

human

communication

(boyd

2010).

Every

one

of

us

can

now

broadcast

opinions

and

insights

publicly

in

an

instant.

Depending

on

likes

and

shares,

our

messages

can

reach

millions

of

people.

The

advent

of

social

media

was

initially

rightly

seen

as

a

liberating

development.

It

promised

more

shared

experiences,

enabling

people

to

better

connect

and

understand

each

other.

It

also

promised

a

more

participatory

political

discourse

nationally

and

internationally.

A

classic

concept

of

a

space

for

democratic

discourse

is

the

“public

sphere”:

a

space

in

which

citizens

can

deliberate

about

matters

of

common

concern

outside

direct

state

or

market

control

(Habermas

1989).

The

internet

frees

people

from

the

requirement

of

physical

presence

in

a

town

square

or

salon

and

enables

the

creation

of

social

media

as

much

larger

and

more

accessible

digital

public

spaces

for

discourse.

They

can

enable

us

to

share

our

insights

and

help

each

other

think,

elevating

our

collective

intelligence

(Benkler

2006).

Social

media

can

also

help

citizens

form

social

movements,

and

have

in

fact

enabled

movements

to

rapidly

mobilize

and

gain

visibility

(though

they

also

created

new

vulnerabilities

to

fragmentation

and

surveillance;

Tufekci

2017).

For

all

these

reasons,

the

promise

of

social

media

ought

to

be

very

much

alive.

Over

the

past

decade,

however,

the

perils

of

social

media

have

come

to

the

fore.

Social

media

are

increasingly

seen

as

responsible

for

social

isolation

(rather

than

connection;

Primack

et

al.

2017),

political

polarization

(through

ﬁlter

bubbles

and

echo

chambers;

Cinelli

et

al.

2021,

Sunstein

2017,

Bakshy

2015,

Haidt

&

Bail

ongoing),

mental

health

issues

(Keles

et

al.

2020,

Haidt

2024),

misinformation

(unreliable

and

misleading

information;

Lazer

et

al.

2018),

and

disinformation

(organized

messaging

campaigns

with

manipulative

intent;

Bradshaw

and

Howard

2019).

A

systematic

review

of

496

articles

on

digital

media

and

democracy

reports

a

mixed

picture:

digital

media

were

associated

with

greater

political

participation

and

information

consumption

in

some

contexts,

but

also

with

declining

political

trust,

rising

populism,

and

increasing

polarization,

especially

in

established

democracies

(Lorenz-Spreen

et

al.

2022).

Rather

than

connecting

us

in

the

right

ways,

the

social

media

we

currently

have

seem

to

isolate

us

as

individuals,

divide

us

into

artiﬁcial

tribes,

polarize

our

political

opinions,

and

sometimes

bring

out

our

worst

impulses—making

us

lose

our

civility

or

enabling

us

to

cause

serious

harm

to

other

people’s

livelihood

and

safety.

Social-media

posts

expressing

animosity

toward

political

out-groups

receive

especially

high

engagement

(Rathje

et

al.

2021).

False

news

on

Twitter

has

been

shown

to

diﬀuse

farther,

faster,

more

deeply,

and

more

broadly

than

true

news,

with

especially

strong

eﬀects

for

political

news

(Vosoughi

et

al.

2018).

Some

feel

that

these

negative

outcomes

are

inevitable

eﬀects

of

heightened

connectedness,

and

that

social

media

simply

hold

a

mirror

to

us,

revealing

ugly

truths

about

human

nature.

However,

there

is

no

reason

for

such

“Lord

of

the

Flies”

fatalism.

The

social

dynamics

on

social

media

reﬂect

not

just

human

nature

but

importantly

also

the

“game

design”

of

current

platforms.

One

theory

suggests

that

the

way

current

platforms

are

designed

ampliﬁes

natural

tendencies

toward

moral-emotional

expression

and

in-group

signaling

(Brady

et

al.

2020).

This

means

that

the

architecture

of

existing

social

media

must

be

re-evaluated

(Landemore

et

al.

2021).

Academic

studies

have

focused

on

documenting

antisocial

behaviors—misinformation,

hate

speech,

and

outrage.

More

work

is

needed

on

how

platforms

can

be

designed

to

encourage

prosocial

behavior,

for

5

example,

by

facilitating

a

sense

of

community

and

by

fostering

collective

problem-solving

(Dörr

et

al.

2025).

It

is

not

at

all

clear

that

the

particular

social

media

platforms

that

dominate

today

show

us

the

limits

of

what

is

possible.

Social

media

platforms

such

as

Facebook,

X,

TikTok,

Instagram,

and

Snapchat

each

started

with

an

inspired

initial

design

and

have

adjusted

that

design

over

the

years

to

increase

advertising

revenue.

This

incentive

structure

gives

rise

to

a

business

model

that

has

been

labeled

“surveillance

capitalism,”

in

which

platforms

extract

behavioral

data,

infer

users’

interests

and

intentions,

and

monetize

through

prediction

and

targeting

(Zuboﬀ

2019).

Companies

are

centrally

motivated

to

increase

“engagement”

because

it

is

the

engine

that

drives

ad

revenue,

which

in

turn

sustains

or

grows

the

platform’s

valuation

(Brady

et

al.

2026).

Users

produce

and

consume

content.

Advertisers

pay

for

access

to

users’

attention

and

data.

To

maximize

ad

revenue,

platforms

must

maximize

both

the

quantity

of

attention

available

(time

on

site,

number

of

sessions)

and

the

quality

of

targeting

(how

precisely

ads

can

be
