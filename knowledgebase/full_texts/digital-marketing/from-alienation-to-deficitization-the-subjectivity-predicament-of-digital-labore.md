the subjectivity predicament of

Contemporary research on digital labor confronts a methodological tension:
while mainstream occupational health psychology offers rich empirical evidence
on the psychological distress of digital workers, it fails to capture the more
fundamental predicament of subjectivity underlying this distress—namely,
Why, When formal autonomy has been elevated to an unprecedented
height, does workers’ inner experience become increasingly passive? We
argue that this explanatory gap stems from implicit theoretical assumptions
in mainstream psychology, which treats psychological needs as ahistorical,
universal constants and presupposes the individual as a rational actor
who calculates gains and losses in response to external pressures and
resources. Fromm’ s psychoanalytic social psychology provides an alternative
theoretical paradigm. Through the core concept of “social character,”
Fromm reveals how socioeconomic structures operate on individuals not as
external variables, but by shaping their deepest need structures and affective
orientations, so that individuals “voluntarily” come to feel, think, and act in
the manner required by the system. This article systematically elaborates
the epistemological implications and methodological value of three core
Frommian concepts—alienation, social character, and escape from freedom—
and proposes that contemporary digital labor cultivates a “digital marketing
character”: in the process of continuous self-curation, self-quantiﬁcation, and
self-commodiﬁcation, workers experience alienation as self-realization. The
psychological effect of this character structure is a diffuse state of “psycho-
emotional deﬁcitization”—a deep-seated experience of constantly feeling
insufﬁciently productive and valuable regardless of objective performance.
We critically examine Fromm’s own framework, acknowledging its limitations
regarding universalist assumptions, normative presuppositions, and historical
boundedness. We also situate this intervention within the broader landscape
of critical traditions—including Foucauldian, psychosocial, psychodynamic,
posthumanist, and platform capitalism scholarship—and explicitly delimit the
contribution as a strategic bridge between critical theory and empirical
work psychology. To move beyond theoretical speculation, we provide a
systematic methodological roadmap with explicit, testable hypotheses, scale
development strategies, and multiple research designs spanning qualitative,
psychometric, longitudinal, SEM, mixed-methods, and comparative approaches.

1 Introduction: when autonomy
becomes a burden—a psychological
problem requiring a new paradigm
Contemporary digital labor presents a phenomenon that
bewilders mainstream psychology: workers have acquired
unprecedented formal autonomy—the freedom to decide when,
where, how intensely, and with whom to work—yet their inner
experience is often characterized by powerlessness, anxiety, and
diﬀuse self-doubt. One video creator with hundreds of thousands
of followers described her state in an interview: “Every time I post
a piece of work, I only allow myself 5 min to celebrate the data,
whether good or bad. After those 5 min, I have to start thinking
about the next topic.”
These accounts point to a particular form of psychological
distress: it breeds beneath the veneer of “autonomous
choice, ” and its nature is not a direct reaction to work
intensity, but a deeper, diﬀuse unease about self-worth.
Mainstream occupational health psychology has extensively
studied such distress, including burnout, anxiety, depression,
and work addiction (Matilla-Santander et al., 2025), yet a
fundamental question remains insuﬃciently interrogated:
Why, When formal autonomy is maximized, does
workers’ subjective experience tend toward passivity and
emptiness?
1.1 The explanatory logic of mainstream
psychological frameworks and its limits
To grasp the theoretical signiﬁcance of this question, it is
necessary to ﬁrst examine the explanatory logic of mainstream
psychological frameworks. The Job Demands-Resources (JD-R)
model understands the psychological distress of digital workers as
an imbalance between job demands and job resources: algorithmic
management generates persistent pressure, while the lack of social
support undermines coping resources, and together they lead
to burnout (Bakker and Demerouti, 2017; Demerouti et al.,
2001; Schaufeli and Bakker, 2004). The Eﬀort-Reward Imbalance
(ERI) model emphasizes that workers invest substantial time
and emotional energy, but the platform’s algorithmic pricing
and evaluation mechanisms render rewards highly uncertain,
and this imbalance triggers sustained stress responses (Siegrist,
1996). Self-determination theory (SDT) approaches the issue from
the perspective of basic psychological needs, suggesting that the
needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness may all be
simultaneously frustrated in digital labor (Bartholomew et al.,
2011; Ryan and Deci, 2000; Sheldon et al., 2001) systematically
analyzed how algorithmic control replaces traditional managerial
authority as the new terrain of labor process contestation; Parent-
Rocheleau and Parker (2022) further delineated the multiple
mechanisms through which algorithmic management aﬀects
workers.
These frameworks have amassed a wealth of valuable
empirical knowledge, but their explanations encounter a systematic
boundary: they all presuppose the individual as a rational actor
who calculates gains and losses when facing external variables,
yet they fail to explain the diﬀuse suﬀering that seems completely
internalized and almost unconscious (Lindebaum and Langer,
2024). Concretely, this limitation manifests at three levels:
First, a weakness in explaining the “autonomy paradox.”
The JD-R model treats autonomy as an inherently positive
job resource (Schaufeli and Bakker, 2004), but in digital labor,
heightened formal autonomy not only fails to yield the expected
increase in wellbeing but may actually exacerbate psychological
distress. Empirical research shows that the relationship between
perceived “job autonomy” and workplace wellbeing among gig
workers is mediated by work alienation, and that perceived
algorithmic control moderates this relationship (Wan et al., 2024).
This implies that “autonomy” in platform labor may not be
genuine self-determination, but rather a “pseudo-autonomy” pre-
framed by a speciﬁc social structure. When workers internalize
this pseudo-autonomy as “my own choice, ” external control is
transformed into self-control, while their originally frustrated
psychological needs can no longer be recognized by themselves as
frustrated.
Second, an ahistorical presupposition about needs themselves.
Self-determination theory treats autonomy, competence, and
relatedness as cross-culturally universal psychological needs, but
it brackets a deeper question: How are the concrete forms and
modes of satisfaction of these needs shaped by social structures?
A feudal peasant and a digital worker both “need” identity,
but the former seeks it within a stable hierarchical order, while
the latter searches for it within a ﬂuid, quantiﬁed ranking
system. The essence of this diﬀerence is that the social form of
needs has undergone fundamental change, rather than merely a
diﬀerence in the “degree of satisfaction.” A Frommian line of
questioning thus enters: under a speciﬁc social structure, What
has the individual “learned to need”? How has the logic of capital

Third, a lack of analysis of the social-structural roots of

very “shape” of this distress carries some sort of social-structural
information. The systematic review by Matilla-Santander et al.

attention to how platform labor profoundly shapes workers’
self-experience.
It is precisely these three explanatory boundaries that constitute
the theoretical starting point of this article. To cross these
boundaries requires the introduction of a diﬀerent theoretical
paradigm—an analytical tool capable of anchoring psychological
phenomena within social structures, without dissolving subjectivity
into a mere epiphenomenon of structure.
1.2 The paradigmatic value of Fromm’s
psychoanalytic social psychology
Fromm’s psychoanalytic social psychology oﬀers precisely
such a paradigm. In disciplinary history, Fromm occupies a
unique position: he received rigorous psychoanalytic training
(studying within the Freudian tradition), was simultaneously
deeply inﬂuenced by Marx’s historical materialism, and had
profound academic aﬃnities with the critical theory of the
Frankfurt School (McLaughlin, 1998). This conﬂuence of multiple
intellectual traditions makes his theory diﬀerent both from
mainstream psychology’s tendency to abstract the individual from
their socio-historical context and from certain radical social
theories that reduce the individual entirely to a product of structure.
O’Donnokoé et al. (2025), in a recent editorial for a special issue on
Fromm, also emphasize that Fromm’s humanistic psychoanalysis
still provides a valuable normative orientation in the face of
contemporary social and political challenges.
The core value of the Frommian paradigm lies in a key
methodological mediating concept—“social character.” Social
character refers to the nucleus of the character structure shared
by the majority of members of a speciﬁc group; its function
is “to channel the psychic energy of the members of a given
society in such a way that their behavior is not a matter of
conscious decision whether or not to follow the social pattern,
but a matter of wanting to act as they have to act” (Fromm,
1947, p. 283). The epistemological signiﬁcance of this concept
is that it breaks the dichotomy between “individual psychology”
and “social structure” prevalent in mainstream psychology. Social
character is neither purely personal, distinct from personality
traits in the sense of individual diﬀerences; nor is it purely
social, diﬀerent from “norms” or “institutions” external to the
individual. It is the interface between society and the individual—
social structure enters the individual’s deepest needs, emotions,
and desires through the shaping of social character, and the
individual, by internalizing this social character, “voluntarily”
acts in ways that adapt to the requirements of the social
system.
Complementing this concept are Fromm’s theories of
alienation and escape from freedom. Fromm extends alienation
from the economic sphere into a psychological dimension,
making it a mode of “psychic experience”—the person does not
experience himself as the source of his own power and as an
active agent, but rather experiences himself and others as objects
to be exchanged and quantiﬁed (Fromm, 1955). Escape from
freedom reveals a paradoxical psychodynamic: after modern
individuals have acquired unprecedented formal freedom, they
develop psychological mechanisms for escaping it precisely
because they lack suﬃcient inner strength to bear the aloneness
and responsibility that such freedom entails (Fromm, 1941).
These three concepts constitute a complete analytical
apparatus: social character connects structure and individual,
alienation describes the experiential quality of this connection,
and escape from freedom explains why the individual remains
within this experience. It is this apparatus that can potentially
provide insights, unavailable in mainstream psychology, to
answer the paradox of the “passivization of subjectivity” among
digital workers.
One might question whether concepts developed in the context
of mid-twentieth-century industrial capitalism remain suitable for
understanding the substantially diﬀerent historical conﬁguration
of contemporary platform-mediated labor. The historical distance
is real, yet it does not invalidate Fromm’s framework. Fromm’s
core concepts—social character as a mechanism for channeling
psychic energy into socially required forms, alienation as a mode
of psychic experience, escape from freedom as a psychodynamic
principle—are formal architectures whose empirical content
is historically variable. The “digital marketing character” we
propose is not a simple application of the marketing orientation
to a new setting but a deliberate reformulation reﬂecting
the qualitatively new conditions of platform-mediated labor:
compulsory self-curation, internalized self-quantiﬁcation, and self-
commodiﬁcation experienced as autonomy. We are not projecting
contemporary problems onto Fromm; we are drawing on his
formal architecture to generate new, empirically testable concepts
adequate to present conditions.

2 The epistemological foundations
of Fromm’s psychoanalytic social
psychology
Before entering the concrete analysis of digital labor, it
is necessary to systematically elaborate the theoretical roots
of Fromm’s core concepts, aiming to clarify what kind of
understanding and explanatory mode these concepts oﬀer to
psychology, what their epistemological presuppositions are, what
they allow us to see that mainstream frameworks cannot, and where
their methodological operational space lies.
2.1 From Freud to Fromm: the social turn
in psychoanalysis
To understand the paradigmatic value of Fromm, one must
ﬁrst understand the critical turn he made in the history of
psychoanalytic theory. Freudian psychoanalysis traced the deep
driving forces of human behavior to biological instincts—libido
and the aggressive instinct—with society establishing civilizational
order through the repression and sublimation of these instincts. In
this framework, society stands as a repressive force opposite the
individual, and psychological conﬂict is the eternal war between
instinct and civilization.
Fromm made a fundamental revision to this framework. He
argued that the key feature distinguishing humans from other
animals lies precisely in the weakening of instinctual determinism:
humans are no longer governed by ﬁxed instinctual patterns,
thereby acquiring a certain “freedom, ” but at the same time, they
lose the natural connectedness to the world that instincts provide
(Fromm, 1941, 1947). This “existential dichotomy” generates
uniquely human psychological needs—what Fromm called the
needs for relatedness, transcendence, rootedness, a sense of
identity, and a frame of orientation. Although these needs are
rooted in the existential condition of humans, their concrete modes
of expression and satisfaction are shaped by speciﬁc socioeconomic
structures.
The theoretical consequences of this revision are far-reaching.
It means that psychoanalysis is no longer a science of eternal
instincts, but is transformed into an analytical tool for examining
the character structure and psychological experience of speciﬁc
groups under speciﬁc historical conditions (Funk, 1998). Fromm’s
view is that the “hoarding tendency” or the “marketing orientation”
in capitalist society are not variants of a universal human
psychological structure, but products of the mode of channeling
human energy required for the functioning of a particular
socioeconomic system. A society that needs enforced saving to
accumulate capital must cultivate tendencies toward thrift and
self-discipline; a society that needs continuously to create new
demands to expand consumption must cultivate a desire structure
of perpetual insatiability. Character structure is the anchoring
device of social structure in the individual psyche (Fromm, 1947).
2.2 Social character: the methodological
mediator between individual and society
The concept of “social character” is the methodological core
of Fromm’s entire theoretical system (Riesman, 1950). If one
were to summarize its epistemological function with a concise
formula, it would be: socioeconomic structure → social character
→ individual behavior and experience. The arrows in this formula
do not indicate a causal relationship—that would be too crude—but
rather a relationship of structural coupling. Social structure does
not “cause” social character, but rather “demands” and “selectively
reinforces” certain character tendencies, making individuals with
those tendencies more likely to “function normally” and ﬁnd
satisfaction within that social system.
The uniqueness of this concept lies in its transcendence of
two common presuppositions in mainstream psychology. One may
be called methodological individualism: treating the individual
as the basic unit of analysis, with society being merely the
result of the aggregation or interaction of individual actions.
The other may be called environmental determinism: treating
social structures (such as class position, working conditions)
as external variables that act directly on the individual, who
responds to these variables through processes such as “cognitive
appraisal” or “social learning.” Fromm’s perspective diﬀers from
both: social character is internalized structure—it is the mode of
existence of structure within the individual, yet it is also distinct
from individual-level trait diﬀerences or cognitive responses to
the environment.
In terms of understanding digital labor, this means: when
discussing the psychological distress of digital workers, we are
not merely discussing how the external stressor of “algorithmic
management” leads to burnout through cognitive appraisal; we
are also discussing a particular historical shaping within the
workers’ deep need structure—they “need” to be continuously
seen, rated, and recognized as “valuable individuals, ” and this
very need, in turn, supports the continuous operation of the
platform economy. This is a deep psychological fact: structurally
produced, yet simultaneously experienced authentically by the

2.3 The psychological dimension of
alienation: from Marx to Fromm
Fromm’s theory of alienation is one of his most original
contributions within the Marxist tradition. In Economic and
Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844, Marx located alienation primarily
in the labor process—the worker is alienated from the product
of labor, the activity of labor, species-being, and other human
beings. Fromm (1955) expanded alienation from a category of
political economy into a category of psychological experience that
permeates the entirety of life.
As Fromm (1955) deﬁned it, “By alienation is meant a mode
of experience in which the person experiences himself as an alien.
He has become, one might say, estranged from himself. He does
not experience himself as the center of his world, as the creator of
his own acts—but his acts and their consequences have become his
masters, whom he obeys, or whom he may even worship” (p. 120).
This experience can occur in labor, but also in consumption, leisure,
and interpersonal relations. When a person buys a commodity in
order to “have” it as a display of identity, they are alienated in
consumption. When a person’s leisure activities are pursued not
out of intrinsic interest, but because these activities are market-
validated lifestyles that can be “shown oﬀ, ” they are alienated
in leisure. When a person views others as potential business
opportunities with a certain “exchange value, ” rather than as whole
beings, they are alienated in interpersonal relations.
The methodological signiﬁcance of this extension is that
alienation is no longer a class-speciﬁc problem occurring only on
the factory ﬂoor, but a diﬀuse psychological experience that can
permeate all spheres of life under a particular social structure.
This makes Fromm’s theory of alienation intrinsically suited for
analyzing digital labor—because in digital labor, the boundaries
between labor, consumption, leisure, and sociality have already
dissolved (Fuchs, 2014; Terranova, 2000). A self-media creator
ﬁlms content during a gathering with friends: is this labor or
leisure? Socializing or producing? When all domains of life are
swept into the torrent of self-commodiﬁcation, alienation is no
longer an attribute of a speciﬁc activity, but becomes the backdrop
of the entire lifeworld.
2.4 Escape from freedom: the
psychodynamic masked by
pseudo-autonomy
Escape from Freedom is Fromm’s most inﬂuential work, yet
its core insights have been used very little in contemporary
psychological research. The book reveals a fundamental paradox:
after modern people acquired unprecedented freedom, they
paradoxically developed psychological mechanisms for escaping it.
The reason lies in the dual nature of freedom—it is both “negative
freedom, ” liberation from traditional bonds (freedom from), and
“positive freedom, ” the need to face the world’s uncertainties alone,
make choices, and bear the consequences (freedom to). When
individuals lack suﬃcient inner strength and external resources to
bear the autonomy required by positive freedom, freedom becomes
a burden, inducing feelings of isolation and powerlessness, which
in turn activate the psychodynamic of escape.
Fromm analyzed three primary mechanisms of escape:
authoritarianism (dissolving oneself into a powerful external
force to gain security), destructiveness (eliminating one’s own
powerlessness by destroying external objects), and automaton
conformity (abandoning one’s individual self to become the kind of
person the cultural pattern expects). The common essence of these
three mechanisms is that the individual trades away an independent
self in exchange for protection from the anxiety of aloneness.
This concept has direct inspirational value for understanding
the pseudo-autonomy paradox in digital labor. If, as Fromm
revealed, freedom can become a burden under speciﬁc conditions,
then the celebrated “autonomy” in digital labor—the freedom to
choose working hours, location, and intensity—may be not only a
resource but also a source of pressure. When workers are granted
high formal autonomy but lack genuine internal resources (such as
a stable sense of self-worth) and external conditions (such as social
security, collective bargaining power) to sustain this autonomy,
they can easily develop the psychodynamic of escape from freedom:
not ﬂeeing the platform, but integrating themselves ever more
actively into the platform’s logic, gaining a substitute for security
by becoming the “most model worker.”
2.5 The limitations of Fromm’s
framework and a qualiﬁed use
The preceding analyses of alienation, social character, and
escape from freedom provide a powerful lens for understanding
the psychodynamics of digital labor, yet no theoretical framework
is without its historical and normative blind spots. We now turn
to a critical examination of Fromm’s own theoretical framework.
His postulation of universal human needs rooted in the existential
condition has been critiqued by social constructionist and cultural
psychological traditions, which argue that needs are constituted
through historically speciﬁc discourses and practices rather than
given by an ahistorical human essence (Burr, 2015; Gergen,
1985, 1991; Moscovici, 2000). His concepts of health and the
productive orientation carry normative assumptions about human
fulﬁllment that reﬂect speciﬁc cultural and historical values rather
than universally valid standards (Foucault, 1965; Szasz, 1960). His
conception of an authentic self that can be realized under the
right social conditions has been challenged by poststructuralist
and Lacanian perspectives, which question the very notion of
a pre-given self to which one can be true (Foucault, 1988;
Stavrakakis, 2007; Žižek, 1989). Moreover, translating his clinically
grounded concepts into empirically operationalizable constructs
inevitably involves a degree of phenomenological reduction—the
rich, existential texture of his descriptions cannot be fully captured
by psychometric scales (Westen, 1998).
In light of these limitations, this article adopts a qualiﬁed and
pragmatic use of Fromm. His concepts are deployed as descriptive
and explanatory tools rather than as normative prescriptions
or metaphysical commitments. The “productive orientation” is

3 Psychoanalytic social psychology
and digital labor studies: spaces of
convergence
Before unfolding the theoretical construction of this article,
it is necessary to clearly map out the “dialogical space” between
the Frommian paradigm and existing psychological research on
digital labor, supplemented by engagement with the broader
interdisciplinary literature on platform capitalism, algorithmic
surveillance, and digital subjectivity.
3.1 Existing achievements and
unresolved questions in the
psychological study of digital labor
Over the past decade, psychological research on digital labor
has amassed substantial ﬁndings. The levels of burnout, anxiety,
and depression among gig economy workers have been repeatedly
measured, and factors such as algorithmic management, lack of
social support, and job insecurity have been identiﬁed as signiﬁcant
predictors (Matilla-Santander et al., 2025; Vallas and Schor, 2020;
Wood et al., 2019). Some studies have begun to attend to deeper
mediating and moderating mechanisms, such as the mediating
role of work alienation between autonomy and wellbeing (Wan
et al., 2024), and how gamiﬁcation mechanisms drive behavioral
addiction. Lang et al. (2023) revealed the signiﬁcant impact of
perceived algorithmic control on the psychological exhaustion of
gig workers. Gandini (2019) analyzed new forms of managerial
control in the gig economy. van Doorn (2017) uncovered the
intersecting dimensions of gendered and racialized exploitation in
platform labor.
However, a few studies have touched upon the core issue that
concerns this article but failed to oﬀer an adequate explanation
within existing theoretical frameworks. For instance, Wan et al.
(2024) demonstrated that gig workers experienced more severe self-
exploitation under conditions of high autonomy, a phenomenon
that the JD-R model cannot fully explain. Similarly, Lang et al.
(2023) found that perceived algorithmic control signiﬁcantly
intensiﬁed workers’ burnout, and that this burnout in turn
mediated the relationship between algorithmic control and work
engagement—suggesting that algorithmic management does not
simply act as an external stressor, but intervenes more deeply in
workers’ aﬀective and motivational systems.
These “unresolved questions” point in the same direction: the
explanation of certain core psychological experiences in digital
labor requires a theoretical perspective capable of connecting
the deep psychodynamics of the individual with the production
requirements of the social structure.
3.2 Resonances of the Frommian
tradition in contemporary organization
studies
In recent years, critical organization studies have witnessed
a clear scholarly turn back to Fromm’s theoretical tradition.
Lindebaum and Langer (2024) proposed the concept of “psycho-
emotional deﬁcitization” in Organization, directly borrowing
from Fromm’s theory of social character. They argue that
workers under cognitive capitalism internalize a socially set
ideal image of the “suﬃciently good worker” and continuously
compare their own present state against this ideal, thereby
experiencing a diﬀuse feeling of “never being enough.” Fuchs
(2020) explored how Fromm’s thought on communication can
provide a critical perspective for understanding digital capitalism
and algorithmic society. Moore (2018) systematically examined
how digital technologies place workers under the pressure of

digital labor. These existing studies constitute the dialogical basis of
this article, but they have yet to integrate Fromm’s concepts into a
coherent analytical framework with implications for psychological
theory and methodology.
3.3 Engaging the broader
interdisciplinary literature
Beyond the psychological and organizational literature, the
phenomena we address have been extensively examined across
several key interdisciplinary streams. Platform capitalism studies
establish the structural foundations: platforms extract data while
externalizing risk onto workers (Srnicek, 2017; van Dijck et al.,
2018; Woodcock, 2020). The governmentality and neoliberal
subjectivity tradition, from Foucault, Foucault’s (1988, 2008)
technologies of the self to Han’s (2017) psychopolitics and
Bröckling’s (2016) entrepreneurial self, dissects how power operates
through the constitution of self-governing, performance-oriented
subjects. Complementing these, critical work on algorithmic
management and the quantiﬁed self clariﬁes the technological
infrastructure of internalized self-quantiﬁcation (Pasquale, 2015;
Zuboﬀ, 2019; Lupton, 2016; Cheney-Lippold, 2017). Why self-
commodiﬁcation comes to feel autonomous is further illuminated
by theories of emotional and free labor (Hochschild, 1983;
Terranova, 2000; Jarrett, 2016) and empirically corroborated by
ethnographies of platform work and “über-alienation” (Rosenblat,
2018; Glavin et al., 2021). Posthumanist approaches (Barad,
2007; Braidotti, 2013; Latour, 2005) challenge the anthropocentric
assumptions of our framework, a limitation we recognize while
maintaining that the ﬁrst-personal phenomenology of suﬀering
remains irreducibly psychodynamic.
Collectively, these bodies of work have substantially advanced
our understanding of the structural, discursive, and technological
conditions of digital labor. Yet they leave open a speciﬁc gap.

The Foucauldian and psychopolitical traditions excel at tracing the

3.4 Situating the contribution: a strategic
bridge, not a theoretical void
We engage this broader critical ﬁeld not to claim that Fromm
ﬁlls a void no other tradition has addressed, but to make explicit the
distinctive role a Frommian framework can play: a strategic bridge
between existing critical traditions and empirical work psychology.
Critical social psychology, the Foucauldian tradition, neoliberal
subjectivity studies, the psychodynamics of work, and platform
capitalism scholarship have all delivered powerful critiques of
mainstream assumptions (Gergen, 1985; Foucault, 1988; Rose,
1999; Dardot and Laval, 2013; Hollway and Jeﬀerson, 2013;
Dejours, 2015; Srnicek, 2017). Our intervention does not compete
with them; it strategically supplements them with a conceptual
resource conspicuously absent from the methodological toolkit of
mainstream work psychology—a psychoanalytic theory of social
character that combines clinical depth with empirical tractability.
Fromm’s framework is suited to this bridging function because
of its unique epistemological position: it is grounded in a clinical
tradition attentive to subjective depth, yet it formulates its core
concepts at a level of abstraction that can be translated into
empirically operationalizable constructs without requiring prior
acceptance of the full Lacanian or Foucauldian apparatus. Two
complementary relationships are especially salient. First, Fromm
and Foucault operate at complementary levels. Foucault provides
the anatomy of techniques and rationalities through which self-
governing subjects are constituted, while Fromm provides the
phenomenology of the resulting inner experience—the anxiety,
deﬁcit, and ﬂight from freedom. The social character concept
speciﬁes the psychological mechanism through which technologies
of the self-become anchored in personality. Second, where
platform capitalism scholarship explains structural conditions and
psychopolitical theory oﬀers philosophical diagnosis, we provide
the characterological and psychodynamic account grounded in
psychoanalytic theory—the missing layer of psychological depth
that links structure to lived experience.
The Frommian framework thus functions not as a replacement
for these established traditions but as a means of formulating
testable hypotheses about digital workers’ psychological distress:
hypotheses that are theoretically anchored in a structural
analysis of platform capitalism and neoliberal governmentality,
while remaining methodologically operational within the
quantitative, mixed-methods, and qualitative designs familiar
to work psychology. Its bridging function is to make available to
empirical psychology a set of historically informed and incisive
concepts for capturing the subjectivity predicament of digital labor.
This, precisely, constitutes the article’s distinctive contribution.
4 Theoretical construction: digital
marketing character,
psycho-emotional deﬁcitization,
and escape from freedom
This chapter constitutes the theoretical core of the entire
article. We will sequentially develop three interrelated theoretical
propositions: (1) digital labor cultivates a historically speciﬁc
type of social character—“digital marketing character”; (2) the
psychological eﬀect of this character structure is a diﬀuse
state of “psycho-emotional deﬁcitization”; and (3) this deﬁcit
experience and the psychodynamic of escape from freedom are
intertwined, driving a recurrent cycle of self-commodiﬁcation and
emotional exhaustion.
4.1 Digital marketing character: the
contemporary transformation of
Fromm’s “marketing orientation”
In Man for Himself , Fromm proposed the “marketing
orientation” character type. “The character orientation which is
rooted in the experience of oneself as a commodity and of one’s
value as exchange value I call the marketing orientation” (Fromm,
1947, p. 68). In the marketing-oriented character structure, “I am
as you desire me”—the individual molds and packages the self-
according to market demands, rather than deﬁning the self-based
on intrinsic qualities.
This character orientation has undergone a qualitative leap
under the conditions of digital labor. While the “market” in the
traditional marketing orientation remained a relatively abstract
metaphor—one needed to “sell” oneself in the job market and
the marriage market, but these markets were not visible in real
time—in the digital platform economy, the market becomes an
enveloping, real-time, omnipresent presence. Ratings, rankings,
follower counts, click-through rates, completion rates—these
metrics allow workers to see at any moment the “stock price” of
their personality on the market (Gandini, 2019; Kellogg et al., 2020;
Wood et al., 2019).
This diﬀerence is a qualitative transformation, not merely a
quantitative intensiﬁcation. This article proposes the concept of
“digital marketing character” to describe the speciﬁc form taken
by the marketing orientation under the conditions of digital labor.
Compared with Fromm’s classic description, the digital marketing
character possesses three qualitative features:
First, the compulsoriness of self-curation. In the traditional
marketing orientation, individuals needed to “package” their image
for speciﬁc occasions such as interviews, sales, or social encounters.
Under the digital marketing character, self-curation becomes a
lifelong task covering all domains of life. Updates on social media,
persona maintenance on work platforms, deliberate guidance of

Second, the internalization of self-quantiﬁcation. Digital labor

indicators. Traditional research treats these indicators as external
management tools (Kellogg et al., 2020; Parent-Rocheleau and

anxiety stems not only from income loss, but more profoundly
from the instantaneous experience of a drop in “self-worth”; when

existential question, “Am I still valuable?” Quantitative indicators
transform from external evaluations into an internal experiential
structure—self-worth becomes measurable by numbers, and must
be measured by numbers. As Moore (2018) reveals, this practice
of self-quantiﬁcation places workers under a persistent pressure of
self-surveillance.
Third, the experience of self-commodiﬁcation as
“autonomous.” This is the most disquieting feature of the
digital marketing character. The traditional presupposition of
alienation theory is that alienated labor is coerced and unpleasant,
and that workers can perceive that “this is not the real me.” The
digital marketing character shatters this presupposition—it enables
the act of “turning oneself into a commodity” to be experienced
as self-expression and self-realization. When a self-media creator
transforms her life into content products, when a digital nomad
turns travel into work and scenery into a backdrop—they genuinely
experience a real sense of pleasure and meaning in the content
they create, in the interaction with followers, in the moment of
landing the next project. This “realness” is precisely the deepest
mode of operation of the digital marketing character, rendering
alienation imperceptible because alienation has donned the cloak
of “self-realization.”
These three features of the digital marketing character form
a mutually reinforcing closed loop: self-curation continuously
produces material available for quantitative evaluation, quantitative
evaluation continuously supplies signals of self-worth being
conﬁrmed or denied, and these signals in turn drive more
vigorous self-commodiﬁcation—all while self-commodiﬁcation
itself is experienced as “I am being myself.” These are not
merely intensiﬁcations of the marketing orientation; they are
transformations reﬂecting the qualitatively new conditions of
platform-mediated labor.
It must be emphasized that the digital marketing character
is not a monolithic singular form. Just as Fromm consistently
stressed that social character is a “shared nucleus” within a
group, rather than an exact replica in each individual, there are
signiﬁcant individual diﬀerences in how digital workers absorb and
embody this character structure. Some workers may have almost
completely internalized the quantitative evaluation system; others
exist in a state of “ambivalent identiﬁcation”; still a few develop
varying degrees of “reﬂective resistance.” These diﬀerences partly
derive from individuals’ life histories and accessible resources,
and partly stem from the individual potential for what Fromm
called a “productive orientation”—the capacity to relate to the
world through love and reason, and to realize one’s powers in
action (Fromm, 1947). This heterogeneity provides the theoretical
possibility for understanding the breaking and repair of the escape-
from-freedom cycle.
4.2 Psycho-emotional deﬁcitization: the
affective consequence of the digital
marketing character
The cultivation of the digital marketing character is not value-
neutral. This article argues that its core psychological eﬀect is a
speciﬁc inner experience—we borrow the term from Lindebaum
and Langer (2024), calling it “psycho-emotional deﬁcitization, ”
and systematically conceptualize it within Fromm’s theoretical
framework.
Psycho-emotional deﬁcitization refers to the process whereby
the individual internalizes an external ideal image of the
“suﬃciently good worker, ” bound to constantly shifting market
standards, and continuously measures their current state against
this ideal, thereby experiencing a diﬀuse sense of “never being
enough” due to the perpetual gap between them. This feeling
constitutes a diﬀuse emotional undertone in self-experience,
distinct from speciﬁc reactions to concrete failures.
This concept has the following qualitative characteristics:
The non-saturability of deﬁcit: This diﬀerentiates it from
“insuﬃciency.” Insuﬃciency points to a concrete shortfall with
an endpoint: “I haven’t yet met this month’s performance target.”
Deﬁcit points to a void that can never be completely ﬁlled: “No
matter how well I do this month, I will have to prove myself again
next month.” This is because when the standard of “suﬃciently
good” is deﬁned by an ever-changing market, “enough” itself is an
inﬁnitely recursive concept—you can never catch up to the market,
because the market is in constant motion.
The internalized nature of deﬁcit: The key is the suﬃx “-
ization”: external standards have been absorbed as a constitutive
component of self-cognition and emotional experience. A digital
worker with objectively excellent performance may still feel, on
a deep level, that they are not working hard enough or are not
valuable enough. The root of deﬁcitization lies in the fact that “they
have taken the platform’s standard as the very measure of self-
evaluation, ” rather than in “whether they have met the platform’s
assessment criteria”—they no longer have any other measure.
The diﬀuseness of deﬁcit: This sense of deﬁcit is not conﬁned
to speciﬁc situations. It does not merely appear during work
but pervades the individual’s entire lifeworld. When a worker
scrolls through successful peers’ cases late into the sleepless
night, when they are unable to enjoy a weekend without any
“output, ” when they half-jokingly call themselves “has-been”
in front of friends—deﬁcitization is the same undercurrent in
all these contexts.
The productivity of deﬁcit: From the perspective of the
social system, the high degree of self-drivenness in psycho-
emotional deﬁcitization makes it a highly “eﬃcient” psychological

4.3 “The fear of becoming superﬂuous”:
escape from freedom in digital labor
At this point, one crucial question remains unresolved: if
psycho-emotional deﬁcitization is a painful experience, What
is the psychological drive that keeps workers trapped in this
painful cycle? Mainstream psychology might explain it through
“economic necessity” or “habit, ” but these explanations cannot
exhaust the phenomenon—many digital workers remain unable
to extricate themselves from this cycle even after their economic
needs are met. This article seeks an answer from Fromm’s theory of
escape from freedom.
In digital labor, formal autonomy—the freedom to accept
or reject gigs at will, to go online or oﬄine anytime, to set
one’s own pace—is a real experience, yet this autonomy is
simultaneously burdensome. When a worker is thrown into formal
freedom but lacks genuine inner strength and external resources
to bear this freedom, freedom transforms into anxiety. This
article argues that, under the conditions of digital labor, this
anxiety crystallizes into a speciﬁc fear—the “fear of becoming
superﬂuous.”
The “fear of becoming superﬂuous” refers to the fact that digital
workers, beneath the veneer of “autonomy, ” deeply experience
the fear that they could at any moment be downgraded by
the algorithm, eliminated by the market, or drowned in the
ﬂood of data. “Superﬂuous” here has an existential connotation:
it is not just about being dismissed by a particular platform,
but, in a more fundamental sense, about no longer being
“needed” by the world.
This fear has speciﬁc structural sources in digital
labor. Boundless competition forces workers to face a
theoretically inﬁnite pool of global laborers (Wood et al.,
2019); algorithmic opacity makes evaluation criteria both
palpably present and frustratingly elusive (Kellogg et al.,
2020); atomized labor deprives workers of collective identity
and the possibility of solidarity (Gandini, 2019). Lang
et al.’s (2023) research provides indirect support: perceived
algorithmic control signiﬁcantly triggers burnout experiences
among gig workers, suggesting that algorithmic management
profoundly intervenes in workers’ aﬀective and motivational
systems.
Consequently, workers develop a speciﬁc mode of escaping
this fear: integrating themselves even more actively into the
platform’s logic—updating more diligently, tracking data
more closely, self-commodifying more thoroughly. This
constitutes a contemporary version of escape from freedom:
the individual, by completely abandoning the independent
self and being entirely engulfed by market logic, obtains
psychological protection from the fear of being “discarded.”
Work—ceaseless, self-proliferating work—becomes the refuge
from existential anxiety.
4.4 An integrated formulation of the
theoretical framework
In summary, the theoretical framework of this article can be
formulated as a causal dynamic chain with three links:
Link 1 (Structure→ Character): The speciﬁc labor organization
of the digital platform economy—algorithmic management,
quantitative evaluation, open competition—cultivates a “digital
marketing character.” The core features of this character structure
are that workers continuously engage in self-curation, self-
quantiﬁcation, and self-commodiﬁcation, and experience self-
commodiﬁcation as self-realization.
Link 2 (Character → Experience): The inner experience of the
digital marketing character is “psycho-emotional deﬁcitization, ” a
diﬀuse, non-saturable, internalized feeling of “never being enough.”
This feeling pervades self-experience, constituting an enduring
emotional undertone, and is qualitatively diﬀerent from speciﬁc
reactions to concrete failures.
Link 3 (Experience → Drive): Psycho-emotional deﬁcitization
activates the psychological mechanism of escape from freedom,
manifested as the “fear of becoming superﬂuous”—a deep-seated
fear of being eliminated, forgotten, and no longer needed. This fear
drives workers to throw themselves even more actively into self-
commodiﬁcation, thereby consolidating and reinforcing the digital
marketing character and completing the closure of the entire cycle.
This theoretical framework does not presuppose workers
to be rational “need-satisﬁers, ” nor passive “recipients” simply
determined by external pressure. It seeks to reveal a more
complex psychological fact: workers suﬀer in behaviors they
themselves choose, while the social-structural roots of this suﬀering
are obscured by the experience of “autonomy, ” making the
suﬀering both genuine yet leaving them unable to identify a clear
perpetrator—ultimately, workers can only attribute the suﬀering
to the fact that “I am still not working hard enough.” It is
necessary to reemphasize that this causal dynamic chain describes
a typical psychological pathway reinforced in a statistical sense
under a particular labor regime, rather than a fated trajectory
that every individual must follow in its entirety. The internal

5 Empirical operationalization: a
methodological roadmap for testing
the framework
A legitimate concern is that the theoretical framework remains
at the level of speculation without a concrete strategy for empirical
validation. In this section, we provide a systematic methodological
roadmap specifying how each core concept can be operationalized,
what research designs are appropriate, and what testable hypotheses
can be derived from the framework.
5.1 Operationalizing the core constructs
The digital marketing character comprises three interrelated
facets—compulsive self-curation, internalized self-quantiﬁcation,
and self-commodiﬁcation experienced as autonomy—
hypothesized to form a coherent latent construct. Development
would proceed through a sequential mixed-methods design. First,
qualitative interviews would be conducted to generate an item pool
that captures the phenomenological texture of the constructs. This
would be followed by expert review and cognitive interviewing.
Finally, exploratory and conﬁrmatory factor analyses would
be performed in larger samples. Convergent and discriminant
validity could be assessed against self-objectiﬁcation (Noll and
Fredrickson, 1998), impression management (Paulhus, 1991), and
entrepreneurial self-eﬃcacy scales.
Psycho-emotional deﬁcitization is deﬁned by non-saturability,
internalization, and diﬀuseness, distinguishing it from burnout and
negative aﬀectivity. A dedicated Psycho-Emotional Deﬁcitization
Scale (PEDS) could be developed with items such as “No matter
how well I perform, I feel I should be doing more” (non-
saturability); “When I am not being productive, I feel like I am
losing my value as a person” (internalization); “Even during my
free time, I cannot stop thinking about whether I am doing
enough” (diﬀuseness). Experience-sampling methodology (ESM)
could complement trait-level measurement by capturing within-
person ﬂuctuations.
The fear of becoming superﬂuous can be operationalized by
adapting existential anxiety scales (Weems et al., 2004) to platform
labor, with items such as “I worry that at any moment the platform
might discard me.” Escape-from-freedom behaviors—proactive
overwork beyond economic necessity—can be measured through
self-report, platform activity logs, and time-use diaries.

6 Discussion
The theoretical contributions of this article can be summarized
at three levels.
First, it provides a new set of conceptual tools for psychology
to understand the subjectivity predicament in digital labor.
Mainstream psychological frameworks are adept at capturing
relationships between clearly deﬁnable and measurable “variables, ”
but when it comes to diﬀuse self-experiences that seem almost
unconscious—such as “always feeling not good enough” or “the
fear of stopping is more terrifying than continuing to run”—
the explanatory power of these frameworks is constrained by
their methodological context. The concepts of “digital marketing
character” and “psycho-emotional deﬁcitization” proposed in
this article are analytical tools constructed within Fromm’s
methodological context, possessing a unique epistemological
function. They allow us to see that certain forms of psychological
distress are not “dysfunctions” at the individual level, but the
normal operational results of a speciﬁc social character structure.
This is fundamentally diﬀerent from simply renaming existing
variables. The methodological roadmap further substantiates this
claim by demonstrating that these concepts can be operationalized,
scaled, and empirically tested.
Second, it builds a methodological dialogue bridge between
mainstream psychology and psychoanalytic social psychology.
Psychoanalysis has long been marginalized in the academic
landscape of contemporary psychology, partly because its
methods are often regarded as lacking operability and testability
(O’Donnokoé et al., 2025). This article responds to this concern,
but adopts a strategy diﬀerent from “translating psychoanalysis into
standard variables”—it positions the contribution of the Frommian
paradigm at the level of the explanatory framework: Fromm’s
theory can generate a set of testable propositions, which can be
investigated using mainstream psychological research designs,
while the source of these propositions is a theoretical tradition with
a completely diﬀerent epistemological foundation. This approach
of “paradigm dialogue” answers the call for methodological
pluralism in contemporary psychology.
Third, it reintroduces Fromm’s theoretical tradition into
the core issues of contemporary psychology. Fromm was a
highly inﬂuential public intellectual and scholar in the mid-
20th century, but has virtually disappeared from contemporary
academic psychological literature. This article seeks to demonstrate
that Fromm’s theory is not merely an academic legacy conﬁned
to textbooks of intellectual history, but a set of analytical tools
with unique penetrative power regarding problems of subjectivity
in contemporary forms of work (Funk, 1998). In the “autonomy
paradox” foregrounded by digital labor, the diagnosis Fromm
made 80 years ago—that freedom can become a burden, and that
humans may escape freedom by abandoning the self—has acquired
renewed empirical relevance. Critically, we have also demonstrated
that a return to Fromm does not require ignoring either his
limitations or the contributions of other critical traditions; rather,
a self-consciously qualiﬁed and strategically positioned Frommian
framework can serve as a productive bridge between critical theory
and empirical psychology.
7 Conclusion
Grounded in Fromm’s psychoanalytic social psychology,
this article has constructed a theoretical framework to
explain the speciﬁc forms of psychological distress among
contemporary digital workers. We argue that the digital platform
economy cultivates a “digital marketing character”: workers
continuously engage in self-curation, self-quantiﬁcation, and
self-commodiﬁcation, and experience this self-commodiﬁcation
as an expression of personal autonomy. This character structure
engenders a diﬀuse “psycho-emotional deﬁcitization”—an inner
state of constantly feeling insuﬃciently good and insuﬃciently
valuable, regardless of objective performance. This deﬁcit
experience intertwines with the escape-from-freedom dynamic of
the “fear of becoming superﬂuous, ” driving a deep cycle in which
overwork and emotional exhaustion repeatedly recur.
Beyond this theoretical core, the article has made three further
contributions. It has situated the Frommian framework within the
broader landscape of critical traditions—including Foucauldian,
psychosocial, psychodynamic, posthumanist, and platform
capitalism scholarship—explicitly delimiting its contribution as a
strategic bridge rather than a claim to exhaustiveness, and critically
examining Fromm’s own limitations regarding universalism,
normativity, and historical boundedness. It has argued for the
formal resilience of Frommian concepts by distinguishing their
formal architecture from their historically speciﬁc content,
demonstrating that the digital marketing character is a deliberate
reformulation, not a projection, of the marketing orientation.
And it has provided a systematic methodological roadmap with
explicit, testable hypotheses spanning qualitative, psychometric,
longitudinal, SEM, mixed-methods, and comparative designs,
moving the framework from theoretical speculation into an
empirically tractable research program.
On a more general level, this article is an invitation regarding
psychological methodology. It seeks to demonstrate that the
tradition of psychoanalytic social psychology harbors conceptual
resources with unique penetrative power for contemporary social
phenomena—resources that can enter academic discourse without
sacriﬁcing empirical testability. Social character, alienation, escape
from freedom—these concepts point toward a mode of analysis that
anchors individual psychology within history and social structure,
without dissolving the individual into structure. For understanding
digital labor as a contemporary form of work, this mode of
analysis oﬀers not only explanations but also a way of reframing
psychological distress from “an individual’s illness” to “a structural
aﬄiction.”
