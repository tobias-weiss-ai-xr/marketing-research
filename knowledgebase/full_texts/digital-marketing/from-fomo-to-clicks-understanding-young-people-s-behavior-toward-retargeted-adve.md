ABSTRACT
The rapid advancements in artificial intelligence have led to highly personalized and programmatic advertising,
using retargeted advertisements that incorporate Fear of Missing Out (FoMO) appeals to enhance customer
engagement. Retargeted advertisements chase customers after they leave a website without conversion. This
study uniquely investigates the persistent influence of retargeted advertisements on young people in the digital
sphere, particularly when synergistically combined with FoMO appeals. It further applies the Theory of Planned
Behavior to understand its impact. FoMO is conceptualized in this study as a higher-order construct, comprising
four lower-order constructs: scarcity, urgency, social proof, and loss aversion. These were included in a self-
administered survey with a sample of 480 respondents. Data were analyzed using PLS-SEM. Findings reveal a
strong relationship between FoMO and attitude, subjective norms, and the intention to click on retargeted ad-
vertisements. Attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control significantly influence young people's
intention to click, ultimately influencing behavior. The influence of FoMO appeals on subjective norms and
behavioral intentions is more pronounced among men. Conversely, attitude serves as the primary precursor of
intention to click for women. These findings offer crucial theoretical and practical implications for stakeholders
navigating the evolving digital advertising landscape.
1. Introduction
The younger generation is growing up in an era dominated by digital
connectivity. In 2024, 79.0% of the global youth population (aged
15–24) was online; this pattern is particularly pronounced in Europe,
where 98.0% of young people access the Internet (Statista, 2025a), and
in Malta, where all individuals aged 16 to 24 report daily Internet use
(Misco, 2023), compared to 90.0% of the adult population.
This surge in connectivity has transformed online advertising into an
omnipresent phenomenon, particularly through social networking sites
(SNSs). In February 2025, Internet penetration reached 67.9% of the
global population, with 5.56 billion users, of whom 5.24 billion were
active on SNSs, representing 63.9% of the world's population (Statista,
2025a). Global data from 2024 demonstrates a modest gender
disparity in internet penetration; 70.0% of men used the Internet
compared to 65.0% of women (Statista, 2024). Faverio et al. (2025)
from the Pew Research Centre report that in the US, nearly half (45.0%)
of teenagers between the ages of 13 and 17 allocate a disproportionate
amount of their time to SNSs, with a staggering 9.0% increase from
2022. Malta serves as a strategic microcosm for this study; Eurostat
(2026) data indicates that 81.6% of its general population and nearly
92.0% of 16–29-year-olds access SNSs. These figures, alongside Malta's
eight-place ranking in the EU for basic digital skills (European
Commission, 2026), closely mirror broader European trends, where
the average SNS adoption for the same youth demographic stands at
89.3%. Consequently, Malta represents a highly representative digital
environment for exploring consumer responses to targeted advertising
within the European Union (Eurostat, 2025).
Advertisers increasingly employ personalized, data-driven strategies
like retargeting to respond to these staggering trends. This technique
delivers tailored advertisements to users who previously visited a re-
tailer's website (Lambrecht & Tucker, 2013). Delivered programmati-
cally, these advertisements aim to rekindle interest, instill desire, and
ultimately convert browsing behavior into sales. Consequently, pro-
grammatic advertising spending has grown into a multi-billion-dollar
industry, with global spending projected to reach USD 1.1 trillion by

For marketers, understanding how young people respond to such
advertising is critical. One persuasive mechanism embedded in online
advertising is the ‘fear of missing out’ (FoMO); an appeal that implies or
explicitly states the risk of exclusion from rewarding experiences
(Hodkinson, 2019). Research shows that young individuals are partic-
ularly susceptible to media influence (Aral & Walker, 2012), rendering
them more vulnerable to FoMO-based appeals.
Despite the growing use of FoMO in digital marketing, academic
research has yet to investigate how FoMO operates within retargeted
advertising. While existing research has explored FoMO within broader
digital or SNS contexts (e.g., Aygar et al., 2019; Franchina et al., 2018),
notably linking FoMO to sleep deprivation, fatigue, and social compar-
ison (Gezgin, 2018; Tandon et al., 2020, 2021), and mental health issues
like depression and digital dementia (Dossey, 2014; Hunt et al., 2018),
its direct role in shaping responses to targeted advertising remains
under-researched. Finally, while the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB;
Ajzen, 1991) provides a robust framework for explaining behavioral
intentions, its application to digital advertising remains limited (e.g.,
Sanne & Wiese, 2018). This study addresses the gap in understanding
how FoMO-driven appeals interact with the TPB framework.
Specifically, it examines how FoMO embedded in retargeted adver-
tisements affects young people's attitudes, subjective norms, and in-
tentions to click on them. It aims to determine whether such advertising
appeals amplify click-through intentions among young people, consid-
ering their susceptibility to subjective norms and perceived behavioral
control, as outlined in the TPB (Ajzen, 1991).
TPB was prioritized over the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA;
Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975) to account for perceived behavioral control
within the retargeting context, as the TRA fails to acknowledge the
external controls and non-volitional factors inherent in user responses
(Ajzen, 2020). Furthermore, technology-centric models such as Tech-
nology Acceptance Model (Davis, 1989) and Unified Theory of Accep-
tance and Use of Technology (UTAUT; Venkatesh et al., 2003; UTAUT 2;
Venkatesh et al., 2012) were deemed unsuitable, as the research
objective focused on psychological drivers, specifically social pressures
and FoMO, rather than the adoption, utility, or ease of use of the tech-
nology itself.
Consequently, the study had two methodological options: to develop
a new instrument or to adapt existing constructs from related studies.
Given the exploratory nature of this research and the lack of prior
frameworks, the latter approach was adopted, paving the way for the
future development of a consolidated and validated scale tailored to
FoMO in advertising. The study seeks to extend knowledge, particularly
on four constructs: scarcity, urgency, social proof, and loss aversion, that
constitutes the FoMO-driven content. Drawing from a study by De
Battista et al. (2021) where constructs were conceptualized under
different names and Lamba (2021), the psychological constructs were
initially identified. Prior research examining the intersection of FoMO
and advertising has typically adopted or adapted Przybylski et al.'s
(2013) self-initiated FoMO scale (e.g., Bright & Logan, 2018; Good &
Hyman, 2020, 2021; Saavedra & Bautista, 2020). Alternatively, studies
have evaluated these four constructs in isolation or in limited combi-
nations (e.g., Akram et al., 2018; Gupta & Shrivastava, 2022; Jang et al.,
2015; Teubner & Graul, 2020; Zhang et al., 2022), thereby leaving a
notable methodological gap in the literature.
In this study, FoMO was tested as a higher-order construct (HOC),
with the four constructs acting as lower-order constructs. A reflective-
reflective (Type I model) HOC was employed for this study (Becker
et al., 2012). Prior research has predominantly treated FoMO as a uni-
dimensional construct within the TPB framework (e.g. Pundir et al.,
2021; Saavedra & Bautista, 2020).
The study further investigates how these factors influence the
intention to engage with advertisements, particularly among young
people, who may respond differently based on age and gender. The TPB
serves as the theoretical foundation, positing that behavioral intentions
are influenced by attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral
control (Ajzen, 1991, 2020). These constructs are not always actively
considered but may operate subconsciously to shape decisions. In a
retargeting context, young people's decision to click may be affected by
favorable or unfavorable feelings toward retargeted advertisements
(attitudes), a perception of the social pressure to click or not click on
these advertisements to take action (subjective norms), and confidence
in their ability to act upon these advertisements (perceived behavioral
control). Together, these elements predict the intentions to click and
actual behavior. This study explores the relationship between externally
initiated FoMO and TPB factors to test the theory's applicability in a
retargeting context through the following two research questions:
RQ1: What is the relationship between externally initiated FoMO and
young people's attitudes, subjective norms, and intention to click on retargeted
advertisements?
RQ2: To what extent do attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived
behavioral control, as posited by the TPB, predict young people's intention to
click and their actual behavior toward retargeted advertisements?
Integrating this theory provides a structured framework for under-
standing how young people process persuasive content in digital envi-
ronments. This research contributes to the marketing and psychology
literature by addressing a gap in understanding how FoMO-laden
retargeted advertisements influence young people. It introduces a
novel conceptual framework that blends behavioral theory with prac-
tical advertising strategies, offering valuable insights for academics and
practitioners alike.
2. Theoretical background and hypotheses development
2.1. Retargeted advertisements
Programmatic advertising has revolutionized the online marketing
landscape through technology-enabled, data-driven communication,
allowing marketers to efficiently target specific audiences in real time
and at a reasonable cost (Gonzalvez-Caba˜nas & Moch´on, 2016; Samuel
et al., 2021). This powerful and effective technique (Gonzalvez-Caba˜nas
& Moch´on, 2016) significantly enhances marketing efficiency and cost-
effectiveness (Shehu et al., 2021). A core application of programmatic
advertising is retargeting, which focuses on individuals who have pre-
viously interacted with a brand on a retailer's website but left without
converting (Chaffey & Ellis-Chadwick, 2022; Lambrecht & Tucker,
2013). Dynamic retargeting delivers customized advertisements across
platforms through tracking cookies and first-party data, aiming to re-
engage users and drive conversions (Groth & Zawadzki, 2016). While
programmatic techniques offer unparalleled precision, they are
frequently criticized for their perceived intrusiveness; specifically, when
personalized factors are fused, as seen in retargeted advertisements,
individuals may perceive a violation of their proximal space and a sense
of ‘creepy’ surveillance (Moore et al., 2015; Zarouali et al., 2017). This
kind of tracking often triggers a defensive psychological shift where
individuals resist or disregard the advertisement, fostering a negative
attitude that may result in adverse behavioral intentions. (Farman et al.,
2020). Despite its growing commercial adoption, academic research on
retargeting remains limited, particularly in exploring user perceptions
and behavior among younger demographics (Moriguchi et al., 2016;
Zarouali et al., 2017).
2.2. Fear of Missing Out (FoMO)
Fear of Missing Out (FoMO) is defined as “a pervasive apprehension
that others might be having rewarding experiences from which one is
absent” (Przybylski et al., 2013, p. 1841). Primarily triggered by content
on social networking sites (SNSs), this phenomenon manifests as anxiety
and discontent when individuals perceive others as leading more ful-
filling lives (Dossey, 2014; Przybylski et al., 2013). FoMO has predom-
inantly been operationalized in psychology literature as a stable,

unidimensional individual trait (Przybylski et al., 2013). FoMO arises
from the perceived need to stay connected with others' experiences,
driven by a desire to avoid being uninformed about their activities and
perceived positive life events; it generates negative emotional states
such as unhappiness and tension. According to Good and Hyman (2021),
Przybylski et al. conceptualize FoMO as “a non-context-specific personal
characteristic” (p. 570), while Hodkinson (2019) emphasizes its “self-
initiated” nature (p. 65); an internally generated disposition that is
theoretically distinct from the construct examined in the present study.
In essence, FoMO stems from individuals' desire to remain socially
relevant and informed, yet it often results in emotional distress. This is
one facet of FoMO.
Hodkinson (2019) draws this distinction explicitly, arguing that ac-
ademic research on FoMO had, until his work, focused almost exclu-
sively on the trait perspective while overlooking a second facet, a
practically consequential form, termed as “externally initiated FoMO
appeals” (p. 67), the momentary psychological state deliberately evoked
in consumers through marketing and advertising appeals. Hodkinson
(2019) defined these commercial FoMO appeals as those originating
from an organization to stimulate demand, usage, or purchase. These
appeals can manifest in four combinations: in-person non-commercial,
impersonal non-commercial, in-person commercial, and impersonal
commercial. This study focuses on the latter, which includes paid ad-
vertisements leveraging FoMO. Hodkinson's taxonomy of FoMO appeals
identifies the specific persuasive elements, including scarcity framing,
time-bound urgency cues, and social-comparative pressure, that mar-
keters use to construct these appeals, and maps the cognitive and af-
fective mechanisms through which consumers respond to them. It is this
externally initiated conceptualization of FoMO, rather than Przybylski
et al.'s dispositional trait, that the present study operationalizes as a
higher-order construct (HOC).
Alfina et al. (2023) indicate that while FoMO is often linked to
negative psychological states, it can positively influence consumer
behavior in a marketing context. However, there is a gap in under-
standing how advertising-induced FoMO influences attitudes toward
advertisements in general and click-through intentions.
Key factors that trigger FoMO in a marketing context are urgency
(limited-time scarcity), scarcity (limited-quantity scarcity), social proof
(where individuals emulate others' behavior), and loss aversion (the
tendency to avoid losses over acquiring gains). Cialdini's (2021) work
identified these factors as elements of persuasion without explicitly
linking them to FoMO. Broader support for treating these four variables
as the primary building blocks of marketing-context FoMO, rather than
an arbitrary selection, comes from Alfina et al.'s (2023) systematic
literature review which identifies scarcity and social/external-pressure
factors among the most consistently documented correlates of FoMO
in consumer behavior research. These elements were subsequently
explored further in relation to FoMO by De Battista et al. (2021) and
Lamba (2021).
Eitan and Naor (2025) developed a tactic taxonomy of marketing-
induced FoMO appeals in which time- and quantity-limitation tactics
map onto urgency and scarcity, while peer-FoMO, early-buyer, and ex-
clusivity tactics map onto social comparison and social proof, consistent
with Cialdini's (2021) principles of social influence and Festinger's
(1954) social comparison theory. The convergence of two indepen-
dently derived taxonomies (Eitan & Naor, 2025; Hodkinson, 2019)
around the same constitutive mechanisms provides theoretical warrant
for treating urgency, scarcity, and social proof as defining, rather than
merely correlated components of externally initiated FoMO.
Despite the growing recognition, research specifically examining
FoMO in a retargeting context is scarce. Hodkinson (2019) was among
the first to link FoMO with marketing appeals, with only a few subse-
quent studies focusing on FoMO in advertising (e.g., Pusenius, 2023;
Weideinger et al., 2021). More recent studies have explored FoMO as a
mediator in purchasing behavior, often linking it to scarcity, loss aver-
sion, and herd behavior (e.g., Gupta & Shrivastava, 2022; Zhang et al.,
2022). While seemingly intertwined with self-initiated FoMO, exter-
nally motivated FoMO from advertisements can intrinsically and
extrinsically drive consumer actions. The following sections will delve
deeper into scarcity, urgency, social proof, and loss aversion.
2.2.1. Urgency
Urgency or ‘limited-time scarcity’ compels immediate purchase by
setting a time constraint on an offer (Jang et al., 2015). It works by
limiting the availability of a product or offer to a set period, generating a
perceived necessity for urgent action. Flash sales, countdown timers,
and stated deadlines are some of the tactics employed by marketers,
which may generate a sense of rush and amplify FoMO. This fear
prompts individuals to click on advertisements and subsequently make a
purchase (Hodkinson, 2019).
2.2.2. Scarcity
Scarcity or ‘limited-quantity scarcity’ signals that only a few items of
a product in stock remain (Jang et al., 2015). Like limited-time scarcity,
this creates a sense of ‘urgency’ and transforms a mere ‘wish-to-have’
into a ‘call-to-action’ driven by the fear that others might acquire the
product before the opportunity disappears, and can also evoke regret
(Hodkinson, 2019). Aydın et al. (2021) confirm that the perception of
scarcity can augment FoMO, leading individuals to engage in impulsive
purchasing. However, a potential drawback is that limited-quantity
scarcity may induce aggression if individuals perceive the desired ob-
ject as unavailable (Kristofferson et al., 2017).
2.2.3. Social proof
Drawing from Festinger's (1954) social comparison theory, the
concept of social proof, also characterized as an “informational social
cue” (Abdul Talib & Mat Saat, 2017, p. 3), is a psychological phenom-
enon where individuals base their actions and decisions on the perceived
behavior of others (Cialdini, 1984). Cialdini mentions four key drivers of
social proof. First, when individuals are uncertain about a choice, the
actions of others around them serve as a crucial guide. Second, in-
dividuals are more likely to emulate the actions of those they perceive as
similar to them, as this enhances the perceived relevance of the
behavior. Third, when the observed person or object is perceived as
authoritative, knowledgeable, or possessing certain expertise, social
proof is augmented, as informed decision-making is better represented.
Finally, the more individuals engage in a particular activity, the stronger
the endorsement of the behavior's correctness and attractiveness, aug-
menting a push to comply.
2.2.4. Loss aversion
Loss aversion, a key heuristic bias, describes the human tendency to
respond more strongly to potential losses than to equivalent gains
(Kahneman, 2013). This psychological principle, rooted in evolutionary
survival mechanisms, suggests that avoiding threats is prioritized over
seizing opportunities, even if it reduces overall utility (Collins, 2017;
Kahneman, 2013). As Lamba (2021) notes, individuals are often more
concerned with losing out by not acting than with gaining by taking
timely action. Collins (2017) further links loss aversion with FoMO,
suggesting that the drive to compensate for a perceived loss can lead to
rapid assessments and actions aimed at preventing future missed op-
portunities. Hence, when a “Do not miss out on this deal” message is
explicitly included in an advertisement, it is more impactful and garners
greater attention than a positively framed “Take advantage of this deal”
message (Lamba, 2021, p. 64). Within the marketing context, this same
asymmetry underlies consumers' heightened sensitivity to potential
transactional losses relative to a reference point (Hu & Nasiry, 2017) and
has been shown to directly mediate FoMO-driven decision-making in
applied settings such as retail investment behavior (Gupta &
Shrivastava, 2022). Loss aversion, like the previous three constructs, is
therefore theorized not as an independent antecedent of FoMO, but as a
psychological mechanism intrinsic to how consumers process the threat

2.3. Theory of planned behavior (TPB)
Developed by Ajzen (1991), the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) is
a well-known psychological paradigm for explaining and forecasting
human action. This theory is an extension of the previous Fishbein and
Ajzen's (1975) Theory of Reasoned Action, with “perceived behavioral
control” (PBC) as the only element included.
The principle of this theory is that individuals' intentions to act are
the most immediate precursors of behavior. The intentions or willing-
ness to act are then influenced by three key determinants: attitude to-
ward an object, subjective norms, and PBC. Hence, the more favorable
these three determinants are, the stronger the willingness to act will be,
thus leading individuals to behave accordingly.
2.3.1. Attitude
Limitations of traditional advertising in targeting consumer prefer-
ences (Gupta, 2019) and tracking digital footprints (Ghose & Todri-
Adamopoulos, 2016) have driven the rise of highly personalized, data-
driven approaches such as programmatic advertising. By 2030, pro-
grammatic advertising is projected to account for nearly 85% of total
advertising revenue (Statista, 2025c). While these advancements
enhance advertisers' ability to reach specific audiences, they also shift
attention to how consumers perceive and evaluate increasingly intrusive
and tailored formats. Consequently, understanding attitudes toward
advertisements becomes critical, as they shape how individuals inter-
pret, respond to, and engage with digital advertisements.
Lutz (1985) defined the attitude toward advertising as “a learned
predisposition to respond in a consistently favorable or unfavorable
manner to advertising in general” (p. 53). It is the individuals' positive or
negative evaluation of a specific behavior, considering the anticipated
effects of engaging in it (Ajzen, 1991). According to Eagly and Chaiken
(1998), attitudes encompass three key components: first, cognitive,
which is the beliefs and evaluations of the attitude object; second, af-
fective, which is the emotional responses or feelings toward the object;
and finally, behavioral, which is the way attitude influences individuals'
intentions (Sethna, 2023) where intentions do not always lead to actions
(Solomon, 2017). Various studies found a strong relationship between
attitude and willingness to act (e.g., Akdo˘gan & Durmaz, 2023; Arora &
Agarwal, 2020; Wang et al., 2020; Wang & Zhang, 2016). Particularly,
in the context of SNS advertising, Sanne and Wiese (2018) identify
attitude as the most significant precursor of behavioral intention.
Consequently, individuals' attitudes can be shaped by various fac-
tors, one of which may come from external sources. Retargeted adver-
tisements carrying a FoMO message can be a vehicle that may influence
the attitude. Studies have tested individual (FoMO) factors in relation
with attitude (e.g., Bhattacharyya & Bose, 2020; Jang et al., 2015; Ku
et al., 2012; Lim, 2016). Furthermore, as done in Saavedra and
Bautista (2020), this study examines three key TPB constructs, namely
attitudes toward retargeted advertisements and subjective norms, to
investigate whether they mediate the relationship between FoMO and
the intention to click on such advertisements. In their study, FoMO was
found to have a negative influence on attitude; hence, due to some
contradictory findings throughout the literature, this hypothesis was
phrased neutrally. Therefore, the three subsequent hypotheses were
formulated:
H1(a). FoMO influences young people's attitudes toward retargeted
advertisements.
H2(a). The effect of FoMO on young people's intention to click on
retargeted advertisements is mediated by their attitudes toward retar-
geted advertisements.
H3. The attitude toward retargeted advertisements positively
influences young people's intention to click on these advertisements.
2.3.2. Subjective norms
Subjective norms represent the perceived social pressure individuals
feel to perform or not perform a behavior (Ajzen, 1991). It is influenced
by individuals' beliefs about what important others think they should do
and their motivation to conform with those expectations. Ham et al.
(2015) divide subjective norms into two distinct types: first, descrip-
tive norms refer to individuals' perceptions of what is typically done by
others in a particular situation, reflecting the typical patterns of
behavior, and second, injunctive norms refer to individuals' perceptions
of what should be done based on the opinions of others, whether they
are in approval or disapproval. If individuals' close friends encourage
them to purchase an item, the subjective norms will favor engaging in
such behaviors.
Like attitudes, subjective norms can also be influenced by external
sources. Retargeted advertisements carrying a FoMO message can in-
fluence subjective norms by augmenting the perceived social expecta-
tions to engage with a product, thereby aiding individuals in returning
to the website they had visited previously. Various studies tested FoMO
as a precursor of subjective norms (Parker & Flowerday, 2021; Radic
et al., 2022). In Saavedra and Bautista's (2020) study, FoMO was
found to positively affect subjective norms. Given that subjective norms
delineate the perceived social pressure to engage in or withdraw from
behaviors, FoMO may influence individuals' understanding of these
norms, especially in the context of online self-disclosure (Beyens et al.,
2016). Based on these studies, the following hypotheses were proposed:
H1(b). FoMO positively affects young people's subjective norms.
H2(b). The effect of FoMO on young people's intention to click on
retargeted advertisements is mediated by their subjective norms.
H4. Subjective norms positively influence young people's intention to
click on retargeted advertisements.
2.3.3. Perceived behavioral control (PBC)
This construct captures the autonomy element of the theory,
encompassing the degree to which individuals believe they possess the
skills, capacity and freedom to assume a particular behavior (Ajzen,
1991). Hence, it reflects on the individuals' assessment of how easy or
challenging it is to perform a specific behavior, shaped by their
perception of internal and external barriers or enablers. The resources
and opportunities required for a specific behavior are influenced by
various factors, including, in this context, encounters with retargeted
advertisements, individuals' perceptions of existing challenges, and
other elements that are perceived to enhance or diminish how feasible
that action seems (Ajzen, 1991; Ham et al., 2015; Sanne & Wiese, 2018).
The foundational aspects of PBC are internal factors, known as self-
efficacy or individuals' confidence in their ability to act, and external
factors, termed controllability or the perceived degree of control over
external factors (Ajzen, 2002; Terry & O'Leary, 1995). For this study,
PBC will specifically refer to young people's perceptions of their ability
to engage with and click on retargeted advertisements.
Various studies, not necessarily marketing related, found a strong
relationship between PBC and intention (e.g., Cheung & To, 2017;
Pundir et al., 2021; Reza Jalilvand & Samiei, 2012; Tommasetti et al.,
2018; Ullah et al., 2021). Sanne and Wiese (2018), which focus on
user engagement and SNS advertising, found no relationship at all.
Intention has been found in several studies to operate as a mediator
between PBC and behavior. (e.g., Mafabi et al., 2017; Qalati et al., 2022;
Thorhauge et al., 2019). The literature demonstrates inconsistent find-
ings on the direct relationship between PBC and behavior. While a direct
association has been observed in certain studies (e.g., Archie et al., 2022;
Choi & Suh, 2022), other research has reported no such significant link
(e.g., Akar & Dalgic, 2018; Muralidharan & Sheehan, 2016). Acknowl-
edging these contextual variations, this study hypothesizes a differential

In past studies, the relationship between FoMO and PBC has not been

have mostly reported no correlation between the two constructs (e.g.,

advertisements.
2.3.4. Intention to click and behavior
In TPB, the intention refers to individuals' willingness to exert effort
to perform a specific behavior (Ajzen, 1991). The personal evaluation of
behavior (attitudes), approval and disapproval from others (subjective
norms), and the belief in the individual's ability to perform a behavior
(PBC) are the three key precursors of behavioral intention.
As a concept, FoMO was found to be associated with behavioral
intention (Flecha Ortiz et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2020; Pundir et al., 2021;
Saavedra & Bautista, 2020). Other studies have explored individual
constructs of FoMO in relation to intention, identifying significant re-
lationships with social proof (Johnson et al., 2022), scarcity and urgency
(Jang et al., 2015; Ku et al., 2012; Lim, 2016). However, when testing
the effect of loss or risk aversion directly on intention, studies found no
relationship (Cvirka et al., 2022; Zhang & Cain, 2017).
Behavior constitutes actions. As the endpoint construct of TPB, it is
the fruit of behavioral intention and PBC. Numerous studies have
identified a strong relationship between intention and behavior (e.g.,
Sanne & Wiese, 2018; Wang et al., 2020), whereas other scholars have
proposed extended frameworks to strengthen this relationship (e.g.,
G¨oncz & Tian, 2020; Tommasetti et al., 2018; Wang & Zhang, 2016).
For this study, the behavioral intention or willingness to perform an
action will be defined as the intention to click on retargeted advertise-
ments. The behavior will be measured by past encounters with retar-
geted advertisements, such as likeability, instances of clicking and
overall engagement. Hence, two hypotheses were formulated:
H1(c). FoMO positively affects young people's intention to click on
retargeted advertisements.
H6. The intention to click on retargeted advertisements positively af-
fects young people's behavior.
2.3.5. Gender and age group differences
To the best of our knowledge, few studies have explored these dy-
namics; therefore, this study investigates whether gender and age
moderate the influence of FoMO on attitude, subjective norms, and
intention to click. Additionally, it seeks to examine whether gender and
age moderate the influence of attitude, subjective norms, and PBC on the
intention to click and, subsequently, how PBC and intention to click
affect actual behavior.
Within the FoMO context, Weideinger et al. (2021) found that men
exhibit greater susceptibility to FoMO-inducing content in SNS adver-
tisements than women. Research suggests that anxiety may manifest
differently across genders, with prevalence observed more among
women (Bahrami & Yousefi, 2011; Lewinsohn et al., 1993, 1998), while
fear varies across different age groups (Beyens et al., 2016; Przybylski
et al., 2013; Schiele et al., 2016; Tandon et al., 2021). Therefore, the
following hypotheses were proposed:
H7. Gender moderates the influence of FoMO on young people's a)
attitudes toward retargeted advertisements, b) subjective norms, and c)
intention to click on retargeted advertisements.
H8. Age moderates the influence of FoMO on young people's a) atti-
tudes toward retargeted advertisements, b) subjective norms, and c)
intention to click on retargeted advertisements.
Previous studies have shown gender differences in the TPB context.
PBC can be more impactful for women when visiting online retailers
(Wang, 2010), while L´opez-Mosquera (2016) showed that women
exhibited stronger subjective norms, PBC, and behavioral intention in
the context of paying behavior. Conversely, men with affective and
positive attitudes were found to be less inclined to purchase new energy
vehicles (Zhan, 2022). Furthermore, Sheoran and Kumar (2022) noted
significant gender and age-based differences, particularly among young
consumers, in how subjective norms and PBC influence intention within
sustainable consumer behavior. Hence, the subsequent four hypotheses
were formulated:
H9. Gender moderates the influence of a) attitude toward retargeted
advertisements, b) subjective norms, and c) perceived behavioral con-
trol on young people's intention to click on retargeted advertisements.
H10. Age moderates the influence of a) attitude toward retargeted
advertisements, b) subjective norms, and c) perceived behavioral con-
trol on young people's intention to click on retargeted advertisements.
H11. Gender moderates the influence of a) the intention to click on
retargeted advertisements, and b) perceived behavioral control on
young people's behavior.
H12. Age moderates the influence of a) the intention to click on
retargeted advertisements, and b) perceived behavioral control on
young people's behavior.
The conceptual model presented in Fig. 1 illustrates the application
of FoMO and TPB to retargeted advertisements as hypothesized in the
preceding sections. Taken together, this literature supports a recon-
ceptualization of FoMO when studied in an advertising context as a
multidimensional HOC, in which urgency, scarcity, social proof, and loss
aversion function as lower-order components that jointly constitute the
externally initiated FoMO experience, rather than as exogenous vari-
ables predicting a separately defined FoMO outcome.
3. Methodology
3.1. Procedure, sample, and data collection
This study employs a survey methodology complemented by statis-
tical hypothesis testing. The research focused on young people in Malta
aged between 13 and 24, a demographic chosen because 13 represents
the minimum age for creating an account on most major social
networking sites (SNSs).
The study utilized non-probability sampling, specifically a combi-
nation of convenience, self-selection, and snowball sampling. An online
self-administered questionnaire was distributed to individuals attending
secondary schools, sixth forms, post-secondary institutions, universities,
and youth agencies/groups. To maximize reach within Malta's bilingual
context, the questionnaire was prepared in both English and Maltese. To
maximize participation, it remained accessible online for 50 days. The
predicted population for this age group in Malta was 60,487 young
people (National Statistics Office, 2023). A total of 521 participants took
part in the study; however, 41 respondents reported no prior exposure to
retargeted advertisements and were consequently omitted from the
analysis. This resulted in a final sample of 480 respondents.

3.2. Questionnaire design
Given its bi-lingual approach, a rigorous translation and back-
translation process was employed to assure linguistic and conceptual
equivalence (Okazaki & Mueller, 2007). The questionnaire was initially
drafted in English and then translated into Maltese by two professional
translators using a parallel translation method. An independent third-
party professional subsequently back-translated the Maltese version
into English to ensure measurement equivalence in both languages.
Hosted on Google Forms due to its inherent user-friendliness and
accessibility, the questionnaire began with an information page that
required informed consent from individuals aged 16 and over. For
participants under 16, parental consent was a prerequisite for survey
completion. To ensure an accurate understanding of retargeted adver-
tisements, a 30-s explanatory video clip (available in both languages and
with subtitles) and a clarifying infographic were embedded within the
respective questionnaires. The instrument, which also collected de-
mographic and internet usage data, had an estimated completion time of
8 min.
The instrument included items designed to measure the four lower-
order constructs incorporated under the higher-order construct of
FoMO and the key components of the theory of planned behavior. Re-
sponses for these items were recorded using a 7-point Likert scale,
ranging from 1 (“Strongly Disagree”) to 7 (“Strongly Agree”).
Prior to distribution, both the English and Maltese versions of the
instrument underwent pilot testing. A two-stage pilot study validated
the research instrument, involving a stakeholder walk-through (N = 6)
discussion to ensure contextual clarity, followed by a blind online pilot
with 20 participants aged 16 to 24 to safeguard against response bias.
Minor comprehension challenges were identified and addressed,
resulting in an optimized final version with improved psychometric
properties.
3.3. Scale development
For the urgency (URG) construct, two items were adopted from
Akram et al. (2018). The scarcity (SCR) construct consisted of four items,
drawn from Aggarwal et al. (2011), Akram et al. (2018), and Shusha and
Touny (2016). Notably, the items adopted from Aggarwal et al.'s (2011)
study, which were originally intended for consumer competitiveness in
scarcity signals, were modified to fit the current research environment.
Four questions on social proof (SP) were adopted from Shusha and
Touny (2016) and Stibe and Cugelman (2019). The loss aversion (LA)
presented unique challenges due to the young age of the respondents;
thus, the language of its three adopted items from Li et al. (2021) in their
experimental study was carefully changed to ensure clarity and
comprehension for minors.
For the theory of planned behavior component, four items were
adapted from An and Kim (2008) and Pollay and Mittal (1993) to spe-
cifically reflect the attitudes toward retargeted advertisements (AAD).
Regarding subjective norms (SN), four items were adopted from Han
et al. (2010), Sanne and Wiese (2018), and Ullah et al. (2021), while
perceived behavioral control (PBC) was measured by three items
adapted from Sanne and Wiese (2018), Tommasetti et al. (2018), and
Ullah et al. (2021). For the intention to click (ITC), two items were
drawn from Tommasetti et al. (2018), while for behavior (BEH), three
items were utilized from Sanne and Wiese (2018) and Tommasetti et al.
(2018).

4. Results
The study sample had a mean age of 18.23 (SD = 2.88) and was
predominantly Maltese, with only 7.5% identifying as either Europeans
or non-European residents in Malta. Females made up almost 56.0% of
the sample. For moderation purposes, respondents were split into two
age groups: minors (13–17 years) with a mean age of 15.59 years (SD =
1.46) and young adults (18–24 years) with a mean age of 20.20 years
(SD = 1.95). Young adults comprised over half of the total sample. In
terms of education, 20.2% were secondary school students, 40.6% were
post-secondary, while 31.9% were tertiary students. The descriptive
statistics of the sample are summarized in Table 1.
Data were analyzed using IBM Statistical Package for the Social
Sciences (SPSS) Statistics vers. 28.0.1.0 (142) (IBM Corp., 2021).
Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted since the FoMO section
was a new component to be evaluated for the first time. SPSS was
selected for its robust data-handling capabilities, ease of use, and
widespread academic adoption. Following Hair, Black, et al.'s (2019)
six-stage process, a principal component factor analysis with Varimax
orthogonal rotation was applied. The suitability of the data was
confirmed by a Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin value of 0.91, which is considered
superb (Field, 2017). Additionally, Bartlett's Test of Sphericity was sig-
nificant (χ2 = 11,854.44; p = .000), supporting the appropriateness of
the factor analysis (Hair, Black, et al., 2019). All commonalities excee-
ded 0.50, indicating a high level of explanation.
Initially, urgency (URG) and scarcity (SCR) loaded onto a single
factor; this was expected, as prior research often treats these two con-
structs as a unified dimension (e.g., Akram et al., 2018). Notably, one
scarcity item SCR01 and one behavior item (BEH01) exhibited cross-
loading or misaligned loading, with BEH01 loading on attitude (AAD).
After establishing a fixed nine-factor solution, confirmatory analysis
validated the theoretically proposed nine-dimensional structure. How-
ever, BEH01 and SCR01 were removed due to low factor loadings (<
0.50). After removing SCR01, the Cronbach's alpha for SCR dropped
from 0.90 to 0.88, while the alpha coefficient for behavior improved
from 0.86 to 0.88 after removing BEH01. The URG and SCR constructs
were treated distinctly following the confirmatory analysis to reflect
their fundamental conceptual differences. Specifically, urgency imposes
a temporal constraint on a benefit, such as a time-limited discount,
without necessarily implying physical product scarcity.
A split sample validating technique verified the stability of the EFA-
derived factor structure. Random sub-samples yielded consistent solu-
tions, with all similarities above 0.50. FoMO indicators showed unidi-
mensional convergence across both sub-samples from SCR04 (0.77) and
social proof (SP04, 0.67), validating their classification as a single
construct (Table 2).
To check for Common Method Bias (CMB), a Harman's single factor
test was conducted using SPSS. The results showed that a single factor
explained 36.4% of the total variance. As this is below Kock's (2015)
50% threshold, it indicated that CMB is not present in the data.
In this study, PBC was the highest construct ranging above the
midpoint of the 7-point scale (M = 5.41, SD = 1.67). The others ranged
from SN (M = 2.59) to BEH (M = 3.82). For the standard deviation, BEH
(SD = 1.77) showed the most dispersed responses, while FoMO (SD =
1.26) had the least spread (Table 3).
FoMO was investigated as a higher-order construct (HOC), whereas
URG, SCR, SP and loss aversion (LA) were exhibited as reflective lower-
order constructs (LOCs) for FoMO, creating a reflective-reflective (Type I
model) HOC (Becker et al., 2012). For this reason, it was decided to
conduct a disjointed two-stage approach (Ringle et al., 2012; Sarstedt
et al., 2019), as studies show it yields similar results to an embedded
approach (Cheah et al., 2019).
In the two-stage process, initially, the LOCs of the FoMO framework
were linked to their corresponding TPB constructs. In the subsequent
stage, the LOC scores were introduced as an additional construct within
the original dataset, whereby the four LOC components were reflectively
associated with FoMO, which was modeled as an HOC. For this part of
the study, partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM)
via SmartPLS version 4.0.9.4 (Ringle et al., 2022) was prioritized over
covariance-based methods (CB-SEM) due to its superior capacity to test
complicated theoretical frameworks, predict and explain correlations,
and successfully manage non-normally distributed social science data
(Hair, Hult, et al., 2017; Hair, Risher, et al., 2019).

surement were conducted twice, once for each stage. For this stage, the
four FoMO LOCs were tested in direct relationship with the TPB con-
structs, namely, attitude, subjective norms, and intention to click (ITC).

pected to be below 0.85. This analysis confirmed the adequacy of all
constructs' distinctiveness, as the highest score in this test was 0.76

were established.

95% confidence intervals to confirm that each FoMO LOC demonstrated
at least one direct significant effect on one of the TPB constructs. The

relationship with all the TPB constructs. Contrastingly, LA had only one
significant association - with SN (β = 0.233, t = 4.008, p = .000). While

4.3. Evaluation criteria of the structural model
This stage was followed by an evaluation of the structural model, for
which five criteria were assessed (Sarstedt et al., 2017) using SmartPLS
(Ringle et al., 2022).
Multicollinearity was tested using variance inflation factor (VIF) for
common method bias (CMB), yielding all values below 1.68, much
below the 3.3 threshold (Kock, 2015). To further test for CMB, a random
dummy variable produced VIF values below 2.18, demonstrating no
presence of CMB in the model.
Variance in the dependent (endogenous) variable ITC was explained
by the four independent (exogenous) variables, indicated by a coeffi-
cient of determination (R2) value of 0.49, meaning that the four ante-
cedent factors could explain 49.0% of the variance of ITC. The regression
model for ITC yielded an adjusted R2 of 0.486. Another strong R2 value
was recorded for BEH (0.44), from ITC and PBC as independent vari-
ables, demonstrating that 44.0% of the variance in BEH can be explained
by its precursors. The adjusted R2 for BEH read 0.438. As FoMO served
as the sole precursor for AAD and SN, the respective R2 values were
modest, with SN scoring (R2 = 0.245) and an adjusted R2 of 0.244, while
the R2 value and adjusted R2 of AAD were 0.246 and 0.244, respectively.
The model's predictive relevance (Q2) was assessed using cross-
validated redundancy analysis, which evaluated the model's ability to
reliably reconstruct observed values using its parameter estimates (Hair,
Black, et al., 2019). By employing PLS-Predict (Shmueli et al., 2019), all
endogenous constructs displayed sufficient predictive power, with Q2
values exceeding 0: AAD (Q2 = 0.239), SN (Q2 = 0.239), ITC (Q2 =
0.292), and BEH (Q2 = 0.200).
A Cohen's f2 coefficients test was conducted to assess the impact of a
specific exogenous variable on an endogenous variable, with values
ranging from 0.02 (small) and 0.35 (large) (Cohen, 1988). The analysis
showed varying effect sizes across hypothesized relationships. AAD
exhibited a medium effect size on ITC (f2 = 0.209), while ITC's effect size
on BEH was substantial (f2 = 0.385). Likewise, FoMO was strong on AAD
(f2 = 0.325) and SN (f2 = 0.325). In contrast, PBC showed a small effect
on ITC (f2 = 0.022) and BEH (f2 = 0.023), while SN showed a similar
small effect on ITC (f2 = 0.027). FoMO showed a small to moderate
effect on ITC (f2 = 0.077).
Regarding model fit, a Standardized Root Mean Square Residual
(SRMR) of 0.08 or lower denotes an acceptable fit, while 0.05 or lower
represents a good fit. The current model produced an SRMR of 0.07,
indicating an acceptable level of fit. This was further evaluated using the
Normed Fit Index (NFI), where values closer to 1 indicate improved fit
(Hair, Black, et al., 2019). The resulting NFI was 0.74.
4.3.1. Hypothesis testing
To test the hypotheses, a bootstrapping run with 5000 resamples was
conducted, employing a parallel processing approach, with statistical
significance evaluated at a 0.05 and a 95% confidence level (Hair, Black,
et al., 2019; Henseler et al., 2009). The PLS-SEM path analysis is pre-
sented in Fig. 2 while Table 7 summarizes the hypothesis testing results
using standardized path coefficients.
Hypotheses H1(a), H1(b) and H1(c) were formulated to examine
FoMO's influence on each of the three TPB constructs. H1(a) investi-
gated the relationship between FoMO in retargeted advertisements and
AAD. Due to inconsistent empirical evidence, no directional prediction
was made. For this study, the results indicated that FoMO has a signif-
icant positive effect on AAD (β = 0.496, t = 12.039, p = .000). H1(b)
tested whether FoMO affects SN, revealing a strong positive impact (β =
0.495, t = 12.350, p = .000). For H1(c), findings showed that FoMO has
a strong positive influence on ITC among young people (β = 0.239, t =
5.360, p = .000). Therefore, H1(a), H1(b) and H1(c) are supported.
The influence of AAD on ITC was assessed by H3, with the findings
indicating a significant positive effect of attitude on ITC (β = 0.422, t =
8.328, p = .000). Subsequently, H4 examined whether SN influences ITC
on retargeted advertising, showing a strong positive effect (β = 0.152, t
= 3.344, p = .001). H5(a) investigated PBC's influence on ITC on
retargeted advertisements, and the findings demonstrated a positive
effect of PBC on ITC (β = 0.107, t = 3.589, p = .000). Hence, H3, H4 and
H5(a) are confirmed.
H5(b) tested if PBC positively affects BEH and a strong relationship
was found (β = 0.116, t = 3.336, p = .001). Finally, H6 investigated if
ITC on retargeted advertisements favorably influences young people's
BEH, and the findings indicated a significant positive influence of ITC on
BEH (β = 0.634, t = 21.752, p = .000). As a result, H5(b) and H6 are both
supported.
4.4. Mediation analysis
Mediation analysis was performed in accordance with Nitzl et al.
(2016) and Zhao et al. (2010) on SmartPLS (Table 8). The results
revealed complementary partial mediation through AAD and SN in the
relationship between FoMO and ITC, thereby providing empirical sup-
port for hypotheses H2(a) and H2(b). Similarly, the results indicated
that ITC played a complementary partial mediating role between PBC
and BEH, as evidenced by its indirect effect, thereby supporting H5(c).

dates three sequential validation steps to ensure valid results. First,
configurational invariance was established, ensuring that all re-

means and variances, which determines the group means and variances

measurement invariance, partial measurement invariance is confirmed
if only the first two stages are satisfied (Hair et al., 2018). For the

adults; and young female adults and young male adults. In contrast, no
measurement invariance could be established between male minors and
young male adults.

5. Discussion
The study aimed to investigate the influence of FoMO appeals in
retargeted advertisements on young people by extending the theory of
planned behavior (TPB), specifically examining its effect on attitude,
subjective norms, and intention to click. It examined the four FoMO
constructs, specifically scarcity, urgency, social proof, and loss aversion,
as lower-order constructs, reflectively defining FoMO as a higher-order
construct (HOC). In this study, FoMO was presented for the first time as a
reflective-reflective type I HOC.
5.1. FoMO and the TPB constructs
FoMO was found to positively influence young people's attitudes
toward retargeted advertisements, subjective norms, and their intention
to click on these advertisements, corroborating Alfina et al.'s (2023)
systematic literature review, indicating that FoMO can favorably shape
consumer behavior in a marketing setting. Additionally, studies inves-
tigating FoMO, particularly self-initiating, found similar results in
diverse contexts (Good & Hyman, 2020, 2021; Parker & Flowerday,
2021; Radic et al., 2022; Saavedra & Bautista, 2020; Zhang & Cain,
2017).
Given their prior interaction, young people may develop a positive
attitude toward a retargeted advertisement that presents an item they
previously encountered on a retailer's website. Additionally, inserting a
message that instills fear of missing an opportunity might pique their
interest. The strong effect size of FoMO on attitude suggests that FoMO
serves as a high-velocity antecedent, significantly shaping individuals'
feelings toward retargeting. In the LOC analysis, social proof was found
to be the most significant construct influencing attitude, implying that if
the message presents a premise that the advertised product is being
purchased or considered proper by others, then their action to click and
proceed to purchase would be securer. Limited-time discounts or a ‘last
item in stock’ message can also shape young people's attitudes,
prompting them to click and act. The advertisement would serve as a
catalyst for individuals to proceed, fueled by the perceived impending
loss of the purchase opportunity. In contrast, loss aversion was found not
to affect attitude, as rather than forming attitudes purely based on ap-
peal, decisions are sometimes shaped by the fear of missing an oppor-
tunity or making the wrong choice. Attitude was found to have a partial
mediating role in the relationship between FoMO and intention to click,
suggesting that the attitude toward retargeted advertisements that carry
messages triggering the fear of missing an opportunity partially explains
the positive relationship between FoMO and intention to click on them.
No moderating effect of gender or age was identified for the FoMO-
attitude relationship, indicating a uniform influence of FoMO across
these demographic groups among young people.
This study suggests that FoMO in retargeted advertisements aug-
ments young people's perception of social pressure to click or not click
on the advertisement that would drive them to the retailer's website
visited before. Social norms influence young people, and pressures may
prevail to the extent that they feel compelled to act in alignment with the
group as observed in the substantial effect size of FoMO as a precursor on
how individuals perceive social pressure of the activity. FoMO messages
would exert more pressure so easing the way to fast complacency. Like
attitude, when LOCs were tested separately, surprisingly, urgency and
scarcity were not found to affect subjective norms. Contrastingly and as
expected, social proof was found to be the most significant determinant
construct, along with loss aversion. Observing others using a product
fosters a sense of safety and validation, reinforcing the desire to conform
to group norms. The study also found that subjective norms partially
contribute to the observed positive correlation between FoMO and the
likelihood to click on retargeted advertisements. For the moderation,
interesting findings show that men demonstrate greater proneness to
FoMO compared to women. Men often experience stronger social pres-
sure to conform due to societal expectations linked to competition,
achievement, and status. This heightened sense of competitiveness can
intensify FoMO, especially when exclusive or limited-time opportunities
are presented. Weideinger et al. (2021) suggest that the susceptibility of
men when encountering FoMO appeals is heightened. Moreover, the
proliferation of unrealistic ideals of male physique perfection may also
be a contributing factor (Diedrichs, 2012).
FoMO as an HOC can also influence the intention to click directly.
Hence, the chasing strategy of retargeted advertisements not only affects
young people's attitude and exerts more pressure on subjective norms
but also prompts them to the extent that they would be willing to click
on the advertisement to make a purchase. The LOC investigation showed
that urgency and scarcity have a strong favorable association with
behavioral intention, confirming other prior studies (Jang et al., 2015;
Ku et al., 2012). Even social proof was found to be a significant precursor
(Abdul Talib & Mat Saat, 2017; Bhattacharyya & Bose, 2020). Hence, if
after visiting a website, young people would be in a phase of deliberating
their next action, these FoMO appeals in form of scarcity, urgency, and
social proof messages might accelerate the transitioning from simply
looking at the advertisement to clicking it. Aversion was not found to be
effective, hence confirming Cvirka et al.'s (2022) and Zhang and Cain's
(2017) studies. Like subjective norms, the moderating factors were
observed to be more pronounced among men than women, a disparity
that was particularly evident when young adults were compared.
The mean of FoMO as an HOC fell below the theoretical mid-point of
the 7-point Likert scale (M = 3.15, SD = 1.26), whereas two individual
constructs scored more: urgency (M = 3.44, SD = 1.77) and scarcity (M
= 3.39, SD = 1.64), while loss aversion (M = 3.13, SD = 1.59) and social
proof (M = 2.84, SD = 1.43) reported less overall mean scores. None-
theless, the data suggests a general tendency for respondents to not
strongly align with or experience the FoMO appeals in retargeted ad-
vertisements or its constituent constructs.
5.2. The TPB constructs
Attitude emerged as the most significant predictor of the intention to
click on retargeted advertisements (Cheung & To, 2017; Saadeghvaziri
et al., 2013; Sanne & Wiese, 2018; Wang et al., 2009; Wolin et al.,
2002), followed by subjective norms (Bamberg et al., 2003; Cheung &
To, 2017; Chu et al., 2015; Ham et al., 2015; Sanne & Wiese, 2018)
and perceived behavioral control (Cheung & To, 2017; Pundir et al.,
2021; Reza Jalilvand & Samiei, 2012; Ullah et al., 2021). Hence, the
more favorable young people's attitude toward retargeted advertise-
ments would be, the more perceived social pressure from their reference
groups to click or not click on an advertisement, and the greater

confidence and ability they feel in response to retargeting, the more
likely they would be to click. The moderation findings indicated that
attitude predicts behavioral intention more among female minors than
adults, as when compared to men, the difference is distinctive and
robust. Hence, the attitude of female minors is more likely to translate
into behavioral intention than that of male minors. Misco (2023) reports
that women of all ages are more likely to be drawn to websites via brand
advertisements than men. Interestingly, subjective norms influence male
minors more significantly than female minors. Males are more sensitive
to the need for social approval, especially in contexts where conformity
and status within their peer group are key drivers of their self-esteem.
Unlike in previous studies (e.g., Akar & Dalgic, 2018; Muralidharan
& Sheehan, 2016), perceived behavioral control was found to influence
behavior directly. This disparity may be due to variations in the study's
design and contextual focus, notably its emphasis on the advertising
domain. Moreover, the study indicated that the intention to click
partially mediates, thus serving as a pathway in the association between
perceived behavioral control and behavior.
Conversely, the small effect sizes observed for perceived behavioral
control and subjective norms on the intention to click indicate that while
these factors may be statistically relevant, they offer negligible unique
explanatory power. This suggests that for this specific population, the
decision-making process is dictated more by emotional engagement and
personal attitude than by perceived ease of use, autonomy or social
compliance.
As expected, the intention to click was found to have a substantial
effect on behavior, confirming previous studies (e.g., Akar & Dalgic,
2018; Muralidharan & Sheehan, 2016; Sanne & Wiese, 2018). The sig-
nificant effect size of the intention on behavior (f2 = 0.385) identifies the
former as the critical engine of the model, confirming that intention is a
robust predictor of actual behavior in this context. Hence, the greater
the willingness among young people to click on retargeted advertise-
ments, the more likely they are to engage with these advertisements and
click.
Among the TPB constructs, subjective norms exhibited the lowest
mean score (M = 2.59, SD = 1.26). Attitude recorded the second lowest
scores from the mid-point (M = 3.02, SD = 1.38). These findings suggest
that young people hold a somewhat negative or neutral attitude toward
retargeted advertisements, while admitting that they perceive little so-
cial pressure from their important reference groups regarding these
advertisements.
On the other hand, the collective mean score of perceived behavioral
control was higher than the mid-point (M = 5.41, SD = 1.67), implying
that young people feel they are mostly in control of what they do and
confident in their behavior when they encounter a retargeted adver-
tisement. Overall, the mean score for intention (M = 3.26, SD = 1.53)
was less than behavior (M = 3.82, SD = 1.77), showing a slightly weaker
propensity to click and ultimately, act when they encounter these
retargeted advertisements.
6. Conclusion
6.1. Contributions
This study shows that FoMO does influence young people. It makes a
significant contribution to the online advertising literature by rigorously
exploring the influence of FoMO appeals in retargeted advertisements
on young people's attitudes, subjective norms, and intentions to click.
Notably, it investigates FoMO as an HOC in relation to the four LOCs.
Previous research has predominantly focused on self-initiated FoMO
(Przybylski et al., 2013); however, this study distinguishes itself by
examining externally initiated FoMO (Hodkinson, 2019). To date, no
empirical study has investigated this specific model, utilizing these four
factors within an advertising context, despite Lamba's (2021) theoretical
reference to them. The study's findings both corroborate and diverge
from existing research. While confirming several established findings, it
also challenges others and uncovers novel insights, particularly con-
cerning the moderating role of gender.
Consistent with prior extensions of the TPB (e.g., Tommasetti et al.,
2018), this study integrates FoMO as an HOC to enhance the model's
predictive power. The findings reveal that while scarcity and urgency
positively influence attitudes and click intentions, loss aversion pri-
marily impacts subjective norms. Notably, social proof emerged as the
only element significantly correlated across all three dimensions: atti-
tude, subjective norms, and intention to click.
A notable implication of this study is the inclusion of minors, a group
that is frequently under-represented in this research area, which pro-
duced fresh findings. The strict ethical framework used for their pro-
tection increased the study's rigor, legitimacy, and depth, giving it a
unique addition to the area.
Retargeting can feel invasive. Hence, providing clear and accessible
opt-out mechanisms empowers them to have control over their digital
footprint. This builds trust and aligns with digital ethics and privacy
regulations that advocate for greater user autonomy, particularly among
younger demographics. Using FoMO messages judiciously is vital; while
FoMO appeals are effective, marketers are cautioned against their
excessive use. Balance is integral in presenting intriguing advertise-
ments without causing any undesired stress. While these tactics drive
clicks, they can also augment subconscious anxiety and fear in young
people.
Raising awareness about how AI-fueled advertisements work is
imperative, mainly when FoMO strategies are employed, as this would
help young people discern and recognize manipulative content.
Focusing more on media and digital literacy education would enable
more critical thinking and evaluation, familiarizing individuals with
persuasive strategies and fostering greater resilience when facing such
advertisements.
More transparency policies are required to govern how these pro-
grammatic advertising strategies chase young people across various
online platforms. Enforcing rules on sharing how data is being collected
and employed for targeting advertisements is essential; advertising that
manipulates basic human emotions, like fear or anxiety, through FoMO
may lead to psychological stress, especially in young people. Hence,
more strict policies should be in place when advertisers are in breach.
6.2. Limitations and future research
Like any other study, this research has its limitations. First, given that
this was the first time that FoMO was investigated using four LOCs and
tested as an HOC, its generalizability may be limited, as the relationships
between such constructs may vary across different contexts. Therefore,
the entire conceptual framework, including FoMO as an HOC, can be
examined in various studies. Conceptualizing FoMO as an HOC may
omit certain self-initiated factors with various implications. Further-
more, the model could be expanded to include externally-driven di-
mensions, such as limited access or exclusivity. Consequently, future
research should investigate this HOC in contexts where FoMO is trig-
gered by external stimuli. Qualitative studies are necessary to under-
stand the effect of FoMO on adults aged 25 and over, while experimental
designs could further investigate cognitive and affective responses to
diverse FoMO-based advertisements. Moreover, given that such appeals
may heighten anxiety, future research should incorporate well-being
outcomes as well (e.g., anxiety and stress).
Second, the parental consent process for minor respondents presents
a potential limitation. The ethical requirement of active parental
permission may have resulted in a ‘gatekeeper effect’, potentially
skewing the sample toward minors from more engaged or permissive
families. Future research should explore alternative recruitment strate-
gies or comparative analyses across varying levels of parental mediation
to assess whether household media rules significantly moderate the
observed effects.
Third, another caveat of this study lies in the potential influence of

social desirability bias, which may have led participants to underreport
attitudes or behaviors perceived as socially undesirable, given the FoMO
approach. Although all efforts were made to prevent biases, nonetheless,
there can still be an infiltration of this bias. Future research may benefit
from combining neuroscience methods, such as eye-tracking and elec-
troencephalogram (EEG) tests, to objectively investigate the underlying
cognitive and emotional processes, thereby complementing self-report
measures and mitigating the influence of social desirability bias.
Furthermore, future studies should employ experiments involving
simulated retargeted advertisements, heat maps and click-through tasks,
to measure objective behavioral responses.
Finally, for moderation purposes, there may be other variables that
moderate the relationships, such as socioeconomic status and educa-
tional background. Further research focusing specifically on various
demographic categories can be conducted to delve deeper into every
aspect that might influence the relationships outlined in this study.
