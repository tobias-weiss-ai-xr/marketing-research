11.1 Introduction and Context
Ostensibly, digital marketing and e-commerce promote growth and
empower agricultural workers (Lovejoy, 2025; Yuvasri & Selvam, 2025).
While there are critical perspectives on rural e-commerce (see Wang et al.,
2022), digital platforms and tools create ways of reaching and engaging
with urban consumers, while simultaneously reshaping direct sales rela-
tionships. Consequently, the digital sphere has become an important
space for marketing strategies and creating new networks that ignore
geographical boundaries. Simultaneously, it has given rise to new forms
of imagining the rural and reshaped the advisory landscape, reconﬁguring
the rural innovation ecosystem.

sized producers in Latvia. However, in Latvia, as elsewhere in Europe,

11.2 Marketing Agricultural Products
While researchers note a resistance to making climate-friendly deci-
sions the responsibility of individuals (Döbbe & Cederberg, 2024),
an increasing number of consumers value and consider the origin and
production methods when making food purchasing decisions. This is
frequently tied to the perceived quality and contribution to the local
economy of products (Feenstra & Hardesty, 2016; Oleksiuk & Rull
Quesada, 2023), as well as the ethical, environmental, and health-related
values associated with them (Aitken et al., 2020;S t a m p ae ta l . ,2020).
Research also notes that traceability information is well-received by
consumers as an attribute of credibility, and consumers are interested in
ensuring that the item they buy is of a reliable origin (Magalhes, 2021).
This is underpinned by a variety of drivers, such as the perceived connec-
tion with nature, the role of local food in reinforcing personal identity,
a search for freshness, taste and authenticity, support for local producers,
and environmental concerns (Sidali et al., 2015). Farmers can make use
of this in the context of marketing their goods, including online. For
instance, it has been indicated that the perception of “authentic rurality”

can be a component of marketing (Basile & Cavallo, 2020), and there
are many ways in which marketing can use nostalgia to sell goods (see
Hartmann & Brunk, 2019).
Product labelling can also function as an important communication
tool, providing consumers with detailed product information (Kumar
et al., 2021) and motivating consumer choices (Aitken et al., 2020). This
is frequently associated with the idea that consumers should be able to
verify the origin of a product through a transparent and reliable process
that eliminates the impact of misleading food labels, and this can be facili-
tated by digital tools (Hasan et al., 2024). Labels can act as informational
cues, and third-party veriﬁcation is a critically important informational cue
for enhancing consumer trust (Gorton et al., 2021).
However, there is a complex relationship between credence goods
(such as “traditional” and “rural” products), third-party veriﬁcation and
certiﬁcation. Speciﬁcally, credence goods are tied to both reputation
and certiﬁcation (Holland, 2016). Indeed, one can make a distinction
between personal trust and system trust (Gruber, 2020) as consumer
perception and preferences may be affective and tied to speciﬁc farmers
based on personal relationships, rather than an impersonal certiﬁcation
system. Furthermore, although third-party certiﬁcation systems play an
important role in organic production and trade, they are not always suit-
able for small-scale operators and local market channels due to a variety
of reasons (Home et al., 2017).
Likewise, the use of different claims and labels can be opportunistic.
For instance, it has been noted that health-related claims are difﬁ-
cult to employ legally in promotional strategies (Stampa et al., 2020).
Nonetheless, farmers frequently promote their products as hormone-
free, local, natural, or antibiotic-free. This resonates with the qualities
valued by consumers (see Richards & Vassalos, 2023), but different nutri-
tional marketing strategies are necessary within different countries (Van
Wezemael et al., 2014).
The communication of values and stories about the farm and its prod-
ucts increasingly involves digital elements, such as social media, as well
as sharing information via email lists (Langusch et al., 2023), and there
is a connection between digital authenticity (being perceived as real)
and value creation (Pedersen & Ritter, 2024). While the development
of engaging content tends to be more resource-intensive (Wieden-
roth & Otter, 2022), digital tools are becoming ever more prominent in
marketing strategies, and many consumers are willing to pay a premium

for products that convey a narrative connecting agricultural production
with consumption (Feenstra & Hardesty, 2016).
For instance, sharing the “story behind the beef” is a tactic some cattle
farmers employ to build and maintain relationships with their consumers
(Langusch et al., 2023). In essence, it means sharing various aspects of
everyday life at the farm, the conditions in which the cattle are being
raised, and other nuances of beef production. Farmers believe that this
approach increases consumer trust which in turn helps grow a loyal
customer base (ibid.). The local origin of beef is also a feature that is high-
lighted in the labelling of the product, and this information can serve as
an effective marketing tool, especially to those customers who are paying
particular attention to the geographical source of their purchased food
(see Filippini et al., 2020;L i m&H u , 2016).
Unsurprisingly, there are also concerns associated with the marketing
messages surrounding beef and meat production more broadly, such as
the humanisation and romanticisation of this industry (see Cairns et al.,
2015). What is more, there is growing contestation surrounding the meat
industry. Researchers have noted the formation of online communities
around meat consumption to mitigate the negative impacts of the food
system on the planet. This, however, has been countered with business-
driven discourse that emphasises “sustainable meat” (Maye et al., 2021).
Furthermore, recent research highlights that farmers themselves
perceive and respond to environmental and economic challenges in live-
stock production in different ways. For instance, they can acknowledge
the relevance of climate change but remain sceptical about the role of
agricultural greenhouse gas emissions and perceive mitigation as a threat
to proﬁtability (Läpple, 2023). However, such attitudes are not uniform
across all farmer groups, and organic farmers tend to be less proﬁt-
driven and more strongly inﬂuenced by ecological values compared with
conventional farmers (Markiewicz-Keszycka et al., 2023). The capacity of
extensive farming systems to sustain such ecosystem services as biodiver-
sity conservation and the safeguarding of cultural landscapes is challenged
by external pressures such as climate change and interactions with wildlife.
These dynamics illustrate the intricate links between extensive livestock
production, food security, and ecosystem services, and point to the neces-
sity of integrated strategies that both strengthen the resilience of these
traditional systems and secure their multiple contributions to society and
the environment (Morales-Reyes et al., 2025).

11.3 Setting the Scene: Beef
in Latvia and Living Labs
Over the last decade, the sector has gone through substantial struc-
tural changes, experienced shifts in consumer preferences and contex-
tual socioeconomic and political turbulence. Both the consumption and
production of beef in Latvia have been steadily declining (European
Central Statistical Bureau of Latvia, 2025; Commission, 2024; Ofﬁcial
Statistics Portal, 2025). This does not necessarily reﬂect a shift to a more
sustainable diet. Data (Ofﬁcial Statistics Portal, 2025) and annual reports
suggest that consumers are choosing cheaper meat products (such as
poultry or pork), with several studies ﬁnding that price is a key factor
affecting consumer choice in Latvia (European Food Safety Authority,
2025;G r i v i n s ,2023). However, conversations with stakeholders and
sectoral experts underline that this is also the result of the ambivalent
perception of the quality of beef in Latvia. Indeed, it has been suggested
that due to historical experiences, people tend to associate beef with low
quality as specialised beef breeding as an independent industry in Latvia
developed only in the 1990s (Skujina & Pilvere, 2023). Sectoral stake-
holders note the growing popularity of vegetarian and vegan diets and
changing public discourse surrounding beef, which focuses on emissions
and environmental impacts (e.g. Brizga & M¯ uri ¸nš, 2025; Svece, 2025).
The combination of structural changes, consumption patterns and
perceived lack of understanding about beef was the basis of the two living
labs in Latvia. Speciﬁcally, the living labs aimed to improve the market
position of beef cattle farmers (especially those who sell their products to
consumers, rather than food processing companies). The common idea
was to promote the beef sector and establish a market for high-quality and
responsibly produced beef (“sustainable meat”). The primary focus would
be on digital marketing solutions that would make the product more
appealing, while providing educational content that dealt with partial
accounts and misconceptions around the environmental impact of cattle
farming in Latvia. However, as we will show below, there are interesting
tensions that provide material for theoretical reﬂection.

11.4 T heoretical Framework
In order to engage with the digital marketing of beef in Latvia and its
impact on the rural innovation ecosystem, this chapter draws on several
complementary bodies of scholarship. We aim to illuminate the tension
that Latvian beef producers face between evidence-based and technical
sustainability claims and the appeal of traditional, place-based narratives
in digital environments, and the implications it has for the relation-
ship with other actors in the ecosystem. First, we draw on Raymond
Williams’ cultural analysis of the juxtaposition of the countryside and the
city (Williams, 1973). This provides a lens for understanding how urban
consumers construct notions of “authentic” rurality. Williams argues that
the opposition between a peaceful, idyllic country and a modern city
persists in literary and popular discourse, shaping expectations that the
rural should represent timeless and authentic values. Although his work
does not address agricultural marketing, the persistence of this cultural
contrast helps explain why consumers remain receptive to storytelling
that emphasises connection to land, heritage, and authenticity, even
when such narratives may mask underlying economic realities and conceal
contemporary development.
Second, Theodore Porter’s insights from Trust in Numbers (Porter,
1995) and from the sociology of quantiﬁcation more broadly foreground
the role of quantiﬁcation as a social technology that confers legitimacy and
reduces uncertainty. In contemporary food systems, sustainability metrics
function as a form of mechanical objectivity: numbers appear neutral,
universal, and fair, thereby allowing producers to present themselves as
responsible and transparent actors. Porter’s argument that quantiﬁcation
often serves rhetorical purposes (i.e. making decisions seem objective)
offers a critical perspective on how sustainability claims and diverse metrics
are deployed by small- and medium-scale farmers to signal compliance and
quality.
Finally, Pierre Bourdieu’s ﬁeld theory supplies a structural view of agri-
cultural marketing as a contested arena where various forms of capital are
mobilised, deployed, and valued by different actors. By thinking about the
relationships between these intersecting capitals, Bourdieu’s framework
helps explain how appeals to evidence, technical product information and
story-based marketing co-exist, compete, and sometimes clash within the
broader ﬁeld of agrifood branding.

In addition to the above, we proceed on the assumption that digital
marketing is embedded in a broader rural innovation ecosystem—a
constellation of interactive social, economic, organisational, physical and
lately digital elements that shape rural innovation processes. Further-
more, our case can itself be regarded as a subsystem. In our example,
cattle farmers provide data to state institutions that aggregate and feed
data into (public) digital platforms, creating a digital link between
databases and on-the-ground production. Public registers (e.g. livestock
databases), platforms and certiﬁcation schemes provide the infrastructure
for building trust with consumers. Farmers can rely on this infrastructure
to bolster their marketing claims, while simultaneously crafting “ecolog-
ical”, “low carbon”, or “high protein” claims. Researchers can either
support or challenge this and develop evidence-based branding narra-
tives (“grass fed”, “regenerative”), highlighting positive environmental
impacts. Opinion leaders (inﬂuencers, researchers or NGOs) in nutrition
and sustainability shape consumer narratives, monitor public registers and
quality criteria, provide assessments of emissions and land use data. Their
public pronouncements and campaigns can inﬂuence consumer percep-
tion and policy. A compelling narrative that gains traction on social media
can trigger a surge in consumer demand for a certain product. Failed
or overstated claims invite corrective campaigns that recalibrate market
expectations and consumer response.
Furthermore, marketing efforts have become increasingly shaped by
the logic of platforms. Platformisation restructures the ecosystem by
introducing additional logics, dependencies, and relationships. Platform
operators create the conditions within which other actors must operate,
innovate, and relate to one another. For instance, traditional agricultural
advisors and extension services, and NGOs must now develop platform
expertise to remain relevant. They become translators—teaching farmers
about Instagram aesthetics, Facebook advertising, or e-commerce integra-
tion. This shifts their role from agricultural knowledge brokers to digital
literacy providers. Crucially, platforms and accounts on such platforms
control valuable data about consumer preferences, market trends, and
engagement patterns that beneﬁt farmers. However, government agencies
and regulatory bodies may struggle to govern platformised agricultural
marketing. Latvian or EU agricultural policies might address production
standards, food safety, or rural development, but controlling the content
that circulates on such platforms remains an ongoing challenge.

Likewise, NGOs and production cooperatives must decide whether
to work with or resist platformisation. Some may develop platform
strategies to amplify their messages. Their certiﬁcation schemes or
quality labels become content for platform narratives. Platforms actively
shape consumer expectations (e.g. Instagram aesthetics create speciﬁc
visual expectations for “authentic” farms). This feeds back through the
ecosystem, inﬂuencing what advisors recommend and promote as “good
practice”. In summary, different actors and elements coexist and vie for
inﬂuence in a contested space while deploying their respective resources
across various digital platforms.
Together, these theoretical strands provide a multidimensional frame-
work for our subsequent analysis. Williams’ cultural critique highlights
the enduring allure of “authentic” rural imagery. Porter’s examination of
quantiﬁcation reveals how sustainability metrics, evidence and data func-
tion as persuasive tools that lend an aura of objectivity. Finally, Bourdieu’s
ﬁeld theory situates both narratives and metrics within the dynamics of
digital marketing, which can be conceived as an ecosystem where different
actors vie for inﬂuence in an increasingly platformised environment.
The interplay of these perspectives guides the qualitative investigation of
how Latvian beef producers integrate technical sustainability data with
storytelling in their digital marketing strategies.
11.5 Methodology and Data
This chapter is based on material primarily gathered in the projects
DESIRA (2019–2023) and CODECS (2022–2026). In both cases,
there was a strong emphasis on participatory and qualitative techniques
(consultations, workshops, focus groups, interviews, online workshops)
employed in the context of a living lab format, complemented by desk
research.
In DESIRA, we collected data through a mix of qualitative engage-
ments and a desk study of existing literature and statistics about cattle
farming and beef consumption obtained from the Central Statistical
Bureau of Latvia. Work began in 2019 and centred on direct engage-
ment with the farming community. A series of consultations with a
broad set of actors were organised, ensuring that individual farmers and
organisational voices were heard. This was followed by a focus group
discussion that brought together farmers, NGOs, and researchers. Semi-
structured interviews added further nuance, allowing the team to capture

11 WEAVING STORIES AND DATA ABOUT AGRICULTURAL … 273
personal experiences and contextual factors. Finally, online and in-person
workshops convened researchers, farmer-organisation representatives and
farmers to co-design solutions and future scenarios and validate ﬁndings.
Throughout, a desk-based review of published literature and statistical
datasets provided additional evidence, helping us triangulate the primary
data gathered from the ﬁeld. This was supplemented with research notes
made by the researchers.
The CODECS project followed a similar format and combined phys-
ical meetings, remote workshops, farm visits, and round table discussions.
The living lab began with a meeting in January 2023. Subsequent mile-
stones include workshops and focus group sessions tied to the project’s
work packages. These activities brought together public administration
ofﬁcials, farm advisors, cooperative representatives, individual farmers,
NGOs focused on nature protection, and researchers. Remote engage-
ment continued in 2024 with shorter meetings and focus group sessions
that mixed farmers, NGO representatives, digital solution developers and
researchers, aiming to reﬁne the implementation process and model the
digital solution. Researchers also conducted several on-farm interviews
with individual farmers to capture the social costs, beneﬁts and imple-
mentation challenges of the digital tools being tested. The ﬁnal activity
was a workshop in November, 2024 that assembled eighteen participants
from public administration, advisory organisations, NGOs, cooperatives,
farmers, a logistics company, media representatives, and researchers to
discuss some initial project ﬁndings. This was supplemented with research
notes made by the researchers and analysis of digital marketing materials
of participating farms and beyond.
It must be noted that the topic explored in this paper was not part
of the initial focus of these projects. It emerged gradually, based on the
observations made by the authors. This makes the account partial and
reﬂexive, and further research is necessary to make deﬁnitive claims.
11.5.1 Point of Departure: The Living Labs and their Concerns
DESIRA
In DESIRA, living lab participants argued that their model of farming,
treatment of farm animals and impact on the local environment are
production aspects that should be well-received by their potential

274 E. KILIS ET AL.
customers. However, the challenge for farmers was to deliver this message
to their customers. There were expectations surrounding digital tools, but
it was noted that the narrative must be embedded in collaborative models
that require customers to adjust to the distribution practices of farmers.
It was emphasised that the story farmers tell should be aimed at
giving reasons why consumers should value their products above other
meat products. Participants noted that consumers have only a limited
understanding of the differences between products (in terms of quality).
Crucially, even when they are informed, they often do not attach value
to the aspects that, according to the farmers, directly contribute to the
quality of the meat. Thus, it was proposed that digital tools could be used
to present the quality while also explaining the higher price and making
it easier to purchase meat for a mutually fair price that reﬂects the added
value of the product.
Consequently, conveying the social, ecological, and ethical signiﬁcance
of the farming models employed by Latvian farmers was considered key. If
consumers considered this an asset, then it would be possible for farmers
to justify the price they would want to receive for their product. Thus,
an extended discussion and a better general understanding was needed
about what factors can affect the price and why it is important to consider
production aspects that go beyond a lower price. Consumers needed to
be convinced to pay a fair price for the quality delivered by the farmers.
Crucially, the participants of the living lab emphasised the importance
of environmentally conscious and extensive farming. In addition to the
way the meat is processed and the knowledge required to raise cattle so
that the resulting meat is of good quality, there are aspects that are tied
to farming practices and the environment in which the animals are raised.
Furthermore, the quality of the meat was characterised via categories such
as animal rights, organic certiﬁcation, natural pastures, etc.
Producers who sold meat to private customers or restaurants were
frequently small or medium-sized. For farms of this size, the safest
strategy to ensure proﬁtability is to increase the value of the product
and to ensure that a higher share of the ﬁnal price ends up in farmers’
pockets. Digital marketing was believed to have signiﬁcant potential in
this regard, but it was noted that greater cooperation between farmers
would be necessary to make full use of it. In fact, this was a key observa-
tion about the work in the living lab. While stakeholders discussed the
potential of various digital tools, the issues that prevented them from
deploying these tools were non-digital. Limited willingness to collaborate

CODECS
The CODECS living lab can be regarded as a continuation of DESIRA.
The focus on Latvian beef and digital marketing was maintained, but the
topic was narrower. Speciﬁcally, we worked with farmers who produce
products from semi-natural grasslands characterised by high biodiversity
(special focus on beef, as cattle are used to graze grasslands and main-
tain their biodiversity). The living lab engaged with a network of farms
across Latvia that seek to combine their efforts under the umbrella brand

them to communicate with clients and potential clients. Joining the Grass-

communicate the value of their product, but also to promote the sales

communication channels.

11.5.2 Emphasising Data While Weaving Stories
The Problem of Ignorance: Conversations with Farmers and Stake-
holders
We noted that a persistent topic in both living labs was perceived igno-
rance and misinformation that should be countered via digital marketing.
In the contemporary landscape, beef cattle farmers ﬁnd themselves navi-
gating criticisms that link livestock production to climate change, defor-
estation, and biodiversity loss. Farmers in Latvia are not immune to this.
However, while scientiﬁc studies highlight the sector’s contribution to
greenhouse-gas emissions (e.g. methane), our experience suggests that
farmers favour shifting to a broader environmental narrative that reframes
both their own role and that of cattle farming, while lamenting the
public’s simplistic treatment of cattle farming.
In DESIRA, our discussions revealed a consistent concern that the
public and, most importantly, consumers, remain largely uninformed
about the origins and environmental implications of beef. This hampered
the perception of Latvian beef and how it compared to the beef produc-
tion practices depicted in materials provided by environmental organisa-
tions and activists, which emphasised negative environmental impacts and
sustainability concerns. There was a palpable concern that this would lead
to a shift in dietary preferences, which, in turn, would endanger the liveli-
hoods of cattle farmers, while simultaneously having a negative impact on
biodiversity.

Furthermore, our interlocutors emphasised that the prevailing igno-
rance among consumers hampers their ability to appreciate the distinctive
qualities and characteristics of Latvian beef and the way farmers main-
tain the animals and the surrounding environment. Speciﬁcally, it was
suggested that in Latvia cattle are frequently birthed and raised under
extensive, low-intensity farming systems that differ markedly from the
intensive practices associated with higher environmental footprints else-
where. Indeed, expert interviews indicated that this was likely one of the
reasons why live cattle are exported to other EU countries where inten-
sive farming methods and fattening are more prominent. However, these
differences are hidden from view and consumer perception is shaped by
concerns over the origins of the meat (i.e. consumers do not trust that
the meat comes from Latvia). Consequently, stakeholders identiﬁed the
need to make reliable data about beef readily accessible, arguing that
transparent information about origins and production methods would
empower consumers to make more discerning choices.
Contrary to popular depictions of cattle farming, farmers highlighted
the ecological advantages of their traditional grazing practices, noting that
extensive herding supports biodiversity. They contended that the integra-
tion of livestock into natural landscapes contributes positively to species
richness, thereby aligning agricultural activity with broader environmental
sustainability goals and attempts to maintain the Latvian landscape.
This perspective underscores a perceived implicit afﬁnity between long-
standing, extensive farming techniques and the preservation of biodiver-
sity, even as emissions considerations and negative environmental impacts
receive comparatively more attention in public discussions surrounding
beef.
Finally, the participants proposed that digital tools represent a cost-
effective avenue for communicating these attributes to the public. By
leveraging modern information-sharing platforms, they believed that it
could be possible to disseminate clear, veriﬁable data and evidence about
Latvian beef production. This would bridge the informational gap and
reinforce the perception of Latvian beef as an environmentally responsible
choice.
Throughout both living labs we noted that farmers increasingly
invoked technical and scientiﬁc logics to demonstrate compliance with
quality standards and environmental commitments. By emphasising trace-
ability, regenerative grazing practices and responsible farming, living lab
participants argued that well-managed herds contribute to the formation

of heterogeneous habitats that support a wide array of plant and insect
species. These sustainability considerations were repeatedly referenced in
discussions and workshops to demonstrate that cattle and cattle farmers
need not be seen as adversaries in the ﬁght against environmental degra-
dation. Indeed, the Grassland Product Label developed by the Latvian
Fund for Nature (LDF) is an example of integrating technical informa-
tion about the way a product is grown in a marketing package, with LDF
scientists operating as de facto allies.
Nonetheless, we noted that farmers simultaneously leveraged social
media platforms. They practise evocative storytelling that emphasises their
connection to the landscape and traditional farming practices, leaning on
cultural and landscape heritage to bolster their image. Pastures populated
by grazing cattle have long been a deﬁning feature of the country’s rural
landscape, contributing to a sense of place that resonates with both local
consumers and tourists. By portraying cattle as integral components of
the Latvian landscape, embodiments of traditional farming knowledge,
and contributors to the scenic value that underpins agritourism, farmers
appealed to the public’s desire for authentic, nature-based experiences.
This ties into broader trends of marketing agricultural products in Latvia.
The Stories in Marketing Materials
An analysis of Latvian farmers’ websites and marketing content reveals
that, in general, veriﬁable evidence related to compliance with ecolog-
ical or sustainability requirements or metrics is seldom referenced. There
is pervasive reliance on claims regarding the ecological and responsible
nature of the farm’s operations, yet these statements are seldom explicitly
supported by ofﬁcial documentation. Exceptions can be observed among
larger farms that are oriented towards selling products in higher volumes
and often operate online stores. Such farms are more likely to showcase
organic farming certiﬁcates or other ofﬁcial documents on their websites.
A counterexample of sorts is the Grassland Product Label, which
was the focus of the CODECS living lab. This label requires adher-
ence to the rules of semi-natural grassland management. 36 farms in
Latvia have currently received this recognition. Farms included in this list
thereby indirectly demonstrate their compliance with the requirements.
Crucially, however, in most cases this information is not published on the
farms’ own websites. Typically, there are no references to participation in
the label’s programme, nor explanations of what compliance with these

rules entails. This observation corresponds with the broader trend—many
farms do not include references to speciﬁc standards or documents on
their websites that would signal compliance with speciﬁc environmental
standards.
Overall, our analysis suggests that content is predominantly shaped
by self-presentation rather than by the provision of educational materials
or technical information. While websites include blog or diary sections,
recipe collections, and emotionally written “About Us” or “Farm
History” sections, concrete information about production methods, certi-

manner. The storytelling frequently emphasises the farm’s history and the
importance of family traditions and continuity.

In some cases, the emotional and personal approach is complemented
by a romanticised narrative. The Karot¯ ıtes farm, for instance, positions
itself through a contrast between urban and rural life:
Our farm story began before we ourselves knew it or sensed it. […] Our
entire life had been danced to the rhythm of the city, permeated by its
lights, ﬁlled with its sounds and opportunities. ( https://karotites.lv/par-

In most cases, the content consists of situational posts reﬂecting
current events in the life of the farm, such as seasonal harvests, the
introduction of new products, or everyday activities.
Despite this situational approach, some recurring themes can be iden-
tiﬁed with relative frequency. A particularly prominent theme is the sense
of place and belonging, which is closely tied to the history of the farm
and the continuity of local farming traditions. This often emphasises both
the deep-rooted connection to a speciﬁc region and the transmission of
family values and work ethic across generations. There is a recurring narra-
tive centred on family farming traditions and attachment to place, which

280 E. KILIS ET AL.
serves not only as the foundation of storytelling but also as a marker
of identity and a means of establishing trustworthiness in the eyes of
consumers.
Narrative elements that seek to create a sense of authenticity, empha-
sise the farm’s local belonging to a speciﬁc parish, municipality, or region,
are frequently employed. Almost invariably, this connection is presented
as a crucial aspect of identity, allowing consumers to perceive the prod-
ucts as locally sourced and therefore trustworthy and of high quality.
However, it should be noted that such communication of belonging
often remains limited to standardised phrases, which recur across multiple
websites without delving into the distinctive features of the region or
landscape.
Many farms employ visual materials that create an impression of cred-
ibility and aesthetic value while fostering an emotional connection with
potential consumers. However, the linguistic style varies signiﬁcantly.
Some websites adopt a personal and emotional tone, emphasising family
history and the personality of the owners, whereas others rely on a
more neutral register, resembling factual descriptions rather than narra-
tive storytelling. This narrative strengthens the sense of belonging to a
speciﬁc region and conveys the image of inherited traditions, but it does
not specify which traditional methods are practised today. Overall, the
reference to traditional methods on Latvian farm websites serves more as
an evocative narrative about speciﬁc agricultural practices.
This is similar to what we see in relation to the discussion of quality.
Farm businesses deﬁne quality by emphasising the local character of
their products and the continuity of family traditions. Quality is tied
to origin, local work culture, and family heritage, and is most often
framed in romantic and emotional terms, closely linked to notions of
the “local”, the “natural”, and the “family based”. Far less frequently
is quality deﬁned through measurable standards, sustainability indicators,
or explicit references to responsible.
We also note that farms engaged in animal husbandry rarely portray
livestock solely as a resource. Animals are frequently depicted as an inte-
gral part of daily farm life, closely associated with care, welfare principles,
and even emotional attachment. This approach constructs an image of
farmers as responsible and conscientious stewards, who regard animal
welfare as an inseparable criterion of both quality and sustainability.

For example, Z/S Zil¯uži emphasises the importance of naturalness and
a balanced diet for cows, explicitly linking these practices to the quality of
the milk produced:

balanced diet is provided with all the necessary nutrients so that the milk
may be as natural as possible. ( https://www.grasbergs.lv/stasts)
Similarly, the Latvian Liellops (Latvian Beef) cooperative highlights
animal welfare as a prerequisite for high-quality production, under-

Z/S Karot¯ıtes extends the narrative further by combining product

These examples illustrate that the communication of animal welfare
in Latvian farms’ websites functions as a marker of trustworthiness and
quality. Narratives about animal care help farms establish an emotional
connection with consumers while simultaneously positioning themselves
as responsible and sustainable producers. However, it should be noted
that such emphases often remain at the level of generalised statements,
without detailed or veriﬁable evidence. Once again, narrative prevails.
11.6 Discussion
The DESIRA and CODECS living labs indicate a persistent perceived
disconnect between actual farming practices and consumer perceptions
of production realities in Latvia. While farmers emphasise extensive,

282 E. KILIS ET AL.
low-intensity grazing, animal welfare stewardship and biodiversity bene-
ﬁts, they believe that consumers undervalue these attributes. Digital
tools ranging from QR-linked geo-maps to self-hosted e-commerce
and label-speciﬁc communication channels were identiﬁed as promising
conduits for popularising products. However, we note that their consis-
tent adoption is hampered by non-digital barriers such as insufﬁcient
collaboration and the absence of shared standards. Furthermore, farmers
must now contend within the algorithmic logics and visibility rules
imposed by the social media platforms they use, and advisory assistance is
required to meet these challenges.
Our analysis shows that marketing content is dominated by story-
telling, rather than concrete, veriﬁable sustainability claims. Most farms
highlight family heritage, regional belonging, and a romanticised “sense
of place” to build trust and convey authenticity. References to veriﬁ-
able metrics or speciﬁc environmental impacts are rare. Even when farms
belong to the Grassland Product Label, their participation is seldom
advertised, and the speciﬁcs of compliance are not explained. Overall,
the narrative strategy prioritises emotional connection and cultural iden-
tity over detailed, evidence-driven accounts of ecological or responsible
production practices. Thus, it appears that Latvian producers lean on
cultural and landscape heritage in marketing materials.
There is, therefore, a discrepancy between what we heard in living
lab activities and the actual marketing content. Indeed, in CODECS
and DESIRA, Latvian cattle farmers frequently articulated the desired
direction of digital marketing strategies in terms of knowledge transfer
and correction of consumer misconceptions. This would correspond to
a classic deﬁcit model. Farmers argued that by foregrounding scientiﬁc
evidence about their farming practices they can raise demand and guide
purchasing decisions.
We suggest that this betrays broader anxieties about the role and
future of cattle farming. The use of technical information functions as
a social technology that manages distrust in contexts of social distance
and uncertainty. Numbers acquire cultural authority because they appear
impersonal, universal, and fair. The farmers we talked to embrace the
idea that in the digital agrifood marketplace, where consumers cannot
directly verify the provenance of products and have concerns about their
environmental impact, these “trust proxies” could, in theory, become
essential. Third-party veriﬁed environmental metrics and databases trans-
late complex ecological performance into visible signals that sit alongside

11 WEAVING STORIES AND DATA ABOUT AGRICULTURAL … 283
product images, allowing consumers to rationalise purchases as data-
driven and socially responsible.
Nonetheless, our examination of marketing materials underlines that
visual and textual content repeatedly invokes motifs of rural authen-
ticity, tradition, and stewardship of the natural environment. Content
about family-run farms, quality and tradition dominate the digital ﬁeld.
Thinking with Bourdieu, these more affective cues generate symbolic
capital that aims to resonate deeply with urban consumers’ nostalgic
imaginations of the countryside as an “authentic” and “natural” space.
While technical product information (certiﬁcates, environmental claims)
also constitutes cultural capital in Bourdieu’s sense, it is the narrative-
based and affective marketing that converts this into symbolic capital,
ultimately driving market advantage.
The emphasis on rural idylls is, therefore, likely not accidental. Urban
consumers, lacking direct relationships with producers, rely on cultural
scripts that equate rurality with “closeness to nature” and tradition (as
per Raymond Williams). Farmers, aware of these expectations, either
consciously or intuitively stage authenticity through visual and narrative
cues that align with urban deﬁnitions of “natural” and “sustainable”.
Producers rely upon nostalgic imaginaries and locally grounded ecolog-
ical practices. In such situations, those who experiment with innovations
like precision agriculture or milking robots risk being labelled “inau-
thentic”, even when such innovations deliver genuine environmental
beneﬁts. Thus, authenticity emerges as a sociocultural construct nego-
tiated between producers and audiences. The digital platform mediates
this negotiation, replacing embodied, personal engagement with symbolic
interaction through digital content.
This tension between evidence-based and narrative approaches has
concrete implications for farmer livelihoods. Metric-driven messages and
third-party certiﬁcation can serve as a powerful trust proxy, but their
impact is muted if it is not woven into a coherent story that resonates
culturally and emotionally. Conversely, compelling storytelling alone may
attract attention but fails to satisfy the growing consumer appetite for
evidence-based sustainability claims.
Furthermore, platform-enabled digital marketing is embedded in an
innovation ecosystem with shifting relationships between farmers, advi-
sors, consumers, and technologies. Marketing increasingly takes place
on platforms that translate metrics and narratives into platform-speciﬁc

284 E. KILIS ET AL.
symbolic capital (likes, shares, follower counts). This alters the relation-
ship between farmers and advisors, as the latter must blend agronomic
expertise with social media competence, helping farms craft compelling
narratives that adhere to platform logics. This also means that the “advi-
sor” can be someone who has not traditionally advised farmers on farming
matters, potentially expanding the scope of customary AKIS activities.
In addition, engaging with platforms means rethinking relationships with
customers, substituting in-person relationships with engagement analytics
that are created by the platform. Nonetheless, it also allows for the
possibility that niche consumers groups co-create meaning and can act
as micro-advocates, amplifying certain narratives beyond the original
farmer’s reach.
In summary, while Latvian cattle farmers publicly endorse a deﬁcit
model that privileges technical data and knowledge, actual marketing
practices reveal a more complex and contradictory reality. The digital
ﬁeld could potentially allow for a hybrid strategy that marries metric-
driven legitimacy with affective storytelling, allowing farmers to navigate
multiple identities simultaneously (as scientiﬁcally credible producers and
as culturally resonant custodians of heritage). Nonetheless, the transition
to digital marketing alters the nature of the relationships between the
actors implicated in the process. Understanding how these trends intersect
with increasing platformisation offers fertile ground for further research
into the politics of trust, authority, and innovation in digital agricultural
markets and rural innovation ecosystems.
11.7 Conclusions
Our living lab investigations expose a set of challenges and opportu-
nities that shape the digital transformation of marketing Latvian beef.
Farmers repeatedly stress that extensive, low-intensity grazing, animal
welfare stewardship and the preservation of semi-natural grasslands consti-
tute the core of their farming model and livelihood. They argue that these
attributes justify a higher price and should be communicated to urban
consumers. Digital marketing tools were considered promising instru-
ments for delivering accurate information and ultimately improving farm
income.
Our analysis of farm websites indicates that, in practice, narrative
still outweighs data. Most farms foreground family heritage, regional
belonging and a romanticised “sense of place”, using emotive language

11 WEAVING STORIES AND DATA ABOUT AGRICULTURAL … 285
and high-quality visuals to construct authenticity. Concrete references
to certiﬁcations and veriﬁable sustainability claims are rare. Because
online communication rewards high-engagement visual narratives, affec-
tive storytelling continues to dominate, while data-rich content often
receives less reach unless it is packaged to ﬁt platform-speciﬁc formats.
More broadly, the digital transformation of agricultural direct
marketing in Latvia exempliﬁes a broader sociological shift: the potential
convergence of quantiﬁed environmental authority and culturally resonant
storytelling within a digitally mediated ﬁeld. Technical metrics function as
trust proxies that compensate for the loss of personal relationships with
producers, while narrative performances satisfy urban consumer longing
for an imagined authentic rurality. The tension between these logics
produces a hybrid and volatile form of authenticity that both empowers
and constrains small-scale farmers. It requires them to seek new forms
of advice, negotiate with potential customers and increasingly rely on
impersonal connections mediated by platforms.
Furthermore, the adoption of digital marketing and sales solutions is
often hampered by non-digital barriers. Limited willingness to co-operate
and the absence of shared marketing standards impede the scaling of any
single technology. In a platform-centric ecosystem, these barriers translate
into fragmented data ﬂows and uneven visibility across platforms, rein-
forcing the very inequities that collaborative standards aim to dissolve.
The evidence therefore suggests that strengthening farmer livelihoods
through digitalisation cannot rely on the rollout of isolated tools. It
must be paired with collective capacity-building programmes and incen-
tives that foster collaboration and shared quality standards across the rural
innovation ecosystem.
Overall, we contend that digital tools have the potential to reshape
direct sales relationships and the rural innovation ecosystem more broadly.
Understanding these subtle sociological mechanisms is essential for poli-
cymakers, platform designers, and civil society actors who seek to foster
a sustainable, equitable agrifood system. We also suggest that future
research should explore how farmer networks can produce hybrid narra-
tives that embed quantitative metrics within affective storytelling and
empirically test the extent to which such integrated communication
improves both market outcomes and the socioeconomic sustainability of
Latvian beef farms in an increasingly digitalised agrifood landscape.
