Abstract
Community-based tourism villages across the developing world increasingly gain formal recognition
through national certiﬁcation and award schemes, yet many fail to convert that recognition into
sustained digital visibility. This paper examines this recognition–visibility gap through a qualitative case
study of Ampiang Parak, a coastal mangrove and sea turtle conservation tourism village in West
Sumatra, Indonesia, which received the 2024 Anugerah Desa Wisata Indonesia national award. In-depth
interviews with eight stakeholders, content analysis of the village’s digital platforms, and ﬁeld
observation reveal three interlocking gaps: a strategic gap between certiﬁcation and operational digital
planning, a representational gap in which residents appear as photographic subjects rather than
narrators of their own story, and an evaluative gap in which digital engagement goes unmeasured.
Drawing on Scheyvens’ empowerment framework, the paper argues that digital-era community-based
tourism requires a ﬁfth, communicative dimension of empowerment—the capacity of a community to
narrate its own tourism product to external audiences—that functions as a precondition for the
economic, psychological, social and political dimensions the framework already describes. The study
proposes a ﬁve-component community-narrated communication model and discusses its implications
for certiﬁcation policy design and destination management in resource-constrained rural settings.
Introduction
Village-level tourism certiﬁcation has expanded rapidly across the developing world as governments look
for low-cost instruments to formalise and promote rural destinations. In Indonesia, the Anugerah Desa
Wisata Indonesia (ADWI) scheme grew from 1,831 registered villages in 2021 to more than 6,000 by
2024 (Kementerian Pariwisata dan Ekonomi Kreatif RI 2024). Comparable programmes operate in
southern and eastern Africa, Latin America, and across much of Southeast Asia: South Africa’s Tourism
Grading Council certiﬁes graded establishments and routes, Botswana and Kenya have built community-
based tourism enterprises into national policy, and Namibia’s community-based natural resource
management programme has formalised village-level tourism operations for over two decades (Kavita &
Saarinen, 2016; Manyara & Jones, 2007; Sebele, 2010). Two regions, separated by an ocean, have
converged on the same policy instrument.
These certiﬁcation schemes promise more than a plaque on a village hall wall. They are designed to
function as a signal that travels outward—to potential visitors, to investment partners, to government
agencies deciding where to allocate further support. A recurring pattern across newly certiﬁed village
destinations, however, is that recognition does not automatically translate into digital voice. Villages
secure a national award, enjoy a brief media moment, and then largely disappear from the platforms
where prospective travellers actually search, compare and decide. This paper calls this phenomenon the
recognition–visibility gap: oﬃcial certiﬁcation operates as an input, while a sustained, audience-facing
communication output rarely follows.
Page 2/15

The gap has practical consequences because community-based tourism (CBT) scholarship has long
identiﬁed limited communicative and managerial capacity as a binding constraint on rural tourism in
developing countries (Manyara & Jones, 2007; Sebele, 2010; Tosun, 2000). What this literature has not
yet examined closely is how formal certiﬁcation interacts with that communicative capacity once a
destination enters the digital era, when the relevant audience is no longer a tour operator or a donor
agency but an anonymous traveller scrolling on a phone. The Indonesian case examined here offers an
unusually clear empirical window onto exactly that interaction because both the certiﬁcation and the
communicative shortfall occur within the same destination and in the same year.
Three gaps in the literature motivate this study. First, community-based tourism research is strong on
economic and institutional participation but considerably thinner on communicative participation—on
who actually gets to speak for a destination once it goes online (Millan-Anaya et al., 2025). Second,
digital marketing research on tourism in the developing world skews toward urban and resort
destinations, with rural, village-scale cases underrepresented relative to their real share of CBT activity
(Jiang & Phoong, 2023; Kurniawati, 2023). Third, the certiﬁcation literature, whether Indonesian or
African, focuses on quality assurance and compliance at the point of assessment rather than on what
happens to digital communication capacity afterwards.
The stakes are not abstract. Certiﬁcation and grading schemes are expanding across multiple
developing countries at the same time that mobile penetration is reshaping how destinations are
discovered (Adeola & Evans, 2019). If certiﬁcation continues to outpace communicative capacity—if
villages keep winning awards without building the narrative infrastructure needed to be found,
understood and remembered online—the plausible outcome is a growing population of destinations that
are formally branded but functionally voiceless. Understanding how this gap forms, and what might
close it, is therefore a question that extends well beyond one Indonesian village.
This paper addresses three questions. First, what does digital marketing communication practice look
like in a community-based tourism village immediately after it receives national-level certiﬁcation?
Second, what barriers prevent formal recognition from converting into sustained digital visibility? Third,
what does this case suggest about how empowerment frameworks commonly used in CBT research
should account for the communicative dimension of community capacity in the digital era?
The paper proceeds as follows. The next section reviews community-based tourism scholarship, digital
marketing communication in rural destinations, certiﬁcation schemes, and the empowerment framework
underpinning the analysis. The methods section describes the case, data collection and analytical
strategy. Findings are organised around three gaps. The discussion compares these ﬁndings against the
wider literature, proposes a ﬁve-component communication model, and extends Scheyvens’ (2002)
empowerment framework. The ﬁnal section sets out implications for practice and future research.
Literature review
Page 3/15

Community-based tourism in the developing world
Community-based tourism refers to tourism in which local communities retain substantial ownership,
control and beneﬁt from tourism activity on their own land or in their own settlements (Scheyvens,
2002). The concept emerged from a critique of top-down, externally ﬁnanced tourism models that
delivered limited local beneﬁt, and it has since become a standard rural development instrument across
much of the developing world (Tosun, 2000). CBT has been documented extensively in southern and
eastern Africa: Botswana’s Khama Rhino Sanctuary Trust combined conservation revenue-sharing with
community governance (Sebele, 2010); Kenya’s community-based enterprises drew on Maasai cultural
assets to diversify income beyond livestock (Manyara & Jones, 2007); and Namibia’s conservancy
programme used devolved natural-resource rights to anchor village-level tourism (Kavita & Saarinen,
2016; Saarinen, 2010). Indonesia’s desa wisata movement follows a parallel logic, formalised through
national schemes such as ADWI but resting on the same underlying premise: that community ownership
of the tourism product is both more equitable and more sustainable than externally controlled
development (Damanik, 2015; Prayudi & Susilo, 2022).
A recent bibliometric analysis of community-based rural tourism research published in this journal found
signiﬁcant growth in the ﬁeld since 2017, with community participation and empowerment among the
most recurrent keywords, but noted that studies concentrating on communicative and digital dimensions
remain scarce relative to economic and governance dimensions (Millan-Anaya et al., 2025). The
constraints documented across regions are strikingly similar: limited managerial capacity, uneven
market access and dependence on a small number of motivated individuals (Manyara & Jones, 2007;
Tosun, 2000). This convergence is not coincidental. Southeast Asian and African CBT scholarship traces
its structural constraints to a single root cause: communities are asked to manage a tourism product
without commensurate investment in the managerial and communicative infrastructure it requires.
Digital marketing communication in rural tourism destinations
The shift of tourism marketing onto digital platforms has not been kind to rural and village-scale
destinations. Studies of Indonesian secondary destinations report a consistent pattern: digital content is
produced reactively around events rather than planned as part of a strategy, posting is irregular, and
audience segmentation is absent (Kurniawati, 2023; Prayudi & Susilo, 2022). Kurniawati’s (2023) study of
Langkat Regency found that promotional efforts remained constrained by limited collaboration between
government, media and community actors, despite digital media being recognised by local stakeholders
as central to tourism promotion. A ten-year systematic review of digitisation’s impact on tourism
development found that, while digital tools increasingly shape economic outcomes, the planning and
evaluation layer that would translate tool use into strategy remains largely absent in rural contexts (Jiang
& Phoong, 2023). Studies of mobile and social media marketing in southern Africa’s hospitality sector
report comparable structural barriers: mobile social media is the dominant channel in practice but is
used inconsistently and without a measurement framework (Chamboko-Mpotaringa & Tichaawa, 2021;
Page 4/15

Nyatsambo et al., 2022). Adeola and Evans (2019) found a non-linear relationship between mobile and
internet penetration and tourism outcomes across Africa, suggesting that connectivity alone does not
translate into marketing effectiveness without complementary communicative capacity.
Read together, the evidence from Indonesia and southern Africa describes the same underlying problem
in two different settings: digital tools are present and even actively used, but the planning, segmentation,
and evaluation layer that would turn use into strategy is largely missing. The rural tourism contributions
model developed by Brito et al. (2023) conﬁrms this pattern, ﬁnding that while tourism’s economic and
social contributions to rural communities can be substantial, the communicative infrastructure needed
to sustain and amplify those contributions is rarely addressed in policy or practice.
Award certiﬁcation schemes and their communication implications
Certiﬁcation and grading schemes serve a quality-assurance function in tourism: they signal to visitors,
investors and funding agencies that a destination meets a deﬁned standard. Indonesia’s ADWI evaluates
villages against ﬁve criteria—attractions, amenities, digital readiness, institutional capacity and resilience
—and grew from 1,831 applicant villages in 2021 to more than 6,000 by 2024 (Kementerian Pariwisata
dan Ekonomi Kreatif RI 2024). South Africa’s Tourism Grading Council operates on a broadly comparable
logic, certifying accommodation and route quality against deﬁned service standards.
What the certiﬁcation literature has examined less closely is what happens after an award is granted.
Most evaluation frameworks—Indonesian and African alike—assess communication readiness at the
point of certiﬁcation rather than tracking whether that readiness is sustained or converted into actual
digital performance afterwards. The underlying assumption of any award scheme is that recognition
itself generates visibility, whether through media coverage, the prestige of the label, or eligibility for
further support. Where that assumption does not hold, certiﬁcation risks functioning as a one-time event
rather than a platform for ongoing destination communication.
Empowerment framework: Scheyvens revisited
Scheyvens’ (2002) framework identiﬁes four dimensions along which tourism can empower or
disempower host communities: economic (whether residents capture a fair share of revenue),
psychological (whether tourism builds pride and self-esteem), social (whether tourism strengthens or
fragments community cohesion), and political (whether residents have a genuine voice in decisions
affecting the destination). The framework has been widely applied in CBT research across Africa, Asia
and Latin America (Manyara & Jones, 2007; Saarinen, 2010; Sebele, 2010). It also aligns with Arnstein’s
(1969) classic ladder of citizen participation, which distinguishes tokenistic involvement from genuine
community control.
This paper argues that the digital era introduces a ﬁfth dimension the original framework did not
anticipate, because it did not need to: communicative empowerment, understood as a community’s
Page 5/15

capacity to narrate its own tourism product to an external, often anonymous, digital audience. The claim
is not that communicative empowerment replaces or competes with the original four dimensions, but
that it increasingly functions as their precondition in any destination whose visitors discover it online
before they arrive. A community can be economically engaged, socially organised and politically
consulted, and remain invisible to the market if no one within the destination holds the capacity,
mandate or platform to tell that story digitally. Empowerment that is not narrated does not travel beyond
the village boundary.
Methods
This study uses a single qualitative case study design (Yin, 2014), with Desa Wisata Ampiang Parak in
Pesisir Selatan Regency, West Sumatra, Indonesia, selected as a paradigmatic case: a destination
chosen because it makes the phenomenon under study unusually visible, rather than because it is
presumed statistically typical. Ampiang Parak is a coastal village built around mangrove conservation,
sea turtle protection and community-based experiential tourism. In 2024, it received the Anugerah Desa
Wisata Indonesia (ADWI) award in the resilience category, one of the scheme’s ﬁve national assessment
criteria, placing it among Indonesia’s more visible rural tourism success stories on paper even as its
digital communication practice remained largely informal. This juxtaposition of national recognition and
limited digital infrastructure is precisely what this paper investigates.
Consistent with Yin’s (2014) logic of case study research, the aim is analytic rather than statistical
generalisation. The paper does not claim that Ampiang Parak is statistically representative of village
tourism as a whole. It claims that the mechanism observed in this case—certiﬁcation proceeding without
a parallel investment in communicative infrastructure—recurs across structurally similar destinations,
and that the case offers a detailed, mechanism-level account of how that gap actually operates, of
practical use to destinations elsewhere that share the same structural conditions.
Data collection took place during 2025 and combined three sources to allow triangulation. First, in-depth
semi-structured interviews were conducted with eight informants purposively selected for their direct
involvement in the destination’s tourism operations and communication practices. The informant pool
comprised the head of the village tourism management body (1), members of the local tourism
awareness group, known as Pokdarwis (3), a tourism oﬃcial from the Pesisir Selatan district government
tourism oﬃce (1), and community members directly engaged in tourism service delivery, including a
homestay operator, a community guide and a sea turtle beach patroller (3). This composition ensured
coverage of managerial, governmental and grassroots perspectives within a single destination case.
Eight informants are consistent with the recommended range for single-site qualitative case studies
employing thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006), and the small pool reﬂects the reality that a
compact group of actors manages Ampiang Parak’s tourism operation. Second, the research team
conducted participatory ﬁeld observation at the destination, documenting how digital content was
produced and how community members were positioned during tourism activities. Third, the team
conducted a content analysis of the village’s digital platforms—social media accounts, its listing in the
Page 6/15

national Jadesta village-tourism database, and available promotional documents—to assess what was
actually published, by whom, and in what pattern.
Data analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s (2006) thematic analysis procedure: familiarisation with the
dataset, initial coding, searching for candidate themes, reviewing and reﬁning those themes against the
coded data, and ﬁnal deﬁnition and naming of themes. The theoretical concepts introduced in the
literature review—the recognition–visibility gap, Scheyvens’ (2002) empowerment dimensions, and the
proposed communicative-empowerment dimension—were used as sensitising concepts during coding
rather than imposed as a ﬁxed coding frame. Three procedures supported the credibility of the analysis:
triangulation across interview, observation and document data; member checking with key informants;
and reﬂexive awareness of the research team’s position as academic observers entering a small
community destination with its own internal dynamics (Bungin, 2013; Kriyantono, 2006).
Findings
The strategic gap: award without strategy
Ampiang Parak’s path to ADWI recognition ran through the resilience category, one of ﬁve criteria in the
2024 assessment cycle. Field observation and interview data show that the qualities that earned the
award—active conservation programming around sea turtles and mangrove habitat, and a community
structure capable of sustaining tourism activity despite disruption—existed well before the award and
largely remained unchanged afterwards. What did not exist, before or after certiﬁcation, was a
documented communication strategy translating that resilience into a sustained digital narrative.
Interviews with destination managers describe a pattern in which digital content is produced in response
to events as they occur, rather than being planned. There is no content calendar linking posts to the
conservation calendar—such as turtle hatching seasons, mangrove planting activities, and school visits
—that would otherwise give the destination’s social media a predictable rhythm. There is no documented
audience persona distinguishing, for example, domestic eco-tourists from school groups from potential
conservation donors. There is no brand-voice document, so tone and terminology vary depending on
whoever happens to be holding the phone that week. Respondents were candid that this informality
reﬂected capacity rather than choice: managing daily conservation and hospitality operations left little
time for content planning, and no one in the village held digital communication as a clearly deﬁned role.
This mirrors a structural gap documented repeatedly in the broader CBT literature, where formal
recognition or grading frequently outpaces institutional capacity to act on it. Sebele’s (2010) account of
Botswana’s Khama Rhino Sanctuary Trust found that communication and marketing capacity lagged well
behind operational capacity throughout. Manyara and Jones (2007) likewise found that Kenyan
community-based enterprises were constrained less by the quality of their tourism product than by weak
promotional capacity when that product needed to reach markets beyond word of mouth. The Ampiang
Parak case adds a speciﬁc twist: the gap persists through and immediately after a national certiﬁcation
Page 7/15

event that explicitly includes digital readiness as one of its assessment criteria. The award process
measured a snapshot; it did not leave behind a strategy.
The representational gap: subjects, not narrators
A second pattern emerged from the content analysis of the village’s digital platforms, read alongside
observations of how content was produced on-site. Residents—the ﬁshers who patrol the turtle-nesting
beach, the women’s groups who manage homestay hospitality, the young people who lead mangrove-
planting activities—appear frequently in photographs and videos. They appear, however, almost
exclusively as the subject of someone else’s documentation rather than as the source of the
destination’s voice. Captions accompanying images of community activity are typically descriptive and
administrative—noting that an activity took place, who organised it, sometimes a date—rather than
carrying the voice of the people pictured.
This produces a speciﬁc kind of representational gap. The conservation narrative that anchors Ampiang
Parak’s identity—sea turtle protection, mangrove restoration—is told largely in a technical and
administrative register: numbers of turtles released, area of mangrove replanted, programme milestones
reached. It is rarely told in the register of the people actually doing that work: why a particular ﬁsher
decided to become a beach patroller, what a planting season feels like, what changed for a household
once homestay income began arriving. Community members are accustomed to being documented
during tourism activity. Still, no structure—training, a content workﬂow, an explicit invitation—has been
built through which their own words and framing would become part of what gets published.
This ﬁnding aligns with Arnstein’s (1969) distinction between participation that is substantive and
participation that is decorative: residents are visibly present in the destination’s tourism activity and in its
imagery, but decisions about what story that imagery tells, and in whose words, remain elsewhere.
Authenticity, in the sense increasingly valued by digital travel audiences, depends on precisely the kind of
ﬁrst-person voice this gap forecloses.
The evaluative gap: communication without measurement
Neither the interview data nor the content analysis turned up evidence of deﬁned key performance
indicators for the village’s digital communication—no stated targets for reach, engagement, follower
growth or conversion of online interest into actual visits. Analytics tools already built into the platforms
the village uses were not consulted during any routine review. When asked how the team knew whether a
particular post had worked, respondents pointed to informal impressions rather than to any tracked
metric.
The absence of measurement compounds the two gaps already described. Without a feedback loop
connecting published content to audience response, there is no mechanism by which the destination
could learn whether the administrative narration style described above is less effective than a more
Page 8/15

personal, resident-voiced alternative, because no one is comparing the performance of different content
types. Communication remains a one-way broadcast rather than a managed practice that adapts to
audience response.
This gap also carries a policy dimension. ADWI’s own assessment criteria include a digital readiness
category, evaluated at a single point during the competition cycle (Kementerian Pariwisata dan Ekonomi
Kreatif RI 2024). Nothing in the scheme, as it currently operates, requires or incentivises ongoing
measurement of digital communication performance after the award is granted. A certiﬁcation scheme
built to reward a snapshot of readiness rather than sustained capacity may be poorly positioned to close
the very gap this paper documents.
Discussion
Convergent patterns across developing countries
The three gaps documented at Ampiang Parak—strategic, representational, evaluative—reproduce
constraints documented independently across multiple CBT contexts over nearly two decades: Sebele’s
(2010) ﬁnding that conservation and governance capacity outpaced marketing capacity in Botswana;
Manyara and Jones’ (2007) ﬁnding that Kenyan community enterprises were limited less by product
quality than by promotional reach; and the more recent ﬁnding that institutional readiness, rather than
technology access, remains the binding constraint on tourism digital marketing adoption in several
African and Asian countries (Chamboko-Mpotaringa & Tichaawa, 2021; Nyatsambo et al., 2022). That
broadly the same pattern surfaces in Southeast Asia and across southern Africa, in destinations with no
contact with one another and under different policy regimes, is itself evidence that the mechanism
generating the gap is structural rather than incidental to any one country’s tourism administration.
The mechanism, as this paper reads it, runs as follows. Certiﬁcation and grading schemes are designed
to assess and reward what a destination already has—its attractions, amenities, and institutional
resilience—measured at a single point in time. They are not built to fund or mandate the ongoing
communicative labour that would let a destination act on that recognition once it is granted. Certiﬁcation
is treated as an output of the development effort. In contrast, in the digital era, it functions better as
input to a separate, subsequent audience-building process that requires its own dedicated capacity, role,
and budget. Where that subsequent investment does not follow—and the evidence here, and in the wider
literature, suggests it usually does not—the destination wins the award and then goes quiet.
The ﬁve-component community-narrated communication
model
Drawing on the ﬁndings and on the literature reviewed above, this paper proposes a ﬁve-component
model of community-narrated digital communication, intended as a practical framework that
destinations and certifying bodies could apply after, not only before, certiﬁcation. Table 1 summarises
the model.

The model is deliberately built from low-cost components, because the destinations it is meant to serve
—village-scale, resource-constrained, frequently managed by volunteers—are exactly the destinations
least able to absorb an expensive digital marketing apparatus. Applying it in other developing-country
contexts would require adaptation rather than direct transplant. Multilingual and multi-ethnic
destinations would need a narrator pool deliberately built across language groups. Lower mobile data
infrastructure would push the content-cadence component toward lighter formats—text and low-
bandwidth images rather than video. Moreover, in destinations where multiple ethnic or clan groups
share governance of a single CBT enterprise, the narrator-pool component would need explicit attention
to equitable representation across those groups.
Extending Scheyvens to the communicative dimension
The ﬁndings support extending Scheyvens’ (2002) four-dimensional empowerment framework with a
ﬁfth, communicative dimension. Ampiang Parak’s residents are economically engaged through
homestay and guiding income, psychologically invested through visible community pride in the
conservation programme, socially organised through the Pokdarwis structure, and at least partially
consulted in destination decisions. However, the representational and evaluative gaps documented
above show that none of this is currently legible to an external digital audience in the residents’ own
voice. The four original dimensions can be substantially present inside a community while remaining
almost entirely invisible outside it, if the ﬁfth dimension is missing.
This matters for how empowerment is typically measured in CBT research. A community-based tourism
evaluation that stops at the original four dimensions—checking whether revenue is shared, whether
residents feel pride, whether the community remains cohesive, and whether residents are consulted—
would likely have rated Ampiang Parak’s empowerment outcomes reasonably well, and would have
Page 10/15

missed entirely the gap that determines whether any of that empowerment becomes visible to the
market, on which the destination depends. Adding a communicative dimension offers a way to connect a
well-established empowerment literature to a newer set of concerns—destination branding, social media
strategy, certiﬁcation design—that has so far developed as a separate research stream.
Conclusion
This paper has made three contributions. Empirically, it documents the recognition–visibility gap in a
certiﬁed Indonesian village through three speciﬁc, interlocking failure modes: strategic, representational
and evaluative. Conceptually, it extends Scheyvens’ (2002) empowerment framework with a
communicative dimension and proposes a ﬁve-component, community-narrated communication model.
Practically, it offers destination managers, local government and certiﬁcation bodies a concrete account
of where digital communication breaks down after an award is won, and what a low-cost remedy could
look like.
For destination management organisations, the central implication is that communicative capacity
should be built into a destination’s operating structure before the marketing need becomes urgent: a
narrator pool and a content cadence can be established with existing staff and modest training. For
policymakers overseeing grading and certiﬁcation schemes, digital criteria currently assessed at a single
point in the certiﬁcation process could be reframed as a continuing obligation tied to a simple annual
reporting requirement, so that certiﬁcation functions as an ongoing relationship rather than a one-time
audit. For village-level managers, the ﬁve-component model offers an operational checklist that requires
no external consultants or a signiﬁcant budget.
The study has limitations that should be acknowledged. It is a single case study, and the analytic
generalisation it supports should not be read as a statistical generalisation to all certiﬁed village
destinations. The Indonesian institutional and cultural context—relative language homogeneity within the
village, the speciﬁc structure of the ADWI scheme, Indonesia’s pattern of mobile and social media
adoption—means some ﬁndings will transfer more readily than others to settings with different linguistic,
infrastructural and governance conditions. The observation period, concentrated within a single research
year, also limits the ability to track whether the gaps identiﬁed here persist, narrow or widen over a longer
post-certiﬁcation horizon.
Three directions for future research follow naturally. A systematic comparative study that places
certiﬁed village destinations from different developing countries side by side would test whether the
recognition–visibility gap operates the same way across regions or diverges in ways a single case
cannot detect. The ﬁve-component model would beneﬁt from direct testing through action research that
implements it and tracks change relative to a baseline. A longitudinal study following destinations from
the point of certiﬁcation onward would establish whether the gaps documented here represent a
temporary post-award lull or a stable, self-reinforcing pattern.

than written consent was chosen because in a small rural community where tourism roles rest on
informal, trust-based relationships, a request for written signatures risked introducing a formality that
could undermine participant trust; verbal consent was documented in the interview records. All
participants’ names have been anonymised, and interview data are attributed only by role. No
participants were in a position of dependency vis-à-vis the researchers, and no remuneration was
offered. Data are stored securely and are accessible only to the research team.

The data supporting the ﬁndings of this study consist of semi-structured interview transcripts and ﬁeld
notes in Bahasa Indonesia, participatory observation records, and a corpus of digital content drawn from
the village’s publicly accessible social media accounts and its listing in the national Jadesta village-
tourism database. The interview guide, the observation protocol, the content-analysis protocol, and the
thematic codebook used in the analysis are provided in full as Supplementary Information
accompanying this article. The digital content analysed remains publicly accessible on the platforms
cited. Full interview transcripts and ﬁeld notes are not publicly available because the study site is a small
community in which the professional and community roles of the eight informants make them readily
identiﬁable, and public release of full transcripts would compromise the conﬁdentiality participants were
assured of when consenting to the study. De-identiﬁed interview data are available from the
corresponding author on reasonable request, subject to the conﬁdentiality conditions under which
consent was obtained. A fuller account of the ﬁeldwork is documented in the underlying research report.
