As sustainability communications become increasingly prevalent in digital
marketing, understanding the factors that shape consumer responses to such
messages has become critical. Drawing on Signaling Theory and Locus of
Control Theory, this study investigates how Generation Z consumers respond
to green website communications and examines the roles of locus of control,
brand activism beliefs, perceived brand genuineness, and trust in shaping
brand evaluations. Using an experimental design, participants were exposed to
either a green or a conventional (non-green) corporate brand website. The
findings reveal that consumers with an internal locus of control respond more
favorably to green website communications than those with an external locus
of control, reporting higher levels of perceived brand genuineness, trust, and
brand attitude. In addition, consumers with stronger brand activism beliefs are
more likely to perceive sustainability-oriented brands as genuine. The results
further demonstrate that green website exposure and brand activism beliefs
influence brand attitudes indirectly through perceptions of brand genuineness
and trust, highlighting the importance of authenticity-based trust formation in
sustainability communication contexts. Brand genuineness emerged as the
central mechanism through which sustainability-related communications are
translated into favourable consumer evaluations. However, no significant
effects were observed for purchase intention, suggesting the persistence of
an attitude–behaviour gap among Generation Z consumers. The study
contributes to the sustainability marketing and consumer behaviour literature
by demonstrating that locus of control functions as a signal-interpretation
mechanism and by identifying brand activism beliefs as an important
consumer predisposition shaping responses to sustainability communications.
The findings provide valuable insights for organizations seeking to
communicate environmental commitments effectively in digital environments.
Overall, this study contributes to a deeper understanding of how sustainable
marketing operates in digital environments and highlights the importance of
aligning sustainability communication strategies with consumers’
psychological orientations.

1 Introduction
Sustainable or green marketing has become a widely adopted
competitive strategy in the global marketplace, particularly within
business-to-consumer sectors (Bhardwaj et al. 2026; Chen et al.,
2024; Kumar et al. 2026; Singh, 2025). It seeks to integrate
sustainability principles into business practices while addressing
the needs and expectations of both consumers and industry
stakeholders (Cui et al., 2025). Within this context, green
advertising has emerged as a prominent sustainable marketing
tool that promotes messages appealing to the values, needs, and
desires of environmentally conscious consumers (Jia et al. 2026;
Zinkhan and Carlson, 1995). Prior studies further demonstrate
that environmental representations in marketing communication
can positively influence consumers’ trust and attitude towards the
brand as well as purchase intentions (Follows and Jobber, 2000;
Kumar et al. 2026; Leonidou et al., 2013; Maree and Wiese 2026).
This perspective is particularly relevant in digital
environments, where sustainability communication increasingly
frames consumption as a means through which consumers can
contribute to broader environmental and social change. Digital
platforms have therefore become central channels through which
brands communicate sustainability initiatives, engage consumers,
and shape perceptions of corporate responsibility and
authenticity (Kumar et al., 2021). Companies such as Patagonia,
The Body Shop and Ben and Jerry’s actively promote
environmental activism and social responsibility initiatives that
encourage consumers to perceive their purchasing decisions as
socially meaningful forms of participation (Chun 2016;
Kencebay and Ertugan 2025; Page and Katz 2010). Such
communication strategies reposition consumption beyond its
utilitarian function by emphasizing consumer agency and the
societal implications of individual purchasing behaviour.
The effectiveness of these communications can be explained
through Signaling Theory (Connelly et al., 2025; Spence, 1973).
Signaling Theory suggests that consumers rely on observable
organizational cues to infer unobservable organizational
characteristics, particularly when information asymmetry exists.
Firms can communicate a product’s overall characteristics
through their brand name, signaling not only its quality but also
intangible attributes such as environmental commitment, social
responsibility, and brand activism. These quality signals play a
crucial role in product positioning, influence consumers’
perceptions of the brand, and alleviate information asymmetry
by reducing search costs and perceived risks associated with
purchase decisions (Karasek and Bryant, 2012). Consistent with
Signaling Theory, brand personality is embedded in the brand’s
intangible characteristics, which serve as symbolic cues that help
consumers interpret and evaluate the brand’s values, identity,
and expected performance.
In sustainability contexts, consumers cannot directly verify the
sincerity of corporate environmental commitments and therefore
depend on signals communicated through organizational
channels such as corporate websites. Green website
communications therefore function as signals that communicate
a firm’s environmental values, sustainability orientation, and
commitment to responsible business practices. However, the
effectiveness of these signals depends not only on their presence
but also on how they are interpreted by consumers. In an era
characterized by increasing skepticism toward sustainability
claims, consumers may question whether such signals represent
genuine commitment or merely symbolic efforts intended to
improve corporate image (Pham and Huynh 2026; Tetrevova
et al., 2025).
Consequently, perceptions of brand genuineness have become
increasingly important in determining the effectiveness of
sustainability communications. Brand genuineness refers to the
extent to which consumers perceive a brand as authentic, sincere,
and consistent with its communicated values (‘t Hart and Phau,
2022). Authenticity research suggests that consumers are more
likely to trust and develop positive relationships with brands
perceived as genuine because such brands are viewed as credible
and value-consistent (Kanjer and Doshi, 2026; Morhart et al.,
2015; Moulard et al., 2021; Napoli et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2021).
Trust subsequently serves as a fundamental mechanism through
which favourable brand evaluations are formed and maintained
(Cardoso et al., 2026; Morgan and Hunt, 1994). Particularly
within sustainability contexts, consumers appear willing to
support brands only when environmental communications are
perceived as authentic and trustworthy rather than symbolic or
opportunistic (Campagna et al., 2023; Södergren, 2021). It should
be underlined that brand authenticity refers to consumers’
perceptions of a brand as genuine, sincere, and consistent with its
communicated values, whereas brand trust reflects consumers’
confidence in the brand’s reliability and intentions.
Understanding these processes is especially important among
Generation Z consumers (born between 1997 and 2012).
Generation Z represents one of the most influential consumer
cohorts, shaping future markets and societal change, due to its
purchasing power, digital engagement, and increasing concern for
environmental and social issues (Ching, 2025; Djafarova and
Foots, 2022; Gohil et al., 2025). As digital natives, Gen
Z consumers are highly exposed to sustainability-related brand
communications through online platforms and frequently use
digital channels to evaluate corporate behaviour (Poulis et al.,
2026). Existing research suggests that this generation places
considerable importance on transparency, authenticity, and
corporate responsibility when evaluating brands (Borah et al.,
2024). At the same time, Generation Z consumers exhibit
heightened skepticism toward corporate sustainability claims and
are particularly sensitive to inconsistencies between communicated
values and organizational actions. Consequently, sustainability
communications that successfully convey authenticity may be
especially influential among this consumer segment.
For several decades, the sustainable consumption literature has
problematized the discrepancy between consumers’ expressed
environmental concerns and their actual marketplace behaviour
(Sukumaran and Majhi 2025). This phenomenon, commonly
referred to as the attitude–behaviour gap, describes the tendency
of individuals to endorse pro-environmental values and
intentions while failing to consistently translate these attitudes
into sustainable purchasing practices (Boulstridge and Carrigan,
2000; Park and Lin 2020; Schäufele and Janssen 2021; Zhuo
et al. 2022). The persistence of this gap has been a central
concern in sustainability research, as it challenges assumptions
that positive environmental attitudes naturally lead to
responsible consumption. Although environmentally conscious
consumers are often critical of corporate sustainability claims

and readily identify instances of greenwashing, they may
themselves exhibit inconsistencies between their stated values
and actual behaviours (Higgins and Tadajewski, 2002).
Consequently, the attitude–behaviour gap continues to represent
a significant theoretical and practical challenge, highlighting the
complexity of ethical decision-making and the limitations of
attitudinal measures in predicting sustainable
consumer behaviour.
Although prior research has established positive relationships
between green marketing, trust, brand attitudes, and purchase
intentions (Papadas et al., 2019; Testa et al., 2019), important
gaps remain in understanding the psychological mechanisms
that explain why Gen Z may respond differently to
sustainability-oriented communication, particularly in digital
environments. Moreover, increasing awareness of greenwashing
and skepticism toward corporate sustainability claims, have
intensified consumers’ critical evaluation of environmental
messages (Delmas and Burbano, 2011). As a result, the
effectiveness of sustainable marketing communication may
depend not only on firms’ sustainability practices and
environmental claims, but also on consumers’ underlying
psychological orientations and beliefs.
One important yet underexplored psychological factor in this
context is consumer locus of control. Consumer responses to
sustainability-related communications are influenced not only by
message characteristics but also by individual psychological
differences (Cheng et al. 2020; Cleveland et al. 2012). Locus of
control reflects the extent to which individuals believe they can
influence outcomes through their own actions (Rotter, 1966).
Within sustainable consumption contexts, consumers with a
stronger internal locus of control are more likely to believe that
their purchasing decisions can contribute to positive
environmental change, whereas consumers with an external
locus of control may perceive such outcomes as largely beyond
their personal influence (Busseri et al. 1998). Prior studies
suggest that perceived consumer effectiveness and personal
agency significantly shape environmentally responsible
behaviour (Ellen et al., 1991; Straughan and Roberts, 1999).
However, limited research has examined the moderating role of
locus of control within sustainable digital
marketing environments.
A second factor that may influence consumer responses is
brand activism belief. Brand activism refers to a company’s
public engagement with social, environmental, and political
issues (Saracevic and Schlegelmilch, 2024). Consumers’ beliefs
regarding brand activism may influence the extent to which
sustainability-oriented communication is interpreted as credible,
authentic, and persuasive. In particular, consumers who hold
favourable beliefs toward brand activism are expected to respond
more positively to sustainability and activism-related messages,
whereas skeptical consumers may perceive such communication
as opportunistic or performative (Mukherjee and Althuizen,
2020). Despite growing academic interest in brand activism,
limited empirical research has examined its role as a
psychological moderating factor within sustainable digital
marketing communication.
Against this background, the present study investigates how
green website communications influence Generation
Z consumers’ brand attitudes and behaviour through
perceptions of brand genuineness and trust. Drawing upon
Signaling Theory, the study proposes that green website
communications function as sustainability signals that influence
consumer evaluations through a sequential process of
authenticity and trust formation. Furthermore, the study
examines locus of control as a psychological boundary condition
influencing the effectiveness of green website communications
and investigates the role of brand activism beliefs in shaping
perceptions of brand genuineness.
To address this objective, the study proposes a conceptual
model in order to depict whether sustainable marketing
communication influences attitude and behaviour formation
through a sequence of psychological and relational mechanisms,
including website trust (Flavián et al. 2006), attitude toward the
website (Chen and Wells 1999), perceived brand genuineness (’t
Hart, B. and Phau 2022), attitude toward the brand and
purchase intentions (Dodds et al. 1991; Spears and Singh 2004).
At the same time, consumer locus of control (Busseri et al.
1998) and brand activism beliefs (Saracevic and Schlegelmilch
2024) are proposed as variables that influence the extent to
which sustainability messages are internalized, perceived as
authentic, and translated into favourable attitudinal and
behavioural outcomes.
This study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, it
advances sustainability marketing research by identifying locus of
control as an important psychological factor influencing the
effectiveness of sustainability communications. Second, it
extends Signaling Theory by explaining how sustainability
signals communicated through corporate websites are translated
into consumer evaluations through perceptions of brand
genuineness and trust. Third, it contributes to the emerging
brand activism literature by examining how consumers’ activism
beliefs shape responses to sustainability-related communications.
Fourth, it contributes to the attitude–behaviour gap literature by
investigating the psychological and perceptual mechanisms
through which sustainability communications influence
consumers’ evaluations and behavioural intentions. Given the
persistent discrepancy between consumers’ pro-environmental
attitudes and their actual consumption behaviour, understanding
the factors that strengthen or weaken the impact of
sustainability signals is essential for explaining why positive
sustainability perceptions do not always translate into supportive
consumer actions. Finally, by focusing specifically on Generation
Z consumers, the study provides important insights into how a
highly influential, digitally connected, and sustainability-
conscious consumer segment evaluates corporate sustainability
initiatives in contemporary digital environments. Moreover, a
conceptual framework is proposed in order to visually depict the
relation among the above variables.
2 Review of literature
2.1 Locus of control (LoC)
Individuals differ in how they perceive and respond to
sustainability-related promotional messages, suggesting that
psychological traits and individual predispositions play an
important role in shaping consumer attitudes and behaviours
Palla et al.                                                                                                                                                           10.3389/fcomm.2026.1904390
Frontiers in Communication 03 frontiersin.org

(Cheng et al., 2020; Cleveland et al., 2012). Among these
individual differences, locus of control (LoC) has emerged as a
particularly relevant construct for understanding sustainable
consumption and environmental decision-making (Chen et al.,
2024; Guazzini et al., 2025; Kalamas et al., 2014).
Rotter (1966) first introduced locus of control within Social
Learning Theory, conceptualizing it as a generalized expectancy
regarding the relationship between an individual’s actions and
subsequent outcomes. He defined internal versus external
control as “the degree to which persons expect that a
reinforcement or an outcome of their behaviour is contingent on
their own behaviour or personal characteristics versus the degree
to which persons expect that the reinforcement or outcome is a
function of chance, luck, or fate, is under the control of powerful
others, or is simply unpredictable” (p. 1). Over nearly six decades
of research, locus of control has been recognized as a
fundamental personality dimension influencing a wide range of
cognitive, motivational, and behavioural processes (Gebresilase
et al. 2025; Gulati et al., 2004; Hamzah and Othman 2023;
Hattrup et al., 2005; Joe, 1971; Lefcourt, 1976; MacDonald,
1971; Manisaligil et al., 2023; Perry and Morris, 2005; Spector,
1982; Whelan et al. 2025).
In the consumer domain, consumer locus of control refers to
consumers’ beliefs regarding the extent to which their actions and
decisions influence marketplace outcomes (Busseri et al., 1998).
Consumers with a stronger internal orientation believe that their
purchasing decisions can meaningfully affect outcomes, whereas
externally oriented consumers perceive limited influence over
marketplace consequences.
The relevance of locus of control extends beyond traditional
consumer behaviour to the domain of sustainable consumption.
In this context, locus of control captures whether consumers
believe that their individual consumption choices can contribute
to environmental improvement. Individuals with a stronger
internal orientation are more likely to perceive sustainable
consumption as an effective means of generating positive
environmental outcomes, whereas externally oriented individuals
may regard environmental problems as largely beyond the
influence of individual action (Cleveland et al. 2012; Kalamas
et al. 2014; Sharma et al. 2022).
Beyond sustainability contexts, recent research has
increasingly examined the relevance of locus of control in digital
and technologically mediated environments, where perceptions
of agency and control are often less transparent and more
difficult to evaluate. Empirical studies have demonstrated the
influence of locus of control on online attitudes, information
processing, and pre-purchase behaviours (Amichai-Hamburger,
2002; Aref and AlShahri, 2021; Hoffman et al., 2003; Walsh,
2010). More recently, locus of control has emerged as an
important variable for understanding human adaptation,
cognitive autonomy, and trust in complex technological systems,
including artificial intelligence and human–machine interactions
(Babiker et al., 2025; Montag et al., 2025).
Taken together, the literature suggests that locus of control
shapes how individuals interpret information, evaluate their
ability to influence outcomes, and engage in goal-directed
behaviours across both environmental and technological
contexts. Consequently, locus of control may serve as an
important boundary condition in understanding consumer
responses to sustainability communications, particularly when
such messages are delivered through digital-mediated channels.
2.2 Signaling theory
Signaling Theory, originally developed by Michael Spence (1973,
1978), explains how individuals make decisions under conditions of
information asymmetry, where one party possesses information that
is not directly observable by another. In such situations, decision-
makers rely on observable signals to infer underlying
characteristics, intentions, or quality. Within marketing contexts,
Signaling Theory has been widely applied to explain how
consumers interpret organizational communications and use them
to evaluate firms, products, and brands (Connelly et al., 2011, 2025).
In sustainability contexts, consumers often cannot directly
verify whether organizations are genuinely committed to
environmental and social responsibility. Consequently, they rely
on observable cues, such as sustainability reports, environmental
certifications, green advertising, and website communications, to
infer a firm’s commitment to sustainable practices (Delmas and
Burbano, 2011). Green website communications therefore
function as signals that communicate a company’s
environmental values, sustainability initiatives, and corporate
responsibility efforts. However, the effectiveness of these signals
depends on whether consumers perceive them as credible,
authentic, and consistent with organizational actions.
Recent research suggests that increasing concerns regarding
greenwashing have made consumers more skeptical of
environmental claims and more likely to scrutinize the
authenticity of sustainability-related communications before
forming favourable evaluations (Campagna et al., 2023; de
Freitas Netto et al., 2020). As a result, perceptions of brand
genuineness have become an important mechanism through
which sustainability signals influence consumer responses.
Authentic and consistent sustainability communications are
more likely to generate trust and favourable brand evaluations,
whereas communications perceived as symbolic or misleading
may undermine credibility and reduce their persuasive
effectiveness (Portal et al., 2019; Södergren, 2021). Brand
genuineness refers to consumers’ perceptions that a brand is
sincere, truthful, and consistent in its values, communications,
and actions (‘t Hart and Phau, 2022; Napoli et al., 2014;
Schallehn et al., 2014). When brands are perceived as genuine,
consumers are more likely to develop trust, emotional
attachment, and favourable behavioural intentions, including
loyalty, advocacy, and purchase intentions (Napoli et al., 2014).
The relevance of Signaling Theory is particularly evident among
Generation Z consumers. As digital natives, Generation
Z consumers frequently encounter sustainability-related
communications through online channels and actively evaluate
whether brands’ environmental claims align with their actions
and values (Djafarova and Foots, 2022). Research suggests that
this generation places considerable importance on transparency,
authenticity, and corporate accountability, making them especially
sensitive to the quality and credibility of sustainability signals
(Borah et al., 2024; Ghouse et al., 2025). Consequently, green
website communications are unlikely to influence Generation
Z consumers directly. Instead, sustainability signals are first

evaluated in terms of their genuineness and credibility before
generating trust and favourable brand attitudes.
2.3 Brand activism
Brand activism, defined as a company’s public engagement
with social, environmental, or ethical issues (Saracevic and
Schlegelmilch, 2024), may enhance consumer trust, positive
attitudes toward the brand, and purchase intentions when
consumers perceive such actions as genuine and value-driven
rather than opportunistic. Unlike traditional corporate social
responsibility initiatives, which often focus on philanthropy or
compliance, brand activism involves taking explicit positions on
controversial or value-laden issues and advocating for specific
causes. In digital environments particularly, websites and online
platforms allow brands to communicate activism initiatives in
ways that foster transparency, emotional engagement, and
perceptions of consumer participation in broader societal change.
As consumers increasingly expect organizations to contribute to
societal well-being, brands have become more active participants in
public discourse on topics such as climate change, social justice,
and sustainability (Vredenburg et al., 2020). Research suggests that
brand activism can generate positive outcomes, including enhanced
brand trust, identification, authenticity perceptions, and purchase
intentions, particularly among consumers whose personal values
align with the brand’s stance (Hydock et al., 2020; Moorman,
2020). However, activist positioning also entails risks, as consumers
may perceive such efforts as opportunistic or inconsistent when
organizational actions fail to align with communicated values.
Consequently, the effectiveness of brand activism depends largely
on the perceived credibility, genuineness, and consistency of a
brand’s commitment to the causes it supports, making authenticity
and trust critical factors in shaping consumer responses.
2.4 Generation Z
Generation Z is commonly described as the cohort that grew up
amid widespread internet access, social media, and rapid
technological development (Djafarova and Foots, 2022; Priporas
et al., 2017). Some studies identifying Generation Z as those born
between 1995 and 2010 (Sakdiyakorn et al., 2021), while other
sources adopt a 1997–2012 range. The present study follows the
latter range for participant eligibility. Compared with previous
generations, Generation Z demonstrates heightened awareness of
social and environmental issues and greater expectations that
organizations align with broader societal values (Dabija et al., 2019;
Francis and Hoefel, 2018; Theocharis and Tsekouropoulos, 2025).
Research further indicates that Generation Z values transparency,
authenticity, and ethical business practices while also exhibiting
skepticism toward traditional advertising and corporate claims,
relying heavily on peer-generated content, online reviews, and
digital influencers (Djafarova and Foots, 2022; Robichaud and Yu,
2022; Viglia et al., 2018). Understanding Generation Z’s responses
is therefore increasingly important for organizations seeking to
communicate sustainability initiatives effectively and develop
meaningful consumer relationships in digitally
mediated environments (Velinov, 2025).
3 Hypotheses development
3.1 H1: the moderating role of locus of
control in Gen Z
Generation Z consumers demonstrate strong concern for
environmental sustainability and social responsibility while
simultaneously exhibiting considerable skepticism toward
corporate sustainability claims and greenwashing practices (Dabija
et al., 2019; Djafarova and Foots, 2022; Halibas et al., 2025;
Varese et al., 2025). As digital natives, Generation Z consumers
actively seek information about brands through online channels
and frequently scrutinize the credibility and authenticity of
sustainability communications before forming evaluations and
purchase intentions. Consequently, understanding the
psychological factors that influence how Gen Z consumers
interpret sustainability-related information has become
increasingly important for both scholars and practitioners.
One such factor is locus of control, which has received growing
attention in sustainability and environmental consumption research
(Chen et al., 2024; Guazzini et al., 2025; Kalamas et al., 2014; Trivedi
et al., 2015). Recent evidence suggests that consumers with stronger
internal control beliefs are more likely to engage in environmentally
responsible behaviours because they perceive individual and
organizational actions as capable of producing meaningful
environmental outcomes (Megha, 2024). Similarly, Backer and
Prasanth (2024) found that internal locus of control positively
influences green buyer behaviour by strengthening consumers’
confidence in the effectiveness of pro-environmental actions.
These relationships may be particularly salient among Generation
Z consumers, who often perceive consumption decisions as a
means of expressing personal values and contributing to broader
societal and environmental change.
Signaling Theory provides a useful framework for
understanding this process (Liu et al., 2008; Spence, 1973).
Because a firm’s true environmental commitment is often difficult
for consumers to observe directly, organizations rely on
sustainability communications to signal their values, intentions,
and environmental responsibility. Green website communications,
in particular, function as informational signals that communicate
a firm’s commitment to sustainable practices. However,
consumers do not interpret these signals uniformly. As concerns
regarding greenwashing continue to increase, particularly among
younger consumers, Generation Z individuals have become
increasingly critical of environmental claims and frequently assess
whether sustainability communications are credible, sincere, and
trustworthy before forming favourable evaluations (Delmas and
Burbano, 2011; Djafarova and Foots, 2022; Prasanthi, 2025).
A critical outcome of this signal interpretation process is
perceived brand genuineness. Brand genuineness refers to
consumers’ perceptions that a brand is sincere, honest, and
truthful in its communications and actions (Napoli et al., 2014).
Although closely related to authenticity, which encompasses
broader evaluations of consistency and fidelity to core values,
genuineness specifically captures perceptions of sincerity and
credibility in brand behaviour (Morhart et al., 2015; Napoli
et al., 2014). Within sustainability contexts, consumers are likely
to perceive brands as genuine when environmental claims are
viewed as reflecting a sincere organizational commitment rather

than opportunistic marketing efforts. Research demonstrates that
perceptions of authenticity and genuineness strengthen
consumers’ emotional and cognitive connections with brands
and contribute to more favourable evaluations (Morhart et al.,
2015; Napoli et al., 2014). Furthermore, perceived authenticity
in sustainable branding has been shown to enhance consumer
trust and brand loyalty (Safeer and Liu, 2023; Södergren, 2021).
Trust represents another important mechanism through which
sustainability communications influence consumer evaluations.
According to Morgan and Hunt (1994), trust constitutes a central
component of successful consumer-brand relationships. Because
perceptions of brand genuineness reduce uncertainty regarding
the sincerity of a firm’s sustainability claims, they serve as an
important antecedent of trust. Empirical research consistently
demonstrates that trust positively influences consumer attitudes
and behavioural intentions toward brands (Kim et al., 2025).
Within sustainability contexts, Alhomaid (2025) found that green
marketing initiatives enhance brand trust only when consumers
perceive sustainability claims as authentic and credible.
Consequently, trust may represent a key mechanism through
which perceptions of brand genuineness translate into favourable
brand evaluations and behavioural responses.
Integrating locus of control and Signaling Theory suggests that
internally oriented Generation Z consumers should be particularly
receptive to green website communications. Because these
individuals are more likely to believe that organizational actions can
contribute to meaningful environmental change, they should be
more inclined to interpret sustainability-related website signals as
credible indicators of genuine corporate commitment. These
enhanced perceptions of brand genuineness should subsequently
strengthen trust in the brand, leading to more favourable brand
attitudes and purchase intentions. In contrast, externally oriented
consumers may remain skeptical regarding the effectiveness and
sincerity of sustainability initiatives, and tend to believe that their
purchase pattern may have no effect on environmental change,
thereby reducing the persuasive impact of green website
communications. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:
H1: Among Generation Z consumers, locus of control
moderates responses to green website communications such that
internally oriented consumers report higher levels of perceived
brand genuineness, trust, brand attitude and purchase intention
following exposure to a green website than externally
oriented consumers.
H1a: Among Generation Z consumers, the positive indirect
effect of green website exposure on brand attitude through
perceived brand genuineness is stronger for internally oriented
consumers than for externally oriented consumers.
3.2 H2: the effect of green website
communications and brand activism beliefs
on brand attitude through brand
genuineness and trust
Consumers are increasingly skeptical of corporate
sustainability claims and may question whether such
communications represent genuine commitment or merely
symbolic marketing efforts (de Freitas Netto et al., 2020; Delmas
and Burbano, 2011). Such skepticism is particularly evident
among Generation Z, who are generally supportive of
sustainability initiatives but are also highly sensitive to perceived
greenwashing and inconsistencies between corporate claims and
actual practices. Consequently, perceptions of brand genuineness
become critical in determining the effectiveness of sustainability
communications (‘t Hart and Phau, 2022).
Research on brand authenticity demonstrates that perceptions
of genuineness are positively associated with trust because
consumers are more likely to trust organizations whose actions
appear congruent with their communicated values (Kleynhans
et al., 2022; Li and Shan, 2025; Portal et al., 2019). Trust
subsequently serves as a central mechanism through which
favourable consumer evaluations are formed (Jamal et al., 2023;
Jongmans et al., 2022). Numerous studies have shown that,
particularly in green marketing contexts, trust enhances positive
attitudes toward brands and strengthens consumer-brand
relationships (Daou et al., 2025; Jesse et al., 2025; Jung et al.,
2014; Liu et al., 2025; Monfort et al., 2025; Tikaro, 2025). Given
Generation Z’s preference for transparency, authenticity, and
corporate accountability, trust may be particularly important in
shaping their evaluations of sustainability-oriented brands.
In addition to organizational signals, consumer predispositions
may also shape perceptions of brand authenticity (Hernandez-
Fernandez and Lewis, 2019). Brand activism beliefs reflect the
extent to which consumers support corporate engagement in
social and environmental issues (Saracevic and Schlegelmilch,
2024). Among Generation Z consumers, support for corporate
activism is often stronger than among previous generations
because younger consumers increasingly expect brands to take
visible positions on societal and environmental challenges.
Consumers who hold stronger brand activism beliefs are therefore
likely to evaluate sustainability-oriented communications more
positively because such communications align with their personal
values and expectations of corporate responsibility (Cardoso et al.,
2026). Consequently, these consumers should be more likely to
perceive brands as genuine and authentic.
Taken together, Signaling Theory and authenticity research
suggest that both green website communications and brand
activism beliefs contribute to perceptions of brand genuineness,
which subsequently foster trust and lead to more favourable brand
attitudes. Because Generation Z consumers place considerable
importance on sustainability, transparency, and corporate
responsibility, these mechanisms are expected to be particularly
influential in shaping their evaluations of sustainability-oriented
brands. The following hypothesis is proposed:
H2: Among Generation Z consumers, the effects of green
website exposure and brand activism beliefs on brand attitude
are sequentially mediated by perceived brand genuineness and
trust, such that both factors independently positively influence
perceptions of brand genuineness, which increase trust and, in
turn, enhance brand attitudes.

4.1 Research design
This study employs a between-subjects experimental design to
examine the effects of sustainable digital marketing tactics on

Generation Z consumers. The design compares responses to
sustainability-oriented brand efforts with responses to non-

4.2 Pretest 1
The purpose of Pretest 1 (Supplementary Appendix C) was to
assess whether the selected products and brands used as
experimental stimuli were appropriate, familiar, and recognizable
to Generation Z consumers. Consistent with recommendations in
experimental consumer research (Perdue and Summers, 1986;
Viglia et al., 2021), participants evaluated the selected product
categories and brands using measures of product category
familiarity, brand familiarity, brand recognizability, and perceived
sustainability orientation adapted from prior literature (Campbell
and Keller, 2003; Chen, 2010; Kent and Allen, 1994; Macdonald
and Sharp, 2000; Zaichkowsky, 1985). The findings confirmed
that the selected product categories and brands were highly
recognizable and relevant to Generation Z consumers, while the
sustainability-oriented brands were perceived as significantly more
environmentally focused than the control brands (e.g., Kumar
and Panda, 2025).
4.3 Stimuli
In contemporary markets, organizations increasingly employ
sustainability-related communications to signal their commitment
to environmental and social responsibility. Corporate websites are
particularly important in this regard because they represent a
primary interface through which consumers evaluate
organizational values, legitimacy, and commitment to
sustainability. This issue is especially relevant for Generation
Z consumers, who rely heavily on digital platforms when seeking
information about brands and frequently assess whether corporate
actions align with communicated sustainability values. According
to Signaling Theory (Connelly et al., 2025; Spence, 1973),
consumers rely on observable organizational cues to infer
unobservable characteristics such as sincerity, credibility, and
trustworthiness. Consequently, sustainability-related website
communications function as signals that may influence consumers’
perceptions of a brand’s underlying motives and commitments.
The experimental stimuli consisted of real brand websites
selected based on the presence or absence of sustainability-
oriented communication (Table 1). Real brands were deliberately
selected to enhance ecological validity and simulate realistic
digital environments familiar to Generation Z consumers,
thereby facilitating natural engagement with the stimuli and
reducing potential confusion during the experimental process.
This choice is consistent with prior consumer research showing
that real-brand designs can enhance external validity and
generalizability when consumers’ responses to actual brands are
of interest (Carrillat et al., 2024; Gidaković and Zabkar, 2022).
The selected brands represented three product categories—
fashion, cosmetics, and ice cream. Moreover, because green
communication is conceptualized as a brand signal, its
interpretation is inherently embedded within the brand context.
The use of different brands across conditions therefore entails a
trade-off between experimental control and ecological validity,
as pre-existing brand associations may contribute to observed
differences; accordingly, the findings are interpreted as evidence
consistent with the proposed signaling mechanism in realistic
branded contexts rather than as an isolated effect of
sustainability communication.
The sustainability condition includes brands widely
recognized for their environmental positioning and activism,
namely Patagonia, The Body Shop, and Ben and Jerry’s. These
brands prominently communicate environmental responsibility,
ethical sourcing, and sustainability-driven values through their
digital platforms.
The control condition includes brands that primarily
emphasize product-related attributes such as style, aesthetics,
convenience, or product performance without foregrounding
environmental sustainability. These include PullandBear, KIKO
Milano, and Halo Top.
4.4 Participants
Data were collected through an online questionnaire
administered via Prolific, an online research platform widely
used in academic research. Prolific provides access to
participants with diverse characteristics, adheres to established
ethical standards, and enables researchers to apply detailed
screening criteria based on demographic and behavioural
characteristics (Palan and Schitter, 2018; Peer et al., 2017).
Participants were born between 1995 and 2008. Although
Generation Z is commonly defined as extending to 2010 or
2012, the upper boundary was set at 2008 to ensure that all
participants were at least 18 years old, in line with ethical
requirements and Prolific’s eligibility requirements. Before
participation, all participants were informed about the study and
provided electronic informed consent by selecting “Yes”.

4.5 Experimental process
The study employed a between-subjects experimental design
to examine the effects of sustainability-oriented brand activism
on Generation Z consumers’ attitudes and pre-purchase
behaviour, while considering the moderating role of consumer
locus of control (LoC) and the mediating role of brand activism
belief as well as exposure to the website of a green oriented
brand (Supplementary Appendix A).
Prior to exposure to the experimental stimuli, participants
completed pre-exposure measures assessing consumer locus of
control and perceptions of brand activism. Consumer locus of
control classified respondents according to stronger internal or
external control orientations (Busseri et al. 1998). Brand
activism reflects consumers’ perceptions of brands that engage
in environmental activism (Saracevic and Schlegelmilch 2024).
Both variables were treated as moderating factors that could
influence consumer responses across the two
experimental conditions.
Participants were randomly assigned to one of two
experimental conditions: (1) exposure to a sustainability-
oriented brand activism website (experimental condition) or (2)
exposure to a non-brand-activism website (control condition).
The experimental stimuli consisted of real brand websites
selected based on the extent to which they communicated
environmental responsibility, sustainability values, and
brand activism.
Following random assignment, participants were instructed to
imagine that they were browsing online and considering
purchasing from the presented brand. Participants then viewed
the assigned website stimulus as long as they wish at their time
and pace (median exposure time 2–3 min). Subsequently,
participants completed post-exposure measures assessing website
user trust (Flavián et al., 2006), attitude toward the website
(Chen and Wells, 1999), perceived brand genuineness (‘t Hart
and Phau, 2022), attitude toward the brand (Spears and Singh,
2004), and pre-purchase intention (Dodds et al., 1991; Spears
and Singh, 2004). After exposure, participants completed
manipulation-check items assessing perceived sustainability
orientation and brand activism. All constructs are measured
using seven-point Likert-type scales ranging from 1 (strongly
disagree) to 7 (strongly agree) or 1 (strongly disagree) to 5
(strongly agree). Measurement items are adapted from
established scales in prior research (Supplementary Appendix B).
The experimental process was designed to examine whether
exposure to sustainability-oriented brand activism websites
produced stronger positive consumer responses than exposure to
non-activism websites and whether these effects differed
according to consumers’ locus of control orientation.
To assess the effectiveness of the experimental manipulation,
participants evaluate the extent to which the brand emphasizes
sustainability using items such as “This brand emphasizes
sustainability,” “This brand appears environmentally
responsible,” and “This brand promotes eco-friendly values.”
Significant differences between conditions indicated
successful manipulation.
4.6 Measurement and operationalization of
consumer locus of control
Consumer locus of control was assessed prior to exposure to
the experimental stimuli using the consumer-specific measure
developed by Busseri, Lefcourt, and Kerton (1998). Following
reverse coding, a composite score was calculated by averaging
responses across the 18 items. The scale demonstrated good
internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = .829).
Consistent with the theoretical distinction between internal
and external control orientations (Nießen et al., 2022),
consumer locus of control was operationalized using an
internal–external classification, an approach also employed in
prior empirical research (e.g., Kader, 2022). Rather than using a
sample-dependent median split, participants were classified
according to the theoretical midpoint of the seven-point
response scale (4). Participants with mean scores ≤ 4 were
classified as relatively external-oriented, whereas those with
scores > 4 were classified as relatively internal-oriented. This
criterion provided a transparent distinction between respondents
positioned on either side of the scale midpoint.

5.2 Hypothesis H2
A serial mediation analysis was conducted using PROCESS
Model 6 with website type (green versus non-green website) as
the independent variable, perceived brand genuineness as the
first mediator, trust as the second mediator, and brand attitude
as the dependent variable. Brand activism beliefs were included
as a covariate (Table 5), (Supplementary Appendix D).
The results indicated that exposure to the green website
significantly increased perceptions of brand genuineness
(b = .632, p < .001). Perceived brand genuineness significantly
predicted trust (b = .820, p < .001), while trust significantly
predicted brand attitude (b = .218, p < .001).

not directly improve brand attitudes. Rather, consumers first
interpret green website information as a signal of brand
genuineness, which subsequently enhances trust and ultimately
leads to more favourable brand attitudes. Therefore, H2
was supported.
6 Conceptual framework
Figure 1 presents the conceptual framework underpinning this
study, which is grounded in Signaling Theory (Spence, 1973). The
model proposes that green website communications serve as
sustainability signals that influence Generation Z consumers’
evaluations of a brand. Specifically, exposure to sustainability-
related website content is expected to enhance perceptions of
brand genuineness, which subsequently increases trust and leads
to more favourable brand attitudes. The framework further
suggests that this process is contingent upon consumers’ locus
of control, such that individuals with a more internal locus of
control are more likely to interpret sustainability signals as
credible and meaningful indicators of corporate commitment. In
addition, brand activism beliefs are expected to positively
influence perceptions of brand genuineness, as consumers who
support corporate engagement in social and environmental
issues may be more receptive to sustainability-oriented
communications. Together, these relationships explain how
organizational sustainability signals and individual consumer
characteristics jointly shape brand evaluations among
Generation Z consumers.
7 Discussion
The present study examined how Generation Z consumers
respond to green website communications and investigated the
psychological mechanisms through which such communications
influence brand evaluations. The findings demonstrate that
green website communications are not interpreted uniformly
among Generation Z consumers (e.g., Nwaukwa and Esenyel,
2026). Rather, their effectiveness depends on individual
psychological characteristics, particularly locus of control.

Internally oriented consumers reported higher levels of
perceived brand genuineness, trust, and brand attitude following
exposure to a green website. Beyond confirming previous
research, linking internal locus of control to pro-environmental
behaviour (e.g., Backer and Prasanth 2024; Megha, 2024), these
findings suggest that locus of control also shapes how
consumers interpret sustainability-related signals. Specifically,
internally oriented consumers appear more likely to perceive
sustainability initiatives as meaningful and credible indicators of
corporate commitment, thereby enhancing the persuasive impact
of green communications.
The results further extend previous research linking internal
locus of control to environmentally responsible consumption
and pro-environmental behaviour (Backer and Prasanth, 2024;
Chen et al., 2024; Megha, 2024). While previous studies
primarily focused on sustainable consumption behaviours, the
present research demonstrates that locus of control also
influences how Generation Z consumers interpret corporate
sustainability communications in digital environments.
Specifically, internally oriented Generation Z consumers appear
more willing to perceive sustainability-related website content as
authentic indicators of genuine corporate commitment, whereas
externally oriented consumers remain more skeptical regarding
the effectiveness and sincerity of environmental initiatives.
Interestingly, no significant interaction effect emerged for
purchase intention, despite significant effects on perceived brand
genuineness, trust, and brand attitude. This finding suggests that
among Generation Z consumers, favourable evaluations of
sustainability communications do not necessarily translate into
immediate behavioural intentions. Consistent with the well-
documented attitude–behaviour gap in sustainable consumption
research (e.g., Park and Lin 2020; Schäufele and Janssen 2021;
Schäufele and Janssen 2021; Zhuo et al. 2022), consumers may
appreciate and support sustainability initiatives while
simultaneously considering additional factors, such as price,
product quality, personal relevance, and brand fit, before forming
purchase intentions. Thus, sustainability communications appear
more effective in shaping cognitive and affective evaluations than
in directly influencing behavioural outcomes.
The findings also provide strong support for Signaling Theory
(Spence, 1973). Green website communications function as
signals through which organizations communicate their
environmental values and sustainability commitments. However,
the effectiveness of these signals depends not merely on their
presence but on how they are interpreted by consumers. For Gen
Z consumers, who are often characterized as highly sustainability-
conscious yet simultaneously skeptical of corporate environmental
claims, perceptions of authenticity become particularly important.
The results indicate that sustainability-related website
communications influence consumer evaluations only when they
are perceived as genuine representations of organizational values.
Collectively, these findings suggest that the effectiveness of
sustainability communications depends less on the mere presence
of environmental claims and more on consumers’ interpretations
of those claims as credible signals of organizational commitment.
A central contribution of the study is the identification of
perceived brand genuineness as a critical mechanism linking
sustainability communications and brand evaluations. Across both
hypotheses, brand genuineness emerged as the primary factor
through which green website communications influence trust and
brand attitudes. Exposure to a green website significantly
enhanced perceptions of brand genuineness, which subsequently
increased trust and ultimately strengthened brand attitudes. These
findings are consistent with authenticity research suggesting that
consumers are more likely to trust and support brands that
appear sincere, transparent, and aligned with their communicated
values (Morhart et al., 2015; Napoli et al., 2014).

The findings further demonstrate the importance of trust as a
downstream consequence of authenticity perceptions. Trust has
long been recognized as a cornerstone of successful consumer–
brand relationships (Morgan and Hunt, 1994). In sustainability
contexts, trust becomes even more important because
consumers often face uncertainty regarding the credibility of
environmental claims. The present findings suggest that Gen
Z consumers do not automatically trust brands that
communicate sustainability initiatives. Instead, trust develops
only after sustainability communications are perceived as
authentic and credible. This finding aligns with recent
sustainability marketing research indicating that authenticity is a
prerequisite for trust formation in green marketing contexts.
Another important contribution concerns the role of brand
activism beliefs. Generation Z consumers, who reported stronger
support for corporate engagement in social and environmental
issues, were significantly more likely to perceive sustainability-
oriented brands as genuine. This finding suggests that brand
activism beliefs function as a value-based lens through which
sustainability communications are interpreted. Consumers who
expect brands to actively address societal and environmental
challenges may perceive sustainability communications as more
congruent with their personal values, thereby enhancing
perceptions of authenticity and trustworthiness. Rather than
directly influencing brand attitudes, such beliefs operate through
authenticity perceptions and trust formation, highlighting the
importance of value congruence between consumers
and organizations.
Interestingly, neither green website exposure nor brand
activism beliefs exerted significant direct effects on brand
attitude once brand genuineness and trust were included in the
model. This finding suggests that positive brand evaluations are
not generated simply through exposure to sustainability
messages. Instead, Generation Z consumers first evaluate
whether sustainability communications are authentic and
trustworthy before forming favourable attitudes toward the
brand. Consequently, authenticity and trust represent essential
psychological mechanisms through which sustainability
communications influence brand evaluations.
8 Managerial implications
The findings provide several important managerial
implications for organizations seeking to engage Generation
Z consumers through sustainability communications.
First, organizations should recognize that simply
communicating environmental initiatives is insufficient to
generate favourable brand evaluations. Generation Z consumers
appear highly attentive to the authenticity of sustainability
claims and are unlikely to respond positively to sustainability
messages that lack credibility. Consequently, firms should
prioritize transparency, consistency, and evidence-based
sustainability communications.

mechanism influencing trust and brand attitudes, managers

Fourth, firms should actively cultivate trust through authentic
communication strategies. Rather than relying on promotional
sustainability claims, organizations should provide detailed
evidence of environmental performance and communicate both
achievements and challenges. Such transparency may strengthen
perceptions of sincerity and reduce skepticism associated
with greenwashing.
Finally, the findings highlight the importance of brand
activism among Generation Z consumers. Organizations that
engage in environmental and social issues should ensure that
activism initiatives are consistent with core organizational values
and supported by concrete actions. When activism is perceived
as authentic rather than opportunistic, it can enhance
perceptions of brand genuineness, strengthen trust, and improve
brand evaluations.
9 Conclusion
This study investigated how Generation Z consumers respond
to green website communications and examined the psychological
mechanisms underlying these responses. The findings
demonstrate that sustainability communications influence brand
evaluations through a process of authenticity and trust
formation rather than through direct persuasion.
The results reveal that Generation Z consumers with an
internal locus of control respond more favourably to green
website communications than externally oriented consumers,
particularly in terms of perceived brand genuineness, trust, and
brand attitude. These findings suggest that individual differences
in perceived control serve as an important boundary condition
influencing the effectiveness of sustainability communications.
The study also demonstrates that green website
communications and brand activism beliefs influence brand
attitudes indirectly through perceptions of brand genuineness
and trust. Brand genuineness emerged as the critical mechanism
through which sustainability-related communications are
translated into favourable consumer evaluations. Trust
subsequently reinforces these positive evaluations and
contributes to stronger brand attitudes.
Overall, the findings suggest that successful sustainability
communication requires more than communicating
environmental commitments. For Generation Z consumers,
sustainability messages must be perceived as authentic, credible,
and consistent with organizational actions. In an era
characterized by increasing skepticism toward corporate
sustainability claims, organizations that successfully establish
perceptions of genuineness and trust are more likely to build
favourable relationships with Generation Z consumers and
strengthen long-term brand value.
10 Limitations and future research
Several limitations should be acknowledged when
interpreting the findings of this study. First, the research
focused exclusively on Generation Z consumers, which may
limit the generalizability of the results to other generational
cohorts that may differ in their sustainability attitudes, digital
behaviours, and responses to corporate communications.
Future studies should examine whether the observed
relationships hold across different age groups and cultural
contexts. Second, the study investigated responses to
sustainability communications presented through a corporate
website, whereas consumers increasingly encounter
sustainability-related messages across multiple digital
touchpoints, including social media, influencer content, mobile
applications, and AI-generated communications. Future
research could explore whether the effects identified in this
study vary across communication channels and technological
environments. Third, although the study examined purchase
intention as an outcome variable, it did not assess actual
purchasing behaviour. Given the persistent attitude–behaviour
gap in sustainable consumption, future research should
investigate whether perceptions of brand genuineness and trust
translate into observable behavioural outcomes. Fourth,
consumer locus of control was operationalized through an
internal–external split based on the midpoint of the
measurement scale. Although this approach enabled the
examination of differences between relatively internal- and
external-oriented consumers, future research could further
investigate locus of control as a continuous construct or
employ alternative validated approaches to capture variation in
control orientations. Fifth, the experimental design employed
real brands and existing websites to enhance ecological validity
and simulate realistic marketplace encounters. Future research
could replicate the study using controlled or fictitious brands,
or the same brand across experimental conditions, to
strengthen experimental control and isolate the pre-attitudinal
effects. Finally, future studies could examine additional
psychological and contextual factors, such as environmental
concern, skepticism toward green marketing, political ideology,
or AI literacy, which may further influence how consumers
interpret sustainability communications and evaluate
sustainability-oriented brands.
