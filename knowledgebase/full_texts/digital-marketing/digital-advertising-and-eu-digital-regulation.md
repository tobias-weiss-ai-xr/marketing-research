# Digital advertising and EU digital regulation

1
Digital Advertising and EU Digital Regulation
Zihao Li

The advent of Big Data, Machine Learning and Artificial Intelligence (AI) has enabled
advertisers to more accurately target prospective customers group and adjust the content in
real-time. According to specific demographics, interests (inferred), and behaviors of online
users, the advertising contents are also different. While digital advertising has various
advantages compared with traditional advertising, many already argue that digital advertising
threatens the fundamental values of privacy, non-discrimination, and digital autonomy,
raising legal and ethical concerns. As a result, EU lawmakers embark on the regulation of
digital advertising. This chapter aims to map the debate of current legal regulation on digital
advertising in the EU, explaining the main issues in regulating digital advertising and
critically discussing the prevailing arguments in the area. The following content will first
discuss the definition of key terms and the historical development of EU regulation on digital
advertising. After that, the current issues and contributions, the main research methods, and
recommendations for future directions will be discussed.

1. Definitions
Digital advertising and EU digital regulation are the two key concepts. Digital advertising,
also called online behavioral advertising, refers to the use of information and
communications technologies (ICTs) to deliver promotional messages to consumers
(Boerman, Kruikemeier and Zuiderveen Borgesius 2017). Unlike the traditional advertising
channel, digital advertising can use the data collected from a wide range of sources, such as
the internet, mobile devices, and social media platforms. One of the most attractive aspects of
digital advertising is the ability to target specific groups or individuals for measurable

 2
campaigns, while traditional advertising often relies on broader reach and brand awareness.
However, such accurate advertising targeting has been criticized in many ways. In order to
operate digital advertising, the most important precondition is that users’ online behavior is
trackable. From cookies to digital fingerprints, advertisers use a large volume of data and
different online identifiers to track online users and analyze their preferences. After tracking,
advertisers use this data to build profiles of users. The Information useful for commercial
purposes will be extracted. For example, how much the users’ behaviors would affect the
strength of an interest category, the type of further actions (e.g., viewing, clicking and
buying) and the relevance of the page (e.g., certain brands-related websites).

The large scale of tracking, profiling and analyzing online users is criticized by the public.
The EU legislators have intervened since the 1990s. The second key concept is digital
regulation, which refers to a set of legislations and regulations by the European Union to
directly or indirectly govern digital advertising. Legal scholars argue that digital advertising
mainly poses three legal and ethical challenges (Wachter 2020). Firstly, massive data
tracking and analysis lead to the ambiguity of the line between the public and private. Users’
private sphere is often invaded by such data collection and processing. Because of
information asymmetry and imbalanced power, users cannot control their personal data,
undermining their digital autonomy. Therefore, privacy and data protection law is the first
and foremost digital regulation. By regulating the collection, and processing of personal data,
privacy and data protection law can regulate digital advertising.

Secondly, discrimination is also a common concern with digital advertising in legal research
(Wachter 2020: p. 13). Because digital advertising analyses users’ behavioral data to decide
the content of advertising, some types of individuals are likely to be treated less favorably

 3
than others. For example, a user who viewed a basketball game may see the higher price of
basketball trainers than other users in advertising. Moreover, digital advertising may
reinforce racial stereotypes and stigmatize users (Sweeney 2013: 13). Thus, discrimination
laws are also candidates for regulating this area. Thirdly, such digital advertising firms are
largely controlled by Internet giants, such as Google or Meta (formerly named Facebook).
Such data monopoly is detrimental to innovation and competition in the market. Therefore,
competition law also governs the digital advertising industry.

2. The regulation of digital advertising from a historical perspective
2.1. From domestic regulation to International Convention and Guidelines
The regulation of digital advertising has a relatively short history compared to traditional
forms of advertising. As discussed above, privacy and data protection law is the foremost
regulation initiated at a national level back in the 1970s in the EU (Rauhofer 2008). The first
domestic data protection legislation was seen in the German federal state Hesse in 1970
(Hessisches Datenschutzgesetz 1970). At the European level, the first legally binding
international instrument is the Convention for the Protection of Individuals with regard to
Automatic Processing of Personal Data (Council of Europe Convention for the Protection of
Individuals with regard to Automatic Processing of Personal Data 1981), which expands and
refines the principles enshrined in the early legal resolution. However, at this stage, the aim
of data protection law is still designed to eliminate potential obstacles to cross-border data
flows. Meanwhile, the OECD Guidelines Governing the Protection of Privacy have also been
initiated (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development 1980). While the
guideline is not legally binding, it sets out eight basic principles regarding the protection of
individual privacy. The principles lay the foundation for regulating digital advertising from
data protection law. These principles include collection limitation, data quality, purpose

 4
specification, use limitation, security safeguards, openness, individual participation and
accountability (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development 1980: 2).

2.2. European Data Protection Directive
Inspired by the Convention and OECD guidelines, the first formal legislation of data
protection at the EU level was the European Data Protection Directive (DPD) in 1995
(European Parliament, 1995). Unlike the Convention, the DPD has dual objectives: (i)
protecting individuals’ personal data; (ii) facilitating the free movement of personal data. The
first objective provides a useful tool to regulate digital advertising. If digital advertising
needs to use personal data for tracking and analyzing, the advertisers must have legitimate
grounds for processing personal data. Moreover, advertisers must comply with all of the
principles of DPD and also have at least one legitimate ground for processing personal data,
including data subject consent; processing is necessary for the performance of a contract;
processing is necessary in order to protect the vital interests of the data subject; processing is
necessary for public interests; processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate
interests pursued by the controller or by the third party. Therefore, DPD in principle
prohibited personal data processing for digital advertising unless it has sufficient legal ground
that is explicitly provided by law.

Furthermore, the DPD provides different rights for individuals to fight against data
controllers, including digital advertisers. In the context of digital advertising, these rights
include the right to information, the right to access and the right to object. Individuals can use
these rights to inquire about the processing information about personal data collection and
processing in digital advertising and can object to it. The dual cores of DPD promote data
protection as a fundamental right regime. Such a fundamental regime is embodied in the EU

 5
Charter of Fundamental Rights of which Article 8 establishes a separate fundamental right for
the protection of personal data. Treaty of Lisbon amended Treaty on the Functioning of the
European Union (TFEU) in 2009, which reiterates the right to data protection in Article 16.
As a result, EU lawmakers have established a high standard regarding protecting personal
data, which can largely apply to digital advertising.

2.3. EU ePrivacy Directive
The EU enacts not only general legislation concerning personal data protection but also
establishes sector-specific legislation on personal data protection, most notably the ePrivacy
Directive dealing with data protection matters in the electronic communications sector
(European Parliament 2002). The ePrivacy Directive holds special significance to digital
advertising practices. By setting out specific requirements for using cookies or similar
technologies (e.g., device fingerprinting) for online tracking and targeting individuals, the
ePrivacy Directive can regulate digital advertising. Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive
adopts an opt-out consent mechanism, which requires users to be allowed to opt out of the
use of cookies and similar technologies for online tracking and targeting. In the context of
digital advertising, digital advertisers must provide users with an easy way to turn off or
block these technologies and must also make it clear to users what the implications of doing
so will be. Therefore, it is also called as “cookies law.” Although the DPD and the ePrivacy
Directive can mutually complement each other, there are still several issues regarding
interactive mechanisms, such as tracking walls and implied consent. These problems will be
discussed in the next section.

3. Critical issues of the existing EU regulations in the context of digital advertising

 6
As the successor of the DPD, the GDPR inherits the underlying framework of the DPD and
demonstrates a strengthened regulatory capacity for digital advertising. The GDPR requires
that the data processing in digital advertising must adhere to the following principles:
lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimization; accuracy;
storage limitation; integrity and confidentiality; and accountability. Furthermore, the GDPR
redefines the users’ consent as “any freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous
indication of the data subject’s wishes by which he or she, by a statement or by a clear
affirmative action, signifies agreement to the processing of personal data relating to him or
her” Such restrictions specifically prohibit pre-ticked opt-in boxes and require distinct
(‘granular’) consent options for distinct processing operations. This high standard for consent
indicates the shift of consent from opt-out to opt-in. In the context of digital advertising,
advertisers must obtain the users’ clear opt-in consent to process their data for advertising.

Additionally, the GDPR’s innovations embody individual rights. For example, the GDPR
empowers individuals with a right to data portability, which requires data controllers to
provide users’ personal data in a structured, commonly used and machine-readable format
and to transmit that data to another controller without hindrance. In digital advertising,
advertisers must provide users with their personal data when users request. The stipulation is
designed to restrain the data monopoly by a few Internet giants by entitling individual
autonomy to switch data controllers. Apart from that, the GDPR grants individuals the right
to object (Article 21) and the right not to be subject to automated decision-making (ADM)
(Article 22). In the context of digital advertising, users have the right to object to data
processing, including the types of digital advertising that involve personal data. For the
digital advertising generated by algorithms and AI, Article 22 empowers individuals with the
right to opt out of the ADM by obtaining human intervention.

 7

3.1. Critical issues of consent mechanism
However, the GDPR demonstrates many issues in practice. Firstly, companies use
manipulative design techniques (aka dark patterns) to nudge individuals to consent.
Alternatively, such a dark pattern design could make it difficult for individuals to truly
express their willingness regarding personal data processing. For example, companies may
bury important information in lengthy terms of service agreements that are not easy to find
for individuals. Moreover, companies may use eye-catching colors (e.g., red color), designs
or language to obtain individuals’ consent. In some scenarios, companies may use vague and
manipulative language or imagery to create a sense of urgency or pressure to obtain
individuals’ consent. Furthermore, due to the information and knowledge asymmetry,
individuals normally do not fully understand the implications of giving their consent.
Especially when privacy policy contains vague or misleading language to describe the data
collection, processing, usage and sharing, individuals cannot make informed decisions.

Apart from the dark pattern design, another critical issue of the consent mechanism is the
public perception of privacy and data protection. Individuals’ stated concerns about their data
are discrepant with their actual behaviors online, which is also known as the “privacy
paradox.”(Barth and de Jong 2017) Individuals normally express worries about protecting
their personal information but engage in behaviors that undermine their privacy, such as
arbitrarily giving their consent when digital advertisers request. On the one hand, individuals
may lack privacy and data protection literacy and often underestimate the risks of giving
consent to digital advertisers. Individuals may have a false sense of security regarding their
personal data online. They may believe that their data is well-protected by the companies
rather than causing harm. Besides that, a fact that cannot be overlooked is that many people

 8
may be willing to consent to collect personal information in exchange for the convenience or
benefits provided by online services. For instance, people are willing to (or do not have a
choice) provide their personal data, email and search results to exchange for Google’s free
online service, including Gmail, YouTube and others. Therefore, the potential failure of the
consent mechanism is the first critical issue in digital advertising regulation, which indicates
that individuals’ autonomy, choice and control of their online identity are undermined.

3.2. Critical issues of right mechanism
The second issue is the GDPR’s right mechanism in legal practice. In the context of digital
advertising, the GDPR grants individuals diverse rights to regulate the infringement caused
by online advertising. For example, as aforementioned, the right to object and the right not to
be subject to ADM are two useful toolkits. However, many individuals may be unaware of
these rights or may not know how to exercise them. Even if some are aware of what data
rights they have and know how to exercise them, the complicated legal procedure is still
challenging for them. Moreover, as scholars argue, there are several preconditions for
exercising these rights (Li 2022: 12). For example, the thresholds of the right not to be
subject to ADM are two. Firstly, in order to apply this article, the decision must be based
solely on automated processing without any evaluation or intervention by other persons,
including online profiling (Steppe 2017: 783). However, in the context of digital advertising,
if the processing of decision-making has human involvement, it is unlikely that individuals
can exercise this right. Secondly, assuming the decision is automatically made by the digital
advertising algorithm, the decision must produce legal effects or similarly significant effects
regarding data subjects. However, the GDPR does not define the legal effect or the similarly
significant effect. According to the guideline issued by Art. 29 WP, only serious impactful
effects fall into the scope of Article 22(1) of the GDPR (Article 29 Working Party 2018). In

 9
the context of digital advertising, it is normally very difficult for individuals to prove that the
advertising produces the significant legal effect.

3.3. Critical issue of the uncertainty of personal data classification
The essential issue of data protection law in regulating digital advertising is the limited scope
of personal data. Compared with other traditional legislation (e.g., competition law or
consumer protection law), data protection law does not require a monopoly position or
consumer eligibility. As long as data processing involves personal data, data protection law
be applied. However, the classification of personal data is still ambiguous. As scholars argue,
online algorithmic pricing can use affinity-data to set prices (Li 2022: 7). By the same token,
such data can be used in digital advertising. Digital advertisers can use such data and
algorithm models to infer the likelihood of users’ interests and preferences rather than
tracking and targeting specific individuals. Furthermore, the current data protection concept
concentrates on the identification criteria. The identification standard indicates that only
certain data can identify or be identifiable to a specific person, the data protection law can
apply. However, in the context of digital advertising, as shown by several studies (Wachter
2020), advertisers can use affinity or de-identified data to infer more private information
about a group of users. In this circumstance, it is difficult to link such data with a specific
individual.

Furthermore, there is uncertainty between the CJEU’s precedents and the Art. 29 Working
Party’s guideline regarding personal data classification. According to Art. 29 WP, the data is
likely to impact certain personal rights and interests, meaning it should be treated as personal
data (Article 29 Working Party 2007). Namely, if the data cannot be directly traced back to
an identifiable person but can impact upon individuals’ rights or interests, then it can be

 10
categorized as personal data. However, However, the jurisprudence of the CJEU does not
fully support the guideline proposed by Art. 29 WP. For example, in YS, M and S case,
(Cases C–141 & 372/12, YS, M and S v. Minister voor Immigratie, Integratie en Asiel 2014)
both the court and the Advocate General of the CJEU believed that the data contained in a
legal document cannot be regarded as personal data. The judge exemplifies that only data that
contain a prominent link with a specific individual can be treated as personal data, such as
name, date of birth, nationality, and gender. However, the CJEU’s precedents are not always
consistent. By contrast, in the case of Nowak case, the court states that assessments,
comments, and the evaluation of data subjects can impact upon a person’s private life, which
constitutes personal data. Therefore, in digital advertising, it is still uncertain under what
circumstances data protection law may apply. The diverse mechanisms in the GDPR are
likely to be nugatory.

4. Current contributions and research
In response to the above critical issues and challenges, the EU’s regulatory paradigm for
digital advertising has recently shifted. The current main regulatory toolkit has not only
limited to the data protection law. With the final approval of the Digital Service Act (DSA)
and Digital Market Act (DMA) by the European Council, both DSA and DMA will regulate
digital advertising in a new manner and supplement the GDPR. Meanwhile, for AI-based
digital advertising, Artificial Intelligence Act (AIA) will also bring a new paradigm for
governance. Starting with AIA, the proposed new Act is not just limited to personal data-
based AI but is beyond the data. While AIA enlarges the regulatory scope, it is still difficult
to cover digital advertising. AIA categorizes artificial intelligence-based services into
different risks. AIA prohibits unacceptable AI risks. Such unacceptable risks compromise

 11
subliminal techniques and manipulative techniques that cause (or are likely to cause) physical
or psychological harm.

As listed in Annex III of AIA, high-risk AI mainly involves AI-based critical infrastructures,
biometric systems and Educational and employment systems. Only if digital advertising is
provided by chatbots or synthetically generated by “deep fake” techniques, such advertising
will be categorized as limited risks by AIA. Under this category, advertisers are required to
undertake transparency obligations. In other words, advertisers must inform users that they
are interacting with an AI system or that the information is provided by an AI system.
However, only transparency obligation is insufficient since individuals still cannot get rid of
digital advertising. Furthermore, the definition of transparency is absent in the proposal of
AIA. The extent to which limited risk AI systems should be transparent is unclear, which
causes further legal compliance difficulty in practice. While AIA is still at the proposal stage,
it is necessary for legislators to reconsider its scope and requirement.

Another EU regulatory paradigm shift is the combination of the competition law approach in
the regulation of digital advertising. It is represented by the DSA and DMA, where both new
Acts strengthen the obligations of large enterprises and platforms. The DSA can cover any
type of digital advertising, including digital marketing, political ads and social media
advertising etc. The DSA introduces two new rules concerning targeted advertising on online
platforms to complement existing rules, such as the GDPR. Firstly, it prohibits digital
advertising based on profiling to target minors (Article 28 (2), DSA). Secondly, it prohibits
digital advertising based on the processing of special categories of personal data, such as
ethnicity, sexual orientation or religious beliefs. (Article 26 (3), DSA). Furthermore, the DSA
also supplement the GDPR concerning the application of dark pattern in consent obtaining

 12
aspect. The DSA clearly bans the use of dark patterns on the interface of online platforms,
which has a high likelihood of misleading and manipulating users into choices that they do
not intend to make. As a result, when advertisers require individuals’ consent for digital
advertising, the dark pattern trick cannot be used anymore.

The DSA has strengthened the individuals’ power to understand and make informed
decisions about the digital advertising they see. Not only the above two restrictions on digital
advertising, but the DSA also stipulates new transparency obligations. DSA requires that
digital advertisers should clearly inform the individual concerning whether and why
individuals are targeted by each digital advertising and who paid for the advertising;
individuals should also clearly see when content is commercially sponsored or organically
posted on a platform. Regarding illegal advertising, platforms have an obligation to notice
and take action. Undoubtedly, the DSA further regulates digital advertising by imposing
stricter obligations on large online platforms. Although the DSA has not yet come into effect,
some of the problems with the regulation of online advertising have already been exposed.
For example, while the DSA clearly prohibits digital advertising that targets minors, how to
implement and how to make the platform compliant are the utmost thorny questions. On the
one hand, the platform needs to assess whether the users are minors, which is likely to cause
further data misuse. Once there is a data breach, it may bring more harm to minor users. On
the other hand, the DSA indeed mentions data minimization. However, it is particularly
challenging to make the platform comply with data minimization, but also to identify the age
of the user efficiently and securely. Thus, compliance with the DSA is another daunting task.

Furthermore, while the DSA bans the processing of special categories data for advertising
purposes, it neglects that the non-special category data also can infer sensitive information

 13
about the data subject. For example, advertisers do not need census information or passport
information to know if a person is from East Asia, their shopping history or social media data
can indicate this as well. If someone buys mooncakes and celebrates Chinese New Year,
there is a high probability of inferring their ethnicity. As a result, digital advertising can still
be related to some sensitive attributes of certain groups of individuals. Moreover, many
toolkits in the DSA (e.g., risk assessment, independent audit) are very useful, but these
obligations only apply to very large online platforms and very large online search engines.
However, digital advertising on other platforms does not require undertaking these duties.
Therefore, it remains challenging as to how other small and medium-sized online businesses
can mitigate the risks caused by digital advertising in advance.

5. Main research methods
There are several mainstream research methods used to explore the area of digital advertising
regulation. The most important and common research method is legal doctrinal research,
which is usually employed by legal researchers (Peczenik 2001). The legal doctrinal method
refers to the method that involves the analysis and interpretation of laws, regulations, legal
precedents and scholarships in the field of digital advertising. By doing so, it aims to
understand the legal framework and the principles that underlie the regulation of digital
advertising and examine the consistency of legislation in different scenarios. Because of its
aims, this method often can identify potential gaps and inconsistencies in the law and
regulation. In addition, legal doctrinal analysis can provide future recommendations for how
the law should be amended or interpreted. For example, the above analysis examines the
application of data protection law to digital advertising, which points out the potential issues
of the current regulatory paradigm.

 14
The second research method is an empirical method. The main aim of empirical research is to
investigate the difference between “law in books” and “law in action.” (Halperin 2011)
Although the legal doctrinal method can analyze the consistency and interpretations in theory
(aka, law in books), it is also essential to understand issues in legal practice (law in action).
Such a method can help examine the effectiveness of legislation and regulations in practice.
Keeping this aim in mind, the empirical method usually employs surveys, interviews, or
social experiments to comprehend how data protection laws are being implemented and their
effect in the context of digital advertising. Furthermore, some researchers also use this
method from an interdisciplinary perspective. It involves different perspectives from other
disciplines to understand the broader implications of data protection laws. For example, in the
context of digital advertising, researchers use knowledge from computer science, sociology,
or psychology to ascertain the regulatory issues of data protection law (Margoni 2021).
However, the drawbacks of this method are also manifested, including the strict requirement
of research design and data collection, sampling, data quality and generalization of results.

6. Recommendations for future directions
As discussed above, while the EU has shifted its regulatory paradigm in regulating digital
advertising, it is still necessary to ascertain the effectiveness of the new legislations such as
AIA, DSA and DMA. Future works can contribute to this area from two aspects. Firstly, AIA
only imposes transparency obligations for limited risk and requires voluntary codes of
conduct for minimal-risk AI. However, except in certain cases, AI-based digital advertising
can hardly be classified as a high-risk AI application. Therefore, it is necessary for future
research to theoretically explore whether the AIA has sufficient toolkits to govern the risks
posed by digital advertising. Secondly, more empirical research is desired to investigate the
effect of the DSA in the context of digital advertising. As aforementioned, compliance with

 15
the DSA is challenging for platforms. Therefore, the effect of the prohibition of certain types
of digital advertising is worthy of further scrutiny.
