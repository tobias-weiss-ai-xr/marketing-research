Background: North Sumatra possesses diverse forms of local wisdom embedded in
Malay, Batak Toba, Karo, Chinese, Indian/Tamil, and other communities. These
cultural assets have economic pote ntial through tourism, culinary businesses, crafts,
traditional clothing, performances, and community-based creative industries. However,
their contribution to employment creation depends on whether cultural assets can be
transformed into sustainable value chains supported by capable community institutions,
inclusive governance, market access, responsible financing, and protection of cultural
and environmental assets.
Research Gap: Previous studies have separately addressed local wisdom and creative
industries, community empowerment and local development, tourism -based
community participation, and Islamic social finance. However, limited attention has
been given to an integrated framework linking local wisdom, community empowerment,
employment pathways, reli gious moderation, Islamic economic values, practical
institutional mechanisms, and multidimensional sustainability in North Sumatra.
Novelty: This study offers an integrated analytical framework connecting local wisdom
assets, community empowerment, creati ve economy value chains, employment
diversification, religious moderation as governance mechanism, Islamic economic
principles (maslahah, 'adl, ta'awun, tawazun), Islamic social finance instruments, and
sustainable livelihood principles in a single coherent model for North Sumatra.
Method: This study uses a qualitative descriptive document-analysis approach based on
scholarly literature, institutional documentation, and prior studies concerning local
wisdom and community-based economic development in North Sumatra. The materials
are analyzed through data reduction, thematic classification, cross -case comparison,
interpretation, and synthesis.
Results: The findings show that local wisdom can generate employment through
interconnected activities such as cultur al guiding, performing arts, craft production,
culinary microenterprises, festival services, digital marketing, homestays, and agro -
cultural tourism. Evidence from Pematang Johar rice -field tourism and Maimun Palace
illustrates how locally rooted attractio ns can create space for farmers, traders,
microenterprises, guides, and service providers. Five priority strategies are identified:
skills and digital market access; institutional collaboration and business incubation;
inclusive multiethnic governance grou nded in religious moderation; Islamic social
finance and halal-livelihood support; and cultural, environmental, and climate resilience.
Conclusion: Maslahah, 'adl, ta'awun, tawazun, and work as ibadah provide normative
principles for broad benefit, fair ac cess, cooperation, balance, and ethical production.

INTRODUCTION
Local wisdom develops through the long -term interaction of communities with their social,
cultural, and natural environments. It is expressed through values, traditions, knowledge, arts, customary
institutions, food, clothing, rituals, and ways of organizing community life. Because these practices are
embedded in collective identity, local wisdom is not only a cultural resource but also a form of social
capital that can support community empowerment and locally rooted economic development. When
development strategies bu ild on community capabilities rather than replacing them with externally
imposed models, local institutions can become more active in mobilizing resources, organizing
production, and distributing benefits (Coy et al., 2022; Raj et al., 2022).
North Sumatra provides a strong context for this discussion because its population is culturally
diverse and its local wisdom is expressed through Malay, Batak Toba, Karo, Chinese, Indian/Tamil, and
other traditions. Cultural performances, ulos and other crafts, tradit ional culinary products, historical
sites, religious -cultural festivals, and rural landscapes create a broad base for tourism and creative -
economy activities. Such assets can become sources of employment when they are connected to concrete
value chains: production, packaging, guiding, event management, transportation, accommodation, food
services, retail, digital promotion, and other supporting services. Previous studies show that local tourism
and community -based creative industries can improve local econo mic participation and create new
livelihood opportunities (Adebayo & Butcher, 2022; Azizah & Muhfiatun, 2018).
Nevertheless, the existence of cultural assets does not automatically reduce unemployment. A
knowledge-to-market and asset -to-employment gap rema ins. Communities may possess valuable
traditions but lack product development skills, digital access, financing, destination management, market
networks, or institutional coordination. In addition, cultural commercialization can generate unequal
benefits or weaken cultural meaning if it is not governed carefully. Thus, the key analytical question is
not merely what local wisdom exists, but how specific local assets can be transformed into economically
viable activities while maintaining community ownership and sustainability.
This issue is especially important in a plural region such as North Sumatra. Religious moderation—
understood as tolerance, inclusiveness, fairness, and peaceful coexistence —can function as a social
governance condition for economic coop eration across ethnic and religious groups. It can reduce
exclusion, strengthen trust, widen participation, and help community institutions manage shared
cultural-economic spaces. The perspective is also compatible with Islamic economics. Maslahah (public
benefit), 'adl (justice), ta'awun (mutual cooperation), tawazun (balance), and productive work as ibadah
emphasize that development should improve human well-being, distribute opportunities fairly, organize
cooperation ethically, balance economic goals wit h cultural and environmental responsibilities, and
protect lawful livelihoods (Chapra, 2008; Dusuki & Abdullah, 2007). Islamic social-finance instruments
can complement these principles by supporting eligible beneficiaries, shared productive assets, and
community institutions when their use follows appropriate governance and Sharia requirements (Sadeq,
2002; Salim et al., 2024).
Previous studies have separately addressed local wisdom and creative industries (Azizah &
Muhfiatun, 2018), community empowerment and local development (Coy et al., 2022; Martati et al.,
2013), tourism-based community participation (Adebayo & Butcher, 2022), local wisdom as a basis for
Islamic economic development (Kurnia & Putri, 2018), Islamic social finance for productive

Local Wisdom and Community Empowerment
Local wisdom refers to knowledge, values, traditions, and cultural practices developed by
communities through long-term interaction with their environment. Beyond preserving cultural identity,
it can function as social and economic capital when c ommunities retain meaningful participation in
deciding how cultural resources are used. Empowerment in this context means improving the capacity
of individuals and community institutions to identify assets, organize production, access markets,
negotiate with external stakeholders, and retain a fair share of the benefits. This perspective is consistent
with community -empowerment research emphasizing participation, institutional capacity, and local
ownership as foundations for sustainable development (Coy et al., 2022; Zikargae et al., 2022).
Creative Economy, Local Economic Development, and Employment Creation
Local economic development seeks to mobilize resources that are specific to a place and convert
them into productive activities. Local wisdom provides inputs for creative industries such as crafts,
culinary products, traditional clothing, cultural performances, heritage tourism, festivals, and
community-based nature tourism. The employment impact may be direct, for example artisans,
performers, guides, f ood vendors, and homestay operators, or indirect through transport, logistics,
packaging, digital promotion, and supply services. Martati et al. (2013) show that local economic
development can support job creation through productive business activity, while Adebayo and Butcher
(2022) underline the importance of community participation in ensuring that tourism generates local
benefits rather than merely external revenue.
Religious Moderation as an Enabling Condition for Local Economic Cooperation
Religious m oderation is relevant to local economic development because plural communities
require social trust and rules of cooperation. In a multiethnic setting, tolerance and inclusive participation
can lower social barriers to joint events, tourism routes, market spaces, business associations, and cultural
promotion. Religious moderation should therefore not be treated as an isolated normative theme. In
this study it is conceptualized as a cross-cutting governance mechanism that supports equal participation,
reduces the risk of exclusion and conflict, and enables collaboration among community organizations,
local government, business actors, and cultural or religious institutions.
Islamic Economics: Maslahah, 'Adl, Ta'awun, Tawazun, Islamic Social Finance, and
Halal Livelihoods
Islamic economics evaluates development not only by the amount of income generated but also
by the quality, legitimacy, and distribution of well-being. The maqasid al-Shariah orientation emphasizes
human welfare and the prevention of harm, whi le maslahah directs economic activity toward public
benefit (Chapra, 2008; Dusuki & Abdullah, 2007). In local -wisdom-based empowerment, 'adl requires
fair access to business opportunities and benefits; ta'awun frames mutual cooperation among
communities an d institutions; tawazun requires balance between commercial growth, cultural
preservation, and environmental carrying capacity; and work as ibadah frames lawful and productive
work as an activity with ethical and social responsibility. A halal -livelihood orientation further requires
products, financing arrangements, market practices, and service delivery to remain lawful, transparent,
non-exploitative, and socially responsible. Islamic social -finance instruments may support these goals:
productive zakat can  assist eligible mustahiq with capability -building or productive assets, infaq can
provide flexible support, and waqf can finance durable shared assets or facilities while preserving the
endowed principal under proper governance (Sadeq, 2002; Salim et al., 2024). These instruments should

Sustainable Livelihoods and Multidimensional Sustainability
A sustainable-livelihood perspective shifts attention from short-term income generation to whether
communities can maintain and improve their livelihood resources over time. Scoones (1998) emphasizes
the interaction of livelihood resources, strategies, institutions, and outcomes; in the present context,
local wisdom and cultural identity are treated as context -specific assets that should be strengthened
rather than depleted. Accordingly, local -wisdom-based development should be assessed across
economic sustainability (stable and diversified income), social sustaina bility (participation, equity, and
cohesion), cultural sustainability (authenticity, intergenerational transmission, and community control),
and environmental sustainability (resource conservation, waste management, carrying capacity, and
protection of natural attractions). Sustainable community-based tourism research in Indonesia likewise
shows that community participation, local leadership, and socio -ecological considerations are essential
to long-term tourism viability (Priatmoko et al., 2021). Climate variability and environmental degradation
can threaten agro -cultural tourism, rural attractions, and resource -based livelihoods; therefore,
adaptation measures such as livelihood diversification, climate -sensitive event planning, water and
landscape protection, emergency preparedness, and ecological monitoring should be integrated into
empowerment strategies.
Analytical Framework and Research Gap
The analytical logic used in this study is: local -wisdom assets → community capability and
institutional empowerm ent → creative-economy value chains → employment and livelihood
diversification. Religious moderation and Islamic economic principles operate across this sequence as
governance and ethical safeguards, while Islamic social finance can function as a targeted  enabling
resource and sustainable-livelihood principles provide criteria for long -term economic, social, cultural,
and environmental viability. This framework addresses the gap in previous research by examining not
only cultural potential but also the mec hanisms through which cultural assets may be converted into
employment opportunities, the barriers that interrupt that conversion, the actors and resources required
for implementation, and the safeguards needed to prevent economic gains from undermining cu ltural
identity, social inclusion, or ecological resilience.
METHOD
This study uses a qualitative descriptive approach with document analysis and literature synthesis.
This methodological clarification reflects the actual evidence base of the study: the an alysis relies on
secondary materials rather than interviews, surveys, or direct participant observation. Document analysis
is appropriate for examining social and institutional phenomena through systematically selected written
materials, while literature r eview methods enable the synthesis of findings across studies and contexts
(Bowen, 2009; Snyder, 2019).
The unit of analysis is the relationship among local -wisdom assets, community empowerment,
employment opportunities, religious moderation, and Islamic e conomic values in North Sumatra.
Documentary cases include cultural assets and destinations discussed in the manuscript and supporting
literature, including Malay cultural heritage associated with Maimun Palace, the multiethnic cultural
landscape of Kampun g Madras, community traditions among Batak Toba, Karo, Chinese, and
Indian/Tamil groups, and Pematang Johar rice -field tourism. These cases are used analytically to
illustrate different ways in which cultural, historical, and rural assets may enter creativ e-economy value
chains.
Sources were selected purposively based on their relevance to one or more analytical themes: local
wisdom, community empowerment, creative economy, tourism, employment creation, religious
moderation, Islamic economics, Islamic socia l finance, sustainable livelihoods, environmental
sustainability, and North Sumatra. Priority was given to peer-reviewed studies and credible institutional
or scholarly documents that provided conceptual explanations, empirical examples, or policy -relevant
evidence. Duplicate references were removed and sources were compared to avoid relying on a single
type of document for major interpretive claims.

Analysis proceeded through five stages. First, relevant statements were extracted from the selected
documents. Second, the data were reduced and coded into thematic categories: (a) local-wisdom assets;
(b) economic value -chain opportunities; (c) employment and livelihood pathways; (d) barriers,
implementation resources, and enabling conditions; (e) normative-governance and Islamic social-finance
principles; and (f) social, cultural, environmental, and climate-resilience considerations. Third, cases were
compared across ethnic and economic contexts. Fourth, the findings were interpreted using community
empowerment, religious moderation, Islamic economics, and sustainable-livelihood perspectives. Fifth,
the results were synthesized into targeted strategies linking specific barriers with actors, financing
options, institutional mechanisms, sustainability safeguards, and measurable indicators. Because no
primary field data were collected, the findings should be interpreted as analytical and policy propositions
rather than causal estimates of unemployment reduction. Future research should validate the proposed
pathways through interviews, community-level observations, enterprise data, environmental indicators,
and employment outcomes.
RESULTS
This section presents the findings of the document analysis. It first maps the local -wisdom assets
and institutional foundations identified across North Sumatra and then shows how those assets can be
translated into concrete employment pathways through creative-economy and community-based value
chains. The results are supported by the comparative cases and transformation matrix presented below.
Mapping Local-Wisdom Assets and Institutional Foundations
The document analysis confirms that North Sumatra has a diverse portfolio of local-wisdom assets
that can support multiple segments of the creative economy. The significance of this diversity is not
simply the number of traditions identified, but the range of economic activities that can be built around
them. Malay cultural heritage can support heritage tourism, performance, culinary businesses, traditional
clothing, and interpretation services. Batak Toba traditions can support ulos production, music, dance,
cultural guiding, and event-based tourism. Karo cultural expressions can be linked with performances,
agro-cultural tourism, culinary products, and local events. Chinese and Indian/Tamil cultural traditions
can support festival economies, culinary enterprises, retail, guiding, and intercultural tourism. Existing
community and cultural organizations provide an institutional entry point for organizing these activities.

contributes to employment when it is converted into  a structured value chain with community

mediated outcome rather than an automatic consequence of cultural diversity.
DISCUSSION
The discussion interprets the empirical patterns identified in the Results section through the lenses
of religious moderation, Islamic economics, community empowerment, and sustainable livelihoods. It
develops the implications of the findings into context -specific empowerment strat egies, policy
interventions, stakeholder responsibilities, and sustainability safeguards for North Sumatra.
Religious Moderation and Islamic Economics as Economic Governance
The role of religious moderation becomes clearer when it is linked to how local economic
opportunities are governed. North Sumatra's cultural economy involves traditions that are associated
with different ethnic and religious communities. Without inclusive governance, cultural events and
tourism development may generate competition ov er representation, access to selling space, public
funding, or control of destinations. Religious moderation can reduce this risk through recognition of
diversity, equal citizenship, dialogue, and shared rules for participation. In economic terms, it strengthens
social trust and lowers barriers to cooperation among cultural associations, MSMEs, government
agencies, tourism actors, and visitors.
QS. Al -Hujurat: 13 provides a normative foundation for recognizing diversity and mutual
understanding: humanity is created into peoples and tribes so that people may know one another. In the
context of this study, the verse supports a shift from merely tolerating diversity toward using diversity
as a basis for constructive cooperation. This logic is reinforced by the principle of ta'awun, reflected in
QS. Al-Ma'idah: 2, which encourages cooperation in what is good and socially responsible. A multiethnic
tourism route, an inclusive festival calendar, shared market space, cooperative production network, or
cross-community creative-economy forum can translate these principles into institutional practice.
The Islamic economic perspective further specifies the quality of the desired development.
Maslahah requires that the use of local wisdom generate broad community benefit rather than narrow
private gain. 'Adl requires transparent access to vendor spaces, training, financing, and partnerships,
including for small businesses and less powerful community groups. Ta'awun supports structured
cooperation among community organizati ons, government, businesses, religious institutions,
universities, and financial actors. Tawazun requires a balance between commercialization and the
preservation of cultural meaning, as well as between tourism growth and environmental carrying
capacity. Work as ibadah and a halal-livelihood orientation reinforce dignity, lawful production, honest
transactions, safe products and services, and responsibility toward workers, consumers, communities,
and the environment. These principles are consistent with the maqasid-oriented view that development
should advance multidimensional human well -being and prevent harm (Chapra, 2008; Dusuki &
Abdullah, 2007).
Accordingly, religious moderation and Islamic economics should not be placed in a separate
symbolic section. They operate as cross -cutting standards for designing empowerment programs,
selecting beneficiaries, organizing ta'awun -based partnerships, managing cultural representation,
distributing economic opportunities, structuring Sharia -compliant financing, and e valuating whether

Context-Specific Community Empowerment Strategy
a.  Skills, Product Development, and Digital Market Access.
Communities need practical capabilities to convert cultural assets into marketable products without
weakening authenticity. Priority activities include product-design training, storytelling and guiding skills,
hospitality, packaging, photography, bo okkeeping, digital marketing, and e -commerce. For ulos,
traditional clothing, culinary products, and souvenirs, digital channels can widen the market beyond
destination visitors. For performances and festivals, digital content can support promotion and
documentation. Government agencies and universities can provide training, while private platforms and
tourism businesses can support market access and mentoring.
b.  Institutional Collaboration and Business Incubation
The role of community organizations such as HITPAM, MABMI, MITSU, PSMTI, PHDI,
customary bodies, village institutions, and tourism groups should extend from cultural preservation to
economic coordination where appropriate. Provincial and district governments can facilitate cluster -
based forums that connect these institutions with cooperatives, universities, tourism businesses, digital
platforms, and CSR programs. Support should include business incubation, legal registration, quality
certification, mentoring, common marketing, and access to shared facilities. Each assisted cluster should
have a simple business plan, named institutional coordinator, target market, implementation timetable,
and measurable employment and sustainability outcomes rather than relying on one-off events.
c. Inclusive Multiethnic Governance Based on Religious Moderation
Empowerment programs should use transparent criteria for participation and avoid giving
economic access only to dominant groups or established actors. Event committees, destination
managers, incubation programs, and public market initiatives should include community representation
and transparent allocation of vendor, training, financing, or performance opportunities. Codes of
conduct for cultural representation can help prevent stereotypes and disrespect. Thi s strategy
operationalizes religious moderation, 'adl, and ta'awun as economic -governance practices that protect
equal participation while strengthening cooperation across communities.
d.  Islamic Social Finance and Halal-Livelihood Support
Islamic social finance can provide a targeted funding layer for empowerment, especially for
vulnerable or low-capital participants, but it should be linked to clear eligibility, governance, mentoring,
and outcome monitoring. Productive zakat may support eligible mustahiq  through tools, training, or
productive activities; infaq can support flexible training, promotion, or emergency needs; and productive
waqf can support shared facilities, training spaces, market infrastructure, or other durable community
assets where the principal is protected and management is entrusted to competent nazhir (Sadeq, 2002;
Salim et al., 2024). Local zakat and waqf institutions, Islamic financial institutions, mosques or religious
foundations, and community organizations can collaborate with l ocal government and incubators to
identify beneficiaries, assess viable enterprises, and prevent grant dependency. Financing should also
reinforce halal livelihoods through lawful products and services, fair contracts, transparent pricing,
worker dignity, and responsible market conduct.
e.  Cultural, Environmental, and Climate Sustainability
Local wisdom loses its economic foundation if cultural meaning is diluted or natural assets are
degraded. Community institutions should define which cultural elements may be commercialized, how
they should be presented, and how a share of benefits can support cultural transmission and
conservation. Tourism sites should apply visitor -management, waste-control, water-management, and
environmental carrying-capacity principles, while craft and culinary clusters should reduce avoidable
resource waste and protect locally important materials. Climate and weather risks should be incorporated
into destination and livelihood planning through seasonal diversification, climate -sensitive event

Barriers and Policy Interventions
The analysis identifies six recurring barriers. First, institutional fragmentation can separate cultural
preservation from business development; the policy response is a local creative -economy coordination
forum linking cultural institutions, tourism and MSME agencies, planning bodies, village governments,
finance providers, and private partners. Second, limited business and digital skills can prevent cultural
products from reaching wider markets; the respons e is continuous training combined with mentoring
and market targets. Third, weak financing and business formalization can limit scaling; the response is a
financing ladder that combines self -financing, cooperatives, appropriate Islamic microfinance,
productive zakat for eligible beneficiaries, infaq support, productive waqf for shared assets, and CSR or
public programs according to the type of need and applicable rules.
Fourth, commercialization may create cultural dilution, imitation, or environmental pressure. This
requires community-defined cultural standards, provenance or branding systems, intellectual -property
awareness, benefit -sharing arrangements, waste and resource -management rules, and destination
carrying-capacity controls. Fifth, unequal access and intergroup sensitivity may reduce trust; transparent
beneficiary criteria, inclusive committees, conflict -sensitive event management, and religious -
moderation principles can help ensure that local economic development becomes a shared project rather
than a source of competition. Sixth, climate variability and environmental degradation may reduce visitor
flows, disrupt agricultural attractions, damage natural assets, or make seasonal livelihoods less reliable.
Adaptation therefore requires risk mapping, diversified products and income sources, climate-sensitive
scheduling, conservation measures, and contingency plans.
For implementation, provincial and district governments should move from general support to a
cluster-based portfolio approach. Each cultural or tourism cluster should have: (1) a mapped asset and
stakeholder base; (2) a defined value chain and target market; (3) an institutional coordinator; (4) a
training, incubation, and financing plan; (5) an inclusion, halal -livelihood, cultural, environmental, and
climate-resilience standard; and (6) a monitoring dashboard. Suggested indicators include the number of
active local enterprises, new or sustained jobs, youth and women's participation, sales growth, digital -
market reach, financing mobilized, n umber of collaborative products or events, the proportion of
benefits retained locally, beneficiary diversity, cultural -conservation activities, and basic environmental
indicators. Such indicators would allow future studies to test whether the proposed str ategies are
associated with reductions in unemployment and improvements in sustainable livelihoods.

Implementation should begin with a limited number of pilot clusters representing different asset
types—for example heritage, crafts, festivals, and agro -cultural tourism—so that resource needs and
coordination problems can be learned before wider replication. The main implementation risks are
insufficient local budgets, limited institutional capacity, weak data, stakeholder resistance, fragmented
authority, capture by established actors, and dependence on short -term grants. These risks can be
reduced through phased pilots, transparent selection criteria, named institutional coordinators, co -
financing, public reporting, community grievance channels, and annual evaluation against employment,
inclusion, business, cultural, and environmental indicators.
Sustainable Livelihoods, Social Cohesion, and Climate Resilience
The sustainable-livelihood lens clarifies that employment creation is insufficient if new jobs depend
on t he degradation of the resources that sustain them. Local -wisdom-based enterprises should
strengthen human capabilities, social networks, financial security, and natural and cultural assets while
reducing vulnerability to shocks (Scoones, 1998). Economicall y, this means income diversification,
stronger local value retention, and reduced dependence on a single event or visitor season. Socially, it
means fair access for youth, women, small producers, and minority groups, with participation in
decisions and tra nsparent benefit distribution. Culturally, it requires community authority over
representation, provenance, intergenerational knowledge transfer, and safeguards against excessive
commodification. Environmentally, it requires protection of landscapes and he ritage sites, visitor
carrying-capacity management, waste and water controls, and conservation -oriented use of local
resources.
Religious moderation contributes to sustainability by supporting trust, tolerance, coexistence, and
fair participation across No rth Sumatra's diverse communities. These social conditions reduce the
likelihood that competition over tourism space, public support, or cultural representation becomes
conflict, thereby strengthening long -term institutional stability. Islamic economic pri nciples add an
ethical dimension: maslahah directs benefits toward collective welfare, 'adl protects fair access, ta'awun
supports shared responsibility, tawazun requires economic -ecological-cultural balance, and work as
ibadah promotes lawful and dignifie d livelihoods. In this sense, religious moderation and Islamic
economics are not only normative additions but mechanisms for social sustainability and accountable
economic governance.
Climate change and environmental degradation add a further vulnerability  layer, especially for
agriculture-linked attractions, rural tourism, and natural landscapes. Adaptation should therefore be
practical and local: diversify products across seasons, avoid overdependence on climate -sensitive

CONCLUSION
North Sumatra's local wisdom has substantial potential to support employment and livelihood
diversification through creative industries, heritage tourism, cultural performances, crafts, culinary
businesses, festivals, and agro -cultural tourism. The main contribution of this study is to show that
cultural potential must be translated into an employment and sustainable-livelihood pathway rather than
treated as an inventory. The pathway runs from local assets to product and service development, market
connection, community institutions, appropriate financing, and scalable value chains, while being
constrained by requirements for inclusion, cultural integrity, environmental protection, and climate
resilience. The Pematang Johar and Maimun Palace cases illustrate both the opportunities and the
management challenges involved in this transformation.
Five strategies are prioritized: strengthening skills, product development, and digital-market access;
building institutional collaboration and business incubation; establishing inclusive multiethnic
governance based on religious moderation; using Islamic social finance and Sharia -compliant
mechanisms as targeted support for eligible and viable empowerment activities; and protecting cultural,
environmental, and climate resilience. Religious moderation supports economic development by
strengthening trust, equal participation, and cooperation across diverse communities. Islamic economic
principles deepen this approach: maslahah directs development toward broad benefit, 'adl toward fair
opportunity and distribution, ta'awun toward mutual cooperation, tawazun toward balance, and work as
ibadah toward ethical and dignified productive activity. Zakat, infaq, and waqf can strengthen
empowerment when they are governed appropriately and linked to capability building, viable livelihood
pathways, and measurable outcomes.
Because this research is based on document analysis and secondary literature, it does not
demonstrate a causal decline in North Sumatra's unemployment rate or directly measure the
effectiveness of the proposed financing and sustainability mechanisms. Its findings should therefore be
understood as context -specific analytical propositions and policy directions. Future research should
conduct field interviews with community organizations, local government, Islamic social -finance
institutions, and business actors; o bserve selected cultural and tourism clusters; collect MSME,
employment, inclusion, and environmental data; and evaluate whether the proposed model produces
measurable changes in job creation, income diversification, social cohesion, cultural continuity, a nd
livelihood resilience.
