Abstract: Social media influencers (SMIs) have now evolved as a pivotal player in digital marketing but the
influence on consumer trust and buying behavior is only partially understood. This study focuses on the
relationship between perceived engagement and authenticity of influencers and their trust and through trust,
purchase behaviour, based on the theories of Source Credibility Theory and Parasocial Interaction Theory. The
data collected were analysed in SPSS with reliability analysis, Pearson correlation, and three simple linear
regressions, as well as an approximate indirect-effect test, using 81 active social media users aged between 18
and 45. Purchase behaviour was significantly predicted by trust, and an approximate Sobel test showed that
part of the association between engagement and purchase behaviour was explained by the association between
engagement and trust, suggesting that engagement affected purchase behaviour directly and indirectly via trust,
in a dual-pathway model.Trust significantly predicted purchase behaviour and engagement and authenticity
strongly predicted both trust and purchase behaviour, as an approximate Sobel test indicated that engagement
was a direct influence on purchase behaviour, and an indirect influence via trust. Interestingly, the mean score
for all three constructs was below the scale mid-line, indicating that even active users are still wary of the
content from influencers. The study provides a consumer-focused perspective on digital persuasion, and
managerial recommendations for establishing trust in the context of an integrated, collaborative approach that
is authentic and transparent, while recognizing the constraints of a small convenience sample and correlational
design.

Introduction
The digital revolution of communications has changed the way consumers interact with brands. Social media
has become the main driver of public opinion, lifestyles, and buying habits. As of 2024, there were around
4.95 billion social media users globally, highlighting the behavioural reach of social media (Statista, 2024).
As a result, social media influencers (SMIs) have gained increased visibility and become key influencers in
guiding consumer attitudes, building brands, and influencing purchase decisions, especially through
customised content (Khurana, Pannu & Dalal, 2024; Shen, Nie & Tong, 2024).
The reason why SMIs are perceived as more trustworthy than traditional endorsers is that influencers
seem to be more authentic and establish proximity through direct communication and sharing (Tian & Li,
2022; Lou & Yuan, 2020). SMIs have a significant number of followers as well as the ability to establish a
parasocial relationship with viewers (Horton & Wohl, 1956). Such emotional association has been linked to

Despite the practical prominence of influencer marketing, academic debate persists regarding its

integrate authenticity , engagement quality , and perceived credibility within a single behavioural framework
(Sokolova & Perez, 2021). This study addresses that gap by examining how perceived engagement and
authenticity relate to consumer trust and, through it, to purchase behaviour.

Research Aim and Objectives
The aim of this study is to assess how social media influencers relate to consumer trust and purchasing
decisions, with particular attention to authenticity, engagement, and perceived credibility. The specific
objectives are:
§ To explore how consumers perceive and form trust in social media influencers;
§ To identify the key factors associated with consumer trust in influencer content;
§ To assess the extent to which trust in influencers relates to purchasing behaviour; and
§ To examine how influencer engagement and authenticity relate to both trust and purchase behaviour.

Research Questions
§ How do consumers perceive and form trust in social media influencers?
§ What are the most significant drivers of trust in influencer endorsements?
§ To what extent is consumer trust in influencers associated with purchase decisions?
§ How are authenticity and engagement related to the trust-purchase relationship?

Significance of the Study
The research has theoretical and practical significance. On a theoretical level, it is an extension of the Source
Credibility Model (Ohanian, 1990) and parasocial perspectives (Horton & Wohl, 1956) that brings consumer
behaviour and relational psychology together in the field of digital marketing. In the real world, it provides
guidance to marketers working with influencers on how they can go about finding the attributes that have
been deemed as most indicative of trust, namely expertise, engagement, and transparency. The results also
contribute to the ongoing debate on the ethics and regulation of influencers, a topic that is increasingly
relevant under current consumer protection laws.

Literature Review
Social media has become more than just a medium for communication in the past 10 years; it's been a key
part of the marketing mix. The social media influencers, people who have created an online persona to gain
loyal followers, are now impacting the public's beliefs and purchasing decisions (Ruangwanit & Thongmak,
2025). Research on the phenomenon has mushroomed in recent times, but there is a lack of literature that
looks at multiple variables or builds simpler models that do not reflect the complex relationship between
influencers and audience behavior. However, early research was based on classic celebrity-endorsement
theories like Source Credibility Theory that cannot adequately capture the nature of the relationship between
the influencer and the audience in an interactive digital context, and more recent research adopted the
Parasocial Interaction Theory proposed by Crespo and Tille (2023), which includes the concept of
interpersonal connection and audience psychology.

Theoretical Framework
This research draws on two complementary pillars: Source Credibility Theory (Ohanian, 1990) and Parasocial
Interaction (PSI) Theory (Horton & Wohl, 1956). Together they help explain consumer trust in influencers
and how that trust relates to behavioural outcomes, addressing both credibility-based persuasion and
relational engagement.

Source Credibility Theory: The Source Credibility Model assumes that the persuasiveness of the message is
a function of the audience's perception of the source's expertise, trustworthiness, and attractiveness (Ohanian,
1990). These dimensions are related to the product domain expertise, transparent and honest behavior, and
personal brand appeal of the influencer in the context of influencers. The dimensions in the influencer context
are related to the product domain expertise, transparent and honest behavior, and personal brand appeal of
the influencer. Various research studies have shown associations between credibility and trust and purchase:
Bevan-Dye (2024) reports that the most important correlate of purchase was perceived trustworthiness over
product quality when referring to partnerships that are clearly disclosed as being paid. Several studies have
been conducted that found associations between credibility and trust and purchase: Bevan -Dye (2024)
reported that the most important correlate of purchase was perceived trustworthiness over product quality
when it comes to partnerships clearly disclosed as paid. The model, however, has its limitations: platform
cultures and audience expectations are not addressed (Ghiassaleh et al., 2024), and the emotional and social
factors that often play a significant role in digital decisions are not taken into account (Schouten, Janssen &
Croes, 2021).

Parasocial Interaction Theory: The theory of emotionally charged, one-sided relationships between the
audience and a media personality is called Parasocial Interaction Theory (Horton & Wohl, 1956). Today,
consumers are creating strong relationships with influencers (Jin, Muqaddam & Ryu,  2021), and the
emotional trust fostered through these relationships makes consumers feel that recommendations are
authentic and relevant, greatly increasing the chance that they will follow the recommendation (Gaëlle
Ouvrein, 2024). While Source Credibility focuses on attributes, PSI focuses on feelings of connection, and
Horng et al. (2024) showed that parasocial connection was positive and that it led to increased purchases of
endorsed products even after the disclosure of the sponsorship. Despite this, PSI has been criticized as not
necessarily predictive but rather descriptive,e and strong attachments can lead to reliance on influencer
guidance (Kim, Kim & Lee, 2024; Park, Paeng & Shin, 2024).

Integrating the Frameworks: The Source Credibility + PSI Theory framework helps to look at trust through
two complementary lenses: a more cognitive perspective on influencer characteristics and a more affective
perspective on emotional attachments and perceived authenticity. Combining both provides a more complete
picture of the factors influencing the development of trust and the impact of trust on consumer decisions,
and directly feeds into the conceptual framework used in this paper. The cognitive, credibility -based
perspective in this study is operationalised by the Trust construct (perceived honesty, transparency and
expertise), and the affective, parasocial perspective is operationalised by the Engagement & Authenticity
construct (see Section 3.5).

Trust in Influencer Marketing: In digital environments, products are not physically checked; therefore,
consumer trust plays a pivotal role in determining behavioural intent towards the product (Zeqiri et al.,
2024). In the context of influencer marketing, trust manifests when the followers think that the influencer is
genuine, regular, knowledgeable, and acting in their best interest (Casaló et al., 2020), making it the factor
that can turn content views into customer reaction. The link between followers' trust and brand attitude and
purchase intention has been demonstrated repeatedly, but researchers do not agree about whether trust is a

Once identified as sponsored content by consumers, consumer trust levels plummeted significantly, as
discovered by Yahya and Çınar (2024), while hidden commercial intent was shown to generate perceptions

Additionally, the credibility of influencers also differs according to the type of influencer; Micro-influencers
often appear more credible than macro-influencers and celebrities, as they are perceived as more relatable
and knowledgeable on the topics they post about (Khurana, Pannu & Dalal, 2024; Chen et al., 2024), and
users also found them more trustworthy and engaging (Mulholland et al., 2025). While previous studies have
identified trust as part and parcel of successful influencers, the dynamic nature of trust (that is, between
influencers and followers) seems to have been understated, and is one aspect this study aims to highlight.

Authenticity and Engagement in Influencer Marketing
Consumers today rely on authenticity and engagement as key indicators to determine the credibility and
influence of an influencer (Qureshi, Shahzadi & Hussain, 2024). However, authenticity is challenging to
conceptualise as several scholars have described it as a “strategically managed performance” which aims to
be “spontaneous” but is “deliberately curated” (Gonçalves et al., 2024; Kunert, 2023); influencers manage to
gauge the level of self-disclosure to foster emotional connections (Harff & Schmuck, 2024). From an empirical
perspective, the trust and engagement of consumers is increased with the perception of authenticity, as
Pramesthi, Prasetyo, and Safitri (2024) showed. When the influencer presents both the strengths and
weaknesses of a product, it is perceived as more trustworthy, and Tran et al. (2024) found that the more the
content is congruent with the influencer's own self-image, the more authentic the product is perceived.
Engagement goes beyond likes and comments; it is about the quality of the relationship between the
influencer and the follower (Casaló et al., 2020). An active response to comments and the sharing of user-
generated content from influencers helps to strengthen the parasocial bond and can convey sincere intent,
which builds trust and loyalty (Shamim & Azam, 2024; Gaëlle Ouvrein, 2024). Del baere, Michael, and
Phillips (2020) found that the more emotionally engaged, the more trusted influencer recommendations are,
even without sponsorship. However, when influencers share content that is heavily commercialized or
collaborate with brands that don't align with their personal brand, audiences quickly pick up on the
inauthentic approach and lose trust in the content’s authenticity , which can lead to a reduction in engagement
and support (Guo et al., 2023; Qureshi, Shahzadi & Hussain, 2024). The dynamics are particularly strong
with younger viewers who are particularly sensitive to “fake authenticity” (Colucci & Pedroni, 2021).

Trust and Consumer Behaviour
Whether it's a matter of trust or a matter of buy is the ultimate test of influencer marketing. The relationship
between trust and consumers' favourable evaluation of endorsed products is also reported as positive, for
instance, by Lou and Yuan (2020), who demonstrated that consumers who trust an influencer were
significantly inclined to have a favourable evaluation of the endorsed products, and by Wang (2024), who
illustrated that trust acts as a mediator between attractiveness, perceived level of expertise and purchase
intention. Other research tempers the relationship and suggests that trust has an impact on brand attitude
but that brand purchase behaviour is also influenced by product relevance, price sensitivity, and familiarity
(Hughes et al., 2019), and that emotional trust can lead to brand purchase but is not sufficient for high-
involvement products (Wei et al., 2022). Such a gap between intention and behaviour has been criticized in
previous research related to attitude-behaviour models (Ajzen, 1991). Another moderator is influencer brand
fit, which is congruence between the image of the influencer and the product: the stronger the congruence
between trust and the influencer's image with the product, the stronger the association between trust and
follower behaviour (Tian & Li, 2022).

In the literature,ture three weaknesses are repeated. First, the variables of credibility, authenticity, and
engagement are often examined separately and not as a part of a combined system. Second, while some
studies consider and examine the cognitive dimension (Source Credibility) of influence, others focus on its
affective dimension (parasocial), with few studies examining both at once (Wei et al., 2022; Han & Balabanis,
2023). Third, methodological approaches generally use a very limited assessment of trust based on Likert
scales and do not incorporate correlation and regression calculations to establish a predictive pattern among
the constructs (Chu et al., 2020; Velazquez-Solis et al., 2023). This study extends a more comprehensive (but
preliminary) understanding of digital persuasion by employing a dual -theoretical, consumer-centred
framework, which considers engagement, authenticity, trust, and behaviour as related constructs.

Conceptual Framework and Hypotheses
Drawing on the integrated framework, the study positions trust in influencers as a central construct shaped
by perceived engagement and authenticity (transparency, self -disclosure, responsiveness, and content
congruence), which reflect the parasocial perspective. Trust, in turn, is expected to relate to purchase
behaviour, and engagement is hypothesised to relate to behaviour both indirectly (through trust) and directly.
The hypothesised relationships are shown in Figure 1 and tested through the following hypotheses:
§ H1: Trust in social media influencers significantly and positively predicts consumer purchase behaviour.
§ H2: Influencer engagement and authenticity significantly and positively predict consumer trust.
§ H3: Influencer engagement and authenticity significantly and positively predict consumer purchase
behaviour.

Methodology
Research Philosophy and Approach
The study adopts a positivist philosophy, assuming an objective reality measurable through systematic
observation, which suits hypothesis testing of predefined variable relationships (Saunders, Lewis & Thornhill,
2021). A deductive approach was followed, with hypotheses derived from Source Credibility Theory
(Ohanian, 1990) and Parasocial Interaction Theory (Horton & Wohl, 1956) and tested through quantitative
analysis (Bryman, 2022).

Research Design
A cross-sectional quantitative survey design was used to obtain information about the present situation of
consumers' responses. The design allows for efficient data collection from a large sample and testing of
correlational and predictive relationships between trust, authenticity, engagement, and purchase behaviour
without the complexity of longitudinal data (Creswell & Creswell, 2022). The psychological constructs were
standardised by the use of structured instruments, the replicability of which was ensured. This is a
correlational design and so cannot be used to draw inferences about causation, which is evident throughout
the results.

Population and Sampling
The study aimed to target social media users aged 18-45, a group that is considered highly active and
responsive to social media and influencer marketing strategies (Hudders & Lou, 2023; Ferraro et al., 2024).
The participants for the study were recruited using non-probability convenience sampling, which is widely
adopted in exploratory and behaviouial digital-marketing research studies because sampling frames are
generally unavailable (Saunders, Lewis & Thornhill, 2021). A total of 81 valid responses were obtained from
the study, which is more than is required for Pearson correlation and simple linear regression, and adequate
to achieve medium to large effect size detection (Hair & Alamer, 2022). Everyone said they follow influencers
and interact with influencer-recommended content.

Data Collection Procedure
A self-administered online questionnaire using Google Form was employed as a method of data collection,
due to its cost-effectiveness, scalability, and ability to take the survey to the target population in its natural
environment online (Evans & Mathur, 2018). The information about the study was distributed via university
email lists, social media groups and networking platforms, and accompanied by a brief introduction regarding
the study purpose, confidentiality and consent. The questionnaire was open for 14 days. No identifying data
were collected so that anonymity was maintained, and responses were combined into a secure data set to be
analysed using SPSS.

Measures and Instrument Validation
The instrument contained 24 closed-ended items, evenly spread over the demographic items and the three
constructs, each consisting of 6 items (see Appendix A). This was in line with Source Credibility Theory,
where the dimensions of trustworthiness and expertise of the influencer's credibility were operationalised by
the sub-dimensions of perceived honesty, transparency, and product knowledge, which are most directly
related to cognitive credibility, the latter being the focus of the study. The attractiveness dimension of the
scale by Ohanian (1990) was not assessed because the study's focus was on cognitive credibility. Engagement
and authenticity were adapted from Audrezet, de Kerviler and Guidry Moulard (2020) and Jin, Muqaddam
and Ryu (2021), which involved capturing emotional resonance, content congruence, and two -way
interaction, and reflecting the parasocial perspective. These purchase-behaviour items were taken from
Casaló et al. (2020) and reflected the respondents' past buying, product research, and intentions to buy.
Engagement and authenticity were treated as a single concept as they were conceptualised as components of
a parasocial connection, or relationship. The items in the attitudinal section were measured on a five-point
Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree). A pilot test with 10 participants resulted in some
minor wording changes. The constructs, definitions, sources, and reliability coefficients are summarized in

The research complied with institutional ethical norms and the standards of the British Psychological Society

Data Analysis Strategy
Data were analysed in IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 28). Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations,
skewness, and kurtosis) summarised response distributions, and Cronbach's alpha assessed internal
consistency, with values above .70 considered acceptable (Tavakol & Dennick, 2011). Pearson correlation
coefficients established the strength and direction of inter-construct relationships, after which three simple
linear regressions tested the hypotheses: trust predicting purchase behaviour (H1), engagement and
authenticity predicting trust (H2), and engagement and authenticity predicting purchase behaviour (H3).
For each model, the model summary (R, R², adjusted R²), the ANOVA (F-test), and the coefficient estimates
(B, SE, β, t, p, and 95% confidence intervals) are reported. Because trust is theorised as a mediating
mechanism, the indirect pathway was additionally examined using the product-of-coefficients estimate and
an approximate Sobel test; the limits of this approach, and the case for confirmatory bootstrapped mediation,
are stated explicitly in Sections 4.5 and 5.6.

Hypothesis Testing
Three simple linear regressions tested the hypothesised paths. Each model is reported with its model
summary, ANOVA, and coefficient estimates.

H1: Trust as a Predictor of Purchase Behaviour
A simple linear regression tested whether trust predicts purchase behaviour. The model was statistically
significant, F(1, 79) = 38.44, p < .001, and explained 32.7% of the variance in purchase behaviour (R² =
.327, adjusted R² = .319). Trust was a significant positive predictor (B = 0.733, β = .572, t = 6.20, p <

Examining the Indirect Pathway
Because the conceptual model positions trust as the mechanism linking engagement and authenticity to
purchase behaviour, the indirect pathway was examined. The three conditions associated with the causal-
steps logic (Baron & Kenny, 1986) are met: engagement predicts the outcome (β = .657, p < .001; H3),
engagement predicts the mediator (β = .690, p < .001; H2), and the mediator predicts the outcome (β =
.572, p < .001; H1). The product-of-coefficients estimate of the indirect effect of engagement on purchase
behaviour through trust is a × b = 0.662 × 0.733 = 0.485, and an approximate Sobel test indicates this
indirect path is statistically significant (z = 5.01, p < .001).
Two caveats are emphasised. First, the b-path used here is the bivariate Trust → Purchase coefficient
rather than the coefficient adjusted for engagement, so the indirect estimate should be read as an
approximate upper bound. Second, because purchase behaviour was not regressed on trust and engagement
jointly , the direct effect (c′) was not isolated, and the present design therefore cannot formally distinguish
partial from full mediation. The pattern of a significant direct path (H3) alongside a significant approximate
indirect path is consistent with a dual-pathway account in which engagement relates to behaviour both
directly and through trust, but a confirmatory bootstrapped test on the raw data (e.g., PROCESS Model 4,
with a bias-corrected confidence interval) is required to estimate the indirect effect precisely. The
decomposition is summarised in Table 9 and the tested model in Figure 3.

Discussion
Interpretation of Findings
The results are consistent in that they reveal a correlation between trust, engagement and willingness to act
on the part of consumers in influencer marketing. The presence of trust was strongly associated with purchase
behaviour (H1) and supported its position as a central mechanism in the effectiveness of influencers (Mrisha
& Xixiang, 2024). The parasocial premise that meaningful one-sided relationships foster affective trust
(Horton & Wohl, 1956; Jin, Muqaddam & Ryu, 2021) was supported by the strong relationship between
engagement, authenticity, and trust. Also, engagement was a direct predictor of purchase behaviour (H3);
the second pathway (indirect) was also confirmed, suggesting that engagement was associated with
behaviour via trust, but also directly (Han & Balabanis, 2023). Importantly, engagement and authenticity
showed the highest proximal correlate of behaviour, slightly outstripping trust; thus , trust should be
considered the focus mediating mechanism, rather than the largest. Combined, these findings suggest an
integrated theory that combines the cognitive component of the Source Credibility Theory and the affective
component of Parasocial Interaction Theory.

Relationship with Existing Literature
The trust behaviour finding is also consistent with the work of Lou and Yuan (2020), who demonstrated that
recommendations made by perceived trustworthy sources are more likely to influence consumer behaviour;
and with Djafarova and Geere (2023), who suggested that trust is a mediator between credibility cues and
consumer decision. This finding in the engagement trust relationship builds upon the relational-marketing
literature, as Audrezet and de Kerviler Moulard (2020) and French and Guidry Moulard (2023) found that
authenticity is not a matter of spontaneity, but rather of a calibrated self-disclosure. In the cases of Pieter and
Angelopoulos (2024), it was found that emotional involvement can relate to behavior even if there is
awareness of sponsorship, which calls into question strictly linear models that consider trust as the only
mediator.

The Scepticism Pattern
One of the interesting things about these data is that all the construct means were below the midpoint of the
scale, despite the fact that all respondents were identified as active followers of influencers. The most
conservative interpretation of the findings is that while consumers can enjoy and engage with the content

Theoretical Contributions
The study makes three tentative contributions. It provides empirical evidence for the need to examine the
Source Credibility and Parasocial Interaction perspectives together, as this gives a more complete picture of
the relationship between influencers and consumer decisions both cognitively and affectively. Second, it
suggests that engagement may be related to both trust and directly to purchase, thus arguing for models to
also include mediation and moderation. Third, even with high levels of relationships between the different
parts of the construct, the overall low means for trust and engagement reveal the fragility of digital trust and
raise questions about the capacity of influencers to persuade in an overcommercialised setting.

Practical Implications
The findings for marketers reveal that it is not enough to simply be seen or reach in order to build trust. The
affective trust of behaviour is rooted in authentic communication, consistent personal branding, and genuine
two-way communication. Partnering with smaller influencers with larger audiences that are not as
committed, but rather relatable, can prove to be beneficial to the brand, as well as special attention to
influencer brand value alignment, as audiences are becoming more critical when it comes to congruence with
sponsored content. Engagement has to do with both trust and behaviour, so it is likely that long -term
partnerships, where influencers can seamlessly integrate brand storytelling, will yield better results than just
a single promotional post.

Limitations and Directions for Future Research
These findings are subject to a number of caveats. The limited generalisability of the findings is due to the
non-probability convenience sampling (81 digitally active, primarily young, highly educated respondents)
and the cross-sectional design, which only allows analyses of the associations at one point in time and does
not enable analyses of trust development and changes over time. Self-reported data may also be subject to
social-desirability and recall biases, and the indirect-effect estimate should be considered provisional until
confirmed by a bootstrapped test. Larger and more representative samples, longitudinal designs, behavioural
data (e.g., click-through and purchase data), and platform-specific and cross-cultural comparisons would
help clarify the boundary conditions of the observed effects in the future.

Conclusion
The study looked at the relationship between social media influencers and consumer perceptions of trust and
purchase using the integrated framework of Source Credibility Theory and Parasocial Interaction Theory. The
results corroborate that trust is a key mediator between influencers and consumers: trust is a significant
predictor of purchase behaviour, engagement and authenticity are significant predictors of trust, and
engagement is also a significant predictor of purchase behaviour, which is partially mediated by trust. So it
seems that influencers do have some power over consumer decisions , although not an absolute one,
particularly when they build trust and credibility and have a transparent relationship with their audience that
is actually an interactive one.
Meanwhile, the low scores in trust, engagement and purchase behaviour are uniformly low, reflecting
increased consumer scepticism, possibly due to the rise of content being germinated and produced for profit,
and the difficulty of separating out genuine content from manufactured content. The main managerial
