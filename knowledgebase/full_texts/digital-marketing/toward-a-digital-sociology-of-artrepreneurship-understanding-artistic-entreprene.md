Artistic entrepreneurship is a rapidly changing field due to the growing domi -
nance of social media. Digital technologies have opened up new avenues for
artists to produce, market, and monetize their creative work, and have also reme-
diated the social mechanisms, identities, and structures through which artistic
value is created and recognised. Research on social media has so far focused
on entrepreneurial, marketing and technological aspects, particularly platform
adoption, digital marketing and economic performance. As a result, little atten -
tion has been given to understanding artrepreneurship as a socially constructed
activity, influenced by platform infrastructures, algorithmic governance, digital
labour, identity performance and networked social relationships. This Perspective
calls for a reorientation from viewing social media as a communication medium
toward understanding platforms as digitally mediated institutional environments
that shape artistic production, visibility, legitimacy, and entrepreneurial opportu-
nity. This article proposes a conceptual framework that integrates insights from
digital sociology, platform studies, social capital theory, and Goffman’s dramatur-
gical perspective to understand contemporary artistic entrepreneurship: Digital
Sociology of Artrepreneurship (DSA). DSA conceptualises artrepreneurship as a
dynamic process in which individual agency interacts with platform-mediated
social structures, with artistic and entrepreneurial outcomes shaped by visibil -
ity, identity performance, networked relations and strategic engagements within
digitally mediated ecosystems. A research agenda for the future and theoretical
implications for sociology, cultural studies, entrepreneurship, and research on a
digital society are presented in the conclusion.

1 Introduction
The platformization of society has substantially changed the context in which artistic entre-
preneurship takes place. While galleries, publishers, exhibitions and other cultural intermediar-
ies traditionally act as the bridges between artists and audiences, digital platforms like
Instagram, TikTok, Y ouTube, Etsy, and Behance have emerged as key infrastructures that artists
use to produce, circulate, and commercialize creative work. As a result, barriers to entry have

been reduced, creating opportunities for artists to build global audi-
ences, develop personal brands, diversify income streams, and trans-
form creative practices into sustainable entrepreneurial careers
(Abidin, 2020; Duffy, 2017). This has created the need to embed artis-
tic entrepreneurship into digitally mediated spaces where creative
production, social interaction and economic exchange all take place.
The term “artrepreneurship” was coined to refer to a combination
of artistry and entrepreneurial skills needed to create viable careers in
the creative industries (Chang and Wyszomirski, 2015; Essig, 2017).
In this article, the terms are used with specific distinctions. The term
“artistic entrepreneurship” is used in an inclusive way and it is a field
of research that has emerged from the field of arts entrepreneurship
known as “entrepreneurial activity in and around artistic practice”
(Chang and Wyszomirski, 2015; Essig, 2017). In this context, “artre-
preneurship” is a more precise term that describes the entrepreneurial
process in which artists engage in artistic production and entrepre-
neurial endeavours in today’s digital and platform-mediated contexts.
An entrepreneur who engages in such entrepreneurial activity is called
an “artrepreneur. ” Thus, artistic entrepreneurship is used as the
broader field, artrepreneurship refers to the entrepreneurial process
examined in this context, and artrepreneur refers to the artist engaged
in that process. Early scholarship focused on entrepreneurial skills,
creativity, opportunity identification, market skills and entrepreneur-
ship development (Bridgstock, 2013; Pollard and Wilson, 2014). With
the advent of digital platforms as the focus of creative practice, more
research looked at how social media can help artists promote their
work, connect with audiences, build professional networks and mon-
etise their artistic output outside of institutional structures (Duffy,
2017). To date, however, most of the scholarship examining digital
platforms has focused on how they can be used for entrepreneurial
work rather than how they create space for entrepreneurial work. This
research, however, has tended to take a more technological or market-
ing lens, seeing digital platforms less as a space to shape entrepreneur-
ial practice and more as a tool to make use of it.
There is now some emerging empirical evidence that a more com-
plex reality exists. As part of this study, a phenomenological explora-
tion of artrepreneurs revealed that social media is not simply a
medium for promotion but a multidimensional space in which artistic
expression, audience interaction, networking, identity construction,
perceived opportunities, and entrepreneurial outcomes are negotiated
throughout interactions on social media platforms ( George and
Mathew, 2025). In recent years, conceptual research on artificial intel-
ligence in artistic entrepreneurship also posited that AI has an addi-
tional impact on platform-based practices by changing how artists
create, how they are considered authentic, how they are visible in the
world, and how they make entrepreneurial decisions, and it has raised
new ethical, institutional, and governance issues about authorship and
creativity (George and Susan Mathew, 2025). These studies imply that,
rather than being only about entrepreneurial abilities, contemporary
artistic entrepreneurship is growingly defined by engagements with
digital infrastructures.
Even with these developments, research on social media and
artificial intelligence mostly focuses on entrepreneurship, marketing
or communication or technology. These methods have produced
important findings on the implications of digital branding, audience
interaction, entrepreneurial behaviour, and technology adoption,
but have tended to lessen the focus on larger sociological processes
shaping, organizing and governing artistic visibility, identity, legiti -
macy and opportunity through platform infrastructures,
algorithmic governance, institutional rules, and networked interac -
tions. Digital platforms are not just the enablers of entrepreneur -
ship, they are becoming the institutional contexts of
entrepreneurship.
The need is addressed by digital sociology that offers an important
theoretical base. Digital sociology sees platforms as institutions that
shape and mediate interaction, structure power and participation, and
reshape everyday life, instead of being neutral tools ( Couldry and
Hepp, 2016). In the same way, platform studies show how algorithmic
logic and data infrastructures and governance frameworks are increas-
ingly shaping cultural production and the allocation of visibility and
recognition (Poell et al., 2021; van Dijck et al., 2018 ). In this view,
artistic entrepreneurship is not an economic phenomenon per se, but
it is a social phenomenon that takes shape over time via interaction
between different populations, including artists and audiences, algo-
rithms and platform institutions.
Following this sociological approach, some recent studies have
started conceptualising the Digital Strategic Engagement as a com-
municative practice consisting of various dimensions, including stra-
tegic platforms, artistic expression, strategic audiences, strategic
networking and strategic interaction within platform ecosystems
(Mathew et al., 2026). This work contributes to the understanding of
how artists strategically interact with digital platforms, but the defini-
tion of Digital Strategic Engagement is mostly one based on measur-
ing behaviour. It is still needed to have a broader theoretical account
of how such practices manifest themselves in digitally mediated social
structures.
In view of this, this Perspective proposes the Digital Sociology of
Artrepreneurship (DSA) as a conceptual framework for understand-
ing artistic entrepreneurship in the platform society. DSA conceptu-
alises artistic entrepreneurship as a socially mediated process
influenced by institutional structures, algorithmic governance, iden-
tity performance, networked social relations, and individual agency,
rather than treating social media and digital technologies merely as
entrepreneurial tools. The synthesis of digital sociology, platform stud-
ies, embeddedness, Bourdieu’s theory of capital and Goffman’s drama-
turgical perspective, is used to illuminate how artistic visibility,
legitimacy and entrepreneurial opportunities arise as a result of the
interplay between artists and digitally mediated social structures. The
rest of the article first outlines the shortcomings of the previous
approaches and then elaborates on the five dimensions of the DSA
framework, and lastly reflects on their implications for sociological
research on entrepreneurial artistic practice.
2 Why existing perspectives on
artrepreneurship are no longer
sufficient
The term artrepreneurship has been introduced to describe the
combination of artistic entrepreneurial skills and the aim of sustain-
able careers in the creative industries (Chang and Wyszomirski, 2015;
Essig, 2017). In the early days, entrepreneurial skills, opportunity rec-
ognition, innovation, marketing and venture creation were the main
focuses of entrepreneurship scholarship (Bridgstock, 2013). As the
social media landscape grew, research was even more revealing of the
ability of digital platforms to extend audiences, cultivate personal

brands, and monetise creative content beyond the cultural sector
(Duffy, 2017).
Although these are important contributions to the field, the cur-
rent literature has mainly approached social media from the perspec-
tive of the entrepreneur or marketing. This view neglects the social
role of platforms as social infrastructure that governs social interac-
tion, regulates visibility, frames participation, and thus affects cultural
legitimacy. Digital platforms are not merely means of enabling entre-
preneurship; they are also the environments in which entrepreneur-
ship takes place.
Entrepreneurship is related to networks, institutions and power
relations from a sociological point of view ( Granovetter, 2008).
Platform society and algorithmic systems, platform governance, and
data infrastructures influence access to audiences, opportunities, and
economic opportunities (Poell et al., 2021 ; van Dijck et al., 2018 ).
Therefore, to succeed in art, creativity and entrepreneurship skills are
not only essential, but, the knowledge of how to maneuver digitally
mediated social landscapes is essential.
Authenticity, engagement, understanding of audience data, keep-
ing up with changes in platforms, and entrepreneurial identity are
essential traits for today’s artists. Artistic production is thus a process
of self-presentation, interaction and acts of community engagement
in the digital realm.
However, the existing theories of the transformation only account
for part of this change. Algorithmic intermediation of social structures
is not sufficiently discussed in the study of entrepreneurship, market-
ing research is focused primarily on the involvement of consumers,
and the study of creative industries does not seriously consider the
digital infrastructures as institutions of culture governance. These
limitations suggest the need for a sociological framework that can
account for the influence of platform-mediated interactions on artistic
entrepreneurship.
Artrepreneurship is proposed in this article as a socially embed-
ded practice that is not solely economic, but rather is an ongoing pro-
cess of negotiating identities, visibility, social relationships, and
entrepreneurial opportunities between artists, audiences, algorithms
and platform infrastructures.
3 Toward a digital sociology of
artrepreneurship
The above discussion highlights that the concept of entrepreneur-
ship in the context of contemporary art cannot be explained and
understood by using only a traditional theory of entrepreneurship and
a digital marketing theory. These viewpoints, although useful for
explaining how artists identify opportunities and use social media to
market their artistic work, do not offer much understanding about the
ways that digital technologies alter the social contexts in which artistic
entrepreneurship is practiced. This article therefore proposes the
Digital Sociology of Artrepreneurship (DSA) as a conceptual frame-
work for placing artistic entrepreneurship within the broader socio-
economic context of the platform society.
Digital Sociology of Artrepreneurship (DSA) is specifically under-
stood as a conceptual framework for analysing artistic entrepreneur-
ship as a socially mediated platform practice. DSA is not intended as
a new field of research, or a stand-alone sociological theory, but rather
a tool for integrating insights from digital sociology, platform studies,
social embeddedness, social capital and dramaturgical theories to
explain how artistic entrepreneurship is constituted through the inter-
play of platform structures, algorithmic processes, social relations,
identity performances and entrepreneurial agencies. Accordingly, this
article uses DSA as a conceptual framework for analysing artrepre-
neurship as a platform-mediated social process in which structural
conditions, algorithmic mechanisms, relational processes, identity
construction, and individual agency are mutually constituted.
Digital Sociology of Artrepreneurship is a conceptual framework
that aims to examine the interplay of digital platforms, algorithmic
systems, networked communities, and platform governance in the
process of creating artistic identity, artrepreneurial practices, and cre-
ative livelihoods. Instead of seeing these factors as separate influences,
DSA considers these factors to be interdependent and examines how
they collectively shape the opportunities, restrictions, relationships
and forms of agency within which artistic entrepreneurship is mani-
fested. DSA therefore conceptualises social media platforms not as
external technological resources that artists simply use, but as consti-
tutive social environments in which artistic production, interaction,
visibility, identity, and entrepreneurial exchange are organised. Artistic
entrepreneurship is a concrete process of the continuous interplay
between individual agency and digitally mediated social structures.
This perspective is consistent with the view that digital technolo-
gies are not merely tools that facilitate social interaction but are con-
stitutive of the social environments in which social life is organized
and experienced ( Orlikowski, 2007). In a platform society, artists
engage in algorithmic ecosystems that are shaped by the norms of
platforms, recommendation systems and visibility and engagement
metrics (van Dijck et al., 2018). These mechanisms not only drive the
spread of artistic content but also reputation, audience trust and the
legitimacy of the entrepreneur.
The five dimensions operate at different analytical levels and func-
tions, but they are conceptually integrated within a single structure–
mechanism–agency process. Platform Embeddedness describes the
structural context of artistic entrepreneurship, Algorithmic Visibility
is a platform-mediated mechanism of selective distribution of atten-
tion, recognition and access, Performative Artistic Identity is a process
of identity construction and negotiation within the context of a plat-
form, Networked Social Capital is the relational resources and recog-
nition created by interactions with audiences, peers, collaborators and
communities, and Digital Strategic Engagement refers to the process
through which artists interpret, respond to, adapt to, and negotiate
these structural, algorithmic, identity and relational conditions. They
should therefore not be understood as five parallel causal variables,
but as five interconnected analytical components through which the
platform-mediated artistic entrepreneurship can be understood.
Conceptually, the relationship can be understood as recursive
rather than strictly linear. Algorithmic visibility is conditioned by plat-
form embeddedness, which provides the institutional and infrastruc-
tural conditions of participation. Artists respond to these conditions
through identity performance, the cultivation of networked social
capital and digital strategic engagement. These responses can affect
subsequent visibility, identity construction and network relations. The
five dimensions are thus a dynamic structure–agency configuration in
which platforms condition action, artists responds strategically, and
their responses are constantly shaped by the algorithmic and relational
feedbacks.
Artificial intelligence can be understood within DSA as an inten-
sifying layer of platform mediation rather than as a separate

dimension. AI-enabled recommendation, content-generation, mod-

while generative AI can transform artistic identity, creative labour and
strategic engagement, highlighting further sociological questions of

The scope of DSA is therefore bounded to artistic entrepreneur-
ship in digitally mediated and platform-dependent environments. It
is not proposed as a universal explanation of all forms of artistic entre-
preneurship. Its analytical value is greatest where platform infrastruc-
tures, algorithmic mediation, networked interaction and digitally
performed identities are central to the organisation of artistic and
entrepreneurial activity.
Digital Sociology of Artrepreneurship does not merely combine
existing sociological theories but synthesizes them into a unified
framework for understanding artistic entrepreneurship in the plat-
form society. Digital sociology explains how digital technologies
restructure social life, while platform studies reveal how platforms
function as institutional infrastructures that govern participation and
visibility. Granovetter’s concept of embeddedness situates entrepre-
neurial activity within socially structured networks, Bourdieu’s theory
of capital explains how recognition and legitimacy are accumulated
through digitally mediated relationships, and Goffman’s dramaturgical
perspective illuminates the performative construction of artistic iden-
tities online. Together, these perspectives demonstrate that artistic
entrepreneurship emerges through the continuous interaction
between individual agency and platform-mediated social structures.
The five dimensions of DSA operationalize this sociological
perspective.
DSA also connects artistic entrepreneurship with the sociology of
digital labour. Platformization is not only a transformation of how
artistic work accesses markets, but also of how creative work is organ-
ised, assessed and maintained. Artists increasingly perform activities
beyond creative production, including content creation, self-presen-
tation, audience management, networking, analytics interpretation,
and continuous adaptation to the platform changes (Duffy, 2017; Poell
et al., 2021).
This also raises questions central to the sociology of work, includ-
ing the distribution of control and autonomy, the intensification of
creative labour, occupational insecurity, and the expansion of unpaid
or aspirational labour required to maintain platform visibility.
Platform governance can therefore operate as a form of indirect
organisational control, as artists adapt their work practices to chang-
ing algorithmic expectations while remaining formally independent.
DSA consequently connects entrepreneurial agency with the organ-
isational and labour conditions under which digitally mediated cre-
ative work is performed.
The theoretical novelty of DSA lies not in introducing entirely new
sociological concepts, but in specifying how established mechanisms
operate together within platform-mediated artistic entrepreneurship.
Creative entrepreneurship explains opportunity recognition, resource
mobilisation and value creation; platform studies explain infrastruc-
ture, governance and algorithmic mediation; digital-labour research
highlights the labour required to remain visible and competitive; and
cultural-production perspectives explain the organisation of cultural
production. DSA connects these insights within a single sociological
account of how platform structures condition visibility and opportu-
nity, how artists construct and negotiate identities, how networked
relationships generate social capital, and how artists exercise strategic
agency within these conditions. Its distinctive contribution is therefore
relational: DSA explains artrepreneurial outcomes as emerging from
the recursive interaction of platform structures, algorithmic visibility,
identity performance, networked social capital and strategic agency
rather than treating these as separate explanations (see Figure 1).
DSA conceptualises artistic entrepreneurship as a platform-medi-
ated social process involving structural conditions, platform-mediated
mechanisms, identity construction, relational resources and individ-
ual agency. The five dimensions— Platform Embeddedness,
Algorithmic Visibility, Performative Artistic Identity, Networked
Social Capital, and Digital Strategic Engagement—are conceptually
distinct but mutually recursive rather than independent causal predic-
tors. AI-enabled platform mediation operates across these dimen -
sions, while contextual and inequality conditions function as
boundary conditions. Together, the five dimensions constitute the
structure–mechanism–agency configuration through which artistic
legitimacy, cultural recognition, creative livelihoods and artrepreneur-
ial outcomes are negotiated.
3.1 Platform embeddedness
Economic action is not autonomous, but is very much part of
social structures (Granovetter, 2008). Artistic entrepreneurship in the
platform society is increasingly embedded in digital platforms that
govern how audiences access content, how artistic work becomes vis-
ible in the cultural field, and how it can generate economic opportuni-
ties. Digital platforms are not just communication technologies, but
institutional ‘environments’ that structure participation, norms of
interaction and distribution of recognition.
It is significant to differentiate infrastructure, institution and
social structure. Platforms are infrastructures that offer the technical
architectures, interfaces, data systems and affordances through which
content is created, circulated, prioritized, and consumed. They are also
institutions with rules, governance mechanisms and norms that
govern participation and establish the conditions under which cul-
tural activity takes place. Through repeated interactions within these
infrastructures and institutional arrangements, patterned social rela-
tionships, advantages and constraints can emerge among artists, audi-
ences and collaborators. Thus, infrastructure provides certain forms
of action, institutional rules structure these acts, and repeated interac-
tions shape opportunities and inequalities (Poell et al., 2021; van Dijck
et al., 2018 ). These conditions are also shaped by differences in
resources and power. Artists with unequal access to digital skills,
financial resources, cultural capital, institutional support, and estab-
lished networks may experience the same platform environment dif-
ferently. Platform embeddedness therefore incorporates not only the
conditions of participation but also the unequal capacities through
which artists can navigate and benefit from those conditions.
Platform infrastructures increasingly evaluate and distribute artis-
tic content through algorithmically mediated systems of ranking, rec-
ommendation and engagement, which are different from the
traditional cultural intermediaries like galleries or publishers. Artistic
practice is then situated in an institutional framing that is co-pro -
duced in the process of the creative work’s visibility, legitimacy and

3.2 Algorithmic visibility
In the platform society, algorithmic systems have become increas-
ingly influential in the organisation of artistic visibility, although vis-
ibility and recognition have long been shaped by cultural
intermediaries and social processes. Recommendation algorithms can
function as contemporary cultural intermediaries, focusing attention
according to platform-specific signals like interaction, consistency and
engagement (Poell et al., 2021). Rather than distributing visibility neu-
trally, these systems selectively distribute attention, circulating and
potentially amplifying the work of some artists and their practices
more than others in digital cultural spaces. Algorithmic visibility can
therefore be seen as a possible avenue of stratification through the
platforms. This does not mean that algorithmic visibility directly
determines artistic success or that every difference in visibility consti-
tutes social stratification. Rather, stratification may emerge when
repeated and uneven patterns of attention and recognition systemati-
cally differentiate artists and artistic practices across platform-specific
ranking, recommendation, moderation and engagement systems.
These disparities in visibility can shape access to audiences, collabora-
tion opportunities, symbolic recognition and ability to transform vis-
ibility into economic and/or career opportunities. From a sociological
perspective, algorithmic visibility becomes meaningful if repeated and
patterned differences in exposure lead to lasting differences in recog-
nition, legitimacy, participation, and/or opportunity.
It can be represented as a sequence of visibility–recognition–
opportunity. Algorithmic selection has an initial impact on the likeli-
hood that specific content of the artistic product will be seen by
viewers. Differential exposure may then affect the attention and inter-
action of the audience and their sense of popularity, leading to recog-
nition and legitimacy within networked communities. When this
recognition is translated into followers, collaborations, commissions,

3.3 Performative artistic identity
Digital platforms have created conditions in which artistic self-
presentation becomes continuous and publicly observable. In digitally
networked environments, artists continuously negotiate multiple roles
as creators, entrepreneurs, educators, influencers, and community
participants. Artistic identity is no longer expressed solely through
creative production but is socially constructed through ongoing inter-
actions with audiences, platform norms, and algorithmic expectations
(Forsyth, 2024). This can create tensions between artistic originality
and the need for platform visibility, as creators negotiate between
maintaining creative authenticity and adapting their practices to plat-
form algorithms. Artistic identity, therefore, needs to be conceived as
a socially negotiated and institutionally mediated performance and
not as an individual attribute. Performative Artistic Identity and
Digital Strategic Engagement are analytically distinct but mutually
related dimensions. Performative Artistic Identity focuses on what the
artists build and shape and how they negotiate their identity in rela-
tion to the audience and the demands of the platform, which involves
presentation and negotiation of the artistic self. Digital Strategic
Engagement has to do with “what artists do” when it comes to their
platform context: that is, the purposive selection, adaptation, com-
munication, interaction and negotiation practices in which they seek
to achieve artistic and entrepreneurial goals. The former concerns
identity construction and symbolic presentation, whereas the latter
concerns strategic agency and action. For instance, an artist may alter
the presentation of their artistic identity to maintain authenticity or
credibility while also adapting how they post, interact with their audi-
ence, network, or select platforms in order to achieve greater visibility
or opportunity.
3.4 Networked social capital
The idea of social capital (Bourdieu, 2008) offers a useful way of
thinking about the ways in which social networks create opportunities
for access to resources, legitimacy, and opportunities. In platform eco-
systems, social capital is increasingly formed and maintained through
digitally mediated interactions among creators, audiences, collabora-
tors, and communities. Networked social capital is not an indicator of
the size of an audience, but rather is the product of trust, reciprocity,
shared recognition and ongoing interaction that increases an artist’s
social standing in platform communities. From this point of view,
cultural legitimacy is therefore understood as a process co-produced
through networks of social relations rather than as the result of indi-
vidual success alone.
3.5 Digital strategic engagement
Digital Strategic Engagement is the process whereby individual
agency is realized within platform-mediated social structures. This
dimension acknowledges artists as more than merely subjects of algo-
rithmic systems; it recognises them as active participants who interact
with platform’s norms, adjust to algorithmic shifts, establish commu-
nities, and negotiate opportunities and constraints in the pursuit of
creative work. Agency hence becomes relational, emerging through
the ongoing interactions among creators, audiences, algorithms and
institutional arrangements.
Recent studies have conceptualized Digital Strategic Engagement
in Artrepreneurship as a multidimensional communicative practice
involving strategic platform use, artistic expression, audience engage-
ment, networking, and adaptive interaction within platform environ-
ments (Mathew et al., 2026). Digital Strategic Engagement does not
simply refer to the uses of digital platforms, but rather to the ways in
which creators strategically utilise platform affordances to maintain
their artistic identities, develop social relationships and respond to
opportunities and limitations in digitally mediated environments
(Mathew et al., 2026).
From this perspective, Digital Strategic Engagement is conceptu-
alised not only as a communicative capability but also as an agentic
dimension of platform-mediated artistic entrepreneurship within the
Digital Sociology of Artrepreneurship framework. Performative
Artistic Identity concerns how artists construct and negotiate their
identities within platform environments, whereas Digital Strategic
Engagement concerns how they strategically engage with these envi-
ronments to pursue artistic, relational and entrepreneurial goals. The
four dimensions outlined above define the institutional, algorithmic,
identity and relational context of artistic entrepreneurship, while
Digital Strategic Engagement refers to how artists engage with and
respond to these dimensions in their everyday digital practices. This
distinction places agency within a relational space rather than an
autonomous one: agency is exercised but is also enabled, limited, and
constantly transformed by platform infrastructures and algorithms,
identity expectations and expectations, and networked relationships.
4 Implications for sociology and a
future research agenda
The sociological contribution of DSA lies in repositioning the
artrepreneur within digitally mediated institutional environments.
Rather than treating social media as a neutral communication or mar-
keting tool, DSA conceptualises platforms as social structures that
shape identity, visibility, relationships and entrepreneurial opportu-
nity. This connects the sociology of digital platforms with the sociol-
ogy of cultural production, work and entrepreneurship by treating
structure and agency as mutually constitutive.
The framework also enables comparative analysis across creative

5 Proposed research questions and
conceptual propositions
The five dimensions of DSA generate a set of research questions
for future empirical investigation:
RQ1: How does platform embeddedness shape artrepreneurial
practices and opportunities across digital platforms?
RQ2: How does algorithmically mediated visibility influence
artistic recognition, legitimacy, audience access and artrepre -
neurial opportunity?
RQ3: How do artrepreneurs negotiate artistic authenticity, cre-
ativity and platform expectations when constructing digital
identities?
RQ4: What is the role of networked social capital in artistic legit-
imacy, collaboration and opportunity for artrepreneurs?
RQ5: How artrepreneurs strategically respond to platform gov-
ernance, algorithmic changes, audience feedback and emerging
digital technologies?
These are the conceptual propositions that can be tested empiri-
cally to further clarify these research questions. These propositions are
conceptual expectations that are designed to direct future empirical
testing, but are not causal claims:
P1: Greater platform embeddedness is associated with greater
dependence of artrepreneurial practices on platform-specific
infrastructures, governance arrangements, and participa -
tion norms.
P2: Algorithmically mediated visibility influences the distribu-
tion of audience attention and may contribute to differences in
cultural recognition, legitimacy, and artrepreneurial opportunity.
P3: Artistic identity is negotiated continually as artistic authen-
ticity, the expectations of the audience, and platform-mediated
norms of visibility and engagement are continually negotiated.
P4: Networked social capital strengthens the capacity of artre-
preneurs to obtain relational resources, recognition, collabora-
tion, and entrepreneurial opportunities within platform
communities.
P5: Digital Strategic Engagement enables artrepreneurs to adapt
to, negotiate, and potentially reshape the structural and algorith-
mic conditions of platform-mediated artistic entrepreneurship.
P6: The relationship between platform-mediated structures and
artrepreneurial outcomes is jointly shaped by identity perfor -
mance, networked social capital, and digital strategic
engagement.
P7: Digital Strategic Engagement may amplify or alter the impact
of algorithmic visibility, as artists react to the visibility mecha-
nisms and audience response on platforms.
Lastly, the proposed perspective has implications beyond the
academic realm. Cultural policymakers need to support artists in
acquiring digital skills while maintaining and strengthening tra -
ditional artistic competencies. Similarly, digital literacy, platform
awareness, community-building skills and ethical understanding
of algorithmic systems should be integrated into arts entrepre -
neurship programmes that prepare artists for digitally mediated
creative work. Platform companies also be encouraged to improve
the transparency, accountability and fairness of their recommen -
dation systems, which can significantly influence creative
livelihoods.
These implications position Digital Sociology of
Artrepreneurship not as a stand-alone entrepreneurship theory,
but as a sociologically informed conceptual framework for under -
standing changes in the nature of creative work and entrepreneur -
ial practice in digital society. Sociological research will become
more and more vital in the future to account for how artists will
navigate the new forms of digital labour, institutional control and
entrepreneurial opportunity as the platform technologies continue
to develop.
6 Conclusion
This Perspective has argued that artistic entrepreneurship in the
platform society cannot be understood solely in terms of individual
entrepreneurial activity or the use of social media as a medium for
promotion. Platforms are becoming increasingly functioning as insti-
tutional spaces that define visibility, interaction, identity construction
and access to entrepreneurial opportunities. DSA provides a frame-
work for understanding this process through five interconnected
dimensions: Platform Embeddedness, Algorithmic Visibility,
Performative Artistic Identity, Networked Social Capital and Digital
Strategic Engagement.
The contribution of DSA lies in connecting platform struc -
tures and algorithmic mechanisms with identity work, relational
resources and strategic agency. Artrepreneurial outcomes are
understood as emerging through the recursive interaction
between structural conditions and situated action rather than as
direct outcomes of individual agency or technology alone. The
framework also highlights the interaction between platform-
mediated visibility and existing inequalities, as well as the poten -
tial for digital technologies and artificial intelligence to transform
creative practice and entrepreneurship.
The research questions and propositions outlined above can be
examined across various creative sectors, platforms and cultural con-
texts, through future empirical studies. Hence, DSA is not proposed as
a general theory of artistic entrepreneurship, but as a theoretically and
