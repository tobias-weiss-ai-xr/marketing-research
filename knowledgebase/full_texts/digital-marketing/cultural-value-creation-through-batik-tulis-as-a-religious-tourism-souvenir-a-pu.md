ABSTRACT
Purpose – This study aims to analyze the value creation of Batik Tulis as a
religious tourism souvenir through the perspective of the purple economy in
the pilgrimage-based religious tourism area of the Tomb of Sheikh Ibrahim
Asmoroqondi, Tuban, East Java, Indonesia. The pilgrimage-based setting
matters for this process because devotional experience at the tomb embeds
the souvenir with spiritual and identity-based meaning that is central to how
its cultural value is formed, rather than the souvenir functioning as an ordinary
tourist commodity.
Design – This study uses a qualitative approach with a case study design.
Data were collected through in-depth interviews with 11 informants,
participatory observation, documentation, and literature review, and were
analyzed through data condensation, data display, and conclusion drawing.
Data management was supported by NVivo, with source and method
triangulation and member checking.
Findings – Within this single religious tourism site, Batik Tulis functions as
a marker of pilgrimage experience and local identity. However, recognition of
cultural value does not automatically lead to purchase because of price
sensitivity, practicality, market access, and limited digital literacy. Economic
value emerges when cultural meaning is mediated through storytelling, digital
marketing, product diversification, and collaboration among craftspersons,
tourism actors, and local government.
Research Implications – This study highlights the importance of managing
cultural products as part of the broader ecosystem of religious tourism
experience, rather than merely as commodities.
Originality – This study offers a purple economy reading, showing that
cultural value in local religious destinations in Indonesia becomes economic
value only when mediated by storytelling, visitor experience orchestration,
digital market access, and multi-stakeholder collaboration.

INTRODUCTION
Religious tourism destinations are not only associated with ritual practice and pilgrimage
activity; they also generate observable social facts, including visitor flows, local economic
circulation, relationships between pilgrims and local business actors, and the ways local culture is
translated into tourism experience. The Tomb of Sheikh Ibrahim Asmoroqondi has become one
of the major religious tourism destinations in Tuban Regency. According to the Head of the
Tourism Division of the Tuban Regency Department of Culture, Youth, Sports, and Tourism
(Disbudporapar), the site attracted 2,108,910 visitors in 2022, ranking second only to the Tomb of
Sunan Bonang among religious tourism destinations in the regency. This substantial number of
pilgrim visits reflects the strategic role of the site in supporting religious tourism development and
the local economy (Tuban, 2023;  Redaksi, 2023). This dynamic positions religious tourism as a
space of interaction between spiritual experience and culture-based economic activity.
Within Islamic studies, pilgrimage-based tourism can be understood as a meeting point
between devotional practice, Muslim social memory, local cultural expression, and community
economic circulation. The souvenir economy that develops around a religious destination is
therefore not only a commercial activity, but also a cultural process through which religious
experience, local identity, and everyday livelihoods are connected.
In this situation, Batik Tulis cannot be understood merely as an economic commodity; it is
also part of a living cultural identity. Tuban batik has developed through the encounter of local,
Chinese, and spiritual cultural elements, reflected in its motifs, techniques, and accompanying
narratives. The presence of Batik Tulis around religious tourism destinations shows the potential
of this cultural product as a souvenir that connects pilgrimage experience with local identity.
Therefore, the cultural analysis in this study is placed specifically within the framework of tourism
economics, not as a purely cultural study (Jin et al., 2022; Galluccio & Giambona, 2024).
A number of studies on cultural tourism show that souvenirs and cultural products act as
connectors between tourists and local culture. Souvenirs not only function as keepsakes; they also
serve as media of identity, experience, and community economic impact. A recent systematic
review identifies authenticity, innovation, consumer behaviour, and technology as key themes in
research on souvenirs and cultural sustainability (Qiu et al., 2024). Destination marketing literature
also emphasizes the importance of storytelling in transforming places into meaningful narratives,
although the operational mechanisms of storytelling in tourism destinations remain fragmented
(Hay et al., 2022; Zhang & Ramayah, 2024). In the context of religious tourism, tourist motivation
moves along a spectrum from sacred to more secular orientations, and these different motivations
shape how visitors evaluate authenticity and attachment to the destination (Melissa Tsai, 2021;
Wang et al., 2024). Within Indonesia specifically, studies on Batik Tulis Gedog in Tuban highlight
its potential to stimulate local economic development despite persistent business-development
constraints (Ramadhana & Kirwani, 2015). Research on Wali-tomb pilgrimage destinations further
demonstrates that Sharia-based destination management can enhance local economic benefits
while safeguarding religious values and cultural authenticity (Kusumaningtyas & Ulfah, 2024).
Moreover, recent evidence from the Sunan Bonang Tomb in Tuban indicates that the
commercialization of sacred pilgrimage sites should be carefully managed to balance tourism
development with the preservation of sacred space and spiritual meaning (Ghozi et al., 2025).
In this study, the purple economy is conceptualized as an economic paradigm that
integrates four core components: (1) cultural value, (2) symbolic meaning, (3) creative production,
and (4) cultural heritage sustainability. Rather than treating culture as a by-product of commercial
activity, the purple economy recognizes cultural resources as productive economic assets capable
of generating market value while preserving authenticity, strengthening cultural identity, and
ensuring the long-term continuity of cultural (Azis, 2019).
The purple economy perspective is relevant for reading this phenomenon because it places
culture at the center of economic value creation, rather than treating it merely as a supporting
element or symbolic commodity. Economic value is not produced only through production
efficiency or market exchange, but also through the meanings, symbols, reputation, and social

economics, which argues that the value of creative products is shaped through social and cultural
processes, where consumers purchase not only material functions but also experiences, identities,

In the context of cultural products such as Batik Tulis, value creation occurs through the
relationship between traditional production practices, symbolic heritage, and consumer
interpretation. Batik does not function solely as a textile commodity; it is also a narrative medium
that represents history, philosophy, and local values. The purple economy therefore enables an
analysis of how cultural meanings are negotiated and translated into economic value without losing
their symbolic depth, especially in culture- and religion-based tourism (Rickly & Canavan, 2024).
This body of work can be organized into four related but distinct strands. First, souvenir
studies examine souvenirs as carriers of place identity, cultural memory, visitor experience, and
local economic value, while increasingly emphasizing their role in sustaining cultural heritage  (Zhu
et al., 2023; Bernardo et al., 2023). Second, heritage tourism studies focus on authenticity, heritage
interpretation, conservation, and storytelling as key determinants of visitor engagement and
destination sustainability (S. Zhang et al., 2023; Muštra et al., 2023). Third, religious tourism studies
investigate pilgrims' motivations, spiritual experiences, and visitor management at sacred
destinations, highlighting the interaction between religious meaning and tourism experiences
(Pande & jaShi, 2023). Fourth, purple economy (or cultural economics) studies conceptualize
culture as a strategic economic resource that generates value through creativity, symbolic meaning,
and cultural assets rather than treating culture merely as a complementary tourism attribute.
Although these four strands have generated valuable insights, they have largely evolved in parallel
with limited theoretical integration. Consequently, insufficient attention has been given to
explaining how the cultural symbolism embedded in religious souvenirs is transformed into
economic value through the purple economy perspective within heritage-based religious tourism
destinations. Addressing this intersection constitutes the principal research gap that this study seeks
to fill.
Against this background, this study aims to analyze the process of cultural value creation
of Batik Tulis as a souvenir in the context of religious tourism through the perspective of the purple
economy. More specifically, it seeks to identify the stages through which cultural meaning and
appreciation emerge within the religious tourism experience, to examine the barriers that constrain
the conversion of cultural value into economic value, and to explore the mediating strategies that
may bridge this gap.
The conceptual framework was developed from the literature reviewed above rather than
from field observations. It positions Batik Tulis as a cultural heritage product embedded within a
religious tourism ecosystem, where cultural value does not automatically translate into economic
value but is mediated by storytelling, digital marketing, and cross-actor collaboration. The
framework integrates insights from souvenir studies, heritage tourism, religious tourism, and the
purple economy to illustrate these relationships. The three components of the framework are
explicitly linked to the study's research questions. The meaning-making stage addresses the first
research question by explaining how cultural meaning and appreciation emerge within the religious
tourism experience. The barrier stage addresses the second research question by identifying the
constraints that hinder the transformation of cultural value into economic value. The mediation
stage addresses the third research question by examining the strategies that bridge this gap and
facilitate value creation. The conceptual flow of the study is presented in Figure 1.

METHOD
Research design
This study uses a qualitative approach with a case study design because it aims to explore
in depth the process through which the cultural and economic value of Batik Tulis is created in the
context of religious tourism. A qualitative approach was chosen to understand how cultural
meaning, religious tourism experience, and local economic practices interact in the real context of
the destination (Subagiyo, 2017). The case study design allows the study to examine the
phenomenon contextually and holistically by drawing on multiple sources of data, so that the
dynamics of value creation are not reduced to a single variable (Miles et al., 2023).
Unit of analysis and research site
The unit of analysis in this study is the process of cultural value creation of Batik Tulis as
a religious tourism souvenir. The analysis focuses on symbolic meaning-making, production
practices, cultural narratives, and economic distribution within the context of a religious tourism
destination. The research was conducted in the religious tourism area of the Tomb of Sheikh
Ibrahim Asmoroqondi, Gesikharjo Village, Palang District, Tuban Regency, East Java. This site
was selected because it provides a direct space of interaction among pilgrimage activities, culture-
based economic practices, and the sustainability of Batik Tulis enterprises.
Data sources and informants
The study uses both primary and secondary data. Primary data were obtained through in-
depth interviews with informants selected purposively based on their direct involvement in the
process of Batik Tulis value creation in the religious tourism area (Neuman, 2014; Moleong, 2018).
The informants included Batik Tulis craftspersons and traders (encompassing craftspersons and
vendors or business actors around the religious tourism area), religious tourists who had purchased
or interacted with Batik Tulis as a souvenir, and representatives of local government involved in
tourism and creative economy development. The distribution of informants across these categories
is summarized in Table 1.

Data saturation
The number of informants was determined based on the principle of theoretical saturation,
whereby data collection ceased when additional interviews and observations no longer generated
new information relevant to the research questions (Saunders et al., 2018; Braun & Clarke, 2021).

Saturation was assessed separately within each of the three informant categories rather than across

findings on tourism development policy, creative-economy facilitation, cross-sector collaboration,
and digital marketing strategy, again without introducing new themes. The convergence of themes
across all three stakeholder groups indicated that sufficient theoretical saturation had been reached
for the purposes of this study. Nevertheless, given the smaller number of informants in the tourist
(n=3) and government (n=2) categories relative to the craftsperson category (n=6), saturation
claims for the former two groups are necessarily more tentative; they are best interpreted as
indicating consistency within the available sample rather than an exhaustive claim that no further
variation could emerge from additional informants. Secondary data included local government
documents, tourism reports, promotional materials, photographs of batik production and sales
activities, and relevant scientific publications used to strengthen the context and analysis.
Data Collection Techniques
Data were collected through semi-structured interviews, participatory observation,
documentation, and literature review (Yin, 2018). Participatory observation was conducted through
direct involvement in production activities and batik sales situations in the tourism area, including
observing interactions among craftspersons, vendors, and visitors. The observation procedure is
more accurately described as non-participant field observation rather than participatory
observation, as the researcher observed the activities of Batik Tulis craftspersons and vendors
without directly participating in the production or sales processes. The fieldwork was conducted
over a three-week period comprising sequential phases: interviews with the Batik Tulis craftsperson
group and its vendor (three days), interviews with individual craftspersons and traders around the
tomb area (three days), interviews with local government officials from the Tuban Regency tourism
and creative-economy offices (five days), interviews with religious tourists and batik buyers at the
site (five days), non-participant field observation (three days), and secondary-data collection from
the craftsperson group and government agencies. During the three observation days, three site
visits were conducted: one visit to the local batik craftsperson group's production site to observe
the batik-making process from wax-drawing through dyeing and drying, and two visits to the Tomb
of Sheikh Ibrahim Asmoroqondi to observe the display, marketing, and sale of Batik Tulis
souvenirs to religious tourists. Each observation visit lasted approximately four to five hours,
covering the peak visitor period from late morning through the afternoon. These observations
were conducted alongside semi-structured interviews, allowing observational evidence to
complement and verify participants' accounts . Documentation was used to collect visual and
written traces such as photographs, brochures, and official reports as comparative material to
strengthen the field findings.

condensation, data display, and conclusion drawing/verification following the framework of   Miles
et al. (2023). Interview transcripts, field observation notes, and documentary materials were
reviewed from the outset of data collection and managed using NVivo 14 (NVIVO, 2025) to
enhance the transparency and traceability of the analytical process. A hybrid coding strategy was

Ethical considerations
All informants involved in this study were adults and participated voluntarily after receiving
information about the research objectives, the intended use of the data, and the measures taken to
ensure confidentiality. Verbal informed consent was obtained before interviews and field
observations commenced. Informants were assigned anonymous codes (I1–I11), and all personally
identifying information was removed from transcripts and research records.
Because the study was conducted within a relatively small and closely connected cultural
tourism community, additional measures were taken to minimize the risk of indirect identification.
Informants were reported only according to broad stakeholder categories rather than identifiable
organizational positions. Furthermore, direct quotations were carefully reviewed to avoid
combining multiple distinguishing characteristics, such as organizational role, length of experience,
specific craft specialization, or other contextual details, that could enable readers to infer an
individual's identity. Where necessary, potentially identifying contextual information was
generalized while preserving the substantive meaning of the participants' accounts. Observation
settings and contextual descriptions were likewise reported at an appropriate level of generality to
protect the anonymity of individual craftspersons, vendors, and other participants. Because the
study focuses on the only recognized batik craftsperson group operating within this tourism area,
full anonymization at the group level could not be entirely achieved, as the group's identity may be
inferable from its association with the specific tourism site described. To mitigate this residual risk,
no individual names, specific job titles, or unique personal identifiers were disclosed in the
manuscript, and all informants were informed during the consent process that although their
individual identities would be protected through coding and category-level reporting, the
community-level setting of the study might remain recognizable. Beyond the anonymous coding
system (I1–I11) and category-level reporting, the identification-reduction measures applied in this
study therefore comprised: (a) reviewing direct quotations to prevent the co-occurrence of multiple
distinguishing attributes, (b) generalizing potentially identifying contextual details while preserving
substantive meaning, (c) reporting observation settings at an appropriate level of generality, and (d)
restricting access to raw transcripts and field notes to the research team throughout the study.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Batik Tulis as a Reinforcer of Pilgrimage Experience and Local Identity
The findings show that Batik Tulis around the Tomb of Sheikh Ibrahim Asmoroqondi is
not treated merely as a commodity but as an integral part of the religious tourism experience.
Although visitors primarily come to the site for pilgrimage, their experience is enriched by
encountering a distinctive expression of local culture that can be taken home as a meaningful
souvenir. The NVivo coding summary on the role of Batik Tulis in the religious tourism experience
is presented in Table 2. This relationship between pilgrimage motivation and awareness of local
cultural products was expressed by a religious tourist, who stated,

“I came to the Tomb of Sheikh Ibrahim Asmoroqondi for pilgrimage and to seek blessings. I also knew
that this place has its own distinctive hand-written batik, so I wanted to buy it as a souvenir of my visit”
(I8, translated from Indonesian).

This perception is echoed by a Batik Tulis craftsperson, who explained,

“Every Batik Tulis we produce carries the local cultural heritage and the Islamic values associated with the
history of Sheikh Ibrahim Asmoroqondi. Through these motifs, we want visitors not only to purchase batik
but also to understand the history and spiritual values represented in this place”  (I2, translated from
Indonesian).

Together, these accounts demonstrate that Batik Tulis functions not only as a commercial
product but also as a symbolic representation of pilgrimage experience, local identity, and cultural
memory, enabling visitors to take home both a tangible souvenir and an intangible cultural
experience.

Recognition of Cultural Value Does Not Automatically Lead to Purchase
Although Batik Tulis carries strong cultural value, the findings indicate that recognition of
this value does not automatically translate into a purchase decision. Batik Tulis competes with other
souvenirs that are cheaper, more practical, and easier to access. The coding summary on the
competition between Batik Tulis and other souvenirs is presented in Table 3.

Table 3 highlights the core issue: the main barrier for Batik Tulis is not the absence of
interest, but the logic of tourist shopping, which is highly sensitive to price and practicality. In
terms of price, Batik Tulis can cost between Rp500,000 and Rp3,000,000, while printed batik is
generally priced between Rp100,000 and Rp150,000. This sharp price gap risks placing Batik Tulis
as a product that is appreciated as good and valuable, yet not prioritised in brief and practical tourist
shopping. This price gap also implies that visitors are not a single undifferentiated market: budget-
conscious pilgrims are more likely to gravitate toward the lower-cost printed batik or other
inexpensive keepsakes, while visitors with greater willingness to pay for authenticity form a smaller
but distinct segment for premium, handmade Batik Tulis. This suggests a need for differentiated
product tiers, for example, premium handmade pieces alongside smaller, more affordable batik
accessories or motif-story cards, rather than a single product-and-price strategy aimed at all visitors
alike. This differentiation was raised directly by informants. A vendor noted that few visitors buy
Batik Tulis specifically because of its price relative to alternatives: “It’s just that few visitors are
interested in buying Batik Tulis as a souvenir because its price is above that of Pekalongan or
Gedog batik the cheapest option here still starts at Rp500,000” (I6, 2025, translated from
Indonesian). A government informant went further, explicitly recommending tiered products:
“Visitors coming to pray or visit the site tend to be more interested in buying affordable
souvenirs… priced around Rp100,000 for three pieces. This is a real opportunity for craftspersons
here if they are willing to diversify by also producing stamped or printed batik, without abandoning
the original hand-written batik” (Informant 11, 2025, translated from Indonesian).
Consequently, strengthening Batik Tulis requires more than additional promotion; it also
requires reorganizing the way the product appears before visitors. Such strategies may include
product variations and price ranges, the packaging of the shopping experience, and easier product
access without sacrificing cultural value. The main issue is not that the product is unattractive, but
that there is a distance between cultural appreciation and the ability or willingness to buy. Visitors
may admire Batik Tulis as an artwork and local symbol, but when tourist shopping operates quickly,
with price sensitivity and convenience in mind, Batik Tulis often loses at the point of decision.
Beyond market factors, structural barriers also arise from the side of the actors. Optimizing
digital marketing is considered important, but it is still constrained by limited access to technology
and limited digital literacy. Many craftspersons and vendors have not been able to use social media
and e-commerce consistently. More specifically, these constraints span several distinct capabilities:
producing consistent product photography and captions, maintaining a regular posting schedule,
managing online orders and payments, and sustaining an e-commerce storefront over time, rather
than any single missing skill. Field evidence indicates the constraint is less a basic literacy gap than
a lack of coordination: individual actors have begun establishing their own social media presence,
a craftsperson-group leader described uploading product photographs together with information
about each motif (I2, 2025, translated from Indonesian), and a vendor described posting content
about the batik-making process and following relevant tourism accounts to widen her reach (I6,
2025, translated from Indonesian). However, this activity remains individual and informal rather
than a coordinated group strategy, posting frequency, content standards, and order-and-payment
handling are not consistent across actors. A government informant described a broader strategic
intent that has not yet been matched at the individual-actor level: “We use several strategies to

The findings confirm that Batik Tulis in the religious tourism area of the Tomb of Sheikh
Ibrahim Asmoroqondi is not merely a textile commodity, but an experiential product. Batik carries
cultural symbols, a sense of authenticity, and spiritual meaning that visitors interpret as reasons to
know or purchase the product. In Tuban, a coastal area with a strong pilgrimage tradition, cultural

that express local culture, such as wayang motifs, as well as motifs carrying religious elements”

succinctly: “Full of spiritual meaning” (I7, 2025, translated from Indonesian). It is worth noting
explicitly that mega mendung, named by I9, is conventionally associated with Cirebon batik rather
than with Tuban’s own Batik Gedog tradition. Its presence among the motifs on offer near the
Tomb most likely reflects that local vendor also stock batik sourced from other regions alongside
locally produced Gedog work, rather than mega mendung being an original Tuban Gedog design;
this distinction is flagged here so that readers do not mistake it for a native Tuban motif. At the
destination level, cooperation between craftspersons and the tourism sector is crucial so that batik
is not merely displayed as a souvenir, but is positioned as part of the religious tourism experience
through education, stories, and interaction. The purple economy framework helps read this
situation: economic value emerges when culture is transformed into creativity, promotion, and
cross-sector collaboration that makes batik both a cultural ambassador and a driver of the local
economy.
This reading of Batik Tulis also connects to Islamic socio-economic studies more directly.
Community-based economic activity organized around a pilgrimage site sits within a broader
tradition of Islamic economic thought that treats livelihood generation around religious practice
and communal welfare (maslahah) as legitimate and interconnected goals, rather than treating the
sacred and the economic as separate domains. Research on Sharia-compliant management of Wali-
tomb pilgrimage destinations in East Java shows that applying Sharia principles to destination
management can strengthen both tourist satisfaction and the local economy (Kusumaningtyas et
al., 2024), while research on the commodification of sacred tomb sites cautions that economic
activation of religious space must be balanced against the preservation of its sanctity (Ghozi et al.,
2025). Read together with these studies, Batik Tulis functions as one concrete mechanism through
which a religious destination's economic and spiritual dimensions are integrated: value is generated
through the pilgrimage experience itself, and the craftspersons, vendors, and local government who
sustain that value form a community-based economic ecosystem consistent with Islamic
conceptions of shared, place-based livelihood. This integration was articulated directly by a
craftsperson, who described the cultural value embedded in her service as honoring “the legacy of
Islamic da’wah brought by the wali along the north coast of Java,” explaining that every batik
product was intended to remind visitors “of the history and spiritual values present in this area,
which are closely tied to the spread of Islam by the wali” (I2, 2025, translated from Indonesian).
This account illustrates, at the level of everyday practice, how craftspersons themselves frame their
livelihood as continuous with, rather than separate from, the religious heritage of the site, lending
direct field support to the maslahah-based reading proposed above.

that buyers pursue meaning, not simply function. This finding strengthens studies showing that the
cultural load perceived by tourists can influence purchase intention through perceived value and

A Cultural Entrepreneurship Model Based on the Purple Economy
The cultural entrepreneurship model based on the purple economy in this study was
developed by connecting two main clusters of findings. Methodologically, the model was built
abductively: its four components were derived inductively from the two clusters of field findings
described below, then interpreted and organized using the purple economy framework introduced
in the literature review, rather than being deduced directly from theory or induced without any
theoretical guidance. The first concerns batik as a reinforcer of pilgrimage experience, where
spiritual meaning, philosophical information, and cultural value act as triggers of visitor attachment.
The second concerns the prominent constraints, opportunities, and strengthening strategies,
particularly price sensitivity, practicality, sales-point accessibility, the need for digital marketing, and
multi-stakeholder collaboration. Conceptually, the purple economy reads culture as a source of
value creation. Therefore, the core of the model lies in how the symbolic value of batik is activated
into economic value through experience orchestration and market integration.

Second, experience orchestration transforms symbolic value into experienced value
through on-site interactions, such as micro-storytelling, brief demonstrations of the production
process, motif-story labels or cards, sales-point arrangement, and the linkage between batik and the

illustrated by I8's account above (see Batik Tulis as a Reinforcer of Pilgrimage Experience and
Local Identity). This experience-orchestration component addresses the price, practicality, and
accessibility barriers reported in Table 3.
Third, market-access integration addresses the gap between cultural appreciation and
purchase decisions constrained by price, practicality, and accessibility. Batik vendor confirmed this
challenge by stating,

“Sales during the pilgrimage season have not increased significantly because hand-written batik is more
expensive than printed batik, and many visitors prefer cheaper products from other regions” (I6, translated
from Indonesian).

This integration may be pursued through differentiated product portfolios and price ranges,
consistency between online and offline narratives, and the activation of promotional networks
through influencers, events, exhibitions, and measured promotional activities.
Fourth, collaborative governance acts as an enabler that maintains the sustainability of the
model. Craftspersons and vendors maintain quality and innovation without breaking away from
tradition; destination managers act as curators of experience and connectors of place-based
narratives; while local government serves as a facilitator of training, assistance, regulation, curation,
and market networks. This collaborative role was highlighted by a local government representative,
who noted,

“The key strategy is stronger collaboration among Batik Tulis producers, government agencies, tourism
managers, and educational institutions. Broader digital promotion and stronger partnerships are needed so
that Batik Tulis becomes a cultural icon of Tuban” (I10, translated from Indonesian).

This governance component builds directly on the marketing strategies in Table 4 and the
multi-stakeholder collaboration patterns in Table 5. In the logic of the purple economy, the output
of the model is purple value, namely cultural value that is monetised appropriately through
increased willingness to pay or buying intention, expanded market reach beyond the destination,
and stronger production sustainability and regeneration of craftspersons.
From the perspective of MSME market integration, the findings on the dominance of
online-offline marketing channels, price and practicality barriers, and the importance of multi-
stakeholder collaboration show that the Batik Tulis market in Tuban extends from the pilgrimage
space to the digital space. Market integration means managing the connection among sales points
at the destination, online channels, and event networks so that visitors can interact with batik
before, during, and after their visit. Thus, Batik Tulis is not understood as the outcome of economic
activity, but as a mediator of the value process within the religious tourism ecosystem.

CONCLUSION
This study shows that the creation of value in Batik Tulis as a religious tourism souvenir
does not move directly from culture to economic transaction, but through a set of interrelated
stages. The cultural value of batik is first interpreted by visitors as a symbolic and spiritual
experience within the context of pilgrimage. This meaning-making process encourages appreciation
of local identity and cultural authenticity, yet such appreciation does not automatically convert into
a purchase decision because it is constrained by price sensitivity, practicality, and limited product
access.
The findings show that the conversion of cultural value into economic value is mediated
by strategies that orchestrate experience and market access. These strategies include strengthening
cultural narratives, arranging the purchasing experience at the destination, integrating offline and
digital channels, and fostering collaboration among craftspersons, tourism actors, and local
government. Within the purple economy framework, culture functions as a source of value creation
when it is activated through creative processes, the communication of meaning, and collaborative
governance, rather than being treated merely as product differentiation.
Therefore, Batik Tulis in religious tourism destinations has real economic potential, but
this potential is not automatic. It depends on the ability of actors and stakeholders to manage the
value creation process consistently and contextually. This study confirms that a culture-based
economy in religious destinations should be understood as a dynamic process that connects
experience, markets, and the sustainability of local culture. The limitation of this study lies in the
limited number of cases and research sites, in the reliance on a single case study with 11 informants,
and in the qualitative, researcher-interpreted nature of the analysis. In particular, saturation claims
for the smaller informant subgroups, visitors (n=3) and local government representatives (n=2),
remain more tentative than for the craftsperson category (n=6), and should be read as indicative
of consistency within the available sample rather than as an exhaustive claim. Future studies may
expand the range of destinations, visitor segments, and comparative approaches to test the
transferability of the model.
By placing Batik Tulis within a pilgrimage-based Muslim destination, this study contributes
to Islamic socio-economic studies by showing how religious space, cultural memory, and local
entrepreneurship can interact to support community-based value creation.
