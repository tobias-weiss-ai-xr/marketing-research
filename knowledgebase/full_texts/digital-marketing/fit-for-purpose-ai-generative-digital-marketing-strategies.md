# Fit-for-Purpose AI-Generative Digital Marketing Strategies

Chapter
Fit-for-Purpose AI-Generative
Digital Marketing StrategiesXolani
Khayelihle NgobeseAbstract
This
chapter examines the development of fit-for-purpose artificial intelligence
(AI)-generative digital marketing strategies for organisations operating in
developing and emerging economies, where digital transformation is often
constrained by affordability challenges, weak infrastructure, limited workforce
capability, governance gaps, and regulatory uncertainty. Existing digital marketing
literature frequently assumes high levels of digital maturity and institutional
readiness that do not adequately reflect the realities of resource-constrained
environments. Consequently, many AI-driven marketing initiatives fail not because
of weak technology but because they lack contextual relevance, strategic coherence,
governance capability, and measurable organisational alignment. Drawing on the
principles of good strategy, this chapter applies diagnosis, guiding policy, and
coherent strategic action to AI-enabled marketing strategy development. The chapter
further introduces an additional principle: evaluating strategic impact. This principle
addresses a persistent weakness in digital marketing strategy, where organisations
overemphasise adoption metrics and operational indicators while neglecting long-
term value creation, customer trust, governance integrity, profitability, and
sustainability. The chapter proposes a fit-for-purpose strategic framework for AI-
generative digital marketing strategy development, supported by a policy framework
for ethical AI governance and risk management. The framework positions AI not as
a technological trend but as a strategic capability requiring alignment between
organisational readiness, governance structures, workforce capability, customer
value creation, and continuous evaluation. The chapter concludes that successful AI
adoption depends on strategic discipline, contextual intelligence, ethical governance,
and measurable strategic outcomes.
Keywords: generative artificial intelligence, digital marketing strategy, strategic
impact evaluation, ethical AI governance, fit-for-purpose strategy, emerging
economies1.
IntroductionThe
digital economy has fundamentally transformed how organisations design,
communicate, and implement marketing strategies [1, 2]. Artificial intelligence (AI),
particularly Generative AI, has emerged as one of the most disruptive forces shaping 1

customer
engagement, operational efficiency, data-driven decision-making, and
competitive positioning [2]. Generative AI technologies now support hyper-
personalisation, predictive analytics, automated content creation, conversational
marketing, customer segmentation, and omnichannel engagement [ 2–4]. These
developments are redefining marketing from a predominantly communication-
oriented function into a predictive, adaptive, and intelligence-driven strategic
capability [2].
Despite these advancements, the global discourse surrounding AI-enabled
marketing strategy often reflects assumptions associated with technologically
mature economies [1]. Existing literature frequently presumes the availability of
advanced digital infrastructure, institutional readiness, financial sustainability,
governance maturity, and highly skilled workforces [ 1, 5]. Such assumptions do not
adequately represent the realities of organisations operating within developing and
emerging economies, where affordability constraints, limited digital infrastructure,
workforce capability shortages, governance weaknesses, and regulatory uncertainty
remain significant barriers to digital transformation [ 1, 4, 5].
Consequently, many AI-driven marketing initiatives fail not because of
technological inadequacy but because they are not fit-for-purpose [5]. Organisations
frequently adopt AI technologies under competitive pressure or market enthusiasm
without adequately diagnosing organisational realities, defining strategic priorities,
or establishing the governance and capability structures necessary for sustainable
implementation [1, 6, 7]. This creates a widening gap between technological
ambition and strategic execution [ 1, 5].
A fit-for-purpose strategy recognises that technology adoption must align with
organisational capability, customer context, governance maturity, operational
readiness, and long-term strategic objectives [1, 7]. Strategies that ignore contextual
realities often result in fragmented implementation, weak return on investment,
reputational risk, poor customer trust, and unsustainable transformation outcomes
[1, 5, 8].
At the same time, generative AI presents significant opportunities for
organisations operating within emerging markets [ 4]. The AI-enabled marketing
strategies can improve customer engagement, enhance customer experience,
strengthen targeting precision, increase operational efficiency, and improve
strategic scalability through hyper-personalisation, predictive engagement,
conversational marketing, and automated content generation [ 2–4]. These
capabilities may help organisations overcome resource limitations by improving
decision-making quality and operational responsiveness [ 1, 2].
Successful implementation, however, requires more than technological adoption
[1, 9]. Organisations must address strategic issues involving affordability,
infrastructure capability, workforce readiness, cybersecurity, governance integrity,
ethical oversight, and regulatory compliance [1, 8, 9]. AI implementation, therefore,
requires coherent strategic thinking rather than isolated technological
experimentation [6, 7].
This chapter addresses this gap by proposing a fit-for-purpose AI-generative digital
marketing strategy framework grounded in the principles of good strategy [7].
Effective strategy is not built on ambition alone but on disciplined problem-solving
that identifies the real challenge, establishes a guiding policy, and designs coherent
actions that reinforce one another [7]. Building on this perspective, the chapter
introduces a fourth strategic principle: evaluating strategic impact [6, 7]. This Marketing

Generative AI in digital marketing: Strengths, challenges, and
opportunitiesGenerative
AI is transforming digital marketing from a communication-centred
activity into a predictive, adaptive, and intelligence-driven strategic capability [2, 3].
Unlike traditional digital technologies that primarily improve operational efficiency,
Generative AI contributes to strategic decision-making by enabling organisations
to interpret customer behaviour, anticipate market changes, personalise
engagement, optimise resource allocation, and automate complex marketing
functions [1, 2].
The strategic significance of Generative AI is particularly important within
developing and emerging economies, where organisations frequently operate under
severe resource constraints [ 1, 4, 5]. Limited budgets, fragmented customer data,
weak infrastructure, workforce capability shortages, and governance limitations
make effective strategic decision-making increasingly difficult [ 1, 4]. Under these
conditions, AI adoption must be carefully prioritised to ensure that investments
create measurable strategic value rather than technological complexity without
sustainable returns [1, 5, 7].
The strategic value of Generative AI, therefore, lies not merely in automation, but
in its ability to support strategic intelligence, organisational adaptability, customer-
centricity, and long-term competitiveness [2, 3, 6]. However, achieving these
outcomes requires organisations to balance the strengths, challenges, and opportunities
associated with AI adoption within a coherent strategic framework [6, 7].3.
Applying the principles of good strategy to AI marketingThe
principles of good strategy provide a valuable foundation for understanding
how organisations should approach AI-enabled digital marketing [ 7]. Effective
strategy is not built on slogans, ambition, or technological enthusiasm but on
disciplined problem-solving that identifies the real challenge and aligns
organisational action toward meaningful outcomes [ 7].
Three interconnected principles underpin effective strategic development:
diagnosis of the core challenge, establishment of a guiding policy, and design of
coherent strategic actions [ 7]. These principles are particularly relevant within AI-
enabled digital marketing because many organisations adopt AI technologies without
clearly identifying the underlying business problem they are attempting to solve
[1, 5].
The first responsibility of strategy is diagnosis [ 7]. Organisations must identify
the central challenge preventing meaningful progress or competitive advantage [ 7]. Fit-for-Purpose

Strategic success further depends on coherent actions that reinforce one another
rather than disconnected initiatives [ 7]. In AI-driven marketing, coherent strategic
action requires coordinated investment in data systems, workforce capability
development, governance and ethical oversight, cybersecurity protection, customer
engagement systems, organisational change management, and performance
measurement mechanisms [1, 8, 9].4.
Evaluating strategic impactMany
organisations implement digital marketing strategies without adequately
assessing whether those strategies create meaningful long-term value [ 2, 3, 6].
Contemporary digital marketing practice frequently relies on vanity metrics such as
clicks, impressions, engagement rates, likes, shares, and follower growth [ 2].
Although these indicators may support operational monitoring, they do not
necessarily reflect strategic success [ 6].
A fit-for-purpose AI strategy must evaluate deeper strategic outcomes, including
customer trust, customer retention, profitability, governance integrity, ethical
compliance, organisational resilience, competitive positioning, and sustainable value
creation [2, 3, 6, 7].
Without evaluation, strategy becomes an assumption rather than evidence-based
decision-making [7]. Evaluating strategic impact is particularly important within
developing and emerging economies, where organisational resources are limited and
investment decisions carry significant operational risk [ 1, 4].5.
Framework for developing fit-for-purpose AI-generative digital
marketing strategiesTo
address the complexity of AI adoption in digital marketing, this chapter
proposes a fit-for-purpose strategic framework grounded in the principles of
diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent strategic action, and evaluation of strategic
impact [9]. Digital transformation is not fundamentally a technology challenge; it is
a strategic and managerial imperative requiring leadership alignment, governance
structures, workforce capability, organisational readiness, agile execution, and
measurable value realisation [ 1].
The proposed framework, therefore, integrates strategic diagnosis, institutional
readiness, governance capability, implementation sequencing, and continuous
evaluation into a coherent organisational process [ 1, 9]. The framework follows Marketing

a
strategic ‘golden thread’, linking problem identification, strategic direction,
organisational readiness, governance systems, implementation, ethical oversight,
and measurable business outcomes [ 1, 9].
The framework begins with diagnosis because organisations must first
understand the strategic challenge before selecting technological solutions [ 9]. Once
the problem is clearly understood, a guiding policy establishes strategic direction and
prevents fragmented decision-making [ 9]. Coherent actions then translate strategy
into aligned implementation across governance systems, workforce capability,
data management, cybersecurity, customer engagement, and operational processes
[1, 8, 9]. Finally, evaluation ensures that implementation produces measurable
business value rather than operational activity alone [ 6, 9].
The framework consists of problem definition and market diagnosis, AI
capability alignment, organisational readiness assessment, data strategy and
governance, execution and strategic orchestration, ethical AI governance and
risk management, and evaluation of strategic impact with continuous optimisation
[1, 2, 8, 9].6.
Synthesis and strategic analysisThe
analysis presented throughout this chapter demonstrates that the successful
adoption of AI-generative digital marketing strategies depends less on
technological sophistication and more on strategic alignment, governance capability,
organisational readiness, and contextual relevance [ 1, 6, 9]. Existing digital
marketing discourse frequently promotes AI as a transformative technological
solution capable of improving customer engagement, predictive analytics,
operational efficiency, and strategic scalability [ 2–4]. However, the literature
simultaneously reveals that technological capability alone does not guarantee
sustainable strategic success [ 1, 5].
The synthesis of the literature demonstrates that AI implementation failures are
frequently strategic failures rather than technological failures [ 1, 9]. Organisations
may possess sophisticated AI systems yet still fail to achieve sustainable value
because leadership alignment, workforce capability, governance structures,
cybersecurity readiness, ethical oversight, and performance evaluation mechanisms
remain underdeveloped [1, 8, 9].
The analysis further reveals that organisations operating within developing and
emerging economies face fundamentally different implementation realities from
those assumed in much of the mainstream AI literature [ 1, 4, 5]. Existing research
often presumes access to advanced infrastructure, digital maturity, financial
capability, highly specialised workforces, and institutional readiness [ 1]. However,
many organisations within developing economies continue to experience
affordability limitations, infrastructure instability, governance weaknesses,
fragmented customer data systems, and cybersecurity vulnerabilities [ 1, 4, 5].
The literature also demonstrates a growing interdependence between AI
capability and governance integrity [ 2, 8, 9]. As AI systems increasingly influence
customer engagement, strategic communication, predictive analytics, and
automated decision-making, governance concerns become central to organisational
legitimacy and customer trust [ 2, 9].Fit-for-Purpose

ConclusionThis
chapter examined the development of fit-for-purpose AI-generative
digital marketing strategies within the context of developing and emerging
economies [1, 4, 5]. The analysis demonstrated that while generative AI offers
substantial opportunities for improving customer engagement, operational
efficiency, predictive analytics, strategic responsiveness, and competitive
positioning, successful implementation depends on far more than technological
capability alone [2–4].
The chapter established that many AI-driven marketing initiatives fail because
organisations adopt technologies without sufficient strategic diagnosis, governance
capability, organisational readiness, workforce development, or ethical oversight
[1, 5, 9]. These failures frequently result in fragmented implementation, weak return
on investment, poor customer trust, reputational exposure, and unsustainable
transformation outcomes [1, 5, 9].
The chapter, therefore, concludes that a fit-for-purpose strategy is essential for
sustainable AI implementation [ 1, 5, 9]. A strategy that ignores organisational
capability, governance maturity, contextual realities, and ethical accountability is
unlikely to succeed, regardless of technological sophistication [ 1, 5]. Conversely,
organisations that align AI implementation with coherent strategic principles,
governance integrity, organisational readiness, and continuous evaluation may
achieve sustainable competitive advantage within increasingly complex digital
environments [6, 9].8.
RecommendationsOrganisations
seeking to implement AI-generative digital marketing strategies
should prioritise strategic diagnosis before investing in technological solutions [ 9].
Understanding the real organisational challenge, customer need, market
opportunity, or governance weakness is essential for preventing fragmented
implementation and misaligned investments [ 1, 5].
Leadership teams should establish clear guiding policies that align AI adoption
with organisational purpose, governance capability, customer value creation, and
long-term sustainability [1, 9]. The AI implementation should not be treated as
a standalone innovation initiative but as an integrated organisational strategy
requiring cross-functional coordination and executive oversight [ 1].
Organisations should also invest significantly in workforce capability
development because digital transformation depends heavily on human competence,
organisational culture, and change management [ 1, 9]. Training programmes
focusing on AI literacy, data analytics, ethical governance, cybersecurity, and digital
strategy should, therefore, become strategic priorities [ 1, 9].Marketing
