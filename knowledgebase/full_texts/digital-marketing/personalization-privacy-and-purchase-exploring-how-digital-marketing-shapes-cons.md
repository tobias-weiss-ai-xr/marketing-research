Abstract
Digital marketing in India has undergone rapid transformation driven by algorithmic targeting, AI-enabled
recommendations, and data-rich platforms. Personalization has become central to consumer engagement, yet
it carries inherent tensions between convenience and privacy. This qualitative study explores how
personalization influences consumer spending across India and how privacy concerns shape these responses.
Guidedby the Stimulus–Organism–Response (S–O–R) framework and Privacy CalculusTheory, 38 in-depth
interviews were conducted with digital consumers from metropolitan (n=18), semi-urban (n=12), and rural
(n=8) regions. Thematic analysis revealed five major themes: (1) Personalization as Convenience and
Relevance, (2) Emotional Triggers and Impulsive Spending, (3) Privacy Anxiety and Algorithmic Intrusion,
(4) Trust as Mediator, and (5) The Personalization Paradox. Findings show that younger consumers value
personalization for its time-saving and relevance, while older consumers demonstrate stronger privacy
consciousness. Across demographics, participants experienced tension between appreciating personalized
suggestions and fearing surveillance. Trust in platforms emerged as a critical boundary-setting mechanism.
This study contributes to understanding personalization in emerging markets by highlighting cultural
variations, digital literacy, and contextual privacy interpretations. Implications urge marketers to adopt data-
empathicpersonalizationpracticesandpolicymakerstoimplementstrongerdigitalprivacyframeworks.

1. Introduction
Personalization has become a strategic core of digital
marketing, particularly within India’s rapidly
expanding digital economy. As platforms harness
data-driven algorithms to tailor content, offers, and
recommendations, consumers experience
unprecedented convenience in navigating online
marketplaces. India’s digitally enabled population—
diverse in age, language, culture, and digital
literacy—interacts with personalization in ways that
reflect unique socio-cultural contexts.
The dual nature of personalization is increasingly
visible: while it enhances relevance and reduces
search effort, it simultaneously raises concerns about
data misuse, behavioral manipulation, and loss of
privacy. In India, where awareness of digital rights
remains uneven, responses to personalization vary
widely. This study explores how consumers negotiate
personalization in their everyday online experiences
and how these negotiations influence spending
behaviour.
2. Literature Review
2.1 Personalization in Digital Marketing
Personalization has become a central pillar of digital
marketing, enabled by rapid advancements in
artificial intelligence, machine learning, and data-
driven algorithms. It involves tailoring marketing
content based on consumer-specific information such
as browsing patterns, previous purchases,
demographic attributes, and psychographic
indicators (Aguirre et al., 2015). Empirical studies
have consistently shown that personalization
enhances perceived relevance, improves engagement,
and contributes to higher conversion rates (Bleier &
Eisenbeiss, 2015; Tam & Ho, 2020). Recent research
further highlights that personalized content is most
effective when it aligns with consumer goals and
reduces information overload, particularly in mobile-
first digital markets (Alalwan, 2022). In India, where
online consumers increasingly rely on digital
platforms for discovery and decision-making,
personalization plays an important role in shaping
shopping experiences.
2.2 Emotional and Behavioral Responses to
Personalized Content
Personalization does not merely provide relevance; it
also triggers emotional responses that significantly
influence consumer behaviour. Studies indicate that
personalized cues evoke excitement, desire, and a
sense of urgency, often leading to impulsive decisions
(Kim & Han, 2020). Neuroscientific evidence suggests
that personalized recommendations activate reward

2.3 Privacy Concerns, Data Surveillance, and the
“Creepy Effect”
Despite its advantages, personalization raises
concerns about privacy, data security, and perceived
surveillance. Consumers often experience discomfort
when ads appear overly tailored or appear
immediately after a conversation or search, a
phenomenon known as the “creepy effect” (Xu et al.,
2012). Earlier research linked this discomfort to
uncertainty about how digital platforms gather and
process personal data; more recent studies highlight
that algorithmic opacity, unfair inference, and lack of
control intensify this discomfort (Gerlach et al., 2022).
Privacy concerns significantly diminish acceptance of
personalization by increasing perceptions of
vulnerability and loss of autonomy (Martin & Murphy,
2017). Emerging research also re-examines the
privacy paradox, finding that privacy concern and
online risk-taking are mediated by perceived
usefulness and inevitable reliance on digital services
(Barth & de Jong, 2021). These patterns align with
Privacy Calculus Theory, which suggests that
consumers systematically weigh perceived risks
against perceived benefits before engaging with
personalized content (Keith et al., 2016).
2.4 Trust and Platform Credibility as Moderators
Trust consistently emerges as a central factor
determining whether consumers accept or reject
personalized marketing. The Personalization–Privacy
literature indicates that consumers tolerate higher
levels of data usage when they trust the platform,
perceive it as secure, or consider it professionally
credible (Awad & Krishnan, 2006; Bleier & Eisenbeiss,
2015). Recent research has refined this
understanding, showing that trust is shaped by
algorithmic transparency, perceived fairness, and
clarity in data handling practices (Gerlach et al., 2022;
Kaushik & Rahman, 2022). In emerging markets,
trust has unique socio-cultural determinants. For
example, Indian consumers often display higher trust
in government-backed or widely recognized
platforms than in lesser-known digital brands (Singh
& Bala, 2020). Studies further demonstrate that
digital trust plays a pivotal role in determining
willingness to adopt online services, particularly in
contexts where awareness of privacy laws is limited
(Datt & Rana, 2023). Thus, trust functions as an
interpretative lens through which personalization is
viewed either as beneficial or intrusive.
2.5 The Personalization Paradox
The personalization paradox captures the
simultaneous attraction to and discomfort with
personalized digital experiences. While consumers
appreciate the convenience and relevance of
personalized content, they also express concern
about privacy loss, over-targeting, and unwanted
data tracking (Aguirre et al., 2015). Recent studies
expand on this paradox by illustrating how perceived
intrusiveness moderates engagement with
personalized ads, including click behavior and
purchase intent (Boerman et al., 2021). Cognitive
dissonance emerges when consumers are aware of
privacy risks yet continue using personalized
platforms for convenience, reflecting a behavioural
tension rooted in both emotional and rational
evaluations (Barth & de Jong, 2021). The paradox is
particularly pronounced in digitally diverse societies
such as India, where consumers often lack full
understanding of how personalization works but rely
heavily on digital ecosystems for daily tasks.
2.6 Digital Consumer Behaviour in India
India’s digital environment is unique due to
substantial cultural, linguistic, and infrastructural
heterogeneity. Research indicates that while urban
and younger consumers tend to embrace
personalization for convenience, rural and older
users demonstrate mixed reactions influenced by
lower digital literacy and uncertainty about data
usage (Thakur & Srivastava, 2018; Sarkar & Sarkar,
2022). Recent studies show that Indian consumers’
perceptions of AI-driven personalization are shaped
by awareness level, perceived control, and feelings of
empowerment or vulnerability (Ghosh & Tripathi,
2022). Additionally, trust in digital personalization is
often shaped by socio-cultural cues such as brand
familiarity, perceived legitimacy, and word-of-mouth
endorsement rather than technical understanding
(Paul & Roshan, 2021). These findings suggest that
personalization acceptance in India is influenced by
contextual, demographic, and psychological factors
that differ from those in Western markets.

Although global literature provides strong insights
into personalization, privacy, and trust, there
remains limited understanding of how these forces
interact within emerging markets marked by diverse
digital literacy levels. Existing studies often rely on
experimental or quantitative approaches and rarely
capture the rich emotional and cognitive processes
that shape personalization responses. Few studies
adopt an integrated theoretical approach combining

3. Theoretical Framework
The theoretical foundation of this study is anchored
in two complementary perspectives: the Stimulus–
Organism–Response (S–O–R) model and Privacy
Calculus Theory. Together, they help explain how
personalized digital marketing influences consumer
reactions within the Indian context.
3.1 Stimulus–Organism–Response (S–O–R) Model
The S–O–R model conceptualizes personalized
advertisements, recommendations, notifications, and
algorithmic nudges as stimuli that initiate internal
processing within consumers. These internal
processes—comprising both emotional and cognitive
reactions—form the “organism” component. In this
stage, consumers experience feelings such as
excitement, curiosity, irritation, or discomfort; they
also engage in cognitive evaluation, assessing
whether the content is trustworthy, relevant, or
intrusive. These internal states ultimately lead to a
behavioral response, such as impulsive buying,
intentional purchasing, disengagement, or deliberate
avoidance of ads.
3.2 Privacy Calculus Theory
Privacy Calculus Theory further enriches this process
by illuminating how consumers consciously or
subconsciously weigh the perceived benefits of
personalization—such as convenience, relevance,
and reduced search effort—against perceived risks
including data tracking, profiling, and surveillance.
This cost–benefit evaluation varies across age groups,
digital literacy levels, and cultural contexts, making it
particularly relevant for India’s diverse population.
By integrating S–O–R with Privacy Calculus Theory,
this study interprets personalization as both a
persuasive and potentially intrusive mechanism,
shaping complex emotional and cognitive
negotiations that influence consumer spending
behaviors.
A key refinement in the model used in this study is
the explicit positioning of Privacy Calculus as the
mediating mechanism between organismic reactions
and behavioral responses. While the S–O–R model
traditionally assumes a direct link between internal
states and outcomes, research increasingly shows
that digital consumption involves an evaluative step
wherein consumers assess whether the benefits of
personalization outweigh perceived privacy risks.
This evaluation—termed the privacy calculus—
mediates the pathway between emotional/cognitive
states (Organism) and behavioral choices (Response).
In this model, trust acts as an embedded component
of the privacy calculus, reducing perceived risk and
enabling acceptance of personalization. When trust is
low, the same organismic reactions (e.g., excitement
or relevance) may not translate into action due to
heightened privacy concerns. Thus, the mediator
clarifies why similar emotional reactions may
produce very different behavioral outcomes across
individuals. The combined framework—S–O–R with
Privacy Calculus mediation—provides a more
nuanced and contextually appropriate explanation of
personalization effects in digital markets such as
India.
4. Methodology
This study adopted a qualitative exploratory design
to deeply understand how Indian consumers
perceive and respond to personalized digital
marketing. Given the lack of sufficient contextual
research in emerging digital markets like India,
qualitative inquiry was deemed appropriate for
capturing nuanced interpretations, emotions, and
lived experiences. A purposive sampling strategy was
employed to ensure diversity across geography, age,
gender, occupation, and digital literacy. A total of
thirty-eight participants were recruited, comprising
eighteen metropolitan, twelve semi-urban, and eight
rural consumers, ranging in age from 18 to 55 years.
Semi-structured interviews lasting between 35 and
55 minutes allowed participants to reflect openly on
their experiences with personalized ads, data privacy
concerns, and purchase behaviours. Interviews were
conducted via phone, video calls, and in-person
sessions depending on accessibility.
All interviews were audio-recorded with consent,
transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using Braun and
Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis. NVivo software
supported the coding process by organizing
participant narratives into meaningful categories.
Analysis involved repeated reading of transcripts,
open coding, theme development, and refinement
through constant comparison. Reflexive memoing
helped maintain researcher awareness of biases
during interpretation. Data saturation was reached
after thirty-three interviews; however, all thirty-eight
transcripts were included to strengthen contextual
richness. Themes were finalised based on recurrence,
representativeness across demographic groups, and
theoretical alignment with S–O–R and Privacy
Calculus frameworks, ensuring credibility,
confirmability, and transferability of findings.

Theme 4: Trust as the Central Mediator of
Personalization Acceptance
Emerging across all interviews was the recognition
that trust serves as a pivotal mechanism governing
whether personalization is interpreted as helpful or
intrusive. This theme integrates subthemes of
platform reputation, perceived security, and cultural
trust patterns, which together illuminate how
consumers consciously categorize companies into
“trustworthy” and “untrustworthy” before engaging
with personalized content. Participants consistently

platforms felt safer. MET-M-32 said, “If the platform
is reliable, I don’t mind sharing data. But random
websites? No chance.” Similarly, SU-F-35 emphasized
the importance of transparency, stating, “I trust
Myntra and Amazon because they look professional.

reputational cues rather than technical knowledge.

narratives show that trust moderates the privacy
calculus: when trust is high, consumers accept
personalization despite potential risks; when trust is
low, the same features become suspicious or
threatening. In this way, trust acts as a psychological
filter shaping the ultimate acceptance or rejection of
personalization.
Theme 5: The Personalization Paradox —
Simultaneous Attraction and Aversion
The final theme synthesizes insights from all earlier
themes, revealing the paradoxical coexistence of
appreciation and discomfort that characterizes the
Indian consumer’s relationship with personalization.
This theme connects subthemes of benefit–risk
evaluation, boundary negotiation, and cognitive
dissonance, showing how consumers constantly shift
between embracing personalization and resisting it.
Participants often described personalization as both
“useful” and “creepy,” highlighting their ambivalence.
MET-F-22 captured this tension succinctly: “It’s
helpful but creepy. That’s the truth.” SU-M-30
similarly expressed conflicted feelings: “I like the
recommendations but also hate how apps seem to
know too much about me.” This oscillation between
attraction and aversion illustrates how
personalization triggers both convenience-driven
engagement and privacy-driven withdrawal. The
paradox was particularly evident in situations where
consumers enjoyed algorithmic accuracy while
simultaneously fearing the mechanisms enabling it.
Rural users echoed this ambivalence, with RUR-F-38
stating, “It is good and bad both. Hard to say whether
I like it or not.” These narratives demonstrate that
personalization is not evaluated in a linear way;
instead, consumers repeatedly renegotiate their
comfort levels as they encounter new digital
experiences, leading to a fluid and sometimes
unstable pattern of digital behavior.
Model Derived - Conceptual Framework
Despite substantial scholarly attention to digital
personalization, prior research reveals fragmented
explanations regarding how consumers cognitively
and affectively interpret personalized marketing
stimuli, and how these interpretations translate into
actual behavioral outcomes. While personalization
has been shown to enhance relevance and facilitate
decision-making, it concurrently raises significant
concerns related to surveillance, data vulnerability,
and loss of autonomy. These competing forces
underscore the need for a theoretically coherent
model capable of explaining the duality inherent in
personalized digital environments. The Stimulus–
Organism–Response (S–O–R) framework offers a
foundational structure for understanding how
external marketing cues evoke internal states,
whereas Privacy Calculus Theory provides insight
into how consumers assess the trade-offs between
perceived benefits and risks associated with data-
driven personalization. However, existing studies
seldom integrate these perspectives to capture the
full sequence through which personalized stimuli
shape consumer behavior, particularly within
emerging digital markets such as India.
To address this conceptual gap, the present study
advances an integrated framework that synthesizes
the S–O–R paradigm with Privacy Calculus Theory,
positioning the benefit–risk evaluation mechanism as
a critical mediating process between internal
psychological reactions and behavioral responses.
This integrated approach more accurately reflects the
complex evaluative processes identified in literature
and observed in empirical data, particularly the
central role of trust in shaping privacy perceptions
and behavioral acceptance. The resulting conceptual
framework thus provides a comprehensive and
contextually relevant lens for analyzing how
consumers negotiate the tension between
personalization and privacy, and for understanding
the emergence of paradoxical behavioral patterns in
contemporary digital marketplaces.

6. Discussion
The findings of this study highlight the complex and
sometimes contradictory nature of Indian
consumers’ engagement with personalized digital
marketing. Personalization was widely perceived as
beneficial because it reduced cognitive load and
enhanced decision efficiency; yet this same
mechanism triggered emotional susceptibility,
especially through impulse-driven cues. These dual
outcomes reinforce the S–O–R framework by
showing how personalized stimuli activate both
positive (excitement, convenience) and negative (fear,
irritation) organismic states that ultimately shape
consumer responses. Privacy Calculus Theory is also
strongly supported, as participants evaluated
personalization through an implicit cost–benefit
negotiation—valuing relevance while simultaneously
fearing intrusion. Notably, the data illustrate that
privacy concern is not linearly associated with digital
literacy; even digitally active consumers experienced
privacy fatigue when personalization felt overly
precise. Trust emerged as a decisive moderator that
shifted perceptions across the benefit–risk
continuum. When trust was high, personalization
was reframed as helpful and intelligent; when trust
was low, the same features became suspicious and
unwelcome. These findings underscore that
personalization outcomes are socio-culturally
contingent: emotional norms, lived experiences,
institutional trust, and technological familiarity
collectively shape how personalization is interpreted.
This contextualized understanding is significant for
emerging digital economies, where regulatory
structures and consumer awareness continue to
evolve.
7. Implications
7.1 Managerial Implications
For practitioners, the results suggest the urgent need
to shift from data-intensive personalization toward
data-empathic personalization—an approach that
prioritizes consumer comfort, transparency, and
ethical relevance. Marketers should clearly
communicate how data is collected and used,
especially in markets where privacy literacy varies
widely. Designing personalization that feels
supportive rather than manipulative may reduce
privacy anxiety and increase acceptance. Emotional
triggers such as FOMO-driven offers should be used
cautiously to avoid encouraging unhealthy impulsive
behavior, which may erode long-term trust.
Platforms can also implement “intensity controls,”
allowing users to adjust the frequency or depth of
personalized recommendations. Overall,
personalization strategies must balance relevance
with restraint.
7.2 Policy Implications
The findings underscore the necessity of robust data
protection policies that are understandable and
accessible to the average citizen. Regulators must
prioritize public education around digital rights,
particularly in semi-urban and rural regions where
privacy awareness is relatively low. As India
implements the Digital Personal Data Protection
(DPDP) Act, clear enforcement mechanisms will be
essential to prevent exploitative targeting and ensure
accountability from digital platforms. Policymakers
may also consider mandating simplified privacy
disclosures and consent dashboards that empower
users to actively manage their data preferences.
7.3 Academic Implications
The study extends theoretical conversations on
personalization by demonstrating how emotional,
cognitive, and cultural dynamics interact within
algorithm-driven environments. It enriches S–O–R by
revealing the simultaneous coexistence of
contradictory organismic states—excitement and
anxiety—that shape behavioral outcomes. The
integration of Privacy Calculus Theory in a culturally
diverse, digitally unequal context provides evidence
that privacy negotiations are fluid, situational, and
influenced by social trust structures rather than
purely rational assessments. Future research may
explore longitudinal behavioral shifts as privacy
regulations solidify and technology literacy rises in
India.
