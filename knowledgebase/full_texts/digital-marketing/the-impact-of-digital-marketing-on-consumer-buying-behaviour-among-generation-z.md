Abstract
:
Digital marketing has changed the way products reach young consumers. Instead of a single advertisement seen on television,
a member of Generation Z now meets a brand through a short video, an influencer's recommendation, a customer review and a dis
count
c
ode, often within the same hour. This paper examines the extent to which such digital marketing activity influences the purch
asing
decisions of Generation Z consumers. Primary data were collected from 150 respondents aged between sixteen and twenty
-
four th
rough
a structured questionnaire, and the responses were analysed using simple percentages, mean scores and comparative charts. The

study
finds that digital marketing has a strong influence on the early stages of the buying process, particularly on product

discovery and

. Influencer

and promotional emails were

and social

credible, review
-
based and value
-

instead of high
-

1.

Introduction

1.1 Background of the study

Marketing has always followed the audience. When families
gathered around a radio, brands bought radio slots; when
television entered Indian homes, the thirty
-
second
commercial became the standard unit of persuasion.
Generation Z
—

the group born roughly b
etween 1997 and
2012
—

is the first generation whose attention has never been
concentrated in one place. Its members move between a
messaging app, a video feed and a shopping application many
times an hour, and marketing has followed them into each of
thes
e spaces.

Digital marketing refers to all promotional activity carried out
through digital channels: search engine advertising, social
media campaigns, influencer partnerships, e
-
mail and
messaging promotions, sponsored content, affiliate links and
application
-
based

offers. Its distinguishing feature is not the
screen but the two
-
way flow. A television advertisement is
delivered; a digital advertisement is delivered, measured,
skipped, commented upon, screenshotted and shared. For a
generation that has grown up insid
e this loop, the boundary
between entertainment, information and advertising has
become unusually thin.

This has practical consequences for firms. Advertising
budgets in India have shifted steadily towards digital
channels, and companies increasingly design products,
packaging and even pricing around how they will appear on a
screen. Whether this expenditure

actually changes what
young consumers buy, or merely changes what they notice, is
a question worth examining carefully.

1.2 Statement of the problem

Firms assume that greater digital visibility produces greater
sales. Young consumers, however, report advertising fatigue:
they skip, block and scroll past promotional material in large
numbers, yet they also buy products they first saw in exactly
that mat
erial. This apparent contradiction
-

high exposure with
low stated trust, and low stated trust with high actual
purchase
-

is the problem this study addresses. Without an
understanding of which digital formats genuinely influence
decisions and at which stage of the buying process, marketing
expenditure risks being spent on visibility that never converts
into behaviour.

1.3 Research question

To what extent does digital marketing influence the
purchasing decisions of Generation Z consumers?

1.4 Objectives of the study

1)

To identify the digital platforms and formats through
which Generation Z consumers encounter products most
frequently.

2)

To measure the perceived influence and perceived
trustworthiness of different digital marketing formats.

3)

To examine at which stage of the buying process digital
marketing exerts the greatest effect.

4)

To compare the responses of Generation Z with those of
an older consumer group.

5)

To suggest practical measures for marketers seeking to
reach this age group.

1.5 Hypotheses

H1:
Digital marketing exposure has a significant positive
effect on product discovery among Generation Z consumers.

H2:
User
-
generated content, such as reviews and influencer
opinion, is more persuasive than brand
-
generated advertising.

H3:
The influence of digital marketing weakens as the
consumer moves closer to the final purchase decision.

1.6 Significance of the study

The findings are relevant to three groups. For businesses,
particularly small and medium enterprises with limited
advertising budgets, the study indicates where marketing
money is likely to produce a return. For students of
commerce, it offers a structured

account of how classical
consumer behaviour models apply in a digital setting. For
young consumers themselves, an understanding of how
persuasion operates online is a form of practical financial
literacy.

1.7 Scope and limitations

The study is restricted to urban respondents aged sixteen to
twenty
-
four, and considers consumer goods commonly
bought by this group
-

clothing, footwear, electronic
accessories, cosmetics and food delivery. It does not extend
to high
-
value purchases such as vehicles or property, where
the decision process is longer and involves family members.
As the data are self
-
reported, the study me
asures perceived
rather than observed influence; this limitation is discussed in
Section 7.

2.

Review
o
f Literature

2.1 The shift from mass advertising to targeted
marketing

Classical marketing theory described a linear path: a
consumer moves from attention to interest, then to desire and
finally to action. This AIDA framework, still taught in
commerce classrooms, assumed that the marketer controlled
the message and the consum
er received it. Later models,
including the Engel
–
Kollat
–
Blackwell framework, added an
information
-
search stage in which the consumer actively
gathers data before deciding, and a post
-
purchase stage in
which satisfaction is evaluated.

Digital channels have expanded the information
-
search stage
enormously. A consumer can, in under a minute, read fifty
opinions from strangers about a product she has never held.
Several researchers have argued that this makes the modern
buying path less li
ke a funnel and more like a loop, in which
consumers repeatedly leave and re
-
enter the search stage
before committing.

2.2 Generation Z as a consumer group

Studies of Generation Z consistently describe three
characteristics. First, this group is comfortable with rapid
content and short attention spans, favouring video formats
under a minute. Second, it displays high scepticism towards
direct advertising claim
s but high trust in peer opinion,
including the opinion of strangers who appear authentic.
Third, it expects a purchase to be completed within the same
application in which the product was discovered, which
explains the growth of in
-
app and social commerce.

Indian studies add a further dimension: price sensitivity
remains high. Even where a brand is admired, discount codes,
exchange offers and festival pricing frequently determine the
final choice. Aspiration and thrift therefore operate together
rather than
in opposition.

2.3 Influencer marketing and social proof

Social proof
-

the tendency to treat the behaviour of others as
evidence of what is correct
—

is the mechanism most often
cited to explain influencer effectiveness. When a
recommendation arrives from a person whom the viewer
follows voluntarily, it is processed as advic
e rather than as
advertising. Research on parasocial relationships suggests
that regular viewers develop a one
-
sided sense of familiarity
with a creator, which reduces resistance to persuasion. The
effect weakens sharply, however, when the promotional
natu
re of the content is perceived as concealed.

2.4 Research gap

Most available studies measure either exposure or attitude.
Fewer examine the stage of the decision process at which
digital influence actually operates, and fewer still compare
Generation Z directly with an older group using the same
instrument. This stud
y addresses both gaps at a small scale.

2.5 Conceptual framework

Drawing on the literature above, the study adopts the model
shown in Figure 2.1. A digital stimulus produces exposure;
exposure is then filtered through psychological, informational
and social processes before a decision is reached; the outcome
of the purc
hase feeds back into the system as a review or a
recommendation, which becomes a stimulus for another
consumer.

3.3 Research instrument

Data were collected through a structured questionnaire of
eighteen items divided into four parts: demographic details,
platform usage, format evaluation on a five
-
point Likert scale,
and buying behaviour. The questionnaire was administered
online and the full item list appears in the Appendix. A pilot
test with fifteen respondents was carried out and two
ambiguous items we
re reworded before final circulation.

3.4 Method of analysis

Responses were tabulated and analysed using percentages,
mean scores and simple correlation. Charts were prepared to
display comparisons visually. Given the exploratory nature of
the study and its school
-
level scope, advanced statistical
modelling was deli
berately avoided; the emphasis is on clear
description supported by transparent figures.

3.5 Ethical considerations

Participation was voluntary and anonymous. No personal
identifiers were recorded, respondents under the age of
eighteen participated with the knowledge of the supervising
institution, and all data were used solely for academic
purposes.

5.

Discussion

Taken together, the findings suggest that the influence of
digital marketing on Generation Z is real but uneven. It is
strongest where the consumer has not yet formed an intention
-

at the discovery stage
-

and weakest at the point of
commitment, where independent evidence dominates. The
common industry assumption that more impressions produce
more sales therefore holds only for the earliest stage of the
journey.

younger group expects the entire process

6.

Conclusion
a
nd Recommendations

6.1 Answering the research question

Digital marketing influences the purchasing decisions of
Generation Z consumers substantially, but selectively. It
determines what this group sees, considers and shortlists; it
does not, by itself, determine what they finally buy. The
decisive influence at

the point of purchase comes from
independent evidence
-

reviews, comparisons and price
-

much
of which is itself generated by other consumers within digital
platforms. The honest answer to the research question is
therefore that digital marketing sets the agenda of choice
more than it dictates the outcome of choice.

6.2 Recommendations for marketers

•

Invest in earned credibility. Encouraging genuine
customer reviews produces a higher return than an
equivalent spend on display advertising.

•

Reduce frequency, raise quality. Repetition of low
-
value
creative material contributes directly to the advertising
fatigue reported by respondents.

•

Disclose partnerships clearly. Concealed promotion works
once; discovered concealment damages trust for a long
period.

•

Design for one
-
tap completion. Every additional step
between discovery and payment produces measurable
loss.

•

Treat price transparency as a marketing asset. Inflated
original prices next to discounts were the most frequently
cited source of distrust in the open
-
ended responses.

6.3 Recommendations for consumers and educators

•

Awareness of persuasion techniques should form part of
commerce and life
-
skills teaching at senior secondary
level.

•

A simple habit
-

leaving the platform to verify price and
reviews before buying
-

restores much of the decision
-
making control that the design of these applications is
intended to reduce.

7.

Limitations
a
nd Scope
f
or Further Research

Three limitations should be kept in view. The sample was
drawn by convenience from an urban school and college
network and is not statistically representative of Generation Z
in India as a whole. The data are self
-
reported, and
respondents may under
-
report

the influence of advertising
because admitting to being persuaded is socially
uncomfortable. Finally, the study captures a single point in
time in a field where platform behaviour changes within
months.

Future work could address these limitations by combining
survey data with observed behaviour, such as actual click and
purchase records shared voluntarily by respondents; by
extending the sample to smaller towns, where platform
preferences differ; and by t
racking the same respondents over
a year to see whether stated scepticism translates into changed
behaviour.
