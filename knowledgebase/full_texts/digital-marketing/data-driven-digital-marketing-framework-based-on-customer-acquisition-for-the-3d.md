ABSTRACT
The 3D printing industry in Indonesia is growing rapidly, yet many SMEs in this sector still run their
marketing without leveraging the customer data they already collect. Existing SME digital marketing
research remains generic and does not account for the  specific attributes of the 3D printing sector,
including material diversification, duration -based pricing, and pronounced segment heterogeneity,
leaving a significant research gap. This study fills this gap by developing a data driven digital marketing
framework based on customer acquisition, consisting of five stages: data collection, segmentation,
customer value analysis, strategy selection, and digital marketing execution with content strategy. The
framework was validated through a case study at Tridiku  Teknokarya Indonesia, a medium scale 3D
printing company with 1,484 orders and 401 unique customers from July to December 2025, analyzed
via mixed methods combining descriptive statistics with cross tabulation. The novelty lies in five
elements: integration of material dimensions and duration -based pricing, use of real transaction data,
linking behavior patterns to content strategy, discovery of a custom order effect (80.5% versus 49.5%
deal rate), and identification of a revenue concentration paradox (4.9 % of orders generating 52.7% of
revenue). Theoretically, the study connects purchase intention theory with 3D printing practice through
the custom order effect and extends customer profitability distribution theory through the revenue
concentration paradox. Managerially, the study recommends structured quotation forms to raise deal
rates, budget allocation by segment profitability rather than volume, data driven upsell pathways, and
a six phases campaign calendar synchronized with seasonal patterns to help SMEs shift from intuition
based to evidence-based marketing.

I.  INTRODUCTION
Over the past decade, additive manufacturing, commonly known as 3D printing, has
experienced remarkable expansion on a global scale.  Wohlers Associates (2024) documented that the
worldwide additive manufacturing market reached $20.035 billion in 2023, reflecting 11.1% growth and
breaching the $20 billion threshold for the first time.  Research and Markets ( 2024) anticipates the
market will expand from $24.9 billion in 2024 to $176.8 billion by 2034, corresponding to a compound
annual growth rate of 21.4%. Markets and Markets (2024) projects a market size of $37.4 billion by 2029,
while Additive Manufacturing Research ( 2025) estimates growth from $15.9 billion in 2024 to $57.8
billion by  2033. Thi s trajectory is propelled by accelerating adoption across healthcare, aerospace,
automotive, and consumer goods industries (Protolabs, 2024). Within Indonesia, the 3D printing service
landscape is predominantly shaped by SMEs that provide printing solutions to both individual and
organizational clients (Holzmann et al., 2020). These enterprises typically manage a handful of Fused
Deposition Modeling and resin printers, addressing demands that span from architectural scale models
and product prototypes to functional industrial components. Such a heterogeneous customer base poses
distinctive challenges for marketing strategy formulation.
According to Kotler and Keller (2016), the efficacy of marketing hinges on a firm’s capacity to
comprehend its customers, categorize them into meaningful segments, and craft offerings tailored to each
segment’s requirements. Nevertheless, a considerable number of 3D printing SMEs execute marketing
activities without sufficient analytical grounding.  The Marketing Centre ( 2024) reported that 67% of
SMEs engage in what they call “marketing in the dark,” where budgets are spent without measurement,
experiments are run without tracking, and channels are scaled without evidence of effectiveness. Within
the 3D printing domain, this behavior manifests as poorly defined target audiences, scattered channel
investments, and a lack of awareness regarding customer acquisition cost per channel (ScaleGrowth,
2026). The challenge is compounded by inherent characteristics of the 3D printing industry.  There is
pronounced material diversity, ranging from budget friendly PLA to premium resin at 14 times the cost
per gram. Pricing frameworks are driven primarily by machine time rather than raw material expense.
Customer segments are markedly varied, from deadline pressured students to manufacturing firms with
recurring part orders.
Prior research has examined digital marketing and customer management as separate
domains. Kumar et al. (2026) introduced an Integrated Digital Marketing Framework for SMEs drawing
upon Resource Based  View, Dynamic Capabilities  Theory, and Network  Theory. Supriyadi and
Pranyoto (2025) formulated a business intelligence driven digital marketing approach for SME market
expansion. Jain  et al. (2024) investigated pricing mechanisms for 3D Printing as a Service.
Holzmann et al. (2020) catalogued business model patterns among 3D printer manufacturers. Pulizzi
(2014) traced the development of content marketing as a strategic discipline.  Despite these
contributions, a notable void  persists. Existing SME digital marketing research tends to remain
generic, failing to account for the specific attributes of the 3D printing sector,  including material
diversification, duration-based pricing, and pronounced segment heterogeneity. Meanwhile, 3D printing
scholarship has predominantly prioritized technical and operational dimensions over data informed

The scientific contribution of this study lies in five elements of novelty. First, the proposed
model explicitly incorporates material dimensions and duration -based pricing structures specific to the

selection to include industry specific pricing and material variables. Second, the investigation draws on

customization behavior functions as a reliable proxy for purchase intention. Fifth, the research identifies
a revenue concentration paradox where a small fraction of orders generates the bulk of revenue while
the highest volume segment does not yield the highest per customer profitability, extend ing customer
profitability distribution theory to the 3D printing context. The framework was validated through a case
study at Tridiku Teknokarya Indonesia, leveraging a dataset of 1,484 orders from 401 unique customers

framework specific to the 3D printing industry; (2) evaluate the framework’s applicability at a medium
scale 3D printing enterprise; and (3) generate data informed digital marketing and con tent strategy
recommendations for each customer segment.
II.  LITERATURE REVIEW
Digital Marketing for SMEs
Chaffey and Ellis Chadwick ( 2019) characterize digital marketing as the attainment of
marketing objectives through the application of digital technologies and media platforms.  For SMEs,
digital channels present an opportunity to access broader markets at comparatively modest cost. Yet, as
Kumar et al.  (2026) observed, numerous SMEs deploy digital tools in a fragmented manner without
systematic integration, thereby failing to realize synergistic effects. Kotler and Keller (2016) articulated
the strategic marketing process across five sequential stages: grasping customer needs, crafting segment-
based strategy, constructing marketing programs, cultivating relationships, and capturing customer value.
This sequential logic underpins the framework proposed in the present study, where each stage builds
upon the analytical output of the preceding one.
Customer Acquisition and Segmentation
Customer acquisition encompasses the process of attracting and converting prospects into
paying customers(Salesforce, 2024). In this study, the acquisition process is examined through a
sequential funnel: inquiry, deal, repeat, and loyal. Wedel and Kamakura (2012) conceptualized market
segmentation as the partitioning of a heterogeneous market into homogeneous subgroups.
BusinessConceptor (2024) offered segmentation examples specific to 3D printing enterprises, noting
that customer categories in this industry often reflect distinct use cases rather than conventional
demographic splits. Within the 3D printing context, effective segmentation extends beyond demographic
variables to encompass need categories, material preferences, and purchasing behav ior patterns. This
multidimensional approach to segmentation aligns with Wedel and Kamakura’s ( 2012) assertion that
segmentation schemes should be grounded in behavioral and contextual data when demographic alone
prove insufficient.

Jain et al. (2024) delineated fixed fee and pay per build pricing models for 3DaaS. Sun et al.
(2024) investigated pricing approaches for 3D printing platforms, emphasizing the role of platform

Price Sensitivity and Purchase Intention
Chatterjee et al. ( 1988) identified reference value, comparison ease, and need urgency as
determinants of price sensitivity.  Zeithaml (1988) conceptualized purchase intention as a customer’s
readiness to transact, shaped by perceived value and perceived risk.  Varian ( 2014) su pplied the
microeconomic underpinnings for analyzing pricing and consumer choice, particularly the role of
information asymmetry in purchase decisions. In the 3D printing setting, factors such as student deadline
pressure and the degree  of customization can exert substantial influence on purchase intention. The
relationship between customization and purchase intention, in particular, has not been empirically
examined in the context of 3D printing services. The present study addresses this gap by quantifying
the deal rate differential between customized and standard orders, thereby linking Zeithaml’s ( 1988)
theoretical construct with operational data.
Content Marketing and Content Strategy
Pulizzi (2014) described content marketing as the creation and distribution of relevant, valuable
content to attract and retain a clearly defined audience.  Holliman and Rowley ( 2014) argued that
effective B2B content must address customer information requirements at every phase of the decision
journey, from awareness through post purchase evaluation. Li et al. (2024) studied customization within
3D printing enabled hybrid manufacturing supply chains, demonstrating that customization capabilities
reshape both supply chain dynamics and customer expectations. For SME 3D printing firms, a content
strategy underpinned by customer data can function as a potent competitive differentiator.  However,
the existing literature offers limited guidance on how 3D printing SMEs should translate customer
acquisition data into specific content themes, formats, and scheduling.  This study bridges that gap by
deriving content recommendations directly from transaction level analysis.
Consumer Behavior Theory
Consumer Behavior Theory, as articulated by Howard and Sheth (1969) in their Theory of Buyer
Behavior, posits that purchasing decisions are influenced by inputs from the marketing environment,
perceptual constructs through which consumers process these s timuli, and learning constructs through
which attitudes and intentions are formed. Ajzen (1991) extended this understanding through the Theory
of Planned Behavior, which argues that purchase intention is shaped by attitudes toward the behavior,
subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control.  In the 3D printing context, Consumer Behavior
Theory explains why customers who provide detailed customization specifications exhibit higher deal
rates. When a customer articulates dimensions, material preferences , or print orientation, they have
progressed through the perceptual and learning constructs to form a concrete purchase intention.  The
urgency of need, such as student thesis deadlines, further accelerates this process by reducing the
time available for deliberation, thereby strengthening the relationship between intention and action. This
theoretical lens directly informs the custom order effect documented in this study, where orders
accompanied by customization descriptions achieved an 80.5% deal rate com pared with 49.5% for
standard orders.
Customer Value Theory
Customer Value Theory, grounded in the work of Zeithaml (1988) and extended by Holbrook
(1994), conceptualizes value as a tradeoff between perceived benefits and perceived costs. Zeithaml’s
means end model posits that customers evaluate offerings based on their perception of what is received
relative to what is given, and that this evaluation shapes purchase intention. In the 3D printing context,

Customer Value Theory illuminates the inverse relationship between order size and deal rate observed
in this study. As order value increases, perceived financial risk rises, and the value tradeoff becomes
less favorable from the customer’s perspective. The finding that large orders exceeding IDR 1 million
convert at only 30.2% while small orders below IDR 50,000 convert at 62.4% reflects the operation of
this value calculus: customers facing higher financial stakes engage in more extensive evaluation,
comparison with alternatives, and deliberation before committing. This theory also explains the material
segment fit phenomenon, where premium materials such as Resin and PETG achieve higher deal rates
than base materials such as PLA, because customers selecting premium materials have already resolved
the value tradeoff in favor of perceived quality over cost.
Price Perception Theory
Price Perception Theory, as formulated by Monroe ( 1973), argues that buyers form subjective
perceptions of price based on internal reference prices, perceived sacrifice, and the perceived quality
signal embedded in the price.  Monroe’s framework establishes that price is not merely an objective
financial figure but a psychological construct that influences both purchase intention and p erceived
product quality. In the 3D printing context, Price Perception Theory provides a lens for understanding
why the deal rate declines systematically as order size increases. Customers evaluating large orders face
greater perceived sacrifice and have m ore opportunities for price comparison with alternative
manufacturing methods such as CNC machining or injection molding.  The finding that print duration
dominates the pricing structure, at 64 to 72% of total price, adds a dimension specific to additive
manufacturing that has not been explored in the price perception literature: when the primary cost driver
is machine time rather than material, customers may struggle to form accurate reference prices, leading
to increased deliberation and lower conversion rates. This  theory directly informs Pillar 2 (Price
Education) of the proposed content strategy, which addresses price perception gaps through transparent
cost breakdowns and estimation tools.
Relationship Marketing Theory
Relationship Marketing Theory, grounded in the commitment trust framework of Morgan and
Hunt (1994), posits that successful long term relationships between firms and customers are built
on commitment and trust, and that these relationships generate superior economic outcomes t hrough
reduced transaction costs, increased customer loyalty, and positive word of mouth.  Grönroos (1997)
extended this perspective by arguing that value is not created at the point of sale alone but throughout
the entire customer relationship, and that firms should shift from a transaction focus to a relationship
focus. Reichheld & Schefter (2000) applied this theory to the digital environment, demonstrating that E
loyalty serves as a primary driver of sustainable profitability. In the 3D printing context, Relationship
Marketing Theory explains the finding that repeat customers generate 71.2% of total revenue and that
the top 20% of customers account for 75% of all revenue.  The Pareto like distribution of customer
profitability, documented in this study, aligns with the relationship marketing principle that retaining
existing customers yields disproportionately higher returns than acquiring new ones. This theory directly
informs Pillar 4 (Retention and Upsell) of the proposed content strategy, which prioritizes WhatsApp
broadcast communications and loyalty content for repeat customers.
Synthesis of Theoretical Perspectives
The four theoretical perspectives converge to provide a comprehensive foundation for the
proposed five stage framework, as illustrated in Figure 1. Consumer Behavior Theory explains how
customers form purchase intentions through perceptual and learning constructs, with customization
behavior serving as an observable signal of advanced intention that can be captured at the data analysis
stage. Customer Value Theory explains the tradeoff between perceived benefits and costs that governs
deal rates across different order sizes and material types, directly informing the customer value analysis
stage. Price Perception Theory explains how subjective price evaluations, reference price formation,
and perceived sacrifice influence conversion at different price tiers, informing both the value analysis
and strategy selection sta ges. Relationship Marketing Theory explains why customer retention drives
the majority of revenue and why relationship building should be prioritized over broad acquisition in the
execution stage. The synthesis reveals that these theories operate at complementary levels of the customer

(Yin, 2018). The case study approach was selected because the study examines a contemporary
phenomenon, namely data driven marketing in the 3D printing industry, w ithin its real operational

The mixed method approach combines quantitative and qualitative components. The
quantitative component involves descriptive stati stical analysis of 1,484 transaction records, including

integration of both approaches enables a more complete understanding of customer acquisition behavior

Purposive sampling was used to select the case study site. Tridiku Teknokarya Indonesia was
selected based on three criteria: (1) the company maintains complete transaction records for each order,
including material specifications, pricing components, and deal outcomes; (2) the company serves all
three customer segments relevant to this study, namely Students, General customers, and Companies;
and (3) the study period of July to December 2025 encompasses one full academic semester cycle,
capturing both peak  demand periods and holiday troughs, which is essential for analyzing seasonal
patterns.
Research Context
The case was conducted at Tridiku Teknokarya Indonesia, a medium scale 3D printing firm
headquartered in Surabaya, Indonesia. The company serves three distinct customer segments: Students,
who primarily request architectural models and academic prototypes; General customers, who order
functional parts, hobby items, and miniatures; and Companies, who require technical components and
functional parts for industrial applications. The firm operates both Fused Deposition Modeling and resin
printing systems capable of processing PLA, PETG, ABS, TPU, and Resin materials. At the time of data
collection, the company maintained an active customer database and recorded transaction details for each
order, including material specifications, pricing components, and deal outcomes.
Population and Sample
The population of interest comprises all transaction records generated by Tridiku Teknokarya
Indonesia during the study period.  A census sampling approach was adopted, meaning all available
transaction records within the defined timeframe were included in the analysis.  The sampling frame
covers the period from July through December 2025, yielding a total o f 1,484 orders from 401 unique
customers. No exclusion criteria were applied beyond the temporal boundary, ensuring that the dataset
represents the complete operational activity of the firm during the study period.  This census approach
eliminates sampling bias and provides a comprehensive basis for segment level comparison.
Data Sources
Data were extracted from the company’s transaction logs, which serve as a secondary data
source. The logs contain structured fields for each order, including:
1. Customer data: unique identifier, segment category (Student, General, Company), order frequency,
deal status (Deal or No Deal), acquisition source (Google, Instagram, Referral, or unrecorded).
2. Product and material data: material type (PLA, Resin, ABS, PETG, TPU), product category
(Architectural Model, Prototype, Miniature, Part), print weight in grams, print duration in hours.
3. Pricing data: material charge in Rupiah, duration charge in Rupiah, finishing charge in Rupiah,
discount amount, total order value.
4. Temporal data: order date, month, week number, lead time from inquiry to completion.
5. Order description: presence or absence of customization specifications provided by the customer.
The total recorded revenue across all 1,484 orders amounts to IDR 442,556,095. Data extraction
was performed by exporting the transaction log from the company’s order management system into a
structured spreadsheet format for subsequent analysis.

IV. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Description of Research Object
Tridiku Teknokarya Indonesia operates as a medium scale 3D printing service provider based
in Surabaya. During the six months study period from July to December 2025, the company processed
1,484 orders from 401  unique customers, generating total recorded revenue of IDR 442,556,095. The
company maintains a diverse material portfolio, including PLA, Resin, ABS, PETG, and TPU, and
serves three primary customer segments: Students (academic purposes), General (individual hobby
and functional use), and Companies (industrial and technical applications).  The firm’s transaction
records provide a comprehensive view of customer behavior, material preferences, pricing structures,

Customer Value and Behavioral Insights
Revenue Contribution and Revenue Concentration Paradox
Although the General segment accounts for the largest share of orders at 60.2%, its revenue per
order is the lowest at IDR 243,619. The student segment, with only 26.1% of orders, generates the
highest revenue per order at IDR 428,918. The Company segment, despite having the fewest customers
at 22, achieves the highest revenue per customer at IDR 2,662,592, driven by a high order frequency of
9.23 orders per customer.
Analysis of price distribution reveals a stark revenue concentration paradox. A mere 4.9% of
orders valued above IDR 1 million contribute 52.7% of total revenue, whereas 42.6% of orders below
IDR 50,000 account for only 2.4%. The two highest price tiers, orders exceeding IDR 1 million and orders
between IDR 500,000 and IDR 1 million, together account for 72.4% of revenue while representing only
13.7% of total orders. This concentration underscores the disproportionate value of high value orders and
the strategic necessity of dedicated nurturing for these transactions. The finding aligns with Cooper and
Kaplan’s (1988) observation that customer profitability is unevenly distributed, but extends it by showing
that the concentration operates not only at the customer level but also at the order level within a single
firm.

Discussion
Theoretical Contributions
This study advances the literature in several directions. First, the proposed framework integrates
material dimensions, duration-based pricing, and content strategy within customer acquisition analysis.
This synthesis has not been encountered in existing SME digital marketing scholarship (Kumar et al.,
2026; Supriyadi & Edi Pranyoto, 2025) . The finding that print duration dominates the pricing structure,
at 64 to 72%, enriches pricing theory in additive manufacturing, extending the work of Jain et al. (2024)
and Bi  and Wu (2025) by providing empirical evidence that duration-based pricing is not merely a
theoretical model but an operational reality with strategic implications.
Second, the study documents the custom order effect.  Orders accompanied by customization
descriptions achieve an 80.5% deal rate compared with 49.5% for standard orders.  This discovery
connects Zeithaml’s (1988) purchase intention construct with 3D printing practice, demonstrating that
customers who articulate specifications have already progressed to the consideration to purchase stage.
The practical corollary is that structured quotation forms can materially elevate conversion rates, a finding
with implications extending beyond 3D printing to any service business where customer specifications
can be solicited at the inquiry stage.
Third, the research corroborates the revenue concentration paradox within the 3D printing
industry. The Pareto like distribution, where 20% of customers generate 75% of revenue and 4.9% of
orders produce 52.7% of revenue, aligns with Cooper and Kaplan’s ( 1988) observation that customer
profitability is unevenly distributed. A further nuance emerges. The highest volume segment, General at
60.2% of orders, does not coincide with the highest per customer profitability. This contests the prevalent
assumption that volume equates to profitability and suggests that marketing resource allocation models
based solely on volume metrics may be systematically misdirected.
Fourth, the study establishes that material segment fit influences deal rate. The student ×
Resin combination at 100% and the Company × PETG combination at 80% demonstrates that material
selection functions not solely as a technical preference but also as a signal of purchase intention.  This
deepens understanding of the relationship between product attributes and purchase intent, suggesting
that in customized manufacturing contexts, the choice of premium materials may serve as a behavioral
marker of customer commitment.
Fifth, the research operationalizes customer acquisition data into a concrete content strategy
comprising seven themes, four pillars, and a six phases calendar. This forges a link between analytical
insight and marketing execution that has been largely absent from both 3D printing research (Holzmann
et al., 2020) and content marketing scholarship (Holliman & Rowley, 2014; Pulizzi, 2014). By tracing
each content recommendation to a specific empirical observation, the study provides a template for how
data driven content strategy can be constructed in practice.

For practitioners in 3D printing SMEs, the study yields several actionable recommendations
grounded in data. First, routine transaction records constitute an underutilized reservoir of marketing

Limitations and Future Research
Several limitations should be acknowledged.  First, reliance on a single case study constrains
the generalizability of findings.  The patterns observed at Tridiku Teknokarya Indonesia may reflect
firm specific or regional factors that do not extend to other 3D printing SMEs. Second, acquisition source
data was captured for only 4.2% of orders, limiting the robustness of channel level analysis and
preventing systematic attribution modeling.  Third, the framework does not yet incorporate predictive
analytics or machine  learning, which  could enable demand forecasting and personalized content
recommendations. Fourth, the study period of six months captures only one academic cycle, and multi
year analysis could reveal whether seasonal patterns are stable or evolving. Future research could extend
validation to multiple firms across different geographic markets, implement CRM systems for systematic
acquisition tracking, integrate predictive modeling for demand forecasting, and test the framework in
adjacent industries such as CNC machining or laser cutting where similar duration-based pricing and
segment heterogeneity may apply.

V. CONCLUSION
This study developed and empirically tested a data driven digital marketing framework based
on customer acquisition, structured across five stages and tailored to the 3D printing industry,
incorporating content strategy and a data informed campaign calendar. Drawin g on the case study at
Tridiku Teknokarya Indonesia with 1,484 orders from 401 unique customers over six months, four
principal findings emerge. First, the framework demonstrated the capacity to convert routine transaction
records into actionable marketing  intelligence, producing a precise mapping of the most profitable
segments and their corresponding strategic approaches. Second, data driven marketing decisions
produce recommendations that diverge from intuition: the highest volume segment is not the most
profitable per customer, orders with customization descriptions achieve a deal rate 31 percentage points
higher than standard orders, and specific material segment pairings exhibit conversion rates substantially
above average. Third, the data driven content strategy, comprising seven themes, four pillars, and a six
phases campaign calendar, provides a concrete execution blueprint where each element is traceable to
specific empirical evidence. Fourth, the framework is accessible to 3D printing SMEs without
demanding sophisticated technological infrastructure. Theoretically, the study extends Consumer
Behavior Theory by demonstrating that customization behavior functions as an observable proxy for
purchase, extends Customer Value Theory by showing that the value tradeoff is mediated by order size
in machine time driven pricing, extends Price Perception Theory by revealing that customers lack stable
reference prices when the primary cost driver is intangible, and extends Relationship Marketing Theory
by confirm ing that the Pareto distribution operates at both the customer and order levels. Several
limitations should be acknowledged. The study relies on a single transaction dataset from one firm,
which may reflect firm specific characteristics, local market condi tions, and academic calendar
dynamics unique to Yogyakarta, constraining generalizability to other firms or regions. The six months
observation period captures one academic cycle but does not allow year over year comparison.
Acquisition source data was available for only 4.2% of orders, and the study employs descriptive rather
than inferential statistical methods. Future research should validate the framework across multiple firms
