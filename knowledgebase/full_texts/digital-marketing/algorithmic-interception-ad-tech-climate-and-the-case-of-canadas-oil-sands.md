Abstract
Oil and gas companies utilize artificial intelligence and advertising technology (ad-tech) to influ-
ence public discourse on climate and energy issues. Through contracts with public relations and
advertising agencies, these companies access programmatic advertising: the automated genera-
tion, purchase, and display of digital ads in real time based on user queries and other targeting
signals. These practices enable ‘algorithmic interception’ , the strategic positioning of narratives
within algorithmically mediated information environments. This article examines algorithmic
interception in the context of greenwashing by analyzing Google Search advertising strategies used
by Canada’s Pathways Alliance (now the Oil Sands Alliance). From a corpus of 1142 paid key-
words and more than 15 000 organic keywords, we analyzed 1149 advertisements and 131 high-
ranking organic results collected between 2022 and 2024. W e found that Pathways Alliance pur-
chased search terms related to climate policy and solutions, such as ‘carbon tax, ’ ‘climate action
incentive, ’ and ‘net-zero, ’ with the result that its advertisements appeared alongside or above results
for government programs, regulatory agencies, and climate science. In practice, this reshaped the
online environment in which people sought information on climate questions and raised con-
cerns about greenwashing. As one of the first studies to examine programmatic advertising in cli-
mate communication, we identify information patterns that are consistent with contemporary
greenwashing as well as significant barriers to transparency and accountability inside these sys-
tems. There are significant information asymmetries created by the opacity of platforms such as
Google, which withhold audience targeting and ad placement details from public scrutiny. As large
language model–based search and generative engine optimization emerge as the next frontier for
algorithmic interception, the need for independent tools to audit how climate narratives are sur-
faced will only intensify.
1.Introduction
In 2024, Google searches for information on cli-
mate policies, solutions, and impacts could trig-
ger sponsored advertisements titled ‘Producing Oil
Responsibly—Path to Reduce CO2 Emissions’ at the
top of results. Purchased by the Pathways Alliance—
an organization representing 95% of Canadian oil
sands production—these ads promoted Canada’s oil
and gas industry as a leader of climate action. At the
same time, official data demonstrated that the oil and
gas sector accounted for the largest share of Canada’s
total greenhouse gas emissions [ 1].
The concern with greenwashing is not new. Y et,
the use of ad-tech and AI systems by energy com-
panies to reshape the information environment in
which people engage with climate content is not
well documented or understood. Oil and gas com-
panies have utilized these systems from companies
like Google to assess public interest in climate top-
ics, target audiences, and automate online ad creation
and placement. These systems are largely opaque,
creating significant information asymmetries that
prevent researchers and regulators from detect-
ing deceptive marketing or other violations of the
law.

1.1.Theevolutionofgreenwashing
Greenwashing, or the exaggeration of environmen-
tal or sustainability claims, has evolved alongside the
oil and gas industry’s growing involvement in cli-
mate communication [2, 3]. Historically, scholarship
defined greenwashing as a ‘deceptive marketing’
tactic primarily limited to misleading claims about
a product or service [ 4]. However, more general
claims to positive environmental performance are
now used by companies to describe their wider
operations and brand identity to a range of stake-
holders, including investors, lenders, and politicians.
As climate change became a core financial and
regulatory risk, greenwashing expanded to encom-
pass company and even sector-wide representa-
tions of environmental, climate, and clean energy
ambitions.
Often, these promotions utilize future-oriented
visions of ‘net-zero’ to protect the social license of
oil and gas companies [ 2, 5]. Montgomery et al
argue that this ‘future-orientation’ can constitute
greenwashing when it decouples long-term ambi-
tion from immediate action [ 4]. By committing to
‘net-zero by 2050’ , firms can claim climate leader-
ship while deferring capital expenditure on emis-
sions reductions. This creates what Aronczyk et al
term an ‘accountability gap’: the claims remain dis-
connected from immediate capital expenditures or
absolute emissions reductions because they reference
an unrealized future [ 2]. Regulators and the pub-
lic cannot evaluate whether future-oriented commit-
ments are sincere or will be honoured; they can only
assess whether current actions are consistent with
them. The surge in future-oriented promises to elim-
inate climate impacts, along with the difficulties in
assessing their validity, has prompted scrutiny from
researchers, civil society organizations, lawmakers,
and law enforcement agencies [4, 6–8].
On 20 June 2024, the Pathways Alliance removed
substantially all climate-related content from its
website and social media platforms. This coin-
cided with the Royal Assent of Bill C-59 (the Fall
Economic Statement Implementation Act, 2023),
which amended the Competition Act to strengthen
provisions on environmental claims. These amend-
ments codified a ‘reverse onus’ framework, requir-
ing entities to prove environmental claims were
supported by ‘adequate and proper substantia-
tion in accordance with internationally recognized
methodology’ [9].
Oil and gas supporters reacted critically. Pathways
Alliance asserted that the requirements were ‘vague’
and threatened freedom of communication [ 10].
Imperial Oil, a founding member of Pathways
Alliance, further contended that these ‘undefined
and subjective’ standards introduced significant
legal uncertainty for Canadian businesses. In a joint
statement with Alberta’s energy and environment
ministers, Premier Danielle Smith characterized
Bill C-59 as an effort to create ‘chaos and uncer-
tainty’ for energy investors, framing the legislation
as ‘absurd authoritarian censorship’[11]. Because the
Competition Bureau, the independent federal law
enforcement agency tasked with promoting mar-
ket competition, had not yet issued detailed inter-
pretive guidance, Pathways Alliance claimed it was
‘forced’ to remove its content to avoid the risk of
frivolous litigation and penalties of up to 3% of global
annual revenue [ 12]. While the 2025 federal budget
subsequently proposed amendments to ease these
requirements, the initial regulatory shift produced an
immediate crisis of accountability for the industry’s
digital claims: industry associations and provincial
officials publicly contested the new requirements,
and Pathways Alliance removed substantial digital
content, several examples of which are analyzed in
this study. However, substantiation requirements are
only effective if claims can be observed, documented,
and accessed in the first place, conditions that auto-
mated advertising systems operating in real time do
not reliably meet.
1.2.Programmaticadvertising
Scholarship on lobbying and traditional corporate
issue management has illuminated the role of pub-
lic relations and advertising in climate obstruction
[3, 6, 8, 13, 14]. However, it largely overlooks the
significance of programmatic advertising technolo-
gies (‘ad-tech’) and AI systems. Ad-tech encompasses
the technologies and systems that enable advertis-
ers to target audiences with customized and auto-
mated online advertisements [ 15]. These systems are
integral to the world’s largest platforms, including
Google. Alphabet, Google’s parent company, has been
described as the ‘largest and most profitable adver-
tising corporation in history’ [ 16], with the major-
ity of its revenue coming from digital advertising
on Google Search. Unlike traditional ‘ad buys’ , pro-
grammatic systems utilize real-time automated auc-
tions to place advertisements. Advertisers select spe-
cific search terms, known as ‘keywords, ’ to trigger
their ads. For each keyword, they set a maximum
bid: the highest price they are willing to pay each
time a user clicks on the resulting advertisement. For
example, a buyer could select ‘carbon capture Canada’
as a keyword and assign a bid price to it. When a
user enters that phrase to search Google, the plat-
form runs an instantaneous auction among all adver-
tisers who have bid on the keywords. Within millisec-
onds, the winning advertiser’s sponsored link appears
at the top of the results page, formatted like organic
content and identified only by a small ‘Sponsored’
tag. Advertisers can purchase hundreds or thousands
of keywords simultaneously, configuring campaigns
to target broad topic areas such as climate policy or
narrow, specialized queries. Google also offers auto-
mated tools to target audiences, select keywords, and
dynamically generate ads by ingesting the advertiser’s

When advertisers systematically use these opportu-

or institutional information, the result is what we
term ‘algorithmic interception’: the strategic posi-
tioning of narratives within algorithmically medi-

advertising. Although our analysis focuses on search
advertising, algorithmic interception may also oper-
ate in emerging AI-mediated systems, such as large
language model-based systems, where narratives can
be surfaced through generated responses rather than
sponsored links.
1.3.Canada’soilsandsandPathwaysAlliance
T o examine these questions, we focused on Pathways
Alliance’s search advertising practices from 2022–
2024. Pathways Alliance comprised five oil and
gas companies, including Imperial Oil, Suncor, and
Cenovus (during final preparation of this manuscript,
the organization rebranded as the ‘Oil Sands Alliance, ’
dropping the climate-oriented ‘Pathways’ framing;
all references here use the former name, consistent
with the period of study). Formed in 2021, Pathways
Alliance has promoted the oil sands as a climate
leader on a pathway to net-zero through promi-
nent nationwide advertising campaigns, despite offi-
cial data identifying the industry as Canada’s largest
source of greenhouse gas emissions [ 1].
These efforts represent the latest iteration of a
long-standing ‘petro-culture’ in Canada, where oil
and gas companies and their supporters have con-
structed national identities around bitumen produc-
tion to secure social license to operate [ 20–22]. In
recent years, such strategies were increasingly orga-
nized through digital infrastructures that condition
what climate information becomes visible, credible,

1.4.Researchobjectives
W e selected Google for this study due to its ubiq-
uity and its relative neglect in the scholarly literature.
Google Search remains a powerful tool for organizing
our contemporary information environment [24]. Its
dominance is evident from investigations that found
Google guilty of maintaining an illegal monopoly in
online search in the US and of abusing its dominant
position in Canada [25].
Our study builds on contemporary greenwashing
research by investigating: (a) the shift from product-
level greenwashing—where individual goods or ser-
vices are marketed with misleading environmental
claims—toward sector-wide strategies that deploy
policy, solution, and future-oriented framings of cli-
mate action to misrepresent an industry’s environ-
mental record; (b) the use of ad-tech and AI tools,
including Google’s automated ad generation, to posi-
tion industry content in relation to climate issues,
events, and searches involving institutional sources;
and (c) the difficulties researchers and regulators face
in accessing timely, relevant, and reliable data on
audience targeting and ad placement—data essential
for detecting and assessing greenwashing that is gen-
erated through automated advertising systems.

2.2.Thematiccodingandanalysis
W e used Attride-Stirling’s (2001) approach to the-
matic coding to organize the corpus into 11 descrip-
tive ‘basic codes’ and three interpretive ‘organizing
strategies’ . W e applied this systematic approach across
three distinct data sources: (1) paid keywords: to iden-
tify the subjects and events prioritized by the organi-
zation; (2) organic keywords: to establish a baseline
of how the organization ranks without paid interven-
tion; and (3) advertising descriptions: to analyze the
explicit framing used in the resulting ad copy.

3.1.3. Conquesting authoritative sources
The third organizing theme we identified was
‘competitive conquesting’—an established digital
marketing tactic in which an organization bids
on keywords associated with competitors or crit-
ical institutions [ 31]. In practice, this means that
a user searching for ‘Environment and Climate
Change Canada’ (the federal department responsible
for national climate policy, regulations, and emis-
sions reporting) could have encountered a Pathways
Alliance advertisement alongside or above official
government results. This tactic, a form of algorithmic
interception, results in industry content appearing
in response to queries directed at federal agencies
and critical research organizations. The organization
also purchased ads on search results for organiza-
tions whose research has critically assessed oil sands
climate and environmental claims, such as ‘Pembina
Institute’ .
3.2.Adcontentanalysis
The content of the Pathways Alliance advertisements
(n = 1149) positioned the organization within discus-
sions of climate policy, action, and solutions. Ads fre-
quently emphasized the organization’s name (30.6%,
n = 352), aspirational and unsubstantiated net-zero
commitments (24.3%, n = 279) [2], general ‘climate
action’ (18.7%, n = 215), and efforts to ‘reduce emis-
sions’ (15.2%, n = 175), which associate the oil sands
sector with climate action and emissions reduction.
T wo additional categories, ‘Create an Impact’ (1.7%,
n = 20) and ‘Other’ (3.0%, n = 34), accounted for the
remaining 4.7% of ads. Eighty-seven percent of ‘net
zero’ ads referred to ‘net zero by 2050’ (n = 243), pro-
jecting a future-oriented vision that scholarship has
identified as characteristic of net-zero greenwashing
[2, 4, 30]. Other ads employed affirmative but unspe-
cific language to emphasize ‘responsible produc-
tion’ (6.4%, n = 74), associating industry activities
with sustainability themes without in-ad substanti-
ation. T ogether, these ads framed climate protection
as an industrial-technical challenge and positioned
Alliance members as contributing solutions.
3.3.Keywordandadanalysissummary
Keyword and ad data show Canada’s oil sands sec-
tor shaping the online information environment sur-
rounding climate policy, solutions, and action in
Canada through paid advertising, particularly dur-
ing specific events between 2022 and 2024, a period
of heightened contention around climate issues.
A notable pattern was the tendency to associate
Pathways Alliance with climate and energy transition
topics, most notably through net-zero claims that tar-
geted user interest in climate policy, solutions, and
actions. Pathways Alliance presented itself as engaged
in addressing climate change, with messaging that
framed climate change as a technical challenge call-
ing for industry-led solutions rather than as a primar-
ily regulatory or political issue. It is worth noting that
there was no evidence of climate science denial in the
content associated with the Pathways Alliance.

4.Discussion
Our results suggest that oil and gas companies can
leverage Google’s ad-tech and AI systems for ad
targeting, generation, and display to position them-
selves as leaders in climate policy and as collab-
orative partners in the energy transition [ 4, 32].
Greenwashing research must account for these sys-
tems and the way they can associate energy companies
with climate action in specific contexts for differ-
ent audiences. These efforts can function to manage
expectations across a wide range of stakeholders,
including investors, lenders, governments, journal-
ists, and other organizations essential for maintain-
ing that social license [ 2, 4]. Y et, they are not visible
at scale to researchers or regulators.
This use of industry-wide and future-oriented
claims is clear in our data. Alliance ads targeted
climate-specific internet searches using general net-
zero framing and linked users to organizational
webpages emphasizing CCS and climate technology
investments. However, the relationship between a
specific ad’s claim and a company’s broader brand
image is complicated both by the dynamic nature of
search advertising and by the growing use of LLM-
based systems that surface brand narratives through
generated responses rather than ads. While tradi-
tional greenwashing may promote isolated ‘green’
projects to encourage mistaken inferences about a
company’s values or general performance, and thus
mislead by omission [ 2], programmatic advertis-
ing enables promotional claims to traverse diverse
digital contexts. These systems can also employ
‘retargeting’—a process where ads ‘follow’ a user
across the web after an initial search, which can sus-
tain the salience of industry narratives across brows-
ing contexts. Through these techniques, promotion
becomes dynamically variable, context-dependent,
and far more difficult for researchers to capture than
a single, static advertisement. Data needed to address
these instances at scale is not accessible to researchers.
This pattern highlights the broader information
asymmetry encountered by other digital advertising
researchers and assessments of digital ad repositories
[21, 33, 34]. Absent regulatory solutions that require
transparency (such as the ad repositories required by
the European Union) or actions to address illegal con-
duct to secure a monopoly advantage (US v. Google),
it will be challenging to address programmatic green-
washing at scale.
Our study builds on research of advertising and
public relations, which tends to emphasize large
agencies, highly visible advertising or social media
campaigns, or traditional forms of corporate issue
management in climate obstruction [ 8, 14, 21].
Increasingly, big technology organizations, includ-
ing Google’s Partner Programs, are integrating insti-
tutional sources of advertising and public relations
expertise into the development of digital, AI, and
automated advertising tools. In this respect, the chal-
lenge is not merely understanding the technical capa-
bilities (affordances) of these platforms, but rather the
evolving network of partnerships between oil and gas
companies, PR firms, and big tech platforms.
5.Conclusion
Oil and gas companies have used ad-tech tools to
access the online search environment for climate
change and to shape the information available for
understanding climate policy, solutions, and action.
In our study, oil sands producers relied on aspira-
tional net-zero claims characteristic of greenwashing
[2, 4, 30]. While existing scholarship on greenwash-
ing has largely focused on messaging and disclosure,
the roles of programmatic advertising and AI tools
deserve wider attention. This paper represents a first
effort to illuminate how these systems of influence are
used.
Identifying advertisements generated program-
matically by automated systems poses particular
challenges. Although the Google Ads T ransparency
Center indicates whether automated ads are shown,
neither the ad content nor the search queries is
documented. As a result, users cannot determine
how an advertisement was targeted, generated, dis-
played, or assessed, never mind its veracity or soci-
etal implications. It is impossible to know if Google
or the purchasing organization created the ad, nor
can researchers assess how advertiser-defined param-
eters interacted with platform optimization systems
to produce specific placements. In some cases, the
advertiser itself may never see, approve, or know
where an ad was displayed, raising unresolved ques-
tions about responsibility for misleading or inaccu-
rate claims generated through automated systems. If
Google creates deceptive advertising on behalf of oil
and gas companies, by drawing on their website mate-
rials, it is unclear who is responsible. Should Google
or companies monitor or report violations of the law
in these instances?
More broadly, this study highlights the chal-
lenges of researchers in monitoring the uses and
consequences of ad-tech tools in climate commu-
nication. Google and its industry partners have
remarkable access to relevant data, while users and
researchers have almost no visibility into the systems
that generate ads. The information asymmetry cre-
ates conditions in which false, misleading, and exag-
gerated claims may proliferate with limited exter-
nal scrutiny. As regulators continue to signal their
intention to enforce rules against deceptive market-
ing, such as greenwashing [ 35], they must recog-
nize programmatic advertising as an especially lucra-
tive and opaque site of promotional communication.
The need for independent ad repositories and detec-
tion tools will only intensify as LLM-based search

next contested frontier. Unlike keyword advertis-
ing, where bidding strategies can be partially recon-
structed through third-party tools, AI-synthesized
responses offer no visible mechanism for auditing
how corporate claims are ingested, weighted, or
surfaced. Moreover, just as keyword bidding facili-
tates an earlier form of algorithmic interception, AI-
generated responses risk embedding oil and gas nar-
ratives through large language model-generated sum-
maries, obscuring the provenance of climate infor-
mation. Beyond this, paid advertising is now enter-
ing these systems directly, with OpenAI running ads
alongside ChatGPT’s responses since early 2026.
Furthermore, the growing climate impacts of AI
systems powered by fossil fuels make this an espe-
cially worrying intersection. Future research must
develop systematic methodologies for monitoring
how generative AI systems represent contested knowl-
edge domains, tracking which narratives they privi-
lege and how their outputs shift over time. Without
such infrastructure, researchers and regulators will
be unable to identify how AI-mediated information
environments shape public understanding of climate
and energy or detect emergent forms of algorithmic
interception.
