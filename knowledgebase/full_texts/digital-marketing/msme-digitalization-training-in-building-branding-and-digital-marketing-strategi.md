Abstract
Digital technology requires Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs) to adapt in order to survive
and compete. MSMEs in Payung Village, Majalengka Regency, still manage branding, promotion, and
business administration largely in conventional ways, and many owners equate digitalization with simply
opening a social media account. This community engagement study explored MSME actors’ perceptions of
the digitalization training delivered through PRISMA 3.0, the community empowerment program of
Politeknik Siber Cerdika Internasional (SCI Polytechnic), and identified the changes in branding and digital
marketing practices they reported. A qualitative descriptive case study design was used. Data were
collected through observation and semistructured interviews with 10 purposively selected MSME owners
out of 25 training participants, conducted before and two weeks after the training, and were analyzed
thematically with triangulation and member checking. Three themes emerged: a shift from viewing
branding as a logo toward viewing it as product identity and story; early adoption of digital marketing, as
7 of 10 informants created or reactivated business social media accounts and 6 redesigned their packaging
labels; and persistent barriers related to content production  skills, time, and internet access. Participants
reported perceived improvements in product visibility and customer inquiries, although changes in sales
and competitiveness could not yet be measured. The findings indicate that digitalization training mainl y
contributes to changes in mindset and early practice, and that continuous mentoring is required before
measurable competitiveness outcomes can be expected.

Keywords:   MSME digitalization; branding; digital marketing; community engagement; Payung village.

INTRODUCTION
The development of digital technology requires Micro, Small, and Medium
Enterprises (MSMEs) to be able to adapt in order to survive and compete in the digital
era. The development of the digital economy has brought major changes in marketing and
business management patterns (Dwivedi et al., 2021; Verhoef et al., 2021). MSMEs as the
backbone of the village economy are required to be able to adapt in order to survive and
develop.  At the global level, digitalization has become a basic condition for the survival
and growth of small businesses. The OECD, (2021) reports that SMEs lag behind large

firms in the adoption of most digital technologies, ranging from a basic online presence
to more advanced applications such as data analytics.
Digital transformation is therefore understood not merely as adding an online sales
channel but as a process that changes how firms create, deliver, and capture value (Kraus
et al., 2022; Vial, 2019). In emerging economies, the use of social media by small firms has
been associated with stronger customer relationships, wider market access, and better
performance (Chatterjee & Kar, 2020 ; Qalati et al., 2021) . However, Eller et al., (2020)
show that SME digitalization depends on IT resources, employee skills, and the digital
strategy of the firm, which explains why many small businesses remain at an early stage
of digital adoption even when digital tools are inexpensive and widely available.
In Indonesia, MSMEs are regulated under Law Number 20 of 2008 (Law of the
Republic of Indonesia Number 20 of 2008) and form the vast majority of business units
while absorbing most of the national workforce (Tambunan, 2019). Based on data from
the Ministry of Cooperatives and SMEs, Indonesia has about 64.2 million MSMEs, which
contribute 61.07 percent of gross domestic product and absorb 97 percent of the total
workforce (Indonesia, 2021). The government has therefore included the digitalization
of MSME marketing among its main support programs (Indonesia, 2021) , and earlier
Indonesian studies argued that digital marketing can become a source of competitive
advantage for MSMEs (Wardhana, 2015).
Nevertheless, marketing constraints and the limited use of technology remain
among the main obstacles for micro and small enterprises, and Tambunan, (2019)
recommends training that focuses on online marketing and entrepreneurship. During the
COVID-19 pandemic, Indonesian SMEs also followed different digital transformation
paths depending on their resources and digital readiness, and some of them only reached
the stage of using digital tools for promotion and sales without transforming their
business model (Priyono et al., 2020).
Based on the results of interviews and observations in Payung Village, most MSME
actors are still running businesses conventionally. This business digitalization is still
understood to be limited to using social media or online sales, not including mindset s,
branding strategies, and business sustainability. In addition, many MSME actors in
Payung Village have not yet developed a brand identity supported by an attractive logo,
packaging, and product narrative. Preliminary observation by the service team identified
around 40 active MSMEs in the village, mostly in processed food, agricultural products,
and handicrafts, and estimated that only about one quarter of them used social media for
business purposes, mainly through personal WhatsApp status.
The digitalization of MSMEs is not only interpreted as the use of social media or
marketplaces but also includes changes in mindset, business strategy and business
sustainability (Verhoef et al., 2021; Vial, 2019). Branding is an important aspect because
it is able to build consumer image, trust and loyalty (Kotler & Keller, 2016; Odoom,
Narteh, et al., 2017 ; Tjiptono, 2019). Digital marketing, in turn, extends the reach of a
brand through low cost online channels that allow small firms to interact directly with
customers (Kannan & Li, 2017; Sanjaya & Tarigan, 2009). In the training examined in this
study, the resource person conveyed this relationship through a simple metaphor:
branding gives a product its "spirit", while digital marketing gives it "wings" to reach a
wider market.
Several previous studies are directly relevant to this problem. Chatterjee & Kar,
(2020) found that perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and compatibility
encouraged the use of social media marketing among Indian SMEs, whereas cost had a
negative effect, and that its adoption improved business outcomes. Qalati et al., (2021)
showed that technological, organizational, and environmental factors drive social media
adoption among SMEs in developing countries, and that adoption in turn improves
performance. Odoom, et al., (2017) similarly reported performance benefits of social

media use among SMEs in an emerging market. Regarding branding, Odoom, Narteh, et
al., (2017) reviewed SME branding research and concluded that small firms tend to treat
branding informally and that the evidence remains scattered, while Malodia et al., (2023)
demonstrated that the digital self efficacy of the entrepreneur strongly influences how
far an SME advances in its digital transformation journey.
These studies provide valuable evidence, but most of them rely on surveys of firms
that have already adopted digital tools or on established SMEs in urban settings. Less is
known about how rural micro entrepreneurs with very limited digital experience
perceive a short digitalization training, how they translate it into branding and marketing
practices, and which barriers remain after such an intervention. Community engagement
reports also tend to describe training activities without systematically documenti ng the
perceptions of participants and the changes they report. The novelty of this study lies in
treating branding and digital marketing as one integrated training content in a village
context and in using qualitative evidence collected before and after t he training to
distinguish improved understanding, early changes in practice, and outcomes that are not
yet observable, such as competitiveness.
This condition has an impact on the low competitiveness of MSME products in
Payung Village, both in attracting new consumers and building loyalty with customers.
Therefore, training and mentoring in MSME digitalization that integrate branding and
digital marketing are needed as an initial step to strengthen the readiness of MSMEs to
compete in the digital era.  Accordingly, this study aims to explore the perceptions of
MSME actors in Payung Village of the digitalization training delivered through PRISMA
3.0, the community empowerment program of Politeknik Siber Cerdika Internasional
(SCI Polytechnic), and to identify the changes in branding and digital marketing practices
that they report after the training.
Specifically, the study addresses three questions: (1) what were the branding and
digital marketing conditions of MSMEs before the training, (2) how did participants
perceive the training content, and (3) what changes and remaining obstacles did they
report afterwards. Theoretically, the study adds qualitative evidence on the early stage
of SME digital transformation in a rural setting. Practically, the findings can guide village
governments, universities, and MSME support agencies in designing follow up mentoring
that moves MSMEs from awareness toward sustained digital practice and, eventually,
measurable competitiveness.
RESEARCH METHOD
This study employed a qualitative descriptive case study design embedded in a
community engagement (community service) program and followed a participatory
approach, in which the service team worked directly with MSME partners in Payung
Village (Sugiyono, 2019). The activity focused on engaging directly with the community
and identifying the needs of MSME partners so that the outcomes could have a meaningful
and sustainable impact on MSME digitalization.
Research Location and Participants
The study was conducted in Payung Village, Majalengka Regency, West Java,
Indonesia. The training was attended by 25 MSME actors, and ten of them were selected
as key informants through purposive sampling using three inclusion criteria: (1) owning
and running an active business for at least one year, (2) attending the full training session,
and (3) being willing to be interviewed before and after the training. The informants
consisted of six women and four men aged 29 to 52 years who run businesses in
processed food, beverages, agricultural products, and handicrafts, as presented in Table

Research Instruments
The main instrument was a semistructured interview guide developed from the
concepts of brand identity, digital marketing use, and perceived business change (Kannan
& Li, 2017; Kotler & Keller, 2016). The guide covered four areas: business profile, current
branding and promotion practices, understanding of digitalization, and changes and
obstacles after the training. An observation checklist was used to record the presence of
a logo, packaging label, business social media account, marketplace listing, and posting
frequency, so that reported changes could be compared with observable practices.
Data Collection Procedure
The first stage involved observing the needs of MSMEs. The service team conducted
direct surveys and interviewed MSME actors to obtain information about their business
conditions. The team also directly observed MSME activities in Payung Village,
particularly their use of digital media. This stage was carried out one week before the
training. The second stage involved interviews with MSME actors. The service team
explored the issues faced by MSME actors through semi -structured interviews, focusing
on their understanding, implementation, and experiences regarding business
digitalization. Each interview lasted about 30 to 45 minutes, was conducted in Indonesian
or Sundanese by two members of the service team at the informant’s place of business,
and was audio recorded with the consent of the informant.
The third stage was the digitalization training. The training was held in a one day
session at the Payung Village hall and was delivered by a practitioner resource person

JDBS Vol.3 No.2 75—86                                                                                                                                            | 79
together with the SCI Polytechnic team. The material covered three modules: the
digitalization mindset, branding and product identity, and digital marketing through
social media and online marketplaces. Participants practiced creating a business account,
taking product photos with a smartphone, and designing a simple logo and label with a
free design application.
The fourth stage involved post -training assistance provided by the presenters and
the service team. The assistance was conducted in person, allowing participants to
identify and discuss obstacles encountered in their business activities. Follow up
interviews and observations of the same ten informants were carried out two weeks after
the training.  The final stage involved documentation, including photographs of the
activities, activity records, and materials related to MSME digitalization. The overall
procedure is summarized in Figure 1.

Data Analysis and Trustworthiness
Interview recordings were transcribed verbatim, translated into English for
reporting, and analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2019) . The
analysis followed six phases: familiarization with the data, initial coding, generating
candidate themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and writing up.
Coding was conducted manually by two researchers who worked independently and then
discussed their differences until agreement was reached.
Trustworthiness was ensured through source triangulation (owners and family
members involved in the business), method triangulation (interviews, observation
checklists, and documentation), and member checking, in which summaries of the
findings were confirmed with the informants (Nowell et al., 2017). The before and after
observation records were tabulated descriptively to support, rather than replace, the
qualitative interpretation.
Research Ethics
Participation was voluntary. All informants received an explanation of the purpose
of the study, gave oral informed consent before the interviews, and were told that they
could withdraw at any time. Their identities were anonymized using the codes I1 to I1 0,
the recordings were stored only by the research team, and photographs were taken and
published with the permission of the participants.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
This section presents the findings according to the three research questions and the
themes generated from the thematic analysis (Table 2), followed by a discussion that
compares the findings with previous studies and relevant theory.
Results
Initial Condition of MSMEs before the Training
The results of this interview show that most MSME actors do not have a clear brand
identity, such as logos, attractive packaging, and product narratives. Marketing still relies
on direct sales and word -of-mouth promotion. Before the training, only 3 of the  10
informants had a logo, 2 used a printed label, and 4 occasionally promoted their products
through personal WhatsApp status, while none had a dedicated business account or
marketplace listing (Table 1).
One informant explained, “I sell my dodol the same way my mother did; people
come to the house or I bring it to the market. I never thought about a logo” (I6). Another
stated, “Online selling is for young people. I was afraid of being cheated” (I10).  These
conditions show that, before the intervention, digitalization was understood mainly as an
optional promotional activity rather than as part of business strategy, which is consistent
with the problem identified in the introduction.
Participants’ Perceptions of the Training Content
The training focused on understanding the basics of MSME digitalization, branding,
and digital marketing. The material was delivered in simple language so that it was easy
to understand by the community and MSME actors. Participants were introduced to the
importance of the meaning of a brand, the use of social media, and strategies for building
relationships with consumers.
Participants perceived the practical sessions as the most useful part of the training.
As I2 said, “Usually training is only listening. Here we immediately made the account and
the logo on our own phones, so I can repeat it at home.” Several informants als o
remembered the metaphor of the “spirit” and the “wings” of a product, which helped them
see branding and marketing as one process.

From Logo to Identity
 After participating in the training, MSME actors began to understand that branding
is not just a logo, but how their products are remembered by consumers. Some MSME
actors began to try to market their products through social media and improve the
appearance of packaging. Digital marketing is starting to be considered able to expand
market reach and open up new consumer opportunities.
For example, I3, who sells jaboticaba (Brazilian grape) seedlings, explained that
buyers used to know his plants only as “the Brazilian grape from Payung”, whereas after
the training he consistently used the brand name “Gentar” on every pot label and on hi s
Facebook page “so that buyers can find me again”. Similarly, I6 stated, “A brand is not only
a sticker. The name, the story of my grandmother’s recipe, and the package must say the
same thing.” These statements show a change in understanding from branding as a visual
element toward branding as a consistent identity and narrative.
Early Adoption of Digital Marketing Practices
Changes in practice were observed during the follow up visit two weeks after the
training (Figure 3). Seven of the ten informants created or reactivated a business account
on WhatsApp Business, Facebook, or Instagram, six redesigned their packaging labels,
and seven had a logo compared with three before the training. Four informants posted
product content at least twice a week, and two listed their products on an online
marketplace. Informants also reported early market responses.
I8 stated, “After I opened WhatsApp Business and posted the catalog, five new
people asked about my frozen dumplings in two weeks, three of them from outside the
village.” In total, five informants reported more customer inquiries, but most of them
were cautious about attributing changes in sales to the training. As I2 explained, “I post
photos regularly now, but it is too early to say whether my sales have increased.”

Persistent Barriers and the Need for Mentoring
Not all informants were able to translate the training into practice. Three informants
had not yet created a business account at the time of the follow up visit. The main
obstacles were limited skills in producing photos and captions, lack of time because the
owners also handled production, weak internet signal and data costs, and dependence on
younger family members to operate the applications. I7 said, “Making content takes time
and the signal in my workshop is weak.

Discussion
The first finding, the shift from viewing branding as a logo toward viewing it as
identity and narrative, is consistent with brand theory, which treats a brand as a set of
associations that differentiates a product and builds trust and loyalty among consum ers
(Kotler & Keller, 2016 ; Tjiptono, 2019). It also echoes the conclusion of Odoom, et al.,
(2017) that SMEs often approach branding informally and need simple frameworks to
connect brand identity with marketing activities. In Payung Village, the metaphor of the
“spirit” of a product functioned as such a framework. However, this study can only show
a change in understanding and in visible brand elements such as names, logos, and labels;
whether consumers actually perceive these brands differently was not measured.
The second finding, the early adoption of social media and marketplace practices,
parallels the results of (Chatterjee & Kar, 2020 ; Qalati et al., 2021) , who reported that
perceived usefulness, low cost, and compatibility with existing routines encourage small
firms to adopt social media. The practical, smartphone based format of the training
appears to have lowered the perceived difficulty of these tools , which supports the
argument of Malodia et al., (2023) that the digital self efficacy of the entrepreneur is a key
driver of digital transformation. Unlike these survey based studies, which link adoption
with firm performance, the present study found only early signals such as customer
inquiries. This differe nce is expected because the observation period was short and
because, as Eller et al., (2020) ; Priyono et al., (2020)  show, performance effects tend to
appear only after firms move beyond promotional use toward deeper changes in their
business model and capabilities.
The third finding highlights that digitalization in a village context is constrained less
by the availability of technology than by skills, time, infrastructure, and trust. This is in
line with (Eller et al., 2020) , who identified employee skills and IT resources as
antecedents of SME digitalization, and with (Tambunan, 2019) , who emphasized the
limited capacity of micro enterprises in marketing and technology. It also reflects the

challenges of managing digital content and online presence noted by (Dwivedi et al.,
2021). From the perspective of digital marketing frameworks, the informants have
entered the stage of building presence and interaction on digital channels but have not
yet reached the stages of systematic content planning, customer data use, and
performance measurement (Kannan & Li, 2017; Sanjaya & Tarigan, 2009).
Taken together, the findings make it necessary to distinguish three levels of
outcome. The first level, improved understanding of branding and digital marketing, was
reported by almost all informants. The second level, changes in practice, was observed in
about two thirds of them. The third level, improved business competitiveness in terms of
sales, market share, or customer loyalty, could not be demonstrated by the available
evidence. Accordingly, this study does not claim that the training increased
competitiveness; rather, it shows that the training built the initial capabilities on which
competitiveness can later be developed, in line with earlier arguments that digital
marketing and integrated promotion can become sources of competitive advantage for

For SCI Polytechnic and the village government, a single training session should be
followed by scheduled mentoring, peer learning groups in which younger family
members support older owners, and simple tools for recording sales and customer
inquiries, so that future evaluations can measure business outcomes rather than relying
on perceptions alone.
CONCLUSION
This study explored the perceptions of MSME actors in Payung Village of the
digitalization training and the changes they reported in branding and digital marketing
through qualitative evidence collected before and after the training. Informants shifted
from viewing branding as a logo toward viewing it as a product identity and story. Most
informants took early steps in digital marketing, such as creating business accounts and
redesigning labels, and some reported more customer inquiries.
Skills, time, internet access, and confidence remained barriers, so the observed
changes represent improved understanding and early practice rather than demonstrated
gains in business performance or competitiveness. Practically, MSME actors in Payung
Village can start with consistent branding and regular content, while program organizers
should provide continuous mentoring. Future research could examine long -term impact
of MSME digitalization by measuring changes in business performance, market reach,
customer engagement, and revenue before and after the implementation of digital
strategies.
