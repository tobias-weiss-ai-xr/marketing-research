Abstract
Digital marketing can increase the visibility of community events, but greater visibility alone does not
guarantee that social, cultural, or economic value will be retained within the community. This article
examines the Satu nagari Satu Event (SNSE) programme in Indonesia to assess whether short-term
event attention can translate into sustained consumption patterns and lasting community beneﬁt. The
analysis draws on a secondary review of a 2023 mixed-methods evaluation that included 105 community
respondents through surveys, interviews, observation, and document analysis. The study examines how
value is created, how long different forms of value last, the role of digital tools in converting attention
into beneﬁt, and the mechanisms by which these beneﬁts may be retained locally. The evaluation found
that SNSE contributed to cultural learning, participation, and community cohesion, with 76% of
respondents perceiving stronger relationships. However, economic beneﬁts were less durable, as 62%
reported that post-event economic value was less than half of event-period levels. While 54% indicated
that cultural promotion continued through social media, inconsistent content, varying digital skills, and
weak integration with local companies limited its effectiveness. To address these issues, the study
proposes the Digitally Extended Community-Event Model, which links resource activation, co-production,
digital dissemination, sustainable consumption, and value retention. Drawing on Social Exchange Theory,
the model treats visibility and digital connectivity as resources that can be converted into community
value and highlights the temporal gap between rapid transactional outcomes and more enduring cultural
or social effects. The studies show that digital marketing can contribute to SDG 12 only where it provides
credible information and clear pathways for audiences to interact with local, culturally responsible, and
environmentally sustainable options.
1 Introduction
Digital marketing is increasingly expected to fulﬁl a new role in sustainability transitions. Instead of
serving solely as a driver of visibility and demand, digital communication can address information gaps,
enhance the visibility of sustainability attributes, connect consumers with responsible products and
services, and inﬂuence choices related to Sustainable Development Goal 12 on responsible consumption
and production. Recent research demonstrates that social media content, advertising, and digital
destination communication can shape sustainable purchasing and travel intentions. However, these
efforts also reveal a persistent challenge: digital attention does not necessarily translate into responsible
behaviour(Rajput & Gandhi, 2025; Tiwari et al., 2025).
This issue is especially acute for cultural events. Events concentrate visitors, transactions,
performances, voluntary labour and media attention within a short period. They can support local food
producers, transport providers, accommodation, craftspeople and cultural groups. They can also
strengthen social interaction, place attachment and collective competence. Yet event studies continue to
warn against equating a well-attended occasion with sustainable development. Event-day indicators
capture a temporary concentration of activity; they reveal much less about who retains beneﬁts, how
long these beneﬁts last, whether environmental pressures are managed, or whether community
Page 2/32

capability accumulates between annual editions (Mair & Smith, 2021; Stevenson, 2020; Thelen & Kim,
2024).
The temporal problem matters for rural and community-based programmes. A festival may create a
proﬁtable market for several days, while the same vendors face limited demand during the following
months. A performance may be ephemeral, while the knowledge, relationships and digital records
produced through its preparation can persist. Economic, social and cultural outcomes consequently
follow different trajectories. This article calls that difference chronological asymmetry: beneﬁts vary not
only in type and magnitude, but also in persistence, convertibility and local retention.
Digital marketing transformation offers a bridge across this time-related divide. Here, transformation is
deﬁned as organisational change in the purpose, capability and measurement of digital marketing, rather
than the adoption of additional platforms. Attendance-oriented promotion asks how content can bring
people to an event. Lifecycle-oriented value retention asks how event-generated content, data,
relationships and visibility can remain useful before, during and after the physical gathering. The second
approach requires systematic capture, ethical storytelling, searchable enterprise information, transaction
or booking pathways, post-event content calendars, and metrics linked to community outcomes rather
than reach alone (Alhaddar, 2025; Bashar et al., 2026; Florek-Paszkowska & Ujwary-Gil, 2025; Rojas et al.,
2024).
The sustainability of this change depends on the consumption that digital visibility enables. A local
purchase is not inherently sustainable. Its contribution depends on ownership, sourcing, cultural
representation, resource use, working conditions, waste, mobility and beneﬁt distribution. This article
therefore operationalises sustainable tourism consumption through four connected dimensions: local
economic retention, cultural responsibility, environmental accountability and temporal distribution of
demand. These dimensions provide a bounded interpretation of Sustainable Development Goal 12 and
prevent sustainability language from becoming a decorative label for ordinary promotion (Fadila &
Mandalia, 2026; Mandalia & Hidayat, 2026).
The Satu nagari Satu Event (One Village One Event, SNSE) programme in Tanah Datar Regency, West
Sumatra, offers a revealing case. A nagari is simultaneously an administrative territory and a socio-
cultural community within Minangkabau society. SNSE encourages participating nagari to organise
events around local cultural practices, food, performing arts, agriculture, natural attractions and
community capabilities. Production is embedded in musyawarah (deliberation), gotong royong (mutual
assistance), customary authority and community organisations. Previous research by Mandalia
identiﬁed the cultural-tourism potential of an early SNSE event, examined Instagram promotion for
Pesona nagari Pariangan, and documented the programme’s integrated marketing communication
(Mandalia, 2023). Related work showed that cultural and natural assets need coordinated infrastructure,
community participation and responsible planning to become viable tourism products (Mandalia &
Apriliana, 2023).
Page 3/32

These studies establish SNSE’s cultural and promotional signiﬁcance, but they leave a harder question
unresolved: does event-generated visibility become durable, locally retained, and sustainability-oriented
value? Related research by Fadila and Mandalia shows how visual social-media aesthetics can inﬂuence
sustainability perceptions, while Mandalia and Hidayat caution that inﬂuencer communication may
privilege aspiration and destination spectacle instead of substantive responsibility (Fadila & Mandalia,
2026; Mandalia & Hidayat, 2026). Together, these contributions suggest that the analytical task is to
trace conversion rather than assume it.
The 2023 SNSE evaluation provides evidence of this conversion issue. Respondents reported cultural
learning, active participation in the community, and closer relationships, with 76 per cent feeling that
community ties had grown closer. Economic beneﬁts were more concentrated during the event period.
62 per cent assessed the post-event economic value-added as being below 50 per cent. Although 54 per
cent reported ongoing cultural promotion via TikTok, Facebook, Instagram or YouTube, content
production was inconsistent and lacked links to business information, bookings, purchases or veriﬁed
sustainability practices. Consequently, this case allows for an analysis of why certain forms of value
persist whilst others disappear.
The study considers three research questions: (1) How does SNSE create social, cultural, economic,
visibility and digital value? (2) Why do these forms of value display different post-event trajectories? (3)
Under what conditions can digital marketing convert event visibility into sustainable consumption and
continuing community beneﬁt?
The article contributes in four ways. First, it distinguishes event value creation, conversion and retention
and identiﬁes chronological asymmetry as a missing dimension of event evaluation. Second, it develops
a dynamic extension of Social Exchange Theory (SET) in which visibility and digital connectivity are
potential resources whose value depends on conversion and distribution. Third, it positions digital
marketing as post-event sustainability infrastructure rather than a pre-event promotional function.
Fourth, it proposes the Digitally Extended Community-Event Model (DECEM), a process model linking
cultural resource activation, co-production, digital capture and circulation, sustainable-consumption
conversion and community value retention.
2 Literature Review and Conceptual Framework
2.1 Event Sustainability, Value and Time
Contemporary event scholarship has moved past operational management and gross economic impact
towards questions of sustainability, legacy, technology and justice. Mair and Smith argue that making an
individual event operationally greener is insuﬃcient if the wider systems of production, consumption and
mobility remain unchanged (Mair & Smith, 2021). This critique is important because event sustainability
is often reported through highly visible actions, such as recycling bins or plastic reduction. In contrast,
Page 4/32

less visible questions of local ownership, cultural authority, unpaid labour and long-term capability
receive weaker attention.
Community events produce heterogeneous value. Economic value includes sales, employment,
enterprise exposure and demand for services. Social value includes interaction, trust, participation,
wellbeing and a perception of collective achievement. Cultural value includes knowledge transmission,
recognition of practitioners, public performance and continuity of local meaning. Visibility value
concerns recognition of a place, group, product, or practice by audiences outside the immediate event.
Digital value concerns the capacity to capture, circulate and convert information, attention and
relationships through digital networks. Recent indicator research conﬁrms that social value requires
dimensions such as community identity, facilities, empowerment, equity and inclusion rather than a
single beneﬁt score (Costa et al., 2025).
Time complicates assessing these values. Turnover is often immediate and measurable, but may
disappear as visitor concentration shifts. Cultural learning and organisational relationships may develop
slowly and persist in less visible forms. Digital records may remain accessible for years, but only create
community value if they are maintained, discoverable and connected to action. A lifecycle perspective
therefore asks three distinct questions: what value is created, how is it converted, and where and for how
long is it retained?
This distinction similarly sharpens the meaning of legacy. Legacy is hardly a list of positive outcomes
attached retrospectively to an event. It is a process through which material and immaterial resources are
intentionally maintained, redistributed or allowed to decay. For small community events, the strategic
issue is often not expensive permanent infrastructure, but whether each event cycle leaves stronger
cultural groups, enterprise networks, digital assets, governance routines and evidence for the next cycle.
2.2 Community Co-production, Authenticity and Social
Sustainability
Community events can operate as social infrastructure because planning, rehearsal, hosting and
problem-solving require repeated interaction. Stevenson identiﬁes social capital, interaction,
participation, sense of place and wellbeing as central pathways through which community events
support social sustainability (Stevenson, 2020). Supportive communities are also a condition of
sustainable event tourism, since local legitimacy, participation and small-business involvement cannot
be substituted by external promotion (Li et al., 2021). SET-based research similarly shows that residents
assess both material and non-material effects when forming support for sustainable tourism (Han et al.,
2023).
Co-production is conceptually different from attendance. Residents who organise an event invest time,
voluntary labour, knowledge, relationships, space and reputation. They may simultaneously be
organisers, performers, hosts, vendors and audience members. Beneﬁts can include income, recognition,
enjoyment, strengthened relationships, new skills and cultural pride. The exchange is therefore collective
Page 5/32

and multidimensional, not a simple transaction between an individual resident and an abstract tourism
industry.
Authenticity provides both opportunity and risk. Event-based activation can increase the frequency and
visibility of cultural practices, create new spaces for intergenerational learning and reinforce local
identity. Yet digital and visitor markets may reward simpliﬁed, spectacular or easily shareable
representations. A systematic review of authenticity in events shows that socially sustainable outcomes
depend on a multi-actor ecosystem in which host communities retain inﬂuence over meaning and
representation (Le et al., 2024). Research on food festivals similarly ﬁnds that weak community
involvement, ambiguous representation, and inadequate resource management can undermine social
and environmental sustainability at the same time (Thelen & Kim, 2024).
For SNSE, this shows that cultural revitalisation should not be judged solely by visibility. Relevant
evidence includes who determines what is presented, whether cultural groups remain active, whether
knowledge circulates within the nagari, and whether economic transformation strengthens the
community’s authority rather than replacing it.
2.3 Digital Marketing Transformation and Sustainable
Consumption
Tourism digitalisation research describes social media, mobile platforms, data analytics and user-
generated content as mechanisms for destination branding, engagement and decision support (Kilipiri et
al., 2023; Lin et al., 2022). Smartphone-based social media can support a sustainable destination
marketing ecosystem, while social-media data can inform recovery and visitor management (Sharmin et
al., 2021). Big-data research also shows potential for monitoring tourism pressures and supporting
decisions, although implementation and governance remain uneven (Rahmadian et al., 2022). These
studies shift attention away from isolated posts towards socio-technical systems.
Digital transformation should therefore be distinguished from digitisation. Digitisation converts material
into digital form; digitalisation introduces digital processes into existing practice; transformation
changes strategy, routines, capabilities, relationships and performance criteria. In a community-event
context, opening an Instagram account represents adoption. Coordinating content capture, cultural
approval, enterprise listings, reservation links, analytics, responsible visitor information and post-event
learning represents transformation. Recent reviews emphasise that insuﬃcient capability, unequal
access and limited implementation in developing destinations constrain the sustainability capability of
digital branding (Alhaddar, 2025; Bashar et al., 2026).
Social media inﬂuence also operates across the travel cycle. It shapes destination image, information
search, norms, intentions, on-site conduct, experience sharing and post-visit reﬂection (Janik & Ryszko,
2026; Lin et al., 2022). However, the literature remains heavily focused on attitudes and self-reported
intention. Evidence that online exposure produces repeated, lower-impact or locally beneﬁcial behaviour
is much thinner (Janik & Ryszko, 2026). This intention–behaviour gap is especially relevant to SNSE
Page 6/32

because high engagement with cultural imagery does not, by itself, show that audiences purchase local
products, book community experiences, or travel outside event peaks.
Information architecture is one mechanism connecting attention to action. Content can identify the
producer, describe cultural provenance, show veriﬁed sourcing or waste practices, provide price and
location information, link to an ordering or booking channel, and state responsible visitor expectations.
Cultural dissemination research in tourism-oriented municipalities shows that adoption strategies
require objectives, actors, content, and platform governance rather than uncoordinated posting (Rojas et
al., 2024). Reviews of tourism experience-sharing likewise demonstrate that user-generated content has
both marketing and meaning-making functions.
This article translates sustainable consumption into four dimensions suitable for community tourism.
Local economic retention asks whether spending remains with local producers and workers. Cultural
responsibility asks whether consumption upholds practitioners and protects meaning, consent and
representation. Environmental accountability asks whether sourcing, materials, waste and mobility
reduce harm. Temporal distribution asks whether demand is spread beyond short peaks and whether
beneﬁts recur. These four dimensions align with broader research that views responsible consumption
as simultaneously environmental, social, ethical and systemic (De et al., 2025).
2.4 Social Exchange Theory as a Dynamic Value-
Conversion Lens
SET remains inﬂuential in studies of resident support because it focuses on perceived beneﬁts, costs,
reciprocity, and dependence. Recent tourism applications indicate that residents evaluate both material
and non-material effects, and that environmental concern, quality of life and trust can condition support
(Erkılıç et al., 2026; Han et al., 2023). Yet conventional applications often reduce exchange to an
individual calculation in which economic beneﬁts are weighed against tourism costs. Such a formulation
is too narrow for community-produced cultural events.
The SNSE case requires three extensions. Firstly, the resources exchanged are both collective and
individual in nature. Cultural knowledge, customary authority, social relationships and community
reputation cannot be treated as privately owned inputs. Secondly, the outcomes vary over time. A beneﬁt
may be immediate but fragile, or delayed but cumulative. Thirdly, resources can change form. A
performance can become a recording; a recording can generate visibility; visibility can lead to discovery;
discovery can generate commissions; commissions can support cultural practice. Therefore, the
theoretical unit is the pathway of value conversion, not a static balance sheet.
Digital value is deﬁned as the community’s capacity to capture, govern, circulate, and convert event-
generated information, visibility, and relationships through digital networks into continuing social,
cultural, environmental, or economic beneﬁt. Exposure is necessary but insuﬃcient. Digital value is
realised only where audiences can ﬁnd credible information and act through accessible pathways, and
where the resulting beneﬁt is retained or fairly distributed in the community.

2.5 Conceptual Framework
The analytical framework developed for this research conceptualises community-event sustainability as
a value-retention process. The central proposition is: The sustainability of cultural events depends not
only upon their ability to create value during the event but also upon local capacity to retain and circulate
that value afterwards.
3 Research Context: Satu Nagari Satu Event
Tanah Datar Regency lies in West Sumatra, Indonesia, and contains 75 nagari across a cultural
environment strongly associated with Minangkabau identity. Performing arts, food, customary
leadership, architecture, agriculture, oral histories and collective institutions are not simply tourism
inputs; they are embedded in community life. This differs from a destination model in which private ﬁrms
assemble attractions from externally controlled resources.
A nagari combines territorial administration with social and customary organisation. Formal government
operates alongside actors such as niniak mamak (customary clan leaders), bundo kanduang (women
associated with Minangkabau matrilineal authority and cultural tradition), youth groups, tourism-
awareness groups, cultural associations and local companies. Authority, knowledge and labour are
dispersed across these actors. Event production consequently depends on negotiation, reciprocal
obligations and the mobilisation of existing institutions.
SNSE was developed to encourage nagari to create events grounded in their distinctive assets.
Programmes vary but commonly include performance, culinary products, customary activities, local
Page 8/32

history, natural attractions, traditional games, agriculture and community enterprise. Mandalia’s study of
Sepekan Alek Anak nagari Andaleh Baruh Bukik showed that an early event assembled performing arts,
traditional games, social activity and a cuisine market, while arguing for deeper visitor participation and
attention to community vendors (Mandalia, 2023).
Promotion became progressively digital. The Pesona nagari Pariangan case identiﬁed Instagram as a
cost-effective way to reach visitors (Mandalia et al., 2024b). Research across the wider programme later
showed the importance of coordinating conventional and digital channels while coordinating
communication with local cultural settings. These studies indicate adoption, but do not establish that
digital communication consistently sustained enterprise activity after event periods. That unresolved
issue is the empirical focus of the present article.
The SNSE has high conceptual value, as its expected outcomes encompass culture, participation,
tourism, and local economic development. The programme also highlights the governance and capacity
constraints commonly encountered in decentralised rural programmes. Each nagari has different assets
and organisational capacities, whilst regional policy seeks to implement standardised programmes
across different areas. Therefore, this case study examines how standardised event policies interact
with locally speciﬁc resources and uneven levels of digital capability.
4 Methodology
4.1 Design and Claim Boundary
This article employs an interpretative embedded case study design and a secondary qualitative re-
analysis of the 2023 SNSE programme evaluation. The case under consideration is the SNSE programme
in Tanah Datar. The embedded units comprise the participating communities and the reported processes
relating to cultural activation, participation, entrepreneurial activities, infrastructure, promotion and post-
event sustainability. The aim is illustrative and theory-building: to identify mechanisms that explain the
event’s value across different temporal trajectories.
The ﬁrst evaluation used a mixed-method design and involved 105 community respondents. Its archive
combined questionnaire-based indicators, synthesised structured in-depth interviews, non-participant
observation and documentary materials. The present study does not treat the questionnaire component
as an independent statistical dataset and conducts no inferential testing. Percentages are kept only as
contextual anchors for patterns also represented in the qualitative materials.
The archives available for re-analysis do not include complete interview transcripts or data on the
number and demographic proﬁles of interviewees, which are stored separately. Consequently, this study
does not claim to have achieved saturation at the transcript level, does not compile quotations, and does
not link paraphrased statements to ﬁctitious participant identities. These limitations reduce the
rhetorical richness but safeguard the integrity of the evidence. The claims made in this study are
Page 9/32

conﬁned to the patterns documented in the evaluation material and the theoretical interpretation of
those patterns.

4.3 Analytical Procedure
The data were analysed through thematic integration. This approach allowed evidence collected through
different parts of the evaluation to be organised, compared and interpreted within a common analytical
framework.
In the ﬁrst stage, the evidence was organised according to six major impact areas identiﬁed in the
original evaluation:
1. Cultural revitalisation and local knowledge;
2. Community participation and collective action;
3. Social cohesion and community connectedness;
4. Cultural events and local economic opportunities;
5. Infrastructure and destination development; and
6. The post-event sustainability gap.
Page 10/32

These impact areas served as initial descriptive categories. They helped the researchers identify where
particular ﬁndings belonged, but they were not the article's ﬁnal themes.In the second stage, the
researchers compared evidence within and across the six impact areas to identify recurring patterns,
connections, and differences. Related ﬁndings were then reorganised into six analytical themes:
1. Cultural and social value creation;
2. Event-generated economic value;
3. Temporal asymmetry and the post-event sustainability gap;
4. Digital visibility and post-event communication;
5. Barriers to digital value conversion; and
6. Sustainable-consumption and community-business opportunities.
This reorganisation brought together ﬁndings that were originally reported under separate impact areas.
For example, evidence concerning cultural revitalisation, community participation and social cohesion
contributed to the theme of cultural and social value creation. Findings concerning local economic
opportunities, infrastructure and destination development informed the analysis of economic value and
community-business opportunities. Evidence concerning changes after an event contributed to the
theme of temporal asymmetry and the post-event sustainability gap.
Digital communication emerged as a cross-cutting theme because it connected several areas of the
evaluation rather than a single category. Community stakeholders frequently identiﬁed social media as a
means of continuing cultural promotion, maintaining destination visibility and connecting audiences with
local businesses after an event had ended. The analysis therefore considered digital communication in
relation to cultural continuity, economic opportunities and the retention of event-generated value.
In the ﬁnal stage, the relationships among the themes were interpreted using Social Exchange Theory
(SET). SET was used to examine the resources contributed by community members and other
stakeholders, the beneﬁts generated through the programme, and the extent to which those beneﬁts
remained within the community. Particular attention was given to the distinction between:
a. Temporary beneﬁts, which were mainly produced during the preparation and delivery of an event;
and
b. Retained beneﬁts, which continued, circulated or developed after the event.
This distinction supported the article's focus on value retention. It allowed the analysis to consider not
only whether SNSE generated social, cultural and economic value, but also whether this value could be
maintained and converted into longer-term community beneﬁts.
A methodological limitation should be acknowledged. The original evaluation was not designed as a
consumer-behaviour experiment. It did not collect transaction-level digital analytics, longitudinal
Page 11/32

purchasing records, or individual visitor-behaviour data. Consequently, this study does not claim that
digital communication directly caused sustainable purchasing or other consumer responses.
Instead, the analysis identiﬁes digital practices reported or observed in the case, the constraints
affecting their continuation after events, and possible mechanisms through which digital engagement
could support sustainable consumption and local business development. These mechanisms are
grounded in the case evidence, but their causal effects have not yet been tested. Future research should
therefore examine the proposed Digitally Extended Community-Event Model (DECEM) using longitudinal
digital-engagement metrics, enterprise sales records, booking data, and visitor-level behavioural
measures.
4.4 Rigour Reﬂexivity and Ethics
Credibility is reinforced through source triangulation, a clear distinction between descriptive indicators
and interpretative claims, and attention to negative cases. The claim-evidence matrix is structured so
that every conclusion can be traced back to one or more types of evidence. Conceptual claims are also
calibrated by strength: observed or reported patterns are distinguished from proposed mechanisms and
future implementation pathways. Consequently, this framework is analytical in nature, rather than a
validated causal model.
Researcher proximity to SNSE created both contextual access and interpretive risk. Familiarity with
Minangkabau concepts improved understanding of nagari institutions, musyawarah and gotong royong,
but could encourage favourable assumptions about community participation. Reﬂexive analysis
therefore treated participation, local purchasing and digital visibility as questions rather than automatic
evidence of sustainability. The account retained contradictions, weak follow-up, and environmental
issues.
The earliest non-clinical social research involved voluntary participation and was reported in aggregated
and anonymised form. No identiﬁable personal data are reproduced. Before submission, the authors
must insert the approving institution, ethics reference, or formal exemption applicable to the ﬁrst
evaluation; this information was not included in the supplied manuscript archive.
5 Findings
5.1 Cultural Activation and Learning
Cultural revitalisation was the clearest programme outcome. SNSE did not introduce cultural practice
into previously empty settings. Participating nagari already maintained activities such as Randai,
Saluang, Silek, local music, dance and cultural-group performance. Approximately half of respondents
reported that cultural activities had already been organised routinely. The change concerned scale,
visibility, coordination and social salience.
Page 12/32

Event preparation transformed dispersed practices into a public programme. Evaluation evidence
described existing activities being enlarged into Baralek Gadang, or major community celebrations.
Preparation created reasons to identify practitioners, rehearse performances, explain traditions and
involve younger residents. Cultural value emerged through doing and organising, rather than through
spectatorship alone.
Cultural groups were important carriers of this value. Eighty-two per cent of respondents reported that
their nagari already had a sanggar, while communities without one were beginning to establish groups.
Existing groups became more active and gained opportunities beyond their own communities. This
indicates a reinforcing pathway in which preparation supports learning, performance generates
recognition, and recognition can encourage further cultural activity.
Nevertheless, this evidence points more towards cultural consolidation than towards long-term
revitalisation. Ongoing practice, intergenerational participation, practitioners’ income and community
control over representation were not measured longitudinally. The SNSE creates supportive conditions,
but sustainability requires evidence beyond the year in which the event takes place.
5.2 Participation as Co-production and Relational
Durability
SNSE relies on intensive local organisation. The planning process involves discussions, formal meetings,
informal discussions, public awareness campaigns, and engagement with community leaders.
Implementation relies on committees, voluntary work, cultural knowledge, and cooperation.
Consequently, residents act as active participants who contribute both tangible and intangible resources,
rather than as passive hosts who merely bear the impacts of tourism.
The process itself created value. Rehearsal required cooperation, venues required preparation, and
visitor management created problems which required joint decisions. Recognition, enjoyment, collective
achievement, interaction and cultural pride provided returns alongside possible income. This helps
explain why strong participation can coexist with modest or short-lived economic beneﬁts: residents
evaluated a multidimensional exchange.
Seventy-six per cent of respondents felt that relationships within the community had become closer. The
qualitative synthesis linked this change to repeated coordination, rather than merely attending events.
The relationships forged during several weeks of preparation can endure even after the temporary
infrastructure is no longer in place. These ﬁndings support the interpretation that events constitute
social infrastructure, although they remain a perceived outcome rather than a measure of long-term
networks.
5.3 Temporary Market and Uneven Economic Continuity
The economic impact has been positive but unevenly distributed. The SNSE has sparked ideas for
developing culinary products, tourism, micro and small enterprises, accommodation, transport, and
Page 13/32

tourist attractions. The increase in visitor numbers has opened up new opportunities for existing traders
and service providers. However, the evaluation ﬁndings indicate that many participating businesses were
already operating. The event’s activities have not consistently generated new businesses or year-round
income.
The temporal pattern is very clear. During the event period, visitors and transactions concentrated, but
demand weakened afterwards. Sixty-two per cent of respondents assessed the post-event economic
value-added as below 50 per cent, and some reported no additional beneﬁts. Consequently, the
programme was more successful at creating market moments than at sustaining the market.
Participants identiﬁed limited follow-up, mentoring and enterprise development as constraints. Most
communities lacked dedicated spaces such as galleries or workshops for micro, small and medium
enterprises. Some settings improved roads, signage, cultural facilities, and destination recognition, but
permanent systems developed more slowly than annual events mobilised activity. Economic conversion
was constrained by both digital and physical infrastructure.
5.4 Digital Visibility without Reliable Conversion
Digital communication continues even after events end. A total of 54 per cent of respondents reported
that cultural promotion is still ongoing, utilising a combination of TikTok, Facebook, Instagram and
YouTube. This conﬁrms previous SNSE research highlighting the relevance of Instagram and integrated
communication (Mandalia, 2023; Mandalia et al., 2024a). Event production generates a wealth of
reusable material, such as performances, interviews, food preparation, handicrafts, local history, visitor
reactions, natural scenery, and backstage collaboration.
The main constraint was not a complete lack of technology. It was discontinuity and weak conversion
architecture. Communities reported diﬃculty maintaining regular content. Account ownership and post-
event scheduling responsibilities were often unclear, and capabilities varied. More importantly, cultural
posts were not consistently linked to searchable producer details, ordering, guide contacts,
accommodation, experience booking, visitor information or sustainability evidence.
This gap distinguishes digital adoption from digital transformation. Adoption was visible in the use of
familiar platforms. Transformation would require governance, capability, interoperable information and
measurement across the event lifecycle. Under the observed arrangement, online attention could
circulate while economic and sustainability value remained unrecorded or unrealised.
5.5 Environmental Accountability as an Enabling Condition
Traﬃc and waste management appeared as practical sustainability challenges. Communities
coordinated with police and transport actors, identiﬁed alternative routes, managed parking and used
waste teams and gotong royong. These actions show local problem-solving capacity, but some disposal
practices required improvement, and the evaluation did not quantify waste volumes, emissions or
material recovery.
Page 14/32

The evidence therefore does not support a claim that SNSE had already established green business
models. It supports a development pathway. Localised supply chains, reduced single-use materials,
reusable equipment, waste separation, food-waste management, local accommodation, walking routes,
shared transport and off-peak demand could form a sustainability-oriented community business model.
Digital communication would contribute by making veriﬁed practices and accountable choices visible.

6 Discussion
6.1 Time Asymmetry Explains Uneven Sustainability
The strongest explanatory ﬁnding is not that SNSE produced both beneﬁts and constraints; most event
evaluations do. The contribution is showing that value forms follow different temporal logics.
Transactions depend on consumer concentration and close quickly. Relational and cultural resources are
embodied in people, groups and routines and may persist. Digital resources can persist at low marginal
cost, but only if governed, maintained and connected to action. Figure 2 summarises these trajectories.
Page 15/32

Temporal asymmetry changes the evaluation. High turnover rates on the day of the event may reﬂect a
short-lived spike rather than a long-term impact on livelihoods. Increased cooperation or less visible
cultural competence may underpin subsequent activities. Therefore, a proper comparison is not simply
economic versus social impacts, but immediate versus enduring value within each domain. This
argument reinforces the call for event research to pay greater attention to sustainability and legacy (Mair
& Smith, 2021)
The ﬁnding also clariﬁes why a single post-event satisfaction score is insuﬃcient. Persistence,
convertibility and distribution are separate properties. Cultural knowledge may persist but not generate
income; digital visibility may persist but be captured by external platforms; sales may occur but ﬂow to
foreign suppliers. A strong sustainability account must trace these properties rather than aggregate
unlike outcomes.

relationships, spaces and reputation. Returns include economic opportunities, but also recognition,

material and non-material life domains jointly inﬂuence resident support (Han et al., 2023).

communication creates awareness and supplies practical information. During the event, it supports
wayﬁnding, interpretation, live engagement and systematic capture. After the event, it can preserve
cultural accounts, make enterprises discoverable, distribute demand and maintain relationships. This
lifecycle interpretation is consistent with research that treats social media as socio-digital infrastructure
rather than a set of isolated promotional variables (Janik & Ryszko, 2026).

The difference between an event-reset and event-accumulation cycle is important. In a reset cycle,
communities mobilise intensively, deliver a temporary beneﬁt, allow attention and routines to decline,
and begin again. In an accumulation cycle, documentation, digital continuation, enterprise development
and evaluation strengthen the starting conditions for the next event.
The model illustrates how SNSE can extend its economic impact beyond the event itself. Pre-event
communication encourages attendance, while visitors’ digital content supports post-event cultural
storytelling and continued destination and product discovery. This ongoing visibility can encourage
future consumption of local products and services, helping to create more sustained economic value for
the community. However, extending this pathway requires more than digital visibility alone. Converting
continued exposure into sustained economic value ultimately depends on the readiness and quality of
the local products, services, and relationships that support it. Digital marketing is not the only missing
element. Content cannot compensate for absent products, weak service quality, unreliable fulﬁlment, or
contested cultural representation. Its infrastructural role is to connect developing capabilities, maintain
information, and reveal where conversion fails. Digital transformation is therefore as much
organisational and relational as technological.
6.4 Converting Attention into Sustainable Consumption
The ﬁndings separate event-generated consumption from event-enabled sustainable consumption.
Event-generated consumption happens when visitors gather at an event and make on-the-spot
purchases. Event-enabled sustainable consumption happens when the event creates information,
relationships, and responsible choices that inﬂuence later or repeated decisions. Digital marketing helps
only if it changes the information and actions around local products, services, and cultural experiences.
For example, a post about traditional food might make people want to try it. However, a post that
focuses on its impact includes details such as who makes it, its cultural signiﬁcance, local ingredients,
how to buy it, its price, and packaging or waste management practices. Similarly, a performance video
might catch people’s attention. Still, a version focused on change would include the group's name,
explain the cultural background and consent, and direct viewers to workshops or booking details. This
shifts the focus from simply showcasing something to empowering people to take action.
This difference aligns with research showing that tourism images, inﬂuencer posts, and credible sources
shape how people think and act (Blanco-moreno et al., 2024; Hussain et al., 2024). It also relates to the
gap between what people intend and what they actually do in sustainable consumption, as well as
differences in digital skills (Alhaddar, 2025). In the SNSE case, this gap appears where communities and
businesses meet: good intentions cannot turn into local action if information, products, or ways to buy
are missing.
However, just turning interest into purchases does not prove sustainability. As Figure X shows, the
sustainability of community cultural events depends on six connected factors: what people buy, who
Page 18/32

owns the production, where the materials come from, how culture is shown, how environmental impacts
are handled, and how beneﬁts are shared.
The ﬁrst three factors are about the local economy. What visitors buy shows if their spending supports
local products and services. Ownership shows if the money stays in the community. Where the materials
come from shows how strong local connections are. These three factors go beyond buying and selling.
Cultural representation is about proper context, recognition, and community involvement. Environmental
management covers waste, packaging, resource use, and related issues. Beneﬁt distribution looks at
whether economic opportunities reach many people in the community or just a few. So, sustainable
consumption is not just about how much people spend, but also about where things come from, who
owns them, cultural respect, environmental care, and how beneﬁts are shared.
Digital marketing can show these factors by linking consumers with producers, cultural meanings,
responsible practices, and ways to buy. But maintaining credibility matters. Sustainability claims should
be clear, evidence-based, and not exaggerated. For example, if a nagari has just started separating
waste, communication should mention that speciﬁc step instead of calling the whole event green. This
aligns with research showing that clear, honest messages build trust, while greenwashing can reduce it
(Abate et al., 2026; Seyﬁ et al., 2025).
Digital marketing can show sustainability practices, but it cannot replace them. Content cannot make up
for missing products, poor service, unreliable delivery, or disputed cultural representation. Its main role is
to build skills, keep information up to date, and show where conversion fails. Digital transformation is as
much about people and organizations as it is about technology.
6.5 Sustainability-Oriented Community Business Models
The evidence supports a sustainability-oriented business-model pathway rather than a claim that formal
green business models already exist. A viable system would connect a digital cultural archive, local-
enterprise directory, community tourism packages, marketplace or ordering links, experience booking,
sustainability information and a coordinated annual event calendar. These components convert
dispersed event assets into a discoverable regional offer.
Such a model creates value through complementarity. Food, performance, homestays, guiding and
landscapes become more viable when combined into bookable experiences. Digital directories reduce
search costs. Shared standards and storytelling improve credibility. A regional calendar can distribute
visitors among nagari and across seasons. This systemic interpretation aligns with digital-sustainability
research that emphasises multi-stakeholder collaboration, feedback, government support, and adaptive
capability (Florek-Paszkowska & Ujwary-Gil, 2025; Jokom et al., 2025).
Environmental performance must be embedded in the offer. Local sourcing can reduce leakage and
strengthen producer links, but operators must also address transport, energy, packaging and waste.
Reusable equipment, waste separation, food-waste protocols, walkable routes, shared transport and
visitor guidance are practical starting points. Digital ticketing or information can reduce some materials,
Page 19/32

although digital tools also carry energy, access and exclusion costs. Green claims should follow
measured practice rather than lead it.
The case further reveals a digital inclusion issue. Capability differences among nagari can cause the
best-resourced communities to capture disproportionate attention. Regional coordination should
therefore provide shared infrastructure without erasing local control. Training, templates, technical
support, accessibility standards, and pooled analytics can reduce inequality, while content approval,
enterprise information, and cultural authority remain locally governed.

7 Implications
7.1 Theoretical Implications
First, event value must be analysed as a process of creation, conversion and retention. This triad moves
beyond impact inventories and makes temporal mechanisms explicit. Second, temporal asymmetry
explains why visible economic outcomes may decay faster than relational or cultural outcomes. It calls
for domain-speciﬁc follow-up periods rather than a single measurement date.
Third, extending SET redeﬁnes visibility and digital connectivity as potential exchange resources. Their
value depends on governance, conversion, and distribution. Fourth, digital marketing becomes part of
sustainability infrastructure because it coordinates information and action across time. This position
avoids technological determinism: platforms enable and constrain practices but do not guarantee
responsible behaviour or equitable beneﬁt.
Fifth, sustainable consumption in community tourism should be assessed through local retention,
cultural responsibility, environmental accountability and temporal distribution. This multidimensional
formulation is more demanding than equating local purchasing with sustainability and provides a clear
bridge between community event research and SDG 12.
7.2 Policy and Managerial Implications
SNSE should be governed as a year-round regional ecosystem rather than a sequence of isolated
occasions. Every participating nagari needs a post-event digital plan that assigns content ownership,
cultural approval, publication cadence, enterprise updates, customer response and data review. Content
should be captured during preparation as well as during the public event because co-production is
central to cultural and social value.
community value retention occurs when the beneﬁts generated remain embedded locally and strengthen
cultural practices, social relationships, environmental resilience and community enterprises.
Three cross-sectoral enabling conditions inﬂuence these seven stages. Institutional support provides
coordination, standards, continuity and equitable access to shared infrastructure. Digital capabilities
encompass content production, data management, customer service, platform governance and
transactional readiness. Environmental responsibility determines whether increased or redistributed
consumption can credibly contribute to sustainability. Therefore, these conditions are not separate
stages, but rather factors that shape the effectiveness of the entire pathway.
Crucially, DECEM should not be interpreted as a universal or linear maturity ladder. Communities may
enter this model at different points, relationships between stages may weaken or fail, and the transition
may be contested. Its analytical value lies precisely in its ability to highlight these disruptions. The model
helps destinations identify whether constraints stem from inadequate content recording, missing
business data, limited cultural acceptance, insuﬃcient responsible products, weak transaction or
fulﬁlment systems, inadequate metrics, or ineffective mechanisms for retaining beneﬁts locally.
Page 21/32

Regional support should extend beyond basic platform training. Communities need skills in short-form
video, photography, storytelling, metadata, accessibility, search visibility, analytics, online customer
service, ethical representation, e-commerce and crisis communication. Training should include reusable
templates, shared tools, and mentoring through at least one post-event cycle.
Every promotional asset should provide an appropriate local action. Not every post requires a sale, but it
should let audiences learn, credit practitioners, contact a guide, ﬁnd a producer, book an experience,
follow a cultural group, or understand responsible conduct. This simple rule turns content planning into
value-pathway design.
The Tanah Datar events site can integrate an annual calendar, village proﬁles, cultural insights, a
directory of businesses, bookable experiences, accessibility information, transport advice and veriﬁed
sustainability practices. The site’s administrators must ensure that it does not merely become a static
directory. Local stakeholders need the right to update information and receive support, while regional
coordination maintains standards, interoperability, and data analysis.
7.3 Measurement Framework
Evaluation should retain event-day indicators but add measures of persistence, conversion and
distribution. Attendance, vendors and turnover remain useful operational measures. They should be
accompanied by three- and six-month indicators for cultural activity, enterprise outcomes, digital
pathways and environmental practice. Measurements must be feasible for communities; a small,
consistent dashboard is preferable to an elaborate framework that goes unused.
Treat attention metrics such as reach, views, and likes as upstream indicators. Intermediate indicators
include proﬁle visits, saves, map requests, enterprise click-throughs and qualiﬁed enquiries. Outcome
indicators include bookings, orders, practitioner payments, off-peak visits, repeat participation, local-
input shares, material reduction and the distribution of beneﬁt among enterprises and groups. No single
indicator establishes sustainability; the dashboard must show relationships across domains.

8 Limitations and Future Research
The study has four principal limitations. First, it reanalyses a programme-evaluation archive rather than a
dataset designed speciﬁcally to test Digitally Extended Community-Event Model (DECEM). Second, full
transcripts and a separate interview-sample proﬁle were unavailable, preventing quotation-based
analysis and claims of transcript-level saturation. Third, descriptive percentages were aggregated and
cannot establish relationships between participant characteristics and perceived outcomes. Fourth, the
evaluation lacked platform analytics, enterprise sales, booking data, waste quantities and longitudinal
follow-up.
These limitations deﬁne the next research design. A prospective multi-event study should collect
baseline, event-period, three-month and twelve-month measures. Platform data should be connected,
with appropriate consent and privacy protections, to enterprise enquiries, sales and bookings. Cultural
groups should deﬁne indicators of representation, consent, continuity and practitioner beneﬁt.
Environmental measurement should include material use, waste, sourcing and visitor mobility.
Comparative work across nagari could then examine how institutional support, digital capability and
environmental practice condition conversion.
Page 23/32

Future research should also examine distributional effects. Digital visibility may favour actors with
stronger devices, skills, language or existing networks. Gender, age, customary authority and enterprise
type may inﬂuence who supplies unpaid labour, who appears in content and who receives income.
Participatory methods and community-controlled data governance would help test whether digital
transformation strengthens collective capacity or reproduces local inequalities.
Finally, Digitally Extended Community-Event Model (DECEM) should be treated as a falsiﬁable process
model. Researchers should identify failed conversions as carefully as successes. High reach with no
enquiry, enquiry with no fulﬁlment, sales with high leakage, or cultural exposure without consent are
theoretically informative breakdowns. Such evidence would reﬁne the model and prevent digital
sustainability from becoming an unquestioned success narrative.
9 Conclusion
SNSE demonstrates that community cultural events can create substantial cultural, social and visibility
value while producing less durable economic outcomes. Cultural learning, active groups, collective
production and closer relationships may persist because they become embedded in people and
community routines. Transactions decline more quickly because they depend on temporary visitor
concentration. This temporal asymmetry explains why event-day success and sustainable development
cannot be treated as equivalents.
Digital platforms offer a bridge, but platform use is not transformation. Transformation occurs when
communities systematically capture event assets, govern cultural representation, circulate credible
information, connect audiences with responsible local actions, measure conversion and retain beneﬁts.
The observed gap in SNSE lay between visibility and this conversion infrastructure.
DECEM outlines the resulting process: resource activation, co-production, event experience, digital
capture, circulation, sustainable-consumption conversion, and community value retention, enabled by
institutional support, digital capability, and environmental responsibility. The model extends SET by
showing that visibility and connectivity are potential resources whose value depends on control,
conversion, temporal persistence and beneﬁt distribution.
For SDG 12, the implication is precise. Digital marketing contributes to responsible consumption only
when it helps people choose locally embedded, culturally responsible, and environmentally accountable
products and experiences through credible, accessible pathways. The strategic aim is not to keep an
event permanently visible. It is to ensure that the social, cultural, economic and environmental capacities
assembled by the event do not disappear when the crowd leaves.
