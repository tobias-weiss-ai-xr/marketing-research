Abstract
Artificial intelligence (AI) is often analysed through technical, organisational, or regulatory
perspectives that treat capability, adoption, and governance as separate issues. This article
conceptualises AI instead as an emerging macro socio-technical regime generated through
the interaction of finance, infrastructure, capability, and productivity. It develops the F–
I–C–P framework to explain how finance enables infrastructure, infrastructure conditions
capability, and capability creates the possibility–but not the guarantee–of productivity and
value realisation. The article also introduces the concept of regime-level AI infrastructure
to identify concrete configurations, such as AI Gigafactories, foundation-model platforms,
national compute infrastructures, public AI platforms, sectoral AI ecosystems, and corporate
AI infrastructures, through which the macro-regime becomes observable and evaluable. The
central analytical problem is structural misalignment: the risk that financial expectations,
infrastructural expansion, technical capability, organisational absorption, productivity re-
alisation, governance capacity, and societal outcomes evolve at different speeds or under
unequal conditions of power. Governance is therefore conceptualised not as a final stage after
technological deployment, but as a transversal and anticipatory steering capacity. Briefly
illustrated through Spain and Chile as non-frontier configurations, the article argues that AI
governance should be understood as regime alignment: the task of anticipating, evaluating,
and correcting structural misalignments before they become embedded in infrastructural
dependencies, organisational routines, market structures, and international asymmetries.

1 Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly described as a general-purpose technology capable of transforming
production, labour, scientific research, education, public administration, communication, defence, and
organisational decision-making. Yet its social and economic consequences cannot be inferred from technical
capability alone. Socio-technical regime theory shows that technological change becomes consequential
through configurations of technologies, infrastructures, markets, institutions, user practices, expectations,
and rules (Geels, 2002, 2004; Markard et al., 2012). From this perspective, AI should not be analysed merely
as a set of models, applications, or tools, but as an emerging macro socio-technical regime.
The central difficulty is that the main dimensions of this regime are often examined separately. Technical
capability is analysed apart from the infrastructures that enable it; infrastructure is examined apart from the
financial commitments that build and discipline it; productivity is discussed apart from the organisational
and institutional conditions required to realise it; and governance is frequently treated as a regulatory layer
added after technological deployment. This separation obscures the structural relations through which AI
becomes socially, economically, and politically consequential. Existing research on AI productivity, labour,
infrastructure, supply chains, and governance provides important insights into these dimensions, but rarely
integrates them into a single regime-level architecture (Acemoglu, 2025; Acemoglu and Johnson, 2023; del
Rio-Chanona et al., 2025; Kwon and Porter, 2025; Muldoon et al., 2026; Valdivia, 2025; Ghosh et al., 2025).
ThisarticleaddressesthatgapbydevelopingtheF–I–C–Pframework( F→I→C→P ), whichconceptualises
finance, infrastructure, capability, and productivity as the generative architecture of the AI regime.
Finance mobilises and allocates capital; infrastructure converts capital into material and computational
capacity; capability defines what AI systems can potentially do; and productivity represents the realisation of
economic, organisational, or public value. This framing builds on research showing that AI’s productivity
effects depend not only on technical progress, but also on task composition, organisational complementarity,
labour institutions, and the creation of socially useful new tasks (Acemoglu, 2025; Acemoglu and Johnson,
2023; Alam et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Sohn, 2025).
The article further introduces the concept of regime-level AI infrastructure. The macro-regime perspective
does not imply that AI exists only as an abstract global system. Rather, the regime is materialised through
concrete configurations in which financial commitments, computational infrastructure, capability production,
organisational dependence, productivity expectations, governance arrangements, and societal consequences
become structurally interconnected. These configurations may include AI Gigafactories, foundation-model
platforms, national compute infrastructures, public AI platforms, sectoral AI ecosystems, and corporate
AI infrastructures. Recent work has already argued that AI governance must increasingly confront AI
not only as a tool, but also as infrastructure (Naser, 2026); infrastructure studies similarly conceptualise
infrastructure as relational, embedded, and organisationally consequential rather than as a neutral technical
layer (Star and Ruhleder, 1996). European AI Gigafactories and generative AI platforms illustrate how
such configurations may combine compute, access, capability production, dependency, governance, and value
expectations (Council of the European Union, 2026; European Commission, 2026; Nzembayie and Urbano,
2026).
The central problem addressed in this article is therefore structural misalignment. AI-related risks do not
arise only from biased models, unsafe systems, or insufficient organisational controls. They also arise when
finance expands faster than realised productivity, when infrastructure becomes concentrated in ways that
restrict access to capability, when technical capability grows faster than organisational absorption, when
productivity gains fail to translate into welfare or legitimacy, or when governance responds too slowly to shape
trajectories before they become locked in. This interpretation is consistent with recent work on structural AI
risk and with path-dependence research showing how early trajectories may become difficult to reverse once
they are embedded in institutional and organisational arrangements (Kilian, 2026; Pierson, 2000).
The article asks: How do finance, infrastructure, capability, and productivity interact to constitute an
emerging macro socio-technical AI regime; under what conditions do concrete AI configurations acquire the
character of regime-level AI infrastructure; and how can governance anticipate and address the structural
misalignments generated by these interactions?
The answer developed in this article is that AI governance should be understood as regime alignment: the
institutional task of keeping financial expectations, infrastructural capacity, technical capability, organisational
absorption, productivity realisation, governance capacity, and societal outcomes within sustainable relations.
Governance is therefore not conceptualised as a final stage after technological development, but as a transversal

2 Conceptual Foundations and Analytical Approach
This article develops an integrative conceptual framework for analysing AI as a macro socio-technical regime.
Its aim is not to assess particular AI systems or to measure national AI readiness, but to explain how finance,
infrastructure, capability, productivity, and governance interact as constitutive dimensions of a broader regime
whose effects extend across economies, organisations, institutions, and societies.
The framework synthesises four bodies of literature: socio-technical systems and transition studies, political
economy and structural power, AI productivity and labour research, and responsible and anticipatory AI
governance. Each of these literatures captures an important part of the AI transformation, but none alone
explains the structural interdependence among capital allocation, computational infrastructure, technical
capability, productivity realisation, and societal outcomes.
2.1 AI as a Macro Socio-Technical Regime
Socio-technical regime theory describes relatively stabilised configurations of technologies, infrastructures,
institutions, markets, user practices, regulations, and cultural expectations (Geels, 2002, 2004; Markard
et al., 2012). AI extends this perspective because it is not confined to a single sector. Its development and
deployment affect finance, labour, science, logistics, education, public administration, defence, communication,
and organisational decision-making. It also depends on a global material base composed of semiconductors,
data centres, cloud infrastructures, energy systems, data resources, and supply chains.
AI should therefore be understood as a macro socio-technical regime: a broad configuration of technical
capabilities, infrastructures, markets, institutions, expectations, and power relations that increasingly shapes
the conditions under which economic and social activity is organised. This regime-level perspective is
necessary because AI-related transformations cannot be explained by technical capability alone. Model
performance, autonomy, and scalability matter, but they are enabled by infrastructures, financed through
specific investment arrangements, embedded in organisations, and mediated by governance.
The proposed framework specifies the internal architecture of this emerging regime by identifying four
generative dimensions: finance, infrastructure, capability, and productivity. These dimensions form a sequence
of interdependence in which finance enables infrastructure, infrastructure conditions capability, capability
creates the possibility of productivity, and expectations of productivity feed back into finance. Governance is
not treated as an additional stage, but as a transversal capacity acting across these relations.

2.2 From AI-as-Tool Governance to Regime-Level AI Infrastructure
Much AI governance still begins from an AI-as-tool perspective. From this view, the central governance
task is to ensure that individual AI systems are safe, accurate, transparent, accountable, fair, robust, and
compliant. These requirements remain indispensable. However, they are insufficient when AI systems become
embedded in the infrastructures, platforms, organisational routines, financial expectations, and institutional
arrangements through which economic and social activity is organised. The governance problem then shifts
from the assessment of isolated systems to the steering of structural relations.
This difference can be expressed schematically as follows:
Gtool → {safety,accuracy,fairness,accountability,compliance}.
This formulation captures the logic of system-level governance. It is appropriate when AI remains a discrete
application or organisational tool. However, it becomes insufficient when AI configurations begin to organise
access to capability, structure organisational workflows, create dependency, concentrate value capture, or
condition future productivity. In such cases, the relevant object of governance is no longer only the individual
AI system, but the structural configuration through which AI becomes infrastructural.
This shift is especially important because AI does not become socially consequential through technical
capability alone. AI capability depends on computational infrastructure, cloud platforms, semiconductors,
data, energy systems, supply chains, specialised labour, capital allocation, organisational absorption, and
governance capacity. These elements are not external background conditions; they shape who can develop
AI, who can access it, who depends on it, and who captures value from it. In this sense, AI increasingly
operates not only as a tool used within organisations, but also as an infrastructural condition for other forms
of activity.
This argument is consistent with recent work suggesting that AI governance instruments must increasingly
confront AI not only as a tool, but also as infrastructure (Naser, 2026). It also draws on infrastructure studies
that conceptualise infrastructure not as a neutral technical layer, but as a relational and embedded arrangement
connecting technical systems, organisational practices, access conditions, and large-scale institutional change
(Star and Ruhleder, 1996). From this perspective, infrastructure is not defined by technical scale alone. It
becomes infrastructural when other actors organise their practices around it, when access to it conditions
participation, and when it becomes difficult to substitute without significant organisational or institutional
disruption.
The concept of regime-level AI infrastructure captures this transition. Many AI systems remain discrete
applications, organisational tools, or sector-specific solutions. Others acquire regime-level infrastructural
character when they integrate financial commitments, computational infrastructure, capability production,
organisational dependence, productivity expectations, governance arrangements, and societal consequences.

2.3 Structural Power and Integrative Conceptual Analysis
A regime-level analysis of AI also requires attention to power. The relevant question is not only what AI
systems can do, but who controls the conditions under which they are financed, built, deployed, accessed,
and governed. Susan Strange’s theory of structural power is useful because it shifts attention from direct
influence over particular decisions to the capacity to shape the frameworks within which other actors operate
(Strange, 1988, 1996).
Applied to AI, structural power resides not only in ownership of models or data, but also in control over
finance, infrastructure, supply chains, standards, cloud platforms, regulatory architectures, and productivity
gains. Actors that control these conditions can shape the direction of innovation, the distribution of value,
the terms of access, and the dependencies faced by others. This is especially important for non-frontier
economies, which may adopt AI systems widely without controlling the financial, infrastructural, or technical
foundations of the regime.
Methodologically, the article is an integrative conceptual synthesis. It does not test a hypothesis using a
dataset or offer a formal dynamic model. Instead, it constructs an analytical framework by bringing together
concepts, mechanisms, and empirical tendencies identified across several research streams. The resulting
propositions are conceptual rather than empirically validated claims; they are intended to clarify relationships
that future research can operationalise and test.
The article also uses Spain and Chile as illustrative international configurations. These examples demonstrate
the usefulness of the framework for understanding non-frontier positions within the AI regime, but they are
not presented as exhaustive comparative case studies. The conceptual contribution is therefore twofold: first,
the article identifies the F–I–C–P architecture as the core generative structure through which AI becomes
socially and economically consequential; second, it reconceptualises governance as a transversal steering
capacity directed toward structural misalignments. This framing is necessary because the societal effects of
AI cannot be explained by capability, adoption, or regulation in isolation. They emerge from the interaction
between capital, infrastructure, technical capability, productivity realisation, institutional capacity, and
structural power.
This framing contributes to technology-in-society research by explaining how technological change becomes
socially consequential through the interaction of economic systems, material infrastructures, organisational
practices, governance capacity, and distributive outcomes. AI is therefore analysed not as an autonomous
driver of social transformation, but as a regime whose consequences depend on the institutional and political-
economic structures through which it is financed, built, adopted, and governed.
3 Finance, Infrastructure, Capability, and Productivity: The Generative
Architecture of the AI Regime
The previous section established why AI governance must move from the assessment of isolated systems to
the analysis of regime-level infrastructures and structural relations. This section specifies the generative
architecture of those relations. Its purpose is not to propose a deterministic sequence or a formal dynamic
model, but to identify the structural order through which AI becomes economically, organisationally, and
socially consequential: capital is mobilised, infrastructure is built, capability is produced, and value may—or
may not—be realised.

The proposed framework conceptualises AI as a macro socio-technical regime generated through the interaction
of four structural dimensions: finance, infrastructure, capability, and productivity.
At its simplest, this architecture can be represented as:
F→I→C→P.(1)
Finance enables the expansion of infrastructure; infrastructure conditions the production and deployment
of capability; capability creates the technical possibility of productivity; and productivity, in turn, sustains
or challenges the expectations upon which further investment depends. The notation is deliberately simple.
It is not an econometric model, but an analytical grammar for relating dimensions that are often studied
separately.
This distinction is central because technical capability does not automatically produce economic or social
value. AI systems may expand the range of automatable tasks, but their translation into productivity depends
on infrastructure, organisational adaptation, institutional direction, labour arrangements, and the creation
of complementary forms of work. Similarly, infrastructure expansion may accelerate technical capability
while generating financial exposure if expected returns fail to materialise within anticipated timeframes.
The core analytical problem is therefore not the growth of any single dimension, but the alignment—or
misalignment—among them.
This section defines the four generative dimensions of the regime. It first examines finance as the mechanism
through which capital is mobilised and risk is allocated. It then analyses infrastructure as the material and
organisational substrate of AI capability. It next defines capability as technical potential rather than realised
value. Finally, it conceptualises productivity as value realisation and introduces the feedback loop through
which expectations of future productivity reproduce or destabilise the regime.
3.1 Finance
Finance refers to the mechanisms through which capital is mobilised, allocated, priced, and exposed to risk in
support of AI development. These mechanisms include venture capital, strategic equity, corporate investment,
public funding, debt, private credit, special-purpose vehicles, securitisation, and other financial arrangements
used to support the expansion of computational infrastructure.
In the AI regime, finance does not merely provide external support for technological development. It shapes
the scale, speed, direction, ownership, and risk profile of technological expansion. Decisions about capital
allocation influence which infrastructures are built, where they are located, who controls them, what returns
are expected, and how risks are distributed across firms, investors, states, and wider economic systems.
This is particularly important because advanced AI development and deployment require large-scale and long-
term investment in semiconductors, data centres, cloud systems, energy networks, connectivity, data resources,
and specialised technical capacity. These investments are often justified by expectations of future productivity,
market dominance, revenue growth, or strategic advantage. Finance therefore becomes structurally linked to
expectations about the future performance of AI systems and the future value they may generate.
The finance–infrastructure relationship can be expressed as:
F→I.(2)
Finance enables infrastructure, but it also disciplines it. It determines not only how much infrastructure
is built, but also what forms of infrastructure are prioritised, which actors gain control over the material
foundations of AI capability, and which risks are shifted across firms, investors, states, workers, and users.
This relationship also introduces a central source of fragility into the AI regime. If expected productivity
exceeds realised productivity over sustained periods, financial commitments may support infrastructure
expansion that is economically, socially, or institutionally difficult to justify. In such conditions, the regime
may become exposed to overinvestment, debt pressure, valuation instability, and pressure to accelerate
automation in order to validate prior investment expectations.
Finance should therefore be understood as a constitutive dimension of the AI regime, not as a background
condition. It links expectations, capital allocation, infrastructure expansion, ownership structures, and risk
distribution. The question is not simply whether AI attracts investment, but how that investment shapes the
material conditions of capability, the concentration of control, and the future trajectory of productivity and
social value.

3.2 Infrastructure
Infrastructure denotes the material and organisational substrate through which financial investment is
converted into operational AI capacity. It includes specialised semiconductors, high-performance computing
systems, data centres, cloud platforms, energy networks, telecommunications infrastructure, data resources,
supply chains, and the specialised technical labour required to maintain and scale these systems.
This understanding is consistent with infrastructure studies, which conceptualise infrastructure not as a neutral
technical layer, but as a relational and embedded arrangement connecting technical systems, organisational
practices, access conditions, and large-scale institutional change (Star and Ruhleder, 1996).
Within the AI regime, infrastructure performs two simultaneous functions. First, it acts as a productive input:
without sufficient computational capacity, energy, connectivity, and data availability, technical capability
cannot be developed, trained, deployed, or scaled. Second, infrastructure operates as a source of structural
power. Control over computational resources conditions who can develop advanced models, who can deploy
them at scale, who can access downstream markets, and who remains dependent on external providers.
This dual function makes infrastructure more than a neutral technical layer. Control over computational
resources has become a central locus of economic concentration, enabling rent extraction and shaping
competition at a systemic scale. Kwon and Porter (2025) show how exclusive access to data and machine-
learning resources can affect innovation and competition. Infrastructure therefore determines not only the
technical frontier of AI development, but also the distribution of access, dependency, and market power.
The infrastructural basis of AI is also embedded in global supply chains. Advanced AI systems depend on
semiconductors, data-centre construction, energy systems, rare minerals, data labour, model deployment
infrastructures, and waste streams distributed across multiple jurisdictions. These chains are politically
contested and socially uneven, generating dependencies that extend beyond the immediate technical system
(Muldoon et al., 2026). From this perspective, AI infrastructure participates in broader dynamics of
supply-chain capitalism, where environmental costs, labour extraction, and infrastructural dependencies
are distributed asymmetrically across global production networks (Valdivia, 2025). The environmental
dimension is particularly relevant in the expansion of data centres, where energy demand, server location,
and decarbonisation pathways shape the social and ecological costs of scaling AI systems (Xiao et al., 2025).
The infrastructure–capability relationship can therefore be expressed as:
I→C.(3)
Infrastructure conditions capability because the development and deployment of advanced AI systems require
access to computational scale, energy, data, and technical platforms. At the same time, infrastructural
concentration limits who can participate in frontier development and who is positioned primarily as an
adopter, client, or rule-taker. In this sense, infrastructure acts as a gatekeeping layer within the AI regime: it
determines what can be built, who can build it, and under what conditions it can be deployed.
3.3 Capability
Capability refers to the technical capacities of AI systems, including their performance, generality, autonomy,
scalability, capacity for tool use, and ability to operate across multiple domains. It should not be understood
merely as the automation of isolated tasks, but as the expansion of systems capable of performing cognitive,
analytical, generative, and increasingly agentic functions at scale.
Capability is central to the AI regime because it defines the range of possible applications and transformations.
More capable systems can automate a wider set of tasks, support more complex decision processes, interact
with tools and digital environments, and potentially operate with increasing autonomy. Following Amodei
(2026), such developments may introduce actor-like properties that shift AI-related risk from the purely
technical domain toward systemic and societal levels.
However, capability should not be confused with productivity, welfare, or social value. A system may perform
well on technical benchmarks, automate specific tasks, or exceed human performance in selected domains
without generating proportional economic or social benefits. Capability represents technological potential;
its consequences depend on how it is embedded in organisations, infrastructures, markets, and governance
arrangements.
This distinction is essential for avoiding technological determinism. The same technical capability may be
used to augment human labour, support professional judgement, create new tasks, or improve public services.

It may also be used to substitute workers, deskill occupations, concentrate returns, or externalise risks. The
direction of technological change is therefore not determined by capability alone, but by the institutional,
organisational, and economic structures that channel its deployment.
The capability–productivity relationship can be represented as:
C→P.(4)
Yet this relationship is conditional rather than automatic. Capability creates the possibility of productivity,
but it does not guarantee value realisation. The translation fromC to P depends on absorptive capacity,

increasing capability may expand automation without producing sustainable productivity gains or broadly

3.4 Productivity and Value Realisation
Productivity refers here to the realisation of economic, organisational, and public value from AI deployment.
It should not be reduced to the mere automation of tasks or the technical efficiency of individual systems.
Rather, productivity concerns whether AI capabilities generate measurable improvements in output, quality,
innovation, cost reduction, organisational performance, service delivery, or the creation of socially useful new
tasks.
For this reason, the framework definesP as productivity and value realisation, not as welfare. This distinction
is conceptually important. Productivity gains may occur without producing equitable distribution, improved
job quality, institutional legitimacy, resilience, or collective well-being. Conversely, some socially valuable
uses of AI may generate public value that is not immediately captured by conventional productivity metrics.
The central analytical issue is therefore how technical capability becomes productive value, and under what
conditions that value contributes to broader social outcomes.
This distinction is consistent with Acemoglu’s account of the macroeconomics of AI. The aggregate impact of
AI depends not simply on technical progress, but on the share of tasks affected, the real cost savings generated,
the distinction between easy-to-learn and hard-to-learn tasks, and the creation of new tasks (Acemoglu, 2025).
In the absence of complementary organisational changes and socially useful task creation, technical capability
may generate limited aggregate productivity gains even under conditions of rapid automation.
The productivity paradox reinforces this point. Advances in algorithmic capability and large-scale investment
in AI have not yet translated clearly into sustained increases in aggregate productivity (Sohn, 2025). This
does not necessarily imply that AI is economically insignificant. It may indicate that capability is expanding
faster than organisations, labour markets, institutions, and measurement systems can adapt. Sohn’s notion
of “effortlessness” is useful in this context because it highlights how AI may assist, reduce, or replace human
effort in ways that are not fully captured by conventional productivity indicators (Sohn, 2025).
The relationship between capability and productivity also depends on whether AI systems complement
or substitute human labour. del Rio-Chanona et al. (2025) show that the employment and productivity
effects of AI vary according to the types of tasks affected and whether systems augment or replace human
workers. Augmentation-oriented configurations may preserve professional judgement while supporting
productivity gains, as illustrated by work on NextGen-AI in engineering and manufacturing (Alam et al.,
2024). Organisational learning and effective use also depend on task complexity and user engagement rather
than technical capability alone (Li et al., 2024).
This perspective connects directly with the broader argument advanced by Acemoglu and Johnson (2023):
technological innovation does not automatically generate shared prosperity. Its effects depend on whether insti-
tutions and organisational arrangements favour complementarity, new-task creation, and broadly distributed
value, or whether they favour substitution, rent concentration, and labour displacement.

question is not only whether AI systems become more capable, but whether those capabilities are embedded

3.5 Expectations, Feedback, and Regime Reproduction
The AI regime is not a simple linear sequence from finance to productivity. It is a recursive structure in
which expectations about future productivity feed back into present financial commitments. Financial actors
invest in infrastructure because they anticipate future revenue, productivity gains, market dominance, or
strategic advantage. These expectations then shape the scale and speed of infrastructural expansion, which
in turn conditions the development of technical capability.
The basic architecture can be expressed as:
F→I→C→P.(7)
However, the regime reproduces itself through a feedback loop:
P e t+1 →F t,(8)
where P e denotes expected productivity or expected future value. This distinction between expected
productivity and realised productivity is crucial. Expected productivity supports investment narratives,
market valuations, infrastructure expansion, and strategic commitments. Realised productivity reflects the
actual capacity of AI systems to generate economic, organisational, or public value once deployed.
The relationship can therefore be expressed as a tension between:
P e =expected productivity (9)
and:
P r =realised productivity.(10)
When P e substantially and persistently exceeds P r, the regime may become vulnerable to structural
misalignment. Infrastructure may expand faster than productive use cases mature. Financial obligations may
depend on returns that are delayed, uncertain, or unevenly distributed. Firms may intensify automation to
justify investment. States may support infrastructure expansion without sufficient capacity to ensure broad
social value. In such conditions, the finance–infrastructure–capability cycle may generate fragility rather than
sustainable transformation.
This feedback dynamic is especially important because AI infrastructure is capital intensive and often
concentrated in a small number of firms and jurisdictions. If expected returns fail to materialise within
anticipated timeframes, financial exposure can accumulate around infrastructure, valuations, debt obligations,
and market expectations. Conversely, if realised productivity is broadly distributed and institutionally
supported, the cycle may reinforce sustainable investment, organisational learning, and social value creation.
This feedback loop is the point at which technological expectation becomes a social and economic force.
Expectations about future AI productivity do not merely describe possible outcomes; they organise present
investment, infrastructure expansion, competitive behaviour, and institutional pressure. For this reason, the
governance of AI must also be understood as the governance of expectations, not only the governance of
deployed systems.
The F–I–C–P architecture therefore captures both the generative power and the fragility of the AI regime.
Finance enables infrastructure; infrastructure enables capability; capability enables the possibility of produc-
tivity; and expected productivity sustains finance. But the stability of the regime depends on whether these
dimensions remain aligned. The following section extends this architecture by introducing governance as a
transversal steering capacity, the conditioning mechanisms that mediate the translation from capability to
productivity, and the societal outcomes through which the regime must ultimately be assessed.
4 Governance, Conditioning Mechanisms, and Societal Outcomes
The F–I–C–P architecture explains how the AI regime is generated, but not how its social trajectory is steered.
Finance enables infrastructure, infrastructure conditions capability, and capability creates the possibility of
productivity; however, this sequence does not determine whether AI produces broad welfare, fair distribution,
resilience, or legitimacy. Similar levels of investment, infrastructure, or capability may generate very different
outcomes depending on institutional capacity, organisational absorption, structural concentration, and the
timing of governance responses.

For this reason, the framework requires a second analytical layer: governance, conditioning mechanisms,
and societal outcomes. Governance is treated not as a final stage after productivity, but as a transversal
steering capacity acting across the regime. Conditioning mechanisms explain why similar technologies produce

productivity from the broader consequences through which the regime must ultimately be assessed.

4.1 Governance as Transversal Steering Capacity
Governance should not be conceptualised as the final element of a linear sequence:
F→I→C→P→G.(11)
Such a formulation would imply that governance intervenes only after finance, infrastructure, capability,
and productivity have already developed. This would reproduce the limits of reactive regulation, in which
institutions respond to technological trajectories once they are already economically embedded or politically
difficult to reverse.
Instead, governance operates transversally across the AI regime:
G↔(F, I, C, P).(12)
Governance shapes the conditions under which capital is allocated, infrastructure is built, capability is
developed, and productivity is distributed. It also receives feedback from the regime in the form of financial
fragility, infrastructural dependency, labour disruption, social distrust, or geopolitical asymmetry. Governance
is therefore both a constitutive condition of the AI regime and an adaptive response to its consequences.
In this sense, governance is not merely regulation. It includes law, technical standards, institutional coordi-
nation, auditing systems, accountability mechanisms, competition policy, public investment, procurement,
safety requirements, data governance, organisational procedures, and international cooperation. Responsible
AI governance remains necessary for transparency, accountability, safety, fairness, robustness, and human
oversight, but regime-level governance must also address the structural interactions through which finance,
infrastructure, capability, and productivity generate systemic effects.
This regime-level view complements recent accounts of AI governance that emphasise adaptive regulatory
approaches, transparency, bias, and the economic influence of increasingly complex AI systems, while shifting
the focus toward the structural relations through which finance, infrastructure, capability, and productivity
interact (Ghosh et al., 2025).
Effective governance depends on four institutional capacities. Observability enables institutions to detect
changes in investment flows, infrastructure concentration, technical capabilities, deployment patterns, labour-
market effects, productivity outcomes, and systemic dependencies. Interpretability enables them to understand
how developments in one dimension affect the others. Controllability provides instruments capable of altering
trajectories, including regulation, financial supervision, competition policy, public infrastructure, procurement,
compute access rules, auditing, and technical standards. Adaptability enables institutions to revise these
instruments as technologies, markets, and risks evolve. Governance effectiveness therefore depends not merely
on the existence of rules, but on the capacity to observe, interpret, influence, and adapt to the evolving
regime.
4.2 Conditioning Mechanisms
The movement from capability to productivity is not automatic. Between the generative architecture of the
regime and its realised outcomes lie conditioning mechanisms that explain why similar technologies may
produce different effects across countries, sectors, organisations, and institutional contexts.
The first mechanism is absorptive capacity. This refers to the ability of an organisation, sector, or country to
recognise, assimilate, adapt, and productively use AI capabilities. It includes human capital, technical skills,
managerial competence, research ecosystems, institutional learning, administrative capacity, and the diffusion
of innovation across firms and public organisations.
The second mechanism is organisational complementarity. This concerns the redesign of tasks, workflows,
roles, incentives, training, and accountability structures required for AI to complement rather than simply
substitute human labour. The distinction between augmentation and substitution is not inherent in the
technology itself; it depends on organisational choices and institutional incentives.

The third mechanism is structural concentration. Concentration refers to the distribution of control over
capital, compute, cloud platforms, data, models, intellectual property, standards, and market access. It affects
the finance–infrastructure relation, the infrastructure–capability relation, and the distribution of productivity
gains.
The fourth mechanism is temporal adaptation and institutional latency. Financial expectations, infrastructure
expansion, technical capability, organisational absorption, productivity realisation, and governance capacity
evolve at different speeds. These differential speeds generate misalignments when institutions cannot adapt
quickly enough to shape technological and financial trajectories before they become locked in.

P= Φ(F, I, C, G|A, O, H, T),(13)

and value realisation depend not only on capability, but on the institutional and organisational conditions
under which capability is financed, deployed, absorbed, and governed.
4.3 Societal Outcomes
The final analytical distinction concerns the difference between productivity and societal outcomes. Pro-
ductivity and value realisation indicate whether AI capabilities generate economic, organisational, or public
value. However, they do not determine how that value is distributed, whether it improves welfare, whether
the regime is resilient, or whether institutions retain legitimacy and social trust.
The framework therefore distinguishes four societal outcomes. Welfare refers to the broader social value
generated by AI, including income, job quality, access to services, public value, opportunities, and collective
well-being. Distribution and dependency concern who captures the benefits of AI and who remains dependent
onexternalprovidersforcompute, cloudservices, models, capital, standards, ortechnicalknowledge. Resilience
refers to the capacity of countries, organisations, and institutions to withstand financial, infrastructural,
geopolitical, environmental, operational, or social shocks affecting the AI regime. Legitimacy and trust
concern whether AI deployment is perceived as fair, accountable, secure, justifiable, and socially beneficial.
The relationship between regime architecture, governance, conditioning mechanisms, and societal outcomes
can therefore be represented as:

where W denotes welfare, D distribution and dependency,R resilience, and L legitimacy and trust. This
expression captures the central normative claim of the framework: the social consequences of AI depend
not only on the expansion of capability or productivity, but on the governance of the structural conditions
through which finance, infrastructure, capability, and productivity interact.

5.1 Regime-Level AI Infrastructure: Concrete Configurations of the AI Regime
The macro-regime perspective does not imply that every AI system constitutes a regime-level configuration.
Many AI systems remain discrete applications, organisational tools, or sector-specific solutions. A system,
platform, facility, or corporate ecosystem becomes analytically relevant as regime-level AI infrastructure
when it integrates several structural dimensions of the AI regime: large-scale financial commitments, material
and computational infrastructure, technical capability production, organisational dependence, productivity
expectations, governance requirements, and societal consequences.
European AI Gigafactories provide a paradigmatic illustration of this distinction. They are not merely
technical facilities for running AI models. They are large-scale infrastructural configurations formally defined
as facilities capable of handling the complete lifecycle from development to large-scale inference of very
large AI models and applications, supported by AI-optimised computing capacity, data-centre infrastructure,
high-capacity storage and networking, secure cloud access environments, and specialised AI-oriented support
services (Council of the European Union, 2026). The Commission also frames them as a means to provide
EU innovators, researchers, industry, SMEs, and the public sector with access to large-scale computing power
and high-quality data, while supporting productivity, innovation, technological autonomy, and resilience
(European Commission, 2026).
The point is not that an AI Gigafactory exhausts the AI regime. Rather, it functions as an observable
regime-level unit: a configuration in which finance, infrastructure, capability, productivity expectations,
governance arrangements, and societal outcomes become structurally interconnected. This makes the concept
useful not only for analysing global AI dynamics, but also for evaluating concrete AI infrastructures before
their dependencies, lock-ins, asymmetries, and governance constraints become difficult to reverse.
The same logic applies to foundation-model platforms and generative AI ecosystems when they provide
non-substitutable, platform-governed capabilities that enable organisational or entrepreneurial activity while
simultaneously generating dependence and exposure to governance shocks (Nzembayie and Urbano, 2026).
The concept is also relevant for national and sectoral AI strategies. A national AI strategy should not be
assessed only by the number of AI applications it promotes, the existence of policy documents, or general
levels of adoption, but by the extent to which it enables, governs, or depends on regime-level AI infrastructures

5.2 Structural Misalignments and Analytical Propositions
Once concrete AI configurations acquire regime-level infrastructural character, the central analytical problem
becomes structural misalignment. A structural misalignment arises when one dimension of the AI regime
creates demands, expectations, dependencies, or risks that the others cannot absorb, support, or govern. The
issue is not that finance, infrastructure, capability, productivity, and governance must evolve at the same
speed, but that their interaction may become institutionally, economically, or socially unsustainable.
This interpretation is consistent with recent work on structural AI risk, which shifts attention from discrete
accidents or misuse toward system-level dynamics, feedback loops, power asymmetries, and governance
leverage points (Kilian, 2026).
The propositions should not be read as isolated hypotheses, but as a regime-level diagnostic. Each proposition
identifies a point at which technological change becomes socially consequential because one dimension of
the regime advances faster than, or becomes structurally detached from, the others. Together, they specify
the analytical mechanism through which AI development may generate systemic vulnerability even when
individual systems appear technically effective.
The first misalignment concerns the relationship between finance and infrastructure. The expansion of AI
infrastructure depends increasingly on large-scale capital commitments to semiconductors, data centres,
cloud systems, energy networks, and specialised technical capacity. These investments are often justified by
expectations of future productivity, revenue growth, or market dominance. However, if realised productivity
does not materialise within anticipated timeframes, infrastructure expansion may become financially fragile.

The result is not simply overinvestment, but the conversion of technological uncertainty into financial exposure.
This relationship supports the first proposition:
Proposition 1—Finance–Infrastructure MisalignmentThe expansion of AI infrastructure depends on
financial structures whose sustainability is conditional on future productivity and revenue expectations; when
expected returns exceed realised value, infrastructural growth may generate systemic financial vulnerability.
The second misalignment concerns infrastructure and capability. Advanced AI capability depends on access
to computational infrastructure, energy, cloud systems, data, and supply chains. Where these resources
are highly concentrated, the distribution of capability becomes correspondingly unequal. Actors controlling
infrastructure do not merely supply technical inputs; they shape the boundaries of innovation, access, pricing,
dependency, and market participation. Control over computational resources has become a central locus of
economic concentration. Kwon and Porter (2025) similarly show how exclusive access to machine-learning
resources and data can affect innovation and competition. This supports the second proposition:
Proposition 2—Infrastructure–Capability MisalignmentThe concentration of AI infrastructure
conditions the distribution of technical capability and reinforces asymmetries in technological, economic, and
strategic power.
The third misalignment concerns capability and productivity. Increases in AI capability expand the range of
tasks that can be automated, supported, or reorganised, but they do not automatically generate aggregate
productivity or social value. The translation from capability to productivity depends on absorptive capacity,
organisational complementarity, task structure, labour institutions, and governance. Acemoglu (2025)
emphasises that the macroeconomic effects of AI depend on the share of tasks affected, the scale of real cost
savings, and the creation of new tasks. Similarly, Acemoglu and Johnson (2023) argue that technological
progress does not automatically generate shared prosperity; it depends on whether institutions favour
complementarity or substitution. This supports the third proposition:
Proposition 3—Capability–Productivity MisalignmentIncreases in AI capability do not produce
proportional productivity gains unless they are accompanied by absorptive capacity, organisational comple-
mentarity, and institutional arrangements that direct technological change toward value creation rather than
mere task substitution.
The fourth misalignment concerns productivity and societal outcomes. Even where AI generates productivity
gains, these gains do not automatically translate into welfare, fair distribution, resilience, or legitimacy.
Productivity may be captured by firms controlling platforms, infrastructure, data, or capital. It may coexist
with labour displacement, declining job quality, increased dependency, environmental externalities, or loss of
institutional trust. Research on work and automation shows that the effects of AI vary depending on whether
systems complement or replace human labour and on the kinds of tasks affected (del Rio-Chanona et al.,
2025). Augmentation-oriented configurations may preserve professional judgement and support productivity
gains (Alam et al., 2024), but substitution-oriented trajectories may produce concentrated benefits and social
disruption. Recent evidence on AI-exposed occupations also suggests that labour-market effects may be
unevenly distributed, with employment declines concentrated among early-career workers in occupations
where AI is more likely to automate rather than augment human labour (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025). This
supports the fourth proposition:
Proposition 4—Productivity–Societal Outcomes MisalignmentProductivity gains generated by AI
do not automatically produce welfare, equitable distribution, resilience, or legitimacy; the translation from
productivity to societal value depends on governance, labour institutions, distributional mechanisms, and the
allocation of externalities.
The fifth misalignment concerns governance itself. As the AI regime accelerates, governance must act across
finance, infrastructure, capability, productivity, and societal outcomes. However, regulatory systems often
respond more slowly than technological and financial systems evolve. Wang et al. (2026) identify persistent
regulatory lag in the governance of generative AI, while Emery-Xu et al. (2025) argue that existing approaches
to AI governance may be insufficient for advanced and transnational AI systems. Governance therefore
becomes a structural condition for managing misalignments before they become embedded in infrastructure,
markets, labour systems, or international dependencies. This supports the fifth proposition:

5.3 Structural Power in the AI Regime
The concept of structural misalignment gains further analytical force when interpreted through Susan
Strange’s theory of structural power. Strange defines structural power as the power to shape the frameworks
within which other actors operate, rather than merely the power to influence particular decisions or outcomes
(Strange, 1988, 1996). This perspective is especially useful for analysing AI because the most consequential
forms of power are not limited to the possession of a specific model, dataset, or regulatory instrument. They
concern control over the structures that determine what can be produced, how it is produced, how it is
financed, and under what rules.
The first question is what is produced. In the AI regime, production includes not only goods and services, but
also computational capacity, models, datasets, platforms, infrastructures, and technical standards. Control
over infrastructure conditions which capabilities can be developed and which applications become economically
viable. Advanced chips, data centres, cloud platforms, proprietary datasets, and energy-intensive computing
systems define the material boundaries of AI production. These infrastructures are embedded in globally
distributed and politically contested supply chains involving semiconductors, minerals, data labour, energy
systems, deployment infrastructures, and waste streams (Muldoon et al., 2026). Their expansion also generates
environmental implications, especially through data-centre energy demand and decarbonisation pathways
(Xiao et al., 2025). From a structural power perspective, infrastructure is therefore not a passive technical
substrate. It determines the conditions under which capability can be produced and scaled.
The second question is how AI is produced and deployed. The same technical capability may be integrated
into organisations in different ways. It may augment human judgement, support learning, and generate new
tasks; or it may be used primarily to automate substitutable work, reduce labour costs, and concentrate

decision-making. This direction of technological change is not technologically predetermined. It is shaped
by organisational incentives, governance arrangements, labour institutions, and business models. When
governance and organisational complementarity are weak, deployment tends to favour short-term efficiency
gains and the automation of easily substitutable tasks. When governance supports augmentation, learning,
and accountability, capability is more likely to generate socially sustainable productivity. Thus, control over
organisational architectures and deployment incentives becomes a form of structural power.
The third question is how AI is financed. Finance shapes the speed, direction, and concentration of the regime.
Capital-intensive infrastructure requires large-scale investment, and these investments are increasingly linked
to expectations of future productivity, revenue, and strategic advantage. Financial structures can accelerate
infrastructure expansion, but they can also externalise risk if expected returns fail to materialise. Actors
with privileged access to capital can scale infrastructure more rapidly, influence technological priorities, and
capture rents from downstream dependence. In this sense, finance is not merely an enabling condition. It
structures the future trajectory of the regime by determining what kinds of AI development are funded, at
what scale, and under what return expectations.
The fourth question is under what rules the regime operates. Governance determines who may deploy AI
systems, under what conditions, with what obligations, and at what cost. Rules concerning standards,
auditing, transparency, liability, competition, procurement, data access, compute governance, and safety
requirements do not merely correct harms after deployment. They shape the conditions of production,
investment, access, and accountability in advance. From this perspective, governance itself is a form of
structural power. It defines the institutional environment within which finance, infrastructure, capability,
and productivity interact.
This Strangean interpretation clarifies why structural misalignments are politically significant. A finance–
infrastructure misalignment may expose some actors to risk while allowing others to capture returns. An
infrastructure–capability misalignment may leave adopting countries dependent on external platforms. A
capability–productivity misalignment may generate automation without broad value creation. A productivity–
societal outcomes misalignment may concentrate gains while weakening welfare, resilience, or legitimacy. A
governance misalignment may leave institutions unable to intervene until dependencies and risks are already
locked in.
The AI regime is therefore not only an arena of technological innovation. It is a configuration of structural
power in which control over finance, infrastructure, capability, productivity, and rules determines the
distribution of opportunities, risks, and dependencies. This is especially relevant for actors outside the
technological core. For such actors, the central issue is not simply whether they can adopt AI systems, but
whether they can influence the structural conditions under which those systems are financed, built, deployed,
governed, and translated into social value. The next section applies this framework to Spain and Chile as two
illustrative configurations beyond the technological core.

The proposed framework can be used to analyse how countries outside the technological core participate

finance, infrastructure, capability, productivity, and governance.
Spain and Chile are useful illustrative configurations because both combine active AI policy agendas with
limited control over the frontier layers of the global AI regime. They show that participation in AI cannot be
reduced to a binary distinction between technological leaders and passive adopters: countries may adopt AI
actively and strengthen governance capacity while remaining dependent on external infrastructures, capital,
platforms, chips, cloud systems, and frontier models.
Spain represents a configuration of supranationally embedded adoption. Its AI trajectory is shaped not only
by domestic capabilities, but also by its insertion into the European Union’s regulatory, infrastructural, and
funding ecosystem. Spain benefits from European and national digitalisation instruments, public investment,

assessments of AI ecosystems, governance, talent, research, and adoption (ILIA, 2025). Its institutional
stability, digital connectivity, and policy orientation give it a stronger AI profile than many regional peers. It
is therefore not analytically useful to describe Chile simply as a laggard or passive adopter. Rather, Chile
illustrates how a country can achieve regional leadership while remaining structurally constrained within the
global AI regime.

may also deepen dependency if the highest rents are captured by foreign infrastructure providers, platform

expertise, and regional leadership without controlling the full-stack conditions of frontier AI development. Its
productivity challenge is therefore to convert AI adoption into local productive development, skills formation,
SME diffusion, public value, and institutional resilience. As the OECD has emphasised in relation to Chile’s
digital transformation, the productivity benefits of digital technologies depend on their diffusion across firms
and sectors, and on complementary investments in skills, innovation, and organisational capacity (OECD,
2025).
The comparison between Spain and Chile highlights three implications of the framework. First, adoption
does not equal control. Both countries may expand AI use across firms, public services, and organisations,
but adoption alone does not determine who controls infrastructure, models, capital, standards, or value
capture. Second, governance capacity does not equal technological sovereignty. Spain benefits from EU
regulatory embedding, while Chile has developed a strong regional governance profile; nevertheless, both
remain dependent on external technological ecosystems. Third, institutional position changes the form of
dependency but does not eliminate it. Spain’s dependency is mediated by European integration, funding,
regulation, and public scientific infrastructure. Chile’s dependency is mediated by regional leadership, external
investment, connectivity, and global technology chains.

7 Governing the AI Regime: Alignment, Anticipation, and Evaluation
The preceding analysis suggests that the governance challenge posed by AI is not limited to the regulation of
individual systems. It concerns the steering of a macro socio-technical regime in which finance, infrastructure,
capability, productivity, and societal outcomes interact recursively. Responsible AI principles remain essential
for the design and deployment of trustworthy systems, but they are insufficient when the object of governance
is a regime rather than a single application. Regime-level governance must also address the structural
conditions under which AI is financed, scaled, deployed, and translated into productivity and social value.
7.1 Governing Structural Misalignments
Governing structural misalignments requires moving beyond compliance-oriented regulation toward regime-
level steering. The task is not only to ensure that AI systems satisfy formal requirements, but to shape
the structural conditions under which AI investment, infrastructure expansion, capability development,
organisational adoption, and productivity distribution evolve. AI governance therefore becomes a problem of
regime alignment: keeping financial expectations, infrastructural capacity, technical capability, organisational
absorption, and societal value within institutionally sustainable relations. This view is consistent with

recent work on the AI-policy-governance nexus, which shows how AI regulation and AI integration can move
governance beyond compliance and toward broader stakeholder accountability (Cordeiro et al., 2026).
The first governance priority concerns the finance–infrastructure relationship. AI infrastructure requires
large-scale investment in semiconductors, data centres, cloud systems, energy, connectivity, and specialised
technical capacity. These investments are often justified by expectations of future productivity, revenue
growth, or market dominance. Governance should therefore improve transparency around AI infrastructure
investment, leverage, debt exposure, private credit, public support, and concentration of financial risk.
Financial supervision should not treat AI infrastructure as a purely technological matter; it should assess
whether investment trajectories remain aligned with plausible productivity and revenue scenarios.
The second priority concerns infrastructure–capability concentration. Since advanced AI capability depends
on compute, cloud platforms, data, energy, and supply chains, actors controlling infrastructure acquire
gatekeeping power over downstream innovation and adoption. Governance should therefore address excessive
concentration through competition policy, interoperability requirements, cloud portability, public or shared
compute infrastructure, procurement rules, open standards, and supply-chain resilience. The aim is not full
technological autonomy, which may be unrealistic for many countries, but functional resilience: the capacity
to reduce vulnerability, diversify dependencies, and preserve meaningful access to AI capabilities.
The third priority concerns the capability–productivity relationship. AI capability does not automatically
become productivity. Governance should therefore support the organisational and institutional conditions
through which capability is translated into durable value. This requires attention to absorptive capacity, skills,
data governance, organisational redesign, SME diffusion, worker participation, public-sector capacity, and
incentives for augmentation rather than substitution-oriented automation alone. Recent work on data-centric
AI governance in European public administration similarly shows that responsible organisational value
depends on institutional arrangements for data quality, monitoring, accountability, and implementation rather
than on technical capability alone (Aguilar et al., 2026). The relevant question is not only whether AI is
adopted, but whether its adoption produces sustainable productivity, complementary forms of work, and
socially useful new tasks.
The fourth priority concerns the translation of productivity into societal outcomes. Even where AI generates
efficiency or output gains, these gains may be unevenly distributed. Productivity may coexist with wage
stagnation, labour displacement, dependency on external providers, environmental costs, or declining public
trust. Governance should therefore evaluate AI productivity in relation to welfare, distribution, resilience,
and legitimacy. This implies the use of distribution-sensitive indicators, job-quality metrics, environmental
accounting, transition support, and public-interest obligations. The social value of AI depends not only on
what it enables technically, but on how benefits, risks, costs, and responsibilities are institutionally allocated.
7.2 Anticipatory Governance of Regime-Level AI Infrastructure
The structural misalignments identified in this article require anticipatory governance. Anticipatory gov-
ernance is not merely faster regulation; it is the capacity to steer emerging socio-technical trajectories
before they become locked into infrastructures, financial obligations, organisational routines, labour-market
arrangements, or dependency relations. Within the AI regime, this means identifying interactions among
finance, infrastructure, capability, productivity, governance, and societal outcomes before they generate
systemic vulnerability.
This concern is especially relevant once concrete AI configurations acquire regime-level infrastructural character.
AI Gigafactories, foundation-model platforms, national compute infrastructures, public AI platforms, sectoral
AI ecosystems, and corporate AI infrastructures may become difficult to reverse once investment commitments,
access rules, organisational routines, technical standards, and dependency relations are stabilised around
them. Anticipatory governance therefore concerns not only the regulation of deployed AI systems, but also
the early steering of infrastructural configurations before they become locked into financial, organisational,
and institutional pathways.
This concern is consistent with path-dependence research, which shows how increasing returns, timing,
and institutional sequencing can make early trajectories difficult to reverse once they become embedded in
organisational and political arrangements (Pierson, 2000).
This broader approach depends on four institutional capacities. Observability enables institutions to
detect concentration, dependency, financial exposure, labour disruption, productivity gaps, infrastructural
bottlenecks, and emerging systemic risks. Interpretability enables them to understand how developments
in one dimension affect the others. Controllability provides instruments capable of altering trajectories,

including regulation, standards, public investment, competition policy, procurement, auditing, compute
governance, financial supervision, infrastructure access rules, and public-interest obligations. Adaptability
enables institutions to revise these instruments as technologies, markets, infrastructures, and risks evolve.

interpret, influence, and adapt to the evolving regime.
International coordination remains necessary, but it should be understood as a condition for governing
regime-level AI infrastructures rather than as a separate governance layer. Even when such infrastructures
are nationally or regionally anchored, their enabling conditions are transnational: finance, semiconductors,
cloud platforms, data flows, standards, energy systems, supply chains, foundation models, and deployment
infrastructures operate across jurisdictions. National governance alone cannot address these dependencies.
Coordination is therefore required around shared standards, compute governance, financial transparency,
supply-chain resilience, environmental reporting, interoperability, institutional capacity-building, and the
participation of non-frontier economies in rule-making processes. Without such coordination, regime-level AI
infrastructures may reproduce the asymmetries they are intended to reduce: frontier actors set standards,
control critical inputs, capture rents, and export dependency, while adopting economies internalise social,
organisational, and regulatory costs.
7.3 Limitations, Evaluative Utility, and Research Agenda
The framework proposed in this article is conceptual. Its purpose is to specify the structural relations through
which AI becomes a macro socio-technical regime, not to provide a complete empirical validation, a formal
dynamic model, or a full evaluative methodology. The discussion of Spain and Chile illustrates analytical
applicability, but it does not constitute a comparative case study. Similarly, the discussion of regime-level AI
infrastructure clarifies the unit of analysis through which the macro-regime becomes observable, but it does
not yet provide a complete assessment protocol for specific infrastructures. Future research should therefore
operationalise the framework across countries, sectors, firms, public administrations, and infrastructural
systems.
Beyond its conceptual contribution, the framework has potential evaluative utility for the next generation
of AI governance. As AI systems become embedded in cloud platforms, organisational workflows, public
services, scientific infrastructures, sectoral decision architectures, and national or regional AI strategies, they
increasingly function less as isolated applications and more as enabling infrastructures. In such contexts, the
relevant question is not only whether an AI system is safe, accurate, or compliant, but whether it creates
durable dependencies, concentration, lock-in, governance latency, substitutability problems, and asymmetric
capacity to capture value. The F–I–C–P framework can therefore be used as a diagnostic lens for assessing
regime-level AI infrastructures before their financial, infrastructural, organisational, and societal effects
become difficult to reverse.
Future research could translate this diagnostic lens into evaluative instruments for AI systems, platforms,
facilities, and organisational ecosystems that function as regime-level AI infrastructure. Such instruments
could include an AI Infrastructure Assessment Framework to determine when AI systems become embedded
in critical organisational, sectoral, or public functions; a Structural Misalignment Risk Scorecard to identify
tensions among finance, infrastructure, capability, productivity, governance, and societal outcomes; an
AI Dependency and Resilience Map to assess reliance on external providers, cloud platforms, compute
resources, models, data, standards, and supply chains; a Regime Alignment Dashboard to monitor whether
financial expectations, infrastructural capacity, organisational absorption, productivity realisation, and
societal outcomes remain within sustainable relations; and an Evaluation Protocol for AI as Infrastructure to
guide regulators, public institutions, firms, and international organisations in assessing access, substitutability,
interoperability, accountability, resilience, and systemic effects.
Five research directions follow.
First, future work should develop indicators for finance, infrastructure, capability, productivity, governance,
and societal outcomes, including indicators of regime-level infrastructural character.
Second, it should analyse dynamic misalignment by examining the different speeds at which financial
expectations, infrastructure expansion, technical capability, organisational absorption, productivity realisation,
governance capacity, and societal outcomes evolve.
Third, it should apply the framework comparatively across frontier economies, non-frontier developed
economies, and emerging or middle-income economies, as well as across sectors such as healthcare, education,
manufacturing, public administration, finance, defence, cybersecurity, and science.

8 Conclusions
The article’s central claim is that AI governance should be understood as regime alignment: the institutional
task of keeping financial expectations, infrastructural capacity, technical capability, organisational absorption,
productivity realisation, governance capacity, and societal outcomes within sustainable relations. This claim
follows from understanding AI not merely as a set of increasingly capable systems, but as an emerging macro
socio-technical regime. AI becomes socially and economically consequential when technical capabilities are
financed, embedded in infrastructure, deployed through organisations, translated into productive value, and
governed within broader institutional and political-economic structures.
The article has developed the F–I–C–P framework to explain this regime architecture. Finance enables
infrastructure; infrastructure conditions capability; capability creates the possibility of productivity; and
expectations of future productivity feed back into present financial commitments. This recursive structure
explains both the transformative potential and the fragility of the AI regime. It shows why technical capability
alone cannot determine AI’s societal trajectory: capability becomes consequential only through financial,
infrastructural, organisational, institutional, and distributive conditions.
A central contribution of the article is to clarify the concrete unit through which the macro-regime becomes
observable. The AI regime should not be understood only as an abstract global system. It is instantiated
through regime-level AI infrastructures: configurations such as AI Gigafactories, foundation-model platforms,
national compute infrastructures, public AI platforms, sectoral AI ecosystems, and corporate AI infrastructures.
These configurations become analytically significant when they integrate capital commitments, computational
infrastructure, capability production, organisational dependence, productivity expectations, governance
arrangements, and societal consequences.
This shift redefines the object of AI governance. The relevant object is not only the individual AI system, nor
even AI adoption at organisational or national level, but the structural alignment among capital allocation,
material infrastructure, technical capability, productivity realisation, governance capacity, and societal
outcomes. Once AI configurations acquire regime-level infrastructural character, they cannot be assessed only
through safety, accuracy, or compliance. They must also be evaluated in terms of dependency, concentration,
substitutability, lock-in, productivity realisation, governance latency, resilience, and legitimacy.
The central analytical problem is structural misalignment. Its significance lies not only in the existence of gaps
among finance, infrastructure, capability, productivity, governance, and societal outcomes, but in the way
these gaps become stabilised through investment commitments, infrastructural dependencies, organisational
routines, market concentration, and delayed institutional response. Misalignment therefore explains why the
governance of AI cannot be reduced to correcting failures in individual systems after deployment. It must
also anticipate how regime-level infrastructures may lock in dependencies, asymmetries, and value-capture
arrangements before they become difficult to reverse.
The article has therefore reconceptualised governance as a transversal steering capacity acting across finance,
infrastructure, capability, productivity, regime-level infrastructures, and societal outcomes. Effective gover-
nance depends on institutional capacities for observability, interpretability, controllability, and adaptability.

Responsible AI principles remain essential, but they must be complemented by anticipatory governance

The illustrative discussion of Spain and Chile showed how the framework can clarify the position of countries

countries can access, shape, govern, or build regime-level AI infrastructures capable of linking compute,
capability development, organisational absorption, productivity realisation, resilience, and societal value
under conditions of dependency.
The framework also has future evaluative utility. It can support the development of indicators, diagnostic
matrices, dependency maps, regime-alignment dashboards, and assessment protocols for AI systems, platforms,
facilities, and organisational ecosystems that function as regime-level AI infrastructure. Such instruments
would allow researchers, regulators, public institutions, firms, and international organisations to identify when
AI configurations become infrastructural, how they distribute dependency and value, and whether governance
capacity is sufficient to intervene before misalignments become locked in.
Future research should operationalise the framework empirically by measuring finance, infrastructure, capa-
bility, productivity, governance, regime-level infrastructural character, and societal outcomes across countries,
sectors, firms, and public administrations. Particular attention should be given to the different speeds at
which these dimensions evolve and to governance latency: the delay between the emergence of structural
misalignments and the capacity of institutions to respond effectively. Further work should also examine
how regime-level AI infrastructures are transformed under geopolitical rivalry, where infrastructure control,
automated scientific capacity, compute chokepoints, supply-chain dependence, and strategic threat perception
may convert technological acceleration into a governance problem of international order.
The societal trajectory of AI will depend not only on how capable AI systems become, but on whether
institutions can govern the regime-level relationships through which capability is financed, scaled, deployed,
embedded, and translated into collective outcomes. The central challenge is therefore not simply to accelerate
AI development, nor merely to regulate individual AI systems, but to align the structures that finance, build,
deploy, evaluate, and govern AI with socially sustainable forms of productivity, resilience, distribution, and
legitimacy.
