Foreword
Dear students, colleagues, and marketing enthusiasts!
Digital marketing proficiency has become an essential competence for students, educators,
researchers, and practitioners seeking to navigate today’s competitive business environment.
This textbook has been designed to provide readers with a comprehensive u nderstanding of
the theoretical foundations of digital marketing and its practical business applications, with
particular focus on the specific characteristics of the Visegrad region.
The textbook is structured to guide readers progressively through the core areas of digital
marketing. It begins by introducing the fundamental concepts of online marketing, strategic
planning, digital branding, consumer behaviour, and personalised marketing approaches.
Building on these foundations, the subsequent chapters explore e -commerce and integrated
digital marketing communication, including content marketing, copywriting, email
marketing, and local digital marketing strategies. The following sections focus on social media
management, search engine marketing, and performance-based advertising, highlighting the
tools and techniques that enable organizations to engage effectively with digital audiences.
The textbook further examines user experience des ign, web analytics, data -driven
management, and predictive analytical tools that support informed marketing decisions.
Finally, it addresses emerging technologies, including artificial intelligence, the Internet of
Things, virtual reality, and augmented reality, demonstrating how innovation is reshaping
marketing practice and creating new opportunities for businesses.
One of the distinctive strengths of this textbook is its strong regional perspective. Throughout
the chapters, readers are introduced to a diverse collection of case studies drawn from the
Visegrad countries - Czechia, Hungary, Poland, and Slovakia. These real -world examples
demonstrate how organizations across the region have successfully implemented digital
marketing within different economic, cultural, and business contexts. By combining
theoretical concepts with practical regional experiences, the textbook enables readers to better
understand both universal marketing principles and the specific challenges and opportunities
present in Central European markets. The Visegrad case studies not only illustrate best
practices but also encourage critical thinking and provide valuable inspiration for students,
academics, and professionals seeking to apply digital marketing knowledge in practice.

Let's get to the point!
Definition of online marketing
Online marketing, also known as digital marketing, is a key area of contemporary marketing.
It refers to all  online marketing activities aimed at reaching the target audience, promoting
the brand, supporting sales, and building long-term customer relationships (Chaffey & Ellis-
Chadwick, 2019; Ryan, 2016).
In practice, online marketing uses a wide range of channels, including websites, email
marketing, social networks, search engines, online advertising , and mobile applications.
These channels enable companies to communicate with customers effectively, in
a personalised manner, and to receive immediate feedback.
From a terminological standpoint, it is appropriate to distinguish between online marketing
and digital marketing . Online marketing is a narrower, historically older term that refers
primarily to the use of the internet (e.g., websites, email, SEO, PPC). Digital marketing is
a broader term encompass ing all digital technologies and devices, including mobile
applications, smart devices, data analytics, and social media activities throughout the entire
customer lifecycle (Kaufman et al., 2024).

Online marketing in the marketing mix
In the marketing mix  framework (4Ps - Product, Price, Place, Promotion), online marketing
functions primarily as a specific form of communication (Promotion) and, in some cases, as
a distribution policy (Place), particularly through e -shops and digital platforms (Kotler &
Keller, 2016).
Unlike traditional forms of marketing communication, which are predominantly one -way
(e.g., print advertising or television commercials), online marketing enables two -way
communication, customer interaction, and rapid feedback. These characteristics contri bute
significantly to its growing importance in integrated marketing communication, where online
and offline activities are interconnected (Fill & Turnbull, 2019).
Advantages of online marketing
Online marketing offers several significant advantages over traditional marketing tools. The
main benefits include:
• Unlimited availability - marketing messages can be available 24/7, without the time
restrictions typical of traditional media.
• Precise targeting - the internet allows communication to be targeted according to the
demographic, geographic or behavioural characteristics of users, which increases the
relevance of the message.
• Measurability and performance tracking - user responses can be measured in detail
using analytical tools such as traffic statistics, click-through rates and conversion rates.
• Flexibility - campaigns can be continuously adjusted based on current results, often
without high additional costs.
• Interactivity and two -way communication - companies can get instant feedback,
engage customers in communication, and build long-term relationships.
• Price variability - the internet offers a wide range of costs, from free tools to extensive
paid campaigns.
These advantages make online marketing one of the most dynamic and effective ways to reach
target groups (Burešová, 2022).
Disadvantages and limitations of online marketing
Although online marketing has many advantages, its limitations must also be considered. The
main disadvantages include:
• Limited reach among certain groups of the population who do not use the internet
regularly.
• Advertising overload, which leads to declining click -through rates and lower user
attention.
• The use of ad-blocking tools reduces the actual reach of campaigns.
• High competition in the online environment, which places increased demands on
content quality and strategy.

• Negative reviews and public feedback can quickly damage a brand’s reputation.
• Restrictions on working with data due to legislation , particularly in the area of

These factors require a thoughtful and ethical approach to planning and implementing online

Online marketing in marketing communication
In marketing communications, online marketing tools are media that convey messages from
the company to the recipient as part of the communication process. A specific feature of the
online environment is the possibility of easy feedback, which allows companies to effectively
monitor audience reactions and adapt their communication in real time.
This approach supports personalisation, builds customer relationships, and fosters a better
understanding of their needs, thus marking a significant shift from traditional communication
models.
Communication mix and online marketing tools
Online marketing tools can be divided into the traditional communication mix: advertising,
public relations, sales promotion, and direct marketing. Websites play a specific role, forming
the central point of most online marketing activities.
The basic groups of tools include:
• Online public relations - working with content, media and communities in the digital
environment.
• Online direct marketing - personalised communication, mainly via e- mail or online
chat.
• Online sales promotion - short-term incentives such as discounts, competitions or
affiliate marketing.
• Online advertising - paid forms of communication, such as PPC advertising, banner
advertising or remarketing.
• Websites - a key tool for brand presentation, conversion, and collecting data on user
behaviour.
The specific use of individual tools always depends on the marketing objectives and target
group.
Models of marketing communication in the online environment
Traditional communication models, such as AIDA, provide a theoretical framework for
understanding how to influence customers. In the online environment, however, they are
increasingly being supplemented by modern models that reflect the customer journey,
interactivity, and long-term relationships.
One of the most significant modern approaches is SEE -THINK-DO-CARE, which segments
the audience by the stage of the purchasing decision and enables systematic planning for
digital communication. This framework transitions from a basic understanding of online

Let's get to the point!
Strategic approach to digital marketing
Digital marketing cannot be viewed merely as a set of individual tools and channels. To
achieve long- term effectiveness, you should observe it strategically in the organisation ’s
overall marketing strategy and customer journey. A strategic approach allows for the
systematic planning of marketing activities, the coordination  of individual tools, and the
evaluation of their contribution to the set goals (Chaffey & Ellis-Chadwick, 2019).
The fundamental starting point for strategic digital marketing is understanding customer
purchasing behaviour and the decision -making process. In the online environment, this
process is dynamic, non -linear, and often influenced by availab le information,
recommendations from other users , and personal experience with the brand. That is why
digital marketing favours models that work with the customer journey and reflect the different
stages of the customer’s relationship with the brand.
Marketing communication models in the digital environment
Traditional marketing communication models, such as AIDA or DAGMAR, are based on
a linear communication model that assumes a customer’s gradual transition from attention to
action. These models provide an important theoretical foundation, but in the digital
DOI: https://doi.org/10.15414/2026.9788055229799

14

environment, they are no longer sufficient on their own, as they do not account for
interactivity, repeated contact with the brand, or long-term customer care (Kotler et al., 2017).
In response to these changes, modern digital marketing communication frameworks are
emerging that work with the entire customer lifecycle. Among the most commonly used are
RACE (Reach - Act - Convert - Engage), the flywheel model, and SEE- THINK-DO-CARE.
These approaches make it possible to structure marketing activities according to the stage of
the decision-making process and better link goals, tools, and performance metrics (Chaffey
& Ellis-Chadwick, 2019; Halligan & Shah, 2018).
SEE-THINK-DO-CARE (STDC) - basic characteristics
SEE-THINK-DO-CARE is one of the most recognised models of strategic digital marketing
communication management. The author of this concept is Avinash Kaushik, who introduced
it to address the need for a customer -oriented, measurable approach to marketing in the
digital environment (Kaushik, 2015).
The basic principle of STDC is to segment the audience by their readiness to buy, not just
demographic characteristics. The model distinguishes four stages of the decision -making
process - SEE, THINK, DO and CARE - each of which requires different communication goals,
content types, tools and methods of measuring success.
STDC enables marketers to allocate budgets effectively, increase the relevance of their
messages and build long-term relationships with customers throughout their entire life cycle.
• SEE phase - awareness and reaching the general public
The SEE phase encompasses the broadest audience: users not currently addressing a specific
purchasing need but may be interested in the brand ’s products or services in the future. The
main goal of this phase is to build brand awareness and create a basic emotional connection
with consumers (Kaushik, 2015).
Communication in the SEE phase focuses primarily on attractive, easily consumable , and
visually striking content. Typically, video ads, social media content, storytelling, influencer
marketing, or blanket online advertising are used. The success of activities in this phase is
measured primarily by metrics such as reach, impressions, and growth in brand awareness.
• THINK phase - consideration and information search
The THINK stage includes users who are already aware of a certain need and are actively
seeking information about possible solutions. However, they have not yet decided to make
a purchase. The goal of communication at this stage is to provide relevant, credible, and useful
information that positions the brand as an expert in the field (Rolný, 2018; Kaushik, 2015).
Marketing activities in the THINK phase are often based on content marketing. These include
blog articles, video tutorials, case studies, webinars, reviews, and product comparisons.
Success is measured by engagement, time spent on the website, number of co ntent
downloads, and repeat visits.
• DO phase - decision and conversion
The DO phase represents the moment when the user is ready to take the desired action,
typically a purchase, registration, or service request. The main goal of communication in this

phase is to maximise conversions and remove obstacles in the decision -making process
(Kaushik, 2015).
This phase primarily employs performance -oriented digital marketing tools such as PPC
campaigns, remarketing, conversion page optimisation, product pages, or price incentives.
Key metrics include conversion rate, cost per conversion, return on investment, and sales
volume.
• CARE phase - customer care and loyalty building
The CARE phase focuses on existing customers who have already converted. The goal of this
phase is to maintain a long-term relationship with the customer, encourage repeat purchases,
and build brand loyalty (Kaushik, 2015).
Effective communication in the CARE phase includes personalised offers, value-added email
marketing, loyalty programmes, quality customer support, and after -sales service. Satisfied
customers can become brand ambassadors who contribute to its further growth through
recommendations and positive reviews.
STDC as a framework for campaign planning and evaluation
SEE-THINK-DO-CARE serves not only as a tool for structuring marketing communication
but also as a practical framework for planning, managing, and evaluating digital marketing
campaigns. Each phase of the model has its own objectives, corresponding communication
type, and specific performance metrics, which enable more effective management of
marketing activities and better interpretation of their results (Chaffey & Ellis -Chadwick,
2019).
STDC allows systematic planning of marketing activities across the entire customer lifecycle
and the delivery of relevant messages at the right time. This approach supports the long-term
effectiveness of digital marketing and strengthens brand value.
STDC and marketing objectives
One of the key benefits of STDC is its alignment with various types of marketing objectives.
Because each stage of the decision -making process fulfils a different function, different
communication objectives must be assigned to it. In the SEE stage, the main goal is to build
brand awareness and memorability. The THINK stage focuses on encouraging consideration,
preference, and perception of the brand as a relevant solution. In the DO stage, goals focus on
conversions, such as purchases or registrations. The CARE stage focuses on customer
retention, repeat purchases, and long-term customer value.
This distinction helps to avoid the common mistake of evaluating all marketing activities
solely based on short-term sales results, regardless of their strategic role.
STDC and performance measurement (KPI)
The marketing objectives of each STDC phase are directly linked to how their success is
measured. Each phase requires different key performance indicators (KPIs) that correspond
to its objectives. In the early stages of the customer journey, it is appropriate to focus on
metrics for reach, attention, and audience engagement. In the DO stage, key metrics focus on
DOI: https://doi.org/10.15414/2026.9788055229799

16

conversion and return on investment. In the CARE stage, the focus shifts to indicators of long-
term customer value and loyalty.
STDC thus supports a strategic approach to measurement that reflects the distinct roles of
individual marketing activities and enables a more realistic assessment of their contributions.
STDC and budget allocation
A strategic approach to digital marketing also involves deciding how to allocate the marketing
budget across the different STDC stages. These stages differ not only in their objectives but
also in their return on investment and time horizon. Activities in the DO stage are often
associated with direct performance and short- term returns. On the other hand , investments
in the SEE or CARE stages tend to be more long-term.
Effective budget allocation must balance short-term performance goals with long-term brand
building and customer relationships. STDC provides a structure that allows this balance to be
managed strategically.
The role of content in each phase of STDC
Content plays a key role in digital marketing, with its function varying significantly across the
different stages of STDC. In the SEE stage, content primarily serves to attract attention and
create an emotional connection with the brand. In the THINK stag e, content is educational
and persuasive, helping users make informed decisions. In the DO stage, its role is to reinforce
decision certainty and remove any barriers to conversion. In the CARE stage, content supports
the customer relationship, increases their satisfaction, and strengthens their loyalty.
Strategic content management within STDC enables greater  communication relevance and
strengthens the brand’s long-term relationship with customers.
Typical strategic mistakes when applying STDC
Although STDC is conceptually simple, strategic errors often occur in its application. The most
common include an excessive focus on the DO phase at the expense of building awareness in
the SEE phase, underestimating the CARE phase and its role in long -term growth, or
attempting to communicate with users at different stages of the decision -making process in
the same way.
Another common mistake is evaluating all marketing activities using the same metrics
regardless of their strategic function. STDC helps prevent these mistakes by clearly defining
the role of each stage in the overall marketing strategy.

The brand image is defined as “perceptions about a brand as reflected by the brand

communicating positive attributes that strengthen consumer association (Romaniuk & Sharp,

Brand image and its perception have a crucial impact on how consumers choose the product
and shape their behaviour. Brand image is also relatively difficult to change - once created, it
often stays in consumers’ minds. Brands with a strong image are loved and bought . Those
with a weak image often exit  the market due to low competitive advantage (Zhang, 2015).

Summary
Consistency is the most critical factor when building a brand in the digital environment . To
ensure long-term growth, the following should be considered:
• Visual Identity: Coherent use of logos, colours, and typography to ensure immediate
recognition.
• Scalability: Naming conventions and identities should be location -agnostic to avoid
a “geographic ceiling”.
• Personification: Using archetypes or naming to create a sense of service and reliability
(e.g., a “gentleman” assistant).
• Digital Trust: Leveraging social proof and historical reviews to reduce the “friction of
the unknown”.

Case Study
In the contemporary digital environment, a brand is more than just a logo or a colour palette;
it is a scalable ecosystem of trust, visibility, and geographic flexibility. The story of Mr.Shuttle
(MrShuttle.com, n.d. ) provides a definitive example of how a company can successfully
navigate the “geographic ceiling” through a strategic rebranding. This case study explores the
transition from KrakowShuttle to Mr.Shuttle, analysing the motivations, the digital execution,
and the long-term impact on the brand’s international expansion.
Company Profile
Founded in Kraków, Poland, in 2005, the company originally operated under the name
KrakowShuttle. During its early years, the brand was a textbook example of successful local
optimisation. It focused on providing high -quality airport transfers and organ ised tours to
world-famous heritage sites like the Auschwitz-Birkenau Memorial and Museum and the
Wieliczka Salt Mine.
From a branding perspective, the name KrakowShuttle was a powerful asset for local SEO
(Search Engine Optimisation). For a traveller typing “"Krakow airport transfer” into a search
engine, the brand name provided immediate relevance and high click -through rates. The
brand built a reputation for reliability, professional service, and local expertise, consistently
earning top ratings on platforms like TripAdvisor. However, this local success eventually
became a strategic limitation. The brand was perceived as a specialist, but only within a very
narrow, fifty-kilometre radius of its headquarters.
Overview
As the company’s leadership looked toward international growth, they identified
a fundamental problem: brand dissonance. While “KrakowShuttle” signalled expertise in
Poland, it lacked credibility when the company attempted to offer services in Prague, Berlin,
or Budapest. A tourist in Lisbon or New York was unlikely to trust a company named after
a Polish city to provide a seamless transfer to their respective locations.

The decision to rebrand was driven by the ambition to transform from a local service provider
into a global travel platform. The primary objective was to decouple the brand identity from
a specific location while retaining the core values of trust and prem ium service established
over a decade.
In 2019, the company officially transitioned to Mr.Shuttle (Figure 2). This name was chosen
with surgical precision for the digital environment. The addition of the “Mr” prefix was
a strategic move toward brand personification. In a digital world where interactions are often
cold and automated, personifying the brand suggests a helpful, professional, and reliable
assistant—a “gentleman” of the travel industry who pays attention to every detail.

The change solved the geographic problem. Mr.Shuttle became a location -agnostic brand.
Whether the service was being provided in Barcelona, London, or Istanbul, the name
remained relevant and professional. This allowed the company to move away from
a fragmented digital strategy (managing multiple local brands) toward a unified, global
digital storefront.
The rebranding of Mr.Shuttle was not merely a cosmetic change; it required a total overhaul
of the digital infrastructure. One of the greatest risks in digital rebranding is the loss of SEO
authority. For years, KrakowShuttle had built immense “ link equity ”. To migrate this to
MrShuttle.com without losing traffic, the company had to execute a flawless technical
transition, using complex 301 redirection maps to ensure that the search engine “trust”
followed the new name.
Furthermore, the brand had to modern ise its Visual Identity (VI) to meet the standards of
a global tech -travel platform. The new design focused on “mobile -first” principles. Since
a majority of  travellers book transfers while on the move, the UI (User Interface) was
optimised for speed, clarity, and instant trust. The colour palette and typography were refined
to evoke a sense of professional stability, to move away from the “local tour operator ” look
toward a “global logistics partner” aesthetic.
In the digital environment, trust is the primary currency. One of Mr.Shuttle’s key strategies
during the rebranding was the seamless integration of social proof. By aggregating thousands
of reviews from its previous incarnation and displaying them under th e new brand,
Mr.Shuttle could leverage its past successes to build future credibility. The brand ’s digital
presence was designed to reduce the “ friction of the unknown” for international customers
who were discovering the brand for the first time in a new city.
The impact of the rebranding was transformative. Freed from its geographic anchor,
Mr.Shuttle rapidly expanded its operations. Within a few years, the company grew from

operating in a handful of Polish cities to providing services at more than  150 locations
worldwide.
The rebranding also allowed for brand elasticity. Under the name Mr.Shuttle, the company
could diversify its offerings—moving beyond simple transfers to include business executive
travel, VIP services, and comprehensive holiday packages— without the brand name feeling
out of place. The digital-first approach enabled the company to compete directly with global

Impact
The Mr.Shuttle case study highlights a vital lesson in digital branding: scalability must be built
into the brand ’s DNA from the outset, or it must be courageously addressed through
rebranding when the time for expansion arrives. In a digital environment, where a business
is just a click away from a global audience, a name that is too specific can become a cage.
Mr.Shuttle can serve as a model of how to:
• Identify the Geographic Ceiling: Recognising when a local brand name hinders global
growth.
• Personify the Digital Experience: Using naming conventions to create a sense of service
and reliability.
• Manage Digital Transition: Balancing the need for a new identity with the technical
necessity of maintaining SEO authority and historical trust.
Mr.Shuttle’s journey from a local shuttle service in Kraków to an international travel platform
is a testament to the power of a well- executed digital brand strategy. It proves that in the
modern world, a brand’s potential is not defined by where it starts, but by how effectively it
communicates its value across borders.

Learning Outcomes:
After completing this lesson, learners will:

 Obtain a clear understanding of how algorithms, social media platforms, influencers,

Let's get to the point!
Online consumer behaviour can no longer be interpreted purely through traditional economic
models. For Generation Z, consumption simultaneously expresses  identity construction,
social connection, and digital self -expression. Algorithms, influencers, and platforms no
longer merely mediate decisions; they actively shape them. Young consumers in Central
Europe occupy a particularly fascinating position: they live simultaneously within global
digital culture and local economic realities. Consequently, their online consumer behaviour
reflects both the homogen ising force of global trends and the persistence of regional
specificities. For the marketer of the future, therefore, the key question will no longer be how
to sell a product, but how to become a culturally relevant actor within an online environment
where attention has become the most valuable currency.
Introduction: consumers are no longer “online” — they live online
Research on online consumer behaviour has reached a radical turning point over the past five
years. Earlier models assumed that the internet was simply a separate purchasing channel.
Today, however, the digital sphere is no longer just a “platform”; it has become a social
environment, a space for identity construction, and a decision -making infrastructure all at
once (Kotler et al., 2021). This is especially true for Generation Z, who are not digital
immigrants but digital natives. They did not learn online existence — they grew up inside it.
For Central European consumers, this reality is shaped by a unique cultural context. Young
Hungarians, Poles, Slovaks, and Czechs are simultaneously connected to global trends while
navigating local economic uncertainties. As a result, their online decisions combine Western
platform culture with the price sensitivity, scepticism, and community orientation
characteristic of post-socialist societies. Classical consumer decision -making models — such
DOI: https://doi.org/10.15414/2026.9788055229799

32

as the Engel -Blackwell-Miniard model or the Theory of Planned Behaviour — remain
relevant, but they are no longer sufficient on their own. Today’s consumers are influenced
simultaneously by algorithms, influencers, micro -communities, and platform architectures
(Dwivedi et al., 2021). This means marketing is no longer merely communication; it has
become behavioural design.
The new drivers of online consumer behaviour
One of the most important characteristics of digital consumer behaviour is the fragmentation
of decision-making. Consumers no longer move linearly from need recognition to purchase.
Instead, they navigate continuous micro-interactions across TikTok videos, Reddit comments,
Discord communities, and algorithmic recommendations ( Djafarova & Bowes, 2021; Lemon
& Verhoef, 2016).
Within the platform economy, attention has become the most valuable resource. Systems such
as TikTok or Instagram Reels do not simply recommend content; they predictively shape our
preferences. The essence of the recommendation economy is that consumers of ten do not
choose what they originally searched for, but rather what algorithms made visible to them
(Zuboff, 2019).
This strongly affects Generation Z, for whom information overload has become a natural
condition. Consequently, heuristic thinking has gained prominence in decision- making:
short-form videos, social proof, comment culture, and “real user experiences” dominate rapid
judgments. A fifteen -second TikTok review can often shape perceptions of a product more
effectively than a professional advertising campaign.
At the same time, authenticity has become the new currency of digital trust. Young consumers
are highly sensitive to overly polished marketing communication. A “perfect” brand message
is often perceived as less credible than raw, spontaneous -looking content (Audrezet et al.,
2020). This is why micro-influencers frequently outperform global celebrity campaigns.
Generation Z as a Paradoxical Consumer
Generation Z consumer behaviour is filled with apparent contradictions. They are critical of
capitalism while remaining intensive consumers. Sustainability matters to them, yet they
continue ordering from fast-fashion platforms. They demand data privacy while voluntarily
sharing personal content every day (Francis & Hoefel, 2018).
These paradoxes do not necessarily reflect inconsistency; rather, they demonstrate adaptation
to platform capitalism. Young consumers have learned to coexist with the contradictions of
digital systems. For them, conscious consumption is not an absolute mor al position but an
ongoing process of compromise management. In Central Europe, price -value sensitivity is
particularly significant. Young consumers in the region are highly active digitally, yet they
live in a more economically uncertain environment than many of their Western European
peers. Consequently, online purchasing decisions combine experience orientation with
economic rationality. The rise of social commerce further intensifies this complexity. TikTok
Shop, Instagram Shopping, and livestream commerce platforms no longer separate
entertainment from purchasing.

Consumption becomes embedded within the process of content consumption itself
(Wongkitrungrueng & Assarut, 2020). This phenomenon is especially risky regarding impulse
buying because gamified platform mechanisms create dopamine -driven behavioural cycles.
FOMO (fear of missing out) and FOBO (fear of better options) are no longer merely
psychological concepts; they have become concrete marketing mechanisms. Limited product
drops, countdown timers, and exclusive online releases artificially generate urgency,
accelerating consumer decision-making (Hodkinson, 2019).
The algorithmic consumer: Who actually controls our decisions?
One of the most important contemporary questions in online consumer behaviour concerns
algorithmic influence. Platforms no longer passively transmit information; they actively
organise our perception of reality. Amazon’s recommendation system, Netflix’s personalised
interface, and TikTok’s “For You” algorithm all learn continuously from behavioural data
(Huang & Rust, 2021). The advantages of the personal isation economy are obvious: more
relevant experiences, faster decisions, and greater convenience. However, this also creates the
phenomenon known as the filter bubble, where consumers increasingly operate within
narrow informational environments (Pariser, 2011). This is not only a political or social issue
but also a marketing challenge, since brand visibility increasingly depends on algorithmic
compatibility.
The emergence of artificial intelligence further accelerates this transformation. AI- based
chatbots, virtual assistants, and generative search systems no longer merely respond to
consumer needs; they anticipate them (Huang & Rust, 2021). The future of digital marketing
will likely not be campaign -centred but rather based on adaptive real -time systems. This
creates a strategic dilemma for companies. The more accurate personal isation becomes, the
stronger consumer discomfort regarding data collection may grow. The phenomenon of
“creepy marketing” emerges when consumers feel excessively monitored (Aguirre et al.,
2015). Consequently, trust has become not only an ethical issue but also a competitive
advantage.
Sustainability, Digital Ethics, and Conscious Consumption
One of the strongest trends shaping Generation Z online consumer behaviour is value-driven
decision-making. Young consumers increasingly expect brands to take positions on social
issues (Kotler et al., 2021). Yet this is an extremely sensitive area.
“Woke washing” and “greenwashing” can quickly backfire, particularly in social media
environments. In digital spaces, corporate transparency is constantly scrutin ised. A single
TikTok video or Reddit post can trigger a reputational crisis within hours (Vredenburg et al.,
2020). At the same time, sustainable online consumption is not merely a moral issue but also
an identity signal. Second -hand platforms such as Vinted  or Depop simultaneously reflect
environmental consciousness and individualism. For young consumers, “vintage” or
“thrifted” products carry cultural status, too.
Interestingly, in Central Europe sustainability is often linked to economic rationality.
Purchasing second -hand products is not only an ethical choice but also a cost- efficient
strategy. This duality requires new marketing approaches.

What is segmentation and why does it matter?
Definition and basic principle
Segmentation is the process of dividing a heterogeneous market into smaller, homogeneous
groups of customers - known as segments - that share similar characteristics, needs, or
behaviours (Kotler and Armstrong, 2014). In the online environment, segmentation is
a fundamental prerequisite for effective marketing. Instead of one message for everyone, you
communicate the right message to the right person at the right time (Villanova et al., 2021).
Imagine the market as a large, diverse group. Some are young students looking for cheap
solutions, others are busy managers willing to pay a premium price for convenience, and
others are parents making decisions based on safety and reliability. Communicati ng with
everyone in the same way means failing to reach anyone effectively. Segmentation solves this
problem by structuring the market into meaningful groups that can be targeted strategically.

From mass marketing to segmentation
Historically, marketing operated on a one-size-fits-all principle - one TV advert, one leaflet,
one message for the entire market. This approach was a logical consequence of limited media
options and a lack of customer data (Kotler and Armstrong, 2014).
With the advent of the internet, the situation changed fundamentally. The digital environment
brought three key changes:
1. Data availability - every online user interaction leaves a digital footprint that can be
analysed
2. The ability to micro -target - advertising platforms enable a message to be delivered
to an extremely precisely defined group
3. Scalability of personalisation - technology allows communication to be personalised
even when dealing with millions of contacts
The result is a shift from mass marketing, through segmentation, to today’s hyper-
personalisation.
What defines a “good” segment?
Not every division of customers constitutes true segmentation. For a segment to be
marketable, it should meet several criteria - M-A-S-D-A (Measurable, Accessible, Substantial,
Differentiable, Actionable) (Kotler and Armstrong, 2014; Wedel and Kamakura, 2000):
Table 1: Criteria for effective market segmentation (M-A-S-D-A)
Criterion Description
Measurable The segment must be quantifiable - its size and characteristics can be determined.
Accessible There must be a way to reach the segment effectively.
Substantial The segment must be economically viable - large enough to justify the investment.
Differentiable Segments must differ meaningfully from one another in their response to
marketing stimuli.
Actionable It must be possible to develop a specific marketing strategy for the segment.

Why segmentation matters - specific benefits
Greater message relevance. Customers today are inundated with thousands of advertising
messages every day. A message that targets their specific needs and context has a significantly
higher chance of attracting attention and prompting action. Studies consistently show that
relevant content increases both click-through rates (CTR) and conversion rates - often by tens
of percentage points compared to generic campaigns.

More effective use of budget . Segmentation allows you to focus marketing expenditure
where it delivers the greatest return. Instead of spreading your budget thinly, you invest in
segments with the highest potential - whether in terms of acquiring new customers or
retaining existing ones.
Deeper understanding of customers. The very process of creating segments forces marketers
to ask themselves: Who are our customers? What motivates them? What problems do they
face? This analysis generates strategic insights that go beyond advertising itself - it influences
product development, pricing, and customer service.
Stronger customer loyalty: A customer who receives relevant communications and feels that
the brand “understands” them is naturally more loyal. Segmentation is therefore the
foundation not only for customer acquisition but also for building long- term customer
relationships and increasing their Customer Lifetime Value (CLV).
Competitive advantage: Companies that can  segment and personalise effectively are better
able to respond to market needs and identify new opportunities more quickly - such as niche
segments that competitors overlook.
Segmentation as a strategic foundation, not a tactical tool
It is crucial to view segmentation not as a one -off activity, but as a continuous strategic
process. Markets change, customers evolve, and new technologies and data sources emerge.
Segments that were relevant three years ago may no longer reflect today’s reality.
In practice, this means regularly reviewing segmentation criteria, testing new hypotheses
about customer behaviour, and integrating segmentation into decision -making across the
entire organisation - from marketing through product development to customer service.
Segmentation isn’t about pigeonholing customers. It’s about better understanding the
people you serve - and communicating with them in a way that truly makes sense to them.
Types of segmentation in online marketing
Online marketing offers a unique advantage over traditional media: access to vast amounts of
real-time user data. This allows you to segment customers across many different dimensions
- from basic demographic characteristics to sophisticated behavioural models powered by
artificial intelligence. In practice, the most effective strategies do more than combine one type
of segmentation; they overlap multiple layers at once.
Demographic segmentation
Demographic segmentation divides users according to measurable personal characteristics
such as age, gender, education, income, marital status or geographical location. It is
historically the oldest and most widespread approach - and for good reason: demographic
data is relatively easy to access and understand (Azman, 2024).
In the online environment, demographic data is available from several sources:
• Registration forms and user profiles - users share data directly
• Advertising platforms - Meta Ads or LinkedIn Ads offer demographic targeting as
a basic feature

• Google Analytics - estimates age and gender based on behaviour and cookies
Although demographic segmentation is a useful starting point, it has its limitations. Two
people of the same age, gender and income may have completely different needs, values and
purchasing behaviour. That is why demographic segmentation in modern online marketing
is almost always combined with other types.
Example: A fashion e-shop displays different banners to women aged 25-34 than to men aged

Geographic segmentation
Sometimes referred to as a subcategory of demographic segmentation, geographic
segmentation divides users according to their physical location - country, region, city or even
a specific neighbourhood. Online marketing has taken on a new dimension thanks to
geolocation technologies in mobile devices (Bonita and Kolb, 2016).
Practical applications include:
• Local advertising - displaying relevant offers based on the user’s location
• Geofencing - automatically triggering an advertising message when a user enters
a defined geographical zone (e.g. the vicinity of a high-street shop)
• Content localisation - adapting language, currency, cultural references or current
events according to the region
Example: A restaurant chain targets push notifications at mobile app users located within 500
metres of a branch, offering a lunch special.
Psychographic segmentation
Psychographic segmentation goes beyond demographic figures and focuses on the
customer’s inner world - their values, lifestyle, interests, attitudes and personality traits. The
aim is to understand not only who the customer is, but why they make the decisions they do
(Sheoran, 2025).
In the online environment, psychographic segmentation is possible through the analysis of:
• Content consumed - what articles, videos, or websites the user views
• Interactions on social media - what they share, comment on, and like
• Search queries - what the user actively searches for
Psychographic profiles form the basis for targeting on platforms such as Meta Ads , which
creates detailed profiles of interests and values based on user behaviour. Psychographic
segmentation is also key to creating personas - fictional representations of ideal customers
that help marketers empathise with the needs of the target audience.

Data sources for segmentation
To segment customers effectively, every business needs high-quality data. But not all data is
equally valuable, reliable , or easily accessible. In online marketing, four basic data sources
play different roles in a segmentation strategy.
Zero-party data is information that a customer consciously and voluntarily shares in exchange
for a better experience. It is not generated through tracking or estimation - the customer
directly states what they want. The zero-party data is fully compliant with GDPR legislation.
Typical collection methods include onboarding quizzes, preference centres within the
customer account, or satisfaction surveys.
First-party data arises naturally as digital traces that customers leave behind with every
interaction with a website, app, or email. A company collects it directly from its own channels
- from its e-shop, CRM system, email marketing, or loyalty programme. Strategically, it is the
most valuable because it is unique and competitors have no access to it.
Second-party data is another company’s first- party data, shared under a direct partnership
agreement. A company does not have automatic access to it - it must actively build it up
through collaboration with strategic partners, such as lifestyle magazines, fitness apps or
affiliate networks. It offers greater scope than first- party data whilst maintaining relatively
high quality.
Third-party data is collected by external entities - data brokers or advertising platforms - from
a wide variety of sources and made available to advertisers. It is key for the rapid acquisition
of new customers; however, it is the least accurate type of data, and its availability is rapidly
diminishing with the gradual phase-out of third-party cookies.

The importance and forms of e-commerce
The spread of the internet has created a new sales channel. Although electronic commerce
accounts for only 23.5% of total retail sales worldwide in 2025, its importance is growing
steadily, and it has become a dominant force in certain sectors ( Ma, 2026). The COVID -19
pandemic has also stimulated the development of e -commerce (Afonso et al., 2024) . For the
time being, it cannot  provide the same experience as traditional stores, which is why m ost
people continue to make a (significant) portion of their purchases in the traditional way.
Electronic commerce, or e-commerce, is a key component of the broader digital economy. It is
the sale or purchase of goods or services, conducted over computer networks by methods
specifically designed for receiving or placing orders, regardless of payment and delivery. An
e-commerce transaction can be between enterprises, households, individuals, governments,
and other public or private organisations (OECD, 2025). The six core types of e-commerce are
B2C - Business to Consumer, B2B - Business to Business, C2C - Consumer to Consumer, C2B
- Consumer to Business, B2A/B2G - Business to Administration (or Government), C2A/C2G -
Consumer to Administration (or Government) (Laudon & Traver, 2023). This chapter
primarily focuses on the B2C sector.
E-commerce is part of e -business, a form of off -premises sales that includes business
transactions (online purchase and sale of products) , maintaining business relationships, and
sharing business information (Vladimir, 1996).

Traditional and online sales channels
In commerce, direct sales occur when a company sells its products or services straight to the
final customer without intermediaries, for example through its own sales force, physical
stores, or company-owned websites. In contrast, a range of intermediaries may stand between
the company and the customer, performing a variety of functions. These intermediaries
constitute a distribution channel (also called a marketing channel), through which products
or services pass until they reach the end customer. Distrib ution channels can include
wholesalers, retailers, distributors, and internet platforms such as Amazon. The key functions
of intermediaries include information provision, promotion, negotiation, ordering, financing,
risk-taking, physical distribution, payment, and transfer of ownership. In a multichannel
marketing system, a firm uses two or more distribution channels (e.g., a retail store, a website,
a dealer network, or a mail-order catalogue) to reach its target customers (Kotler et al., 2024).
In an omnichannel marketing system, a firm integrates all available channels (such as physical
stores, websites, mobile apps, and social media) so that customers can move smoothly across
channels during their search and purchase process, experiencing the brand as a single, unified
system rather than separate channels. An omnichannel system focuses  on the customer
journey, emphas ising coordination and interaction between channels , rather than  treating
them independently (Verhoef et al., 2015).
On the internet, sales processes are typically shorter, but complex systems exist there, too. In
traditional forms, it is relatively rare for consumers to be reached directly by the company
(e.g., through brand shops, farmers' markets, vending machines). But on the internet, this can
be easily achieved through a company ’s web shop , allowing businesses to connect directly
with customers and keep the retail margin for themselves or offer products at more favourable
prices. A commercial web store or other intermediary may also be involved in the sales
process.
Compared to traditional distribution channels, e-commerce has numerous advantages (Table
1). The online environment offers speed (fast orders with only a few clicks), convenience
(shopping from home), flexibility (continuous opening hours), and interactivity (chat with the
store). Large amounts of information can be collected by the company for process optimisation
and personalisation, while making it easier for customers to compare and make decisions (e.g.,
price comparison sites). A wider selection of goods can be offered, reaching a broader
consumer base—even bypassing retailers. Of course, e-commerce also presents challenges, as
systems need to be built, numerous logistical tasks need to be solved (e.g., small -volume
shipping), and the range of competitors is incredibly wide, resulting in fierce competition.

model (Lavidge & Steiner, 1961) or on other approaches (FiveStones, 2021).
Imagine the sales process as a funnel: at the top, you target a broader consumer base, and then
strive to guide them all the way through to the purchase (or even beyond). Of course, you
may lose some of the interested parties along the way, so the funnel gradually narrows. The
four basic elements are awareness, interest, decision, and purchase or action, which can be
supplemented with additional steps (Figure 1).

printed texts, but at the same time less filtered. It is no longer so important for the message to
be timeless.
The essence of real-time marketing is to respond to current issues almost immediately. Most
offline tools now have online equivalents (e.g., online newspapers, online radio), visualisation
has become more prominent, and images and videos are playing an increasingly important
role compared to text. It is possible to send personal ised messages, and both precision and
interactive marketing are becoming more popular.
The physical and digital worlds are also converging in communication, as content
consumption is now largely on demand (e.g., Netflix, Spotify). A mobile -first ecosystem has

Categorising online marketing communication tools

widely used. They divide media channels into the following sections (Stephen & Galak, 2012;
Macnamara et al., 2016). Paid media encompasses any third-party platform that charges the
company or brand, such as banner ads on a news portal. In this case, the content is produced
by the company or brand, but is published by another party. In the case of an owned platform,
the owner also produces the content found there. This is essentially a closed system controlled
by the owner, who decides what content can be posted on it. An example of this is a company
or brand website. Earned media includes all content appearing on any platform that is about
the company or brand and is produced by a third party. These are typically PR appearances,
for example. Shared media refers to content shared on social media platforms, which may
result from referrals (verbal recommendations) or co -creation (collaboration in content
production, such as influencers or collaborations between multiple brands). Sometimes a fifth
element is also added to the PESO model. Leased media refers to content produced by a
company or brand and published on social media platforms they manage. Although the social
media page appears to belong to the brand, Facebook, TikTok, or another owner  actually
controls it, and their rules apply.
Another classification is based on who initiates the communication. The essence of outbound
marketing is that the company or brand sends unsolicited messages to the target audience (in
the form of television commercials, billboards, or banner ads), often interrupting their current
activity, which understandably often causes consumer dissatisfaction and leads to ad
avoidance and the use of ad blockers. The goal of inbound marketing is for potential
customers to find the company or brand through content that is important to them. Inbound
marketing includes, for example, consumer-initiated phone calls, website optimisation (SEO),
email marketing (when you subscribe to a newsletter and read it), and content marketing ,
which will be described in detail in the next chapter.

marketing communication requires integrating both online and offline communication.
Integrated campaigns often build on online and offline tools and channels, combining them
to achieve the best possible effect.

Displays
Displays remain one of the most important areas of online marketing communication today.
You see them on portals, social media platforms, mailing accounts, online games, mobile apps
and in countless formats. Displays can be considered online posters that users can see while
browsing or watching videos. The difference is that they can be clicked on, although the click-

The first digital display advertisement, a banner, appeared in 1994 on Wired Magazine ’s
website (thefirstbannerad.com, 2026). Since then, such ads have undergone major changes,
with various formats emerging and display spending growing dynamically alongside the

Display ads include:
• banners (these can be traditional displays with various sizes placed on websites;
responsive ads that dynamically adjust their appearance to fit different sizes;
interstitials, which are full-screen ads that appear on websites or mobile apps during

• rich media (interactive ads that have engaging elements like animation, audio, video,

• videos (e.g., pre-roll ads in YouTube that automatically play before the video selected
by a user)
• audio (e.g., short appearances in a podcast; it can be a prerecorded ad or can be told
by the podcaster)
• e-DM (electronic direct marketing, e.g., individual emails or newsletters)
• listings (registration in portal databases; marketplaces are often free, but advertisers
pay to be highlighted, e.g., with a photo, graphic, or more detailed description)
The content of the advertisement depends largely on the brand, product, objectives, target
group, and platform characteristics. However, each visual and copy must be  connected,
consistent, and reinforce each other. A/B testing is often used to increase the performance of
individual page elements . The concept of A/B testing is to show different variations to
different consumers, and measure which variation is the most effective (Siroker & Koomen,
2013). Personalisation also increases efficiency, so it is worth creating multiple versions of the
ad. With the help of AI, this is becoming easier, allowing marketers/agencies to generate many
versions quickly and cheaply. With the help of advanced advertising systems, very small and
specific groups can be reached, which is called microtargeting.

The placement of displays is of fundamental importance. The following options are available:
• Traditional method: the advertiser or its agency contacts the media owner, and they
agree on the exact location and parameters of the advertisements
• Programmatic advertising: an automated big data system that allows marketers to bid
for the privilege to publish personalised online advertising in the right place, to the right
people, at the right time (Samuel et al., 2021). The goal of programmatic advertising is
to facilitate the sale of advertising space, optimi se campaigns, and increase efficiency
with replacing human negotiation with a software solution which is capable of
conducting real-time auctions, in which ads are sold when a user loads a web page (IAB,
2023); in automated systems a budget control can be set for a day or for the total
campaign to ensure costs be spent evenly and do not exceed the planned spend
• Retargeting (remarketing):  the system displays targeted ads to users who have
previously interacted with a brand (by visiting a website or abandoning a cart) as they
browse other sites, to repeat the message and achieve conversion (Adobe for Business,
2026)
• Native (display) ads: these ads match the look and feel of the surrounding content of
a site or app; visitors still know they're ads, but they look similar to publisher content,
fit “natively” to the page, so provide a better user experience for visitors (Google, 2026)
• Sponsorships: a special kind of brand appearance on sites, which is usually a longer-
term collaboration, e.g., a financial company may sponsor a section of an online portal
There are different kinds of payment models of digital displays:
• Flat rate / Fixed fee / Time-based (TB): the advertiser pays a fixed price for a specific
duration or number of impressions, regardless of the ad’s performance, e.g., the
banner is visible for one day or a week; it is often used in direct deals with publishers
(Adobe for Business, 2026) or at the beginning of a brand awareness campaign
• Cost Per Mille (CPM) : the marketer pays for every 1,000 impressions/views the ad
receives (best for brand awareness campaigns) . Somewhat similar is Cost Per View
(CPV), where the company pays when the video has been watched (to some extent)
• Cost Per Click (CPC) : the advertiser pays only when a user clicks on the banner or
video ad (the model is best for driving traffic to a website/landing page)
• Cost Per Action/Acquisition (CPA) : the company pays only when an ad leads to
a specific action, such as a form submission, app download, or a sale
• Cost per lead (CPL): advertisers pay based on the number of leads generated by the
ad (these models are used when the primary goal is action from the user, e.g., lead
generation)
• Auction system : In many cases, prices are not fixed ; instead, the advertiser or its
agency sets a maximum bid, and through an automated real -time bidding process
(completed in a fraction of a second), the winner obtains the ad space

Retail media
In online advertising, retail media refers to the digital retail point -of-sale advertising
opportunities and data assets that retailers, sales platforms or specific marketplaces make
available to brands for advertising campaigns (IAB, 2024a). The essence of this dynamically
developing field is that it reaches users at the moment they intend to make a purchase.
Therefore, online retail medi a is the modern, digital equivalent of traditional point- of-sale
(POS) and point-of-purchase (POP) advertising. Advertisements are not seen as a distraction
or unwanted noise but often help consumers make choices and improve their experience. The
retailer is responsible for the right balance, keeping the customer’s needs in mind, because too
many or irrelevant ads can impair site usability (IAB, 2024b).
Unlike traditional online advertising channels, retail media relies on retail data collected
directly within the shop system, including customer behaviour, purchase history, and product
movements. This depth of data enables targeted advertising based on actual purchase intent
and specific shopping cart contents, not just general online interests. The service may include
classic placements such as search engine-like product listings, banner ads on product pages,
cross-sell or upsell recommendation blocks, and promotional slots on the homepage or in the
checkout area (Wolfenstein, 2026).
Initially, experts linked retail media mainly to the bottom of the sales funnel (i.e., direct sales),
but today it covers the entire process. This means that brands can accompany consumers from
awareness to actual conversion.
Although there are recommendations on formats (IAB, 2025), advertising sizes are not yet
fully standard ised, which opens opportunities for unique solutions. Native ads that are
seamlessly integrated into websites are less intrusive and therefore much more acceptable.
Another advantage is brand safety: advertisers can be sure of their ads running in a controlled,
high-quality environment.

What is content marketing?
Content marketing is one of the most important tools of digital communication because it
responds to changes in customer behaviour . T oday, people are actively interested in
information, searching for reviews, guides, and recommendations, and expecting useful and
relevant content from brands. Instead of one-off advertising, companies are increasingly using
a strategy based on building relationships and trust through valuable content.
Content marketing is  the systematic planning, creation , and distribution of content that is
relevant, meaningful, and consistent across channels for the target audience. The key is to
offer the audience value-added information that meets their needs and interests. Such content
gradually contributes toward building trust between the brand and the customer, while also
creating a perception of the company as an expert authority in the field. It is also important to
note that the goal is not immediate sales or a one-time interaction, but a long-term relationship
that strengthens customer loyalty and encourages them to return (Pulizzi, 2021).

Content marketing is often associated with inbound marketing, which embodies a broader
communication framework based on the principle of “attracting rather than interrupting ”.
Within this framework, content marketing is the primary  implementation tool because ,
through content, customers can be addressed naturally  and accompanied on their customer
journey (Halligan, Shah, 2020; Kotler et al., 2021). From a practical point of view, inbound
marketing is a kind of process framework, and content marketing is its “fuel”.
The purpose of content marketing: to entertain, persuade, inspire, and educate

The basic idea behind content marketing is simple: content should give consumers something.
It is not a classic advertising message that merely persuades the customer to buy. Content
marketing must bring value - otherwise it will be classified as a common and often
unsuccessful form of marketing.
Content can therefore be understood as a means to four key objectives:
Entertain: content as a way to hold attention
One of the most effective ways to work with an audience is the ability to engage and entertain
them. Entertainment allows you to reach even users who were not originally looking for the
brand and creates space for positive emotions associated with it . In the digital environment,
entertaining content is also very easy to share and can generate significant organic reach.
DOI: https://doi.org/10.15414/2026.9788055229799

75

Interactive formats (quizzes, games, polls), visual content, or viral elements that encourage
comments, sharing, and audience engagement are often used for this purpose. Social
networks serve as a key distribution channel here, as they disseminate content to a wide
audience while building a community around the brand (Kotler et al., 2021).
Entertaining content should not come across as aggressive advertising. Instead, it is a natural
part of digital conversation - the brand thus ceases to be a “seller” and becomes a partner that
the audience follows voluntarily.
Convince: provide evidence, value and arguments
Once the content has captured the audience ’s attention, the next step is to persuade.
Persuasion in content marketing is not based on pressure, but on providing value and
evidence. Users perceive the brand as offering something that makes sense . It possesses
quality and brings benefits.
For this reason, case studies, references, and expert content that show real results and benefits
play an important role. This type of content is particularly crucial in the later stages of the
customer’s decision-making process, as it acts as social proof , increasing brand credibility
(Chaffey, Ellis-Chadwick, 2019). Videos that combine education and emotional appeal can
also have a persuasive function and are therefore effective throughout the customer journey
(Kotler et al., 2021).
Inspire: build a relationship and a brand that customers want to identify with
Content marketing should not only inform and argue but also inspire. Inspiration builds
brand relationships and transforms audiences into active participants. In practice, inspiration
often comes from authentic experiences, community elements, and recommendations.
The inspirational level appears especially when the brand shows values, customer stories, or
provides motivational content. A key tool here is storytelling - the ability to tell stories in
a way that evokes emotions and allows customers to identify with the brand. Stories are more
natural and memorable than traditional advertising messages and therefore have a strong
effect in content strategy (Fog et al., 2020).
Inspirational content also promotes credibility - customers feel that the brand “understands
life” and is not just selling a product.
Educate: answer questions and help customers make decisions
Education is among the most enduring  pillars of content marketing. In the digital
environment, people often look for answers to questions such as “How does it work?”, “How
to choose it?”, “What is best?”, or “Why do I need it ?”. A brand that provide s clear, expert,
and useful explanations becomes an authority and simultaneously makes it easier for
customers to make decisions.
Typical educational formats include blog articles, tutorials, reviews, and expert analyses,
which can generate organic traffic while building brand credibility (Fishkin, Hogenhaven,
2019). E-books and white papers are also powerful tools, providing deeper , more systematic
insight into a topic while generat ing leads (Pulizzi, 2021). Podcasts offer the opportunity for
long-term contact with the audience and strengthen loyalty by serving as a regular source of
inspiration or education (Pulizzi, 2021).

Content marketing in practice: formats and strategies
Content marketing encompasses a wide range of formats that serve different functions within
the customer journey. While blog articles and infographics are particularly well-suited for
reaching audiences in the early stages, case studies and testimonials support purchas e
decisions. Video is a versatile format that can be used throughout the customer journey, and
podcasts are suitable for building long -term relationships (Kotler et al., 2021; Pulizzi, 2021;
Chaffey, Ellis-Chadwick, 2019).
However, creating content at random is not enough to ensure success. Content marketing
must be guided by a strategy that determines:
• who the content is intended for (working with personas),
• what the brand’s tone of voice will be,
• which channels will be used for distribution,
• how often it will be published,
• how the contribution of the content will be measured (Pulizzi, 2021).
A consistent tone of voice is essential for brand credibility - a long -term, consistent
communication style strengthens brand recognition and contributes to a sense of authenticity
(Fog et al., 2020). Equally important is the selection of distribution channels to ensure that
content reaches the right people at the right time (Chaf fey, Ellis -Chadwick, 2019; Pulizzi,
2021).
Trends in content marketing
Content marketing has undergone significant changes in recent years, primarily in response
to the rapid emergence of generative artificial intelligence, changes in user behaviour and
developments in search. While it used to be possible to attract traffic w ith simpler
“informational” content, today it is increasingly clear that simply summarising publicly
available information is not enough. The reason is that a large proportion of basic queries are
quickly satisfied by tools such as ChatGPT or AI responses in search engines, and users often
do not even need to click through to the brand ’s website. This has given rise to one of the
strongest current trends: the growing importance of content that comprises original opinions,
unique experiences, data, or expert context, and thus offers something that cannot be replaced
by a standard automated summary (Loktionova, 2025).
Another significant trend is the growing role of video content, especially shorter formats for
social networks. Video marketing drives  brand awareness and engagement  through
emotional, authentic , accessible content, while helping brands appear more personal and
strengthening brand-audience relationships. This has prompted a strategic shift from purely
article-based content to a multi-format approach that uses short videos, reels, shorts, or even
live broadcasts. The ability to distribute this content effectively across channels is also
important - not only publishing it, but also actively promoting its reach (Loktionova, 2025).
Another significant phenomenon is the growing integration of AI into content creation.
Artificial intelligence is now used for  more than writing texts. It designs structures, creates
scripts, edits videos, personalises different content variants,  and rapid ly produces them.

Email marketing today extends far beyond traditional newsletter distribution. It has evolved

manage data-driven communication ecosystems. And perhaps this is the greatest paradox of
email marketing: the more technological the world becomes, the more valuable the human
voice will be.
The importance and forms of email marketing
Over the past two decades, email marketing has been declared “dead” countless times: f irst
social media, then influencer marketing, later TikTok -driven attention economies, and most
recently AI-powered search systems.
Yet email remains one of the highest -return channels in digital marketing, especially within
the owned media ecosystem (Litmus, 2023). This paradox is fascinating in itself: even while
user attention spans increasingly shrink, email still manages to create deeper and more
sustainable relationships between brands and consumers.
In Central European markets, particularly in Hungary, Poland, Slovakia, and the Czech
Republic, the importance of email marketing has grown significantly in recent years. One
reason is the rapid evolution of digital maturity, while another is the tightening of data
privacy regulations, which have elevated the strategic value of first-party data (Kumar et al.,

2021). Today, brands no longer merely  send advertisements through email; they build
relationships, shape communities, and design micro-level consumer experiences.
For a long time, there was a stereotype suggesting that younger generations, especially
Generation Z, “do not read emails.” However, current research paints a far more nuanced
picture. Young consumers do not reject email itself; they reject poorly targeted, irrelevant, and
manipulative communication (McKinsey & Company, 2022). This fundamentally requires
a new strategic mindset from marketers.
The evolution of email marketing: From mass sending to hyper-personalisation
The early era of email marketing was dominated by mass communication. Companies sent
identical messages to tens of thousands of users with minimal segmentation. Although this
approach could produce short -term results, over time it led to consumer fatigue a nd
increasing perceptions of spam (Chaffey & Ellis-Chadwick, 2022).
Modern email marketing, by contrast, is built on behavioural logic. Companies have evolved
beyond collecting data; now they analyse digital behavioural patterns. Open rates, click paths,
purchasing cycles, and engagement metrics form predictive systems that enable highly
personalised communication (Wedel & Kannan, 2016).
One of the most important characteristics of hyper -personalisation is that it goes far beyond
inserting the user’s name into the subject line. The content, timing, offer, visual structure, and
CTA (Call -to-Action) all adapt dynamically to user behavio ur. A streaming platform, for
example, sends completely different emails to someone who watches horror movies than to
someone primarily interested in documentaries.
This process is closely linked to relationship marketing theory, which argues that long -term
customer relationships generate greater value than one -time transactions (Morgan & Hunt,
1994). In this context, email marketing is no longer simply a sales channe l; it becomes
a relationship management system.
The role of psychology in email marketing
The effectiveness of email marketing is heavily rooted in psychological mechanisms. One of
the best -known examples is the principle of reciprocity. When a brand provides valuable
content, education, or exclusive offers, consumers are more likely to respond  positively to
future communications (Cialdini, 2021).
Another important factor is cognitive load. Emails that are excessively long, overly complex,
or contain too many CTAs significantly reduce conversion rates. Neuromarketing research
suggests that users decide within seconds whether an email is relevant to them (HubSpot,
2023a). A particularly interesting phenomenon is “inbox intimacy,” referring to the
psychological closeness email can create. Unlike social media, where communication is
filtered through algorithms, email enters the user’s personal digital space directly. This creates
greater trust but also greater responsibility for brands (Rekettye et al., 2015).
Authenticity is especially critical for Generation Z. Overly polished and excessively corporate
communication often becomes counterproductive. Younger consumers tend to value
communication that feels human, transparent, and conversational (Deloitte, 2023). This is

Automation and artificial intelligence in email marketing
Email automation has become one of the most transformative developments in modern
marketing. Marketing automation systems allow communication to react to consumer
behaviour in real time. This is especially important in a fragmented digital environment where
user attention constantly shifts (MarTech Alliance, 2024).
One of automation’s greatest advantages is scalability. A well -designed onboarding flow,
abandoned cart sequence, or retention campaign can simultaneously feel personal while
reaching massive audiences. This paradox is precisely what makes email marketing
exceptionally effective.
The rise of artificial intelligence has opened entirely new dimensions in email communication.
AI is now capable of optimising subject lines, predicting ideal send times, generating content,
and developing segmentation models (Jarek & Mazurek, 2019). Nevertheless, it is important
to understand that AI does not replace strategic thinking. Generative systems can easily
descend into homogeneous and impersonal communication unless brands establish a unique
voice.
The email marketer of the future will likely not be an “email creator” but rather a data
interpreter and experience designer. AI automates technical processes while simultaneously
increasing the value of human creativity and strategic intuition.
Data privacy, trust, and ethical challenges
One of the biggest challenges in email marketing today is no longer technological —it is
relational and trust-based. The introduction of the GDPR fundamentally transformed digital
marketing practices across Europe (European Commission, 2020). Consumers now manage
their personal data much more consciously and increasingly expect transparent
communication.
As a result, first-party data strategies have become critically important. Brands can no longer
rely heavily on third-party cookies; instead, they must organically build their own databases
(Forrester, 2023). Email marketing plays a central role in this process because it enables direct
communication with users.
Ethical email marketing means more than legal compliance. At its core, it involves respecting
the consumer’s attention. Aggressive scarcity tactics, manipulative countdown timers, or
clickbait subject lines may increase open rates in the short term, but th ey ultimately damage
brand equity.
Central European consumers are particularly sensitive to issues of authenticity. In post -
socialist societies, institutional trust has historically been lower, requiring brands to approach
transparent communication with greater intentionality (Inglehart & Norris, 2017).

Email marketing rarely functions optimally in isolation anymore. Within today’s digital
ecosystem, email is part of a broader omnichannel system. Consumers simultaneously

a consistent brand voice. Consumers expect to encounter the same personality across
Instagram, email, and customer service chats. Inconsistent communication reduces brand

In Central European markets, the relationship between offline and online interactions is

Today, more than 60% of emails are opened on mobile devices (Campaign Monitor, 2023) ,

Future trends: where is email marketing heading?
The future of email marketing will likely revolve around three major directions: artificial
intelligence, data privacy, and hyper-personalisation.
AI is expected to create increasingly sophisticated predictive models capable not only of
forecasting when users will open emails but also how they may emotionally react to them. At
the same time, this raises significant ethical concerns.
Simultaneously, privacy -first marketing will continue gaining momentum. Because
consumers increasingly want control over their own data, the most successful brands will be
those that communicate transparently and create genuine value.
The third major trend is the return of human -centred communication. In an era of digital
overload, paradoxically, more personal and empathetic communication becomes
a competitive advantage. Email marketing therefore remains relevant not merely as
a technological channel, but as a platform for human connection.

Let's get to the point!
The concept and role of local marketing in digital marketing
Digital marketing has changed both marketing tools and consumer behavio ur. Today,
consumers often make quick decisions on their mobile phones based on their location. For
example, when users search for “coffee shops nearby,” they expect fast and relevant results.
Businesses therefore need communication that fits the consumer’s location and situation.
Local marketing focuses on consumers in a specific geographic area. It helps businesses reach
consumers at the right place and time. Mobile technologies make it possible to provide
location-based information instantly.
While o nline marketing uses internet channels, digital marketing includes all technology -
based marketing activities. Local marketing forms  part of digital marketing and focuses on
geographic relevance (Chaffey & Ellis-Chadwick, 2022). It has become increasingly important
because mobile devices connect digital information with physical locations. Local marketing
is especially important in retail and services, where consumers often search online before
visiting stores. “Near me” searches usually show strong purchase intention. Research also
shows that businesses with strong online visibility perform better in the market.
The digital behaviour of local consumers
Understanding digital consumer behavio ur is essential for successful local marketing.
Consumers often search for quick solutions depending on their location, time, and current
needs.

Mobile devices strongly influence shopping and search habits. Consumers frequently use
smartphones while moving around cities or shopping areas. Relevance and timing are
therefore very important in local marketing.
Online reviews also play a major role in local decisions. Consumers often trust reviews when
choosing restaurants, shops, or services. Positive reviews help businesses build trust and
attract customers (Karunaratna et al., 2026).
Another important trend is the rise of “micro -moments.” These are brief  situations when
consumers quickly search for information and expect immediate answers. Effective local
marketing connects online searches with offline visits and purchases (Grewal et al., 2020).
Local marketing tools in the digital space
Local marketing uses several digital tools together. One of the most important is local SEO,
which helps businesses appear in location-based searches.
Effective local SEO includes:
• Accurate business information
• Location-based keywords
• Managing online reviews
• Structured data
Location-based advertising is another important tool. Businesses can target users in specific
cities or areas with relevant ads. Research shows that geographically relevant mobile ads
increase consumer response (Bernritter et al., 2021).
Social media also supports local marketing through location tags, reviews, and local
interactions. Additional technologies include geo -fencing and proximity marketing, which
send messages to consumers based on their physical location. Local marketing is mo st
effective when all channels work together consistently (Hennig -Thurau et al., 2013;
Kingsnorth, 2022).
Proximity marketing and location-based technologies
Proximity marketing allows businesses to communicate with consumers based on physical
proximity. Smartphones and location technologies make this possible.
Common technologies include the following:
• GPS tracking
• Geo-fencing
• Bluetooth beacons
• Push notifications
Geo-fencing creates virtual areas where messages are automatically activated. Beacon systems
can even send messages inside stores.

Consumers react positively when messages are useful and relevant (Loussaief et al., 2023). For

Because these systems use personal data, privacy and transparency are very important.
Businesses must use location -based communication responsibly and build consumer trust

Local marketing strategies in practice
Successful local marketing requires strategic planning. Geographic targeting should support
business goals and fit the target audience.
Two common approaches are:
• Continuous digital presence
• Campaign-based communication

promotions or store openings. The best results usually come from combining both
approaches. Consumers often search online before visiting physical stores, so businesses must
connect online and offline experiences (Grewal & Roggeveen, 2020).
For example, a café may use local SEO and social media before opening and later launch
geographically targeted ads and online coupons. Data analysis also helps businesses improve
their strategies over time (Wedel & Kannan, 2016).
Measuring and optimising local marketing performance
Local marketing should create measurable business results. Measuring performance is

Key performance indicators include:
• Local search rankings
• Click-through rates
• Navigation requests

• Store visits
• Conversion rates
Modern analytics systems help businesses measure geographic performance and optim ise

Companies that have invested in social media marketing have seen improvement in their

Digitalisation, and especially social media, has transformed communication and social

It is becoming increasingly difficult to capture consumers’ attention with online content. That

audiences. The strategies companies adopt to attract and maintain customers often include

User-generated content
User-generated content (UGC) may be defined as “any kind of text, data, or action performed
by online digital systems users, published and disseminated by the same user through
independent channels, that incur an expressive or communicative effect either in an
individual manner or combined with other contributions from the same or other
sources”(Santos, 2021).

Social media are online platforms that contain user -generated content (Wyrwoll, 2014) . On
social media, views on products and services are exchanged through both comments and
reviews. These platforms enable users to share information, images, videos, etc., and provide
a space for interaction. It is a perfect environment for companies to gather, analyse, and use
UGC for marketing purposes.
Nowadays, user -generated content plays a huge role in how consumers perceive brands
(Keyzer et al., 2017; Luca, 2015). Favourable social media campaigns enhance their brand
perception (Wyrwoll, 2014) by stimulating electronic word of mouth (eWOM), which drives
greater engagement and, consequently, may boost sales (Kim & Johnson, 2016; Mayrhofer et
al., 2020; Sriyanti & Lukitaningsih, 2026).
Marketing through UGC platforms may:
• support gathering information about customers and their needs,
• increase brand visibility,
• build the company’s reputation,
• increase brand trust and authenticity,
• create emotional engagement and develop more personalised relationships with
customers,
• influence purchasing decisions (Jusuf, 2022; Luca, 2015; Mayrhofer et al., 2020;
Sriyanti & Lukitaningsih, 2026; Wyrwoll, 2014)
Companies make use of UGC available online, for instance, by reposting their positive
comments or publishing positive reviews. They may also encourage social media users to be
creative and publish new content. Motivating users to publish about the company is done by:
• encouraging feedback after purchase,
• rewarding social media users in contests in exchange for posting content featuring
their products or specific hashtags,
• sending them new products to test and share their opinions online,
• encouraging them to leave reviews as a part of the brand community,
• launching interactive social media challenges or branded AR filters that prompt users
to participate,
• offering exclusive discounts or loyalty points in exchange for tagging the brand in
a post or story.
UGC is typically far more effective than conventional marketing content, because consumers
view peer recommendations as more credible than company -generated content, since the
company’s ultimate goal is to push sales and generate profit (Sriyanti & Lukitaningsih, 2026).
A threat to the future of UGC used by companies in their marketing activities is the growing
number of fake reviews, which are becoming increasingly difficult for the average consumer
to distinguish from actual customer feedback. This problem is further in tensified by the
development of AI-based tools that enable rapid, mass creation of fake profiles and content,
complicating the verification of UGC authenticity.

Influencer marketing
Online influencer marketing is “a strategy in which a firm selects and incentiv ises online
influencers to engage their followers on social media in an attempt to leverage these
influencers’ unique resources to promote the firm’s offerings, with the ultimate goal of
enhancing firm performance” (Leung, Gu, & Palmatier, 2022).
Content created by influencers seems more authentic to consumers. Frequent user exposure
to their posts fosters a sense of familiarity, leading followers to feel like they know the
influencer well, which contributes to greater trust (Leung, Gu, & Palmatier, 2022).
Furthermore, unlike other forms of online advertising, which are typically interruptive and
passively received, influencer marketing relies on voluntary media consumption. While social
media users often avoid, skip, or even block online advertisements, i nfluencers actively
choose the creators they follow. This is a major advantage and a great support for companies
looking to reach their customers. It is therefore not surprising that influencer marketing has
gained popularity in recent years and continues to grow. Influencers are perceived as an
effective marketing communication channel and have a measurable impact on consumer
purchasing behaviour, especially among younger generations (Kádeková & Holienčinová,
2018).
Factors that enhance the effectiveness of influencer marketing include, among others ,
visibility, reflected in indicators such as the number of followers, comments, and post likes;
engagement expressed through frequent posting and active interaction with followers; and
creativity and originality to capture audience attention. However, the  key factor remains
perceived authenticity and credibility (Bakker, 2018; Duffek et al., 2025).  When influencer
posts appear overly commercial, they lose effectiveness because consumers expect
authenticity and tend to disengage from content perceived as advertising (Bakker, 2018).
To maintain perceived authenticity, the influencer's image must align with the brand’s values
and the character of the advertised product (Leung, Gu, & Palmatier, 2022) . A lack of
consistency can undermine the credibility of the message and reduce the campaign’s overall
effectiveness. To assess an influencer’s attractiveness and suitability for marketing purposes,
practitioners may rely on the VisCAP model (Table 1). This framework enables a structured
evaluation of key influencer characteristics, including visibi lity, influence, credibility, and
attractiveness.

Storytelling
Storytelling in social media is the strategic use of narratives to communicate a brand's
message, values, and identity to its audience. Instead of merely presenting product features,
storytelling weaves emotional, relatable, and authentic stories that reso nate with consumers
on a personal level. In the digital age, where users are overwhelmed by information,
a compelling narrative helps a brand stand out and fosters a deeper connection. Effective
storytelling can transform passive followers into active brand advocates by making the
brand’s journey, mission, or customer experiences memorable and engaging. It serves as
a glue that holds other strategies together. For instance, both user -generated content and
influencer campaigns are significantly more effective when they tell a consistent and engaging
story about the brand.
From a theoretical perspective, the effectiveness of storytelling in social media is heavily
supported by narrative transportation theory (van Laer et al., 2014). When consumers become
deeply immersed or “transported” into a compelling narrative, their emotional engagement
increases while their critical, counter -arguing cognitive processes decrease. In a digital
landscape saturated with overt promotional content, storytelling allows brands to bypass
consumers’ natural scepticism. Consequently, a well-crafted story not only captures attention
but also seamlessly integrates the brand’s message into consumers’ mindsets, leading to more
positive brand attitudes, enhanced trust, and stronger purchase intentions (Thomas
& Grigsby, 2024).
To implement storytelling effectively, brands often utilise several key narrative types:
• Origin stories (heritage storytelling) allow customers to understand the brand’s roots,
its core mission, and the passion behind its creation.
• Behind-the-scenes content human ises the company by showcasing the people,
craftsmanship, and daily processes involved in making a product.
• Customer-driven narratives  - often intertwined with user -generated content -
highlight real -life experiences, essentially turning the buyer into the hero of the
brand’s tale. From a psychological perspective, the human brain is wired to process,
retain, and recall narratives far better than isolated facts or technical specifications.

Therefore, a well-crafted story not only captures initial attention but also ensures long-
term brand recall and loyalty.
Summary
Nowadays, marketers need to acknowledge the growing role of the digital environment,
especially social media, which serves as a platform for sharing customer experiences and
contributes substantially to  consumer attitudes and behaviour. The importance of sharing
experiences and emotions is reflected in the marketing practices discussed in this chapter. To
summarise:
• User-generated content is perceived as more authentic and evokes a stronger
emotional engagement, which is why it is often considered more persuasive than
content created by companies.
• Influencers play a key role in creating online content and engaging consumers in
discussions about brands and products, exposing them to promotional materials.
Their authenticity and emotional bond with their audience can increase brand
visibility, build trust, and positively impact brand perception.
• By translating brand values into accessible narratives and authentic experiences, the
message becomes more memorable. Storytelling helps establish a clear brand identity,
build brand awareness, and create emotional connections with the target audience.
However, it is important to remember that social media marketing should only be part of
a company’s overall marketing strategy. The extent of this will vary depending on the
industry and the specific characteristics of the target audience. Even more effective results are
achieved through integrated marketing strategies that combine online and offline activities
and make thoughtful use of both traditional (e.g. , TV, radio, outdoor) and digital channels
(Appel et al., 2020).
To sum up, in today ’s digitally saturated environment, capturing and retaining consumer
attention requires more than traditional advertising. Marketers must leverage user-generated
content, influencer partnerships, and engaging storytelling to cut through the noise. UGC
provides social proof and authenticity, while influencers act as trusted mediators bridging the
gap between brands and niche audiences. Meanwhile, storytelling binds these elements
together, translating brand values into compelling narratives. However, to maximise
effectiveness, these social media tactics should not exist in isolation. The most successful
brands integrate their digital efforts with broader, omnichannel marketing strategies,
ensuring a cohesive and consistent brand experience across both online and offline
touchpoints.

Introduction to Search Engine Marketing
Every day, over 8.5-16.4 billion searches are conducted on Google. People search for products,
services, answers to questions, and solutions to their problems—and it is precisely when they
are actively searching for something that they are most likely to make a purchase decision.
Search Engine Marketing (SEM) is an umbrella term for all marketing activities aimed at
gaining visibility in search results. It encompasses two key disciplines that are closely related
but operate on different principles: SEO and PPC. In the next chapter, PPC will be discussed
in detail.
A Brief History and Evolution of SEO
The origins of search engine optimisation date back to the mid -1990s. At that time, SEO was
relatively simple —search results could be influenced primarily by modifying content,
keyword density, and basic HTML tags (Straight North, 2025). The first search tools enabling
website indexing included Archie (1990) and WebCrawler (1994).
DOI: https://doi.org/10.15414/2026.9788055229799

116

The year 1997 marked a significant milestone when Google began developing the PageRank
algorithm, which evaluated the quality and quantity of backlinks. This change signalled a shift
away from purely technical manipulations toward an emphasis on website credibility (Veglis
and Giomelakis, 2021).
With the growing popularity of the internet at the turn of the millennium, unethical methods
emerged—so-called keyword stuffing (excessive insertion of keywords) and link farms
(websites artificially interconnected by masses of links with no real value). Google’s response
was the Florida Update in November 2003, which significantly reduced the effectiveness of
these techniques. Conversely, websites with high-quality and relevant content were boosted
(Veglis and Giomelakis, 2021).
In 2011, the Panda update was released to target low -quality content, followed in 2012 by
Penguin to combat spammy link structures. The year 2013 brought Hummingbird —
a revolution in the understanding of search queries, as Google began interpreting user intent
rather than just keyword matches (Baker, 2021). Together, these steps shifted SEO from simple
tricks to a strategic field requiring a long-term approach.
How Search Engines Work
Before diving into strategy, it is important to understand how search engines work. The entire
process rests on three pillars (Enge et al., 2023):
• Crawling - search robots (known as crawlers or “spiders”) constantly scour the
internet, monitoring both the content and structure of pages and creating a site map
by following links. The robots.txt file plays an important role here, as it determines
which parts of the website should be accessible to crawlers, while the sitemap.xml file
provides a list of all important pages and facilitates their indexing (Clarke, 2023).
• Indexing - the pages found are analysed and stored in a massive database - the search
engine index. Text, images, metadata, and structured data are analysed. The quality of
indexing depends on the technical condition of the website - whether it is responsive,
loads quickly, and uses the secure HTTPS protocol (Chaffey and Ellis -Chadwick,
2019).
• Ranking - when a user enters a query, the algorithm evaluates hundreds of factors and
ranks the results by relevance and quality. Google uses machine learning and artificial
intelligence—for example, through algorithms  such as RankBrain or BERT —to best
understand the user’s intent, not just the specific words they entered (Enge et al., 2023).
SEO - Search Engine Optimisation
SEO (Search Engine Optimisation) is a set of techniques and strategies aimed at improving
a website’s visibility in organic search results. Unlike paid advertising, SEO does not deliver
immediate results—it is a long -term investment that, when approached correctly, generates
a steady and cost- effective flow of visitors. SEO enables websites to rank highly in search
results and attract targeted traffic, and this strategy can continue to generate visitors for
months or even years after implementation (Clarke, 2023).
SEO is a distinct field with entire books, courses, and specialised agencies dedicated to it. Our
goal here is not to turn you into an SEO specialist, but to give you a solid foundation  to

a combination of high-quality content, proper structure, and technical details that together tell
search engines: “This page is exactly what the user is looking for.”
Content and keywords. Content is the foundation of all SEO. However, it is not enough to
insert keywords into the text—Google now understands context and the semantic
relationships between words. The topic  must be covered  comprehensively, naturally, and
with clear added value for readers because search engines prefer pages that truly explain the
subject matter rather than merely mention keywords (Clarke, 2023). Keywords should appear
naturally in the page title (H1), subheadings (H2, H3), the introduction, the meta description,
and the URL.
Page structure. A clear hierarchical structure of headings helps both users and search engines
navigate the content. Internal linking —connecting relevant pages within the website—then
helps distribute authority and guide users to additional content. A well -organised page
hierarchy helps search engines understand how different parts of the website are related and
enables new subpages to be indexed faster (Clarke, 2023).
Meta tags. The title tag and meta description are the first things a user sees in search results.
Although the meta description does not directly influence rankings, it significantly affects the
click-through rate (CTR)  and boosts rankings. A well -written title should include the main
keyword and be enticing, while the description should briefly summarise the page’s content
and encourage clicks (Chaffey and Ellis-Chadwick, 2019).
Off-page SEO
Off-page SEO takes place outside your website and reflects the outside world’s view of your
site. A key factor is link building—acquiring backlinks from other websites. Search engines
view backlinks as votes of confidence—the more links come from relevant and trustworthy
sources, the higher the relevance of the target page. However, it is not just about quantity, but
especially about the quality of the links (Enge et al., 2023).
Google evaluates the following in particular:
• Authority of the linking website - a link from a reputable media outlet has significantly
higher value than a link from an unknown blog
• Relevance - a link from a thematically related website is more valuable than a link from
an unrelated source
• Anchor text - the text on which the link is placed tells the search engine what the linked
page is about

• Naturalness of the backlink profile - a sudden surge in a large number of links may be
perceived as manipulation
In addition to link building, off-page SEO also includes brand mentions without links, reviews
on external platforms, and social media activity. Although Google states that likes and shares
alone are not a direct ranking factor, they indirectly contribute to increased traffic and content
dissemination (Chaffey and Ellis-Chadwick, 2019).
Technical SEO
Even the best content will not help if the search engine can’t properly crawl, index, and display
the page. Technical SEO addresses this layer.
Key areas of technical SEO include:
• Page load speed - slow pages deter users, and Google penalises them in rankings
• Mobile optimisation - Google uses “mobile -first indexing,” meaning it primarily
evaluates the mobile version of a website. This forces web developers to place mobile
usability at the centre of their strategy (Clarke, 2023).
• HTTPS and security - an encrypted connection is a fundamental requirement for
a website’s trustworthiness. Google explicitly confirms that HTTPS is one of its
ranking factors (Chaffey and Ellis-Chadwick, 2019).
• Crawlability and indexing - a properly configured robots.txt file and sitemap.xml
ensure that Google finds and indexes the correct pages
• Structured data (Schema markup) - helps Google better understand content and can
lead to the display of so -called rich snippets in search results, which increases
attractiveness and click-through rates (Enge et al., 2023)

Ethical and Unethical SEO Practices
In SEO, there are various approaches to website optimisation. Some methods align with
search engine guidelines and focus on long -term user benefits, while others employ
manipulative tactics to improve rankings quickly. Marketers need to understand these
differences, as an improperly chosen strategy can lead not only to short-term results but also
to the website being penalised (Clarke, 2023).
White-hat SEO  embraces an ethical approach to optimisation that follows official search
engine guidelines (the so-called Google Webmaster Guidelines). It focuses on creating high -
quality, relevant content, properly optimising technical aspects, and acquiring backlinks
naturally. The advantages are the long-term stability of results and website credibility (Enge
et al., 2023).
Black-hat SEO uses unethical and prohibited methods that attempt to deceive search engine
algorithms. These include keyword stuffing (excessive insertion of keywords), cloaking
(displaying different content to search engines and users), and link farms (networks of
websites artificially generating large numbers of links with no real value). These techniques
may improve rankings in the short term, but if detected, they lead to penalties, and in extreme
cases, even to the complete removal of the website from the index (Clarke, 2023).
Grey-hat SEO  operates on the edge of ethics —this includes, for example, buying links,
excessively exchanging links, or using automated tools for content generation. Grey-hat SEO
can yield results, but it poses a higher risk if search engines tighten their rules (Enge et al.,
2023).
Measuring SEO Success
SEO is a long-term process whose results are not immediate. Therefore, you should monitor
performance metrics and systematically evaluate the strategy's effectiveness (Clarke, 2023).
The most commonly tracked KPIs include (Enge et al., 2023):
• Organic traffic - the number of visitors coming from unpaid search results
• Keyword rankings - the website’s visibility in SERPs for selected queries
• CTR (Click-Through Rate) - the click-through rate from search results
• Bounce rate and dwell time - indicators of how long users stay on the website and
whether they leave quickly
• Number of conversions from organic traffic - registrations, orders, or other target
actions that demonstrate the business value of SEO
ROI (Return on Investment) is also a significant indicator of success. SEO is more cost-effective
than PPC campaigns because it drives long-term organic traffic without paying for every click.
Calculating ROI involves comparing SEO costs (specialist labour, tools, content creation) with
the value of conversions generated. However, you should  consider the long-term horizon—

and Ellis-Chadwick, 2019).
Tools such as Google Analytics and  Google Search Console evaluate success  by measuring
traffic, keyword rankings, and conversion rates.

Let's get to the point!
Search engine advertising is  a form of online marketing that focuses on displaying paid
advertisements within search engine results pages (SERPs) (Kapoor et al., 2018). The pay-per-
click (PPC) model charges advertisers for actual clicks rather than advertisement impressions.
The concept of pay -per-click advertising was first introduced by Jeffrey Brewer in 1998 and
was subsequently adopted by Google in 2000. PPC advertising is utilise d both within search
engine results and across display networks, where advertisements are shown on websites and
digital platforms visited by users, including on corporate websites, social media platforms,
and other online content environments (Burešová, 2022; Halagan, 2021).
PPC advertising is considered one of the most effective forms of online marketing
communication. Its effectiveness stems from the ability to precisely target specific customer
segments, which contributes to relatively cost -efficient advertising campaigns. In essence,
advertisements are displayed primarily to users actively searching for relevant information
through search engines or consuming online content, with the advertising message tailored
to the user’s search intent or the context of the content being viewed (Janouch, 2020).
Furthermore, PPC advertising enables marketers to reach users across a wide range of digital
platforms. Advertising campaigns can be configured to target audiences based on
demographic characteristics, interests, geographic location, and keyword searches, allowing
for highly personalised communication strategies (Koman et al., 2020; Burešová, 2022). This
form of paid search marketing involves placing advertisements that appear above, alongside,
and occasionally below organic search results on search engines such as Google, Microsoft
Bing, Yandex, and Yahoo. Securing prominent positions within these results generally
requires participation in an auction -based bidding system. Beyond search engines, PPC
advertising has become increasingly prevalent on social media platforms, including Facebook,
Instagram, and Pinterest (3G Elearning, 2021).

PPC Advertising Systems
PPC systems are based on an auction model, meaning that advertisers can set the maximum
amount they are willing to pay per click. However, the selected bid price significantly
influences both the frequency and the position in which the advertisement is dis played
(Burešová, 2022). The dominant player in PPC advertising systems is Google Ads. Developed
by Google, it enables the creation, management, and optim isation of advertising campaigns
and individual ads (Berčík et al., 2023).
The auction system used by Google is based on the following operating principles: each
advertiser submits, through the advertising system, the maximum price they are willing to
pay for a recurring click on their advertisement. However, achieving the highest ad position
and ranking is not determined solely by bid amount; it also depends on the platform
algorithm’s evaluation of ad relevance and quality. In most cases, search engine algorithms
prioritise advertisements that are most relevant to a specific user search query (Berčík et al.,
2023). Within a Google Ads account, it is also possible to define campaign objectives. The
primary objectives include sales, website traffic, brand awareness, lead generation, product
and brand consideration, and app promotion (Janouch, 2020).

Types of PPC Campaigns
According to Janouch (2020), PPC campaigns can be classified into three primary categories

The first category comprises brand -building campaigns, which are primarily designed to

The second category includes performance -oriented campaigns, whose primary objective is
to generate measurable conversions, such as product purchases, registrations, or form
submissions. The evaluation of these campaigns usually focuses on performance indi cators
including conversion rate, total number of clicks, number of conversions, and cost per

The third category consists of traffic -generation campaigns, which are particularly relevant

relevant traffic, including session duration, bounce rate, and users’ navigation behavio ur

• Shopping advertising - product-based ads that display product images, prices, and

Process of Creating an Effective PPC Campaign
According to Janouch (2014) and Koman et al. (2020), developing an effective PPC campaign

CHAPTER 15: UX AND UI
Peter Šedík

Learning Outcomes:
After completing this lesson, learners will:
 Describe concepts of User Experience (UX) and User Interface (UI).
 Analyse the use of UX and UI design in websites or e-commerce platforms.
 Understand the role of UX and UI in creating effective digital experiences.
 Identify and discuss differences between UX and UI.

Let's get to the point!
UX and UI are two closely related terms that are frequently used together, and their
significance has grown substantially in recent years. This growth is largely driven by the fact
that effective UX and UI design contribute to higher user retention on websites. Both concepts
are fundamentally user-centred. Their primary objective is to enable users to easily locate the
information they need, complete desired actions such as purchases without difficulty, and
ultimately return to the website and potentially recommend it to others (Gállova, 2024).
According to the illustrated Dictionary of Digital Marketing, the UX and UI can be defined as
follows:
• “UX stands for User Experience. UX refers to how a user interacts with a website or app (where
they click, which pages they visit). UX can be shaped by testing differences in page layouts,
CTAs, colours, content, etc. to improve conversion rates. User experience (UX) design is the
process design teams use to create products that provide meaningful and relevant experiences
to users. This encompasses  the entire process of acquiring and integrating the product,
including usability and functionality.
• “UI stands for User Interface. User interface is the area with which a user interacts with
something through a digital device. Good UI should be fluid and easy for most users to
understand” (3G Elearning, 2021).
The theory explains UX design as the creation of the experience that a customer has when
interacting with a product, service, or e -commerce platform. A high -quality customer
experience is influenced by both the appearance and behaviour of the interface through which
the user navigates. The user interface handles how  individual visual elements and
information are organised on a website. UI design refers to all visual elements by which users
interact with e-shops or websites. These include buttons, icons, menu items, text, colours, and
images (Toszegi, 2021).

In practice, however, it is often difficult to distinguish between these two types of design, as

They share a very similar objective: to satisfy the customer while continuously focusing on
user needs and capabilities. Emphasis is placed on design that ensures simple and highly
efficient use of the product or system. User interface and user experience frequently operate
as interdependent components that fit together. In the simplest terms, the key difference is
that UX design focuses on how a product functions, whereas UI refers to the object’s visual
appearance. The interplay between UI and UX creates a seamless alignment between
functionality and a positive user experience, and these attributes are inseparable from

Five fundamental principles of UI design
• Clarity - a core component of a functional user interface, as effective visual design
prioritises and communicates information.
• Familiarity - the use of established user interface principles and interaction patterns.
• User control - emphasis on the user’s sense of control (the feeling that they can do what
they want within the system).
• Hierarchy - the arrangement of visual elements in a way that conveys the relative
importance of each element and guides the user towards the desired action.
• Accessibility - products should be designed for all users (Berčík et al., 2023; Stašíková,
2021).
UX and UI are processes consisting of seven iterative steps:
• UX research - identifying user behaviour and comparing it with the desired behaviour.
Modern UX research relies on analytical tools that can monitor user behaviour and
evaluate a website’s key performance indicators.
• UX design - creating solutions that eliminate discrepancies between current and
desired user behaviour on the website. This stage involves identifying UX activities
with the highest potential to achieve the desired behavioural state.
• UI design - generating technical UI interventions that have the greatest potential to
influence UX derived from website use. The focus is primarily on interface elements.
• Testing – conducting tests on a smaller sample of users before launching the revised
website into live operation. Testing is monitored, and user activities are evaluated.
• Implementation - applying proposed modifications after successful testing.
• Monitoring - continually tracking and evaluating the current and desired states.
• Reporting – analysing monitoring results and report ing them to the website owner
(Kuchta et al., 2024).

Introduction to Data-driven marketing
Marketing professionals use data to guide their decisions. In the past, many marketing
decisions were based on intuition or “what worked last time,” even when the external
environment (customers, competition, channels) had changed. Therefore, data serves as
evidence, precisely identifying who actually converts, which channel is more effective, and
how customers behave during the purchasing process (Lilliard, 2025).
Data-driven marketing is the practice of making strategic and tactical marketing decisions
based on accurate, up-to-date data sourced from various channels. It shapes strategy, allocates
budgets, and optimises marketing campaigns (Mironov, 2026).
Wedel and Kannan (2016) describe marketing analytics as a discipline that supports decision-
making in data -rich environments, especially in areas such as marketing -mix optimisation,
personalisation, and privacy-aware analytics.

touchpoints across the customer journey, rather than treat channels  as isolated. Chaffey and

converting, and retaining customers across digital platforms.

Google Tag Manager (GTM)
Google Tag Manager (GTM) is not a reporting tool, but a measurement management system.
It loads and controls tags, resolves variables, monitors browser or data-layer events, and sends
structured data to downstream systems such as GA4 (Google, n.d.-c).
GTM’s logic is compact yet powerful. A tag measure s an action, such as the number of page
views, purchases, or ad-conversion hits. A trigger establishes the condition under which an
action is to be executed. A variable resolves the value required by the tag or trigger, for
example, the page URL, transaction ID , or button text. The dataLayer is a JavaScript
object/queue used by GTM and the Google tag to pass structured information, including
events and variables, into the tag -management system. Google ’s developer documentation
specifies that the data layer should be initialised before the container snippet, and that its
messages are processed first-in, first-out (Google, n.d.-c).
The interface is organ ised around a few stable surfaces. All accounts  is a page that lists
accounts and containers. Inside a container, the Workspace is where tags, triggers, variables,
and folders are edited;  Versions stores published snapshots; Admin controls settings and
user management; and the upper-right controls are used for Preview and Submit/Publish.
GTM relies on three pillars: tags, triggers, and variables.
DOI: https://doi.org/10.15414/2026.9788055229799

165

Tags are snippets of code (e.g., GA4 config, Facebook Pixel, custom HTML) that send data to
analytics/marketing tools. They execute website actions such as firing events or pixels when
conditions are met. They define what action GTM should take (Google, n.d.-d).
Examples:
• The GA4 Configuration tag (sends data to your Google Analytics account).
• A Facebook Conversion tag (notifies Facebook that a purchase happened).
• Custom HTML (a piece of your own code, like a chat widget).
Triggers define when tags fire, based on events like page views ( “All Pages ”), clicks (on
buttons/links), form submissions, timers, or custom data layer pushes. They use conditions
(e.g., Page URL matches regex) to ensure precise timing (Ficilcom, 2023).
Examples:
• All Pages: Fires as soon as any page on your site starts to load.
• Click: Fires only when a specific button (like “Add to Cart”) is clicked.
• Form Submission: Fires when a user successfully fills out a contact form.
• Timer: Fires after a user has been on the page for 30 seconds.
A variable is a placeholder for a value that changes depending on the situation. It provides
the “extra details” that both tags and triggers need. It stores data for use in the tag or as a filter
in the trigger.

Let's get to the point!
The development of the internet and digital technologies is significantly transforming the way
marketing communication is conducted. Market organisations have gained the ability to
integrate the real and virtual worlds seamlessly and increasingly to personalise their
relationships with consumers. One of the elements driving digital progress is data (Bounfour,
2016), which brands collect in both physical and digital environments . From a digital
marketing perspective, the challenge is not so much generating and collecting data. Instead,
it is using the data effectively to generate value (Romaniuk, 2020). Customer information is
gathered both online and offline and, when properly processed, can be used to optimise the
shopping experience (Figure 1). However, this requires a comprehensive approach to all data
generated by each consumer (Błażewicz, 2021).

Effective use of data in digital marketing should create seamless experiences across a unified
communication environment (Rogala, Pilarczyk, 2020), even when different tools and forms
interact with consumer s. This requires  coordinated, integrated management of all
communication channels with the audience (Wiktor, 2013). As a result, the brand’s dialogue
with the customer becomes increasingly fluid and multi -channel, whilst the consumer

However, the experiences gathered across various touchpoints with the brand should remain
consistent, leaving the impression of a coherent marketing communication environment
(Rogala, Pilarczyk, 2020). Such multichannel interaction with the customer, which integrates

touchpoints with the consumer, but above all their interaction centred on each recipient
(Hajduk, 2019). As a result, it becomes possible to utilise the most appropriate forms and
channels of communication for an individual, the specific situation, place and time.
The omnichannel concept presented here results from a gradual transformation of marketing
communication tools, which have evolved from traditional forms through digital marketing,
to solutions based on data and advanced technologies (Figure 2). This development has
enabled a shift from mass communication to personalised and context -sensitive interaction
with the audience.

In contrast to traditional marketing, where communication is static and limited in
personalisation, digital marketing tools have introduced interactivity and the ability to tailor
content to the specific audience. The next stage of development is SMART mar keting, which
relies on data, automation, and advanced technologies such as AI, IoT , or immersive
technologies to create consumer experiences. As a result, marketing communication is
becoming increasingly contextual, personalised, and integrated, which forms the basis for the
effective functioning of the omnichannel model.
Practice shows that modern consumer s no longer operate exclusively in a single
communication channel. Instead, they move freely between various touchpoints, both online
and offline (Błażewicz, 2021). This process is dynamic and often non -linear (Bajak, Spendel,
2023), with individual communication channels intertwining and complementing one
another. New technologies therefore play a crucial role in coordinating the overall interaction
with an individual consumer. They enable the collection and analysis of user data and ensure
continuity of communication regardless of place, time, and form of contact.
In this context, it is worth taking a closer look at Internet of Things technologies and virtual
and augmented reality solutions. The Internet of Things enables the integration of devices and
the collection of information in the physical world (Szozda, 201 7), allow ing marketing
communications to be better tailored to the audience’s current activities and the overall
context. AR and VR technologies, in turn, expand the scope of interaction with a brand,
enabling the creation of engaging, immersive experiences combining elements of the physical
and digital worlds (Berbeka, 2016).
The Internet of Things (IoT) refers to an infrastructure of interconnected devices and other
physical objects equipped with sensors that collect data, monitor conditions, support decision-
making and optimise processes (Dobbs, Manyika, Woetzel, 2015). In marketing
communications, this enables the collection of information on consumer behaviour in the
physical world and its integration with digital systems. In turn, t his results in  a better

understanding of consumer needs, more effective monitoring of user behaviour, and effective
alignment with contextual and behavioural expectations (Dudhane, Pitambare, 2015). The IoT
therefore facilitates a shift from a reactive to a proactive approach to the consumer, in which
marketing communication can be initiated even before the recipient becomes aware of a need.
Furthermore, smart objects make possible interaction between the digital and physical worlds
and the smooth integration of offline and online sales channels (Królewski, Sala, 2016).
The capabilities of the Internet of Things are complemented by immersive technologies, which
open up new possibilities for consumer interaction with brands. Augmented reality (AR)
enables digital elements such as illustrations, content, simulations, or other visual forms to be
superimposed onto the user’s real-world environment (Peddie, 2017). Virtual reality (VR), on
the other hand, creates a fully digital environment, forming an alternative version of reality
(LaValle, 2023). Marketing communication using such technologies therefore takes the form
of an experience in which the recipient is not merely a passive observer but an active
participant in the interaction with the brand or its product range.
The use of AR and VR technologies in marketing enables the presentation of products and
services that are more engagingly and interactively  tailored to the individual needs of the
user. For example, AR solutions allow clothing to be “tried on” virtually or visualised in a real-
world environment (Sunan et al., 2023), while VR can simulate brand-related experiences
(Soni, Yadav, Soni, 2022). Such applications build stronger relationships with consumers and
increase their engagement level.
The integration of the physical and digital worlds, made possible in part by Internet of Things
technologies and immersive solutions, is leading to a shift in the role of marketing
communication. In the classical approach, three basic functions of marketing communication
are identified: informational, persuasive, and competitive (Wiktor, 2013). Increasingly,
however, in an omnichannel environment, marketing communication is becoming part of
a broader consumer experience designed to build long-term relationships. Today, consumers
are no longer merely recipients of promotional activities. Instead, they are encouraged to take
an active role and become brand advocates (Kotler, Kartajayi, Setiawan, 2017). Marketing
communication thus takes on a contextual and situational character, and its effectiveness
depends on the extent to which it is tailored to the users’ current needs and activity. As
a result, solutions enabling the precise identification of a consumer’s presence in physical
space and its integration with digital communication systems are becoming increasingly
important.

selection of footwear and accessories from various brands (Modivo, 2026). The brand is part
of the CCC Group, which is committed to intensive technological development with a strong

focus on omnichannel customer experiences, modern stores equipped with digital solutions,

Overview
The business model of eobuwie.pl is based on a multi -channel approach to the customer,
characterised by strong integration of digital and physical channels. At the same time, it
utilises the concept of a phygital store, which involves combining physical sh opping
experiences with digital tools. It combines, on the one hand, the ability to view and touch
products and build relationships with customers in the physical world, and, on the other, the
use of digital tools for data analysis, the creation of personalised offers, and trend forecasting
(Iannilli, Spagnoli, 2021). The operating model driving eobuwie.pl represents one of the most
advanced examples in the Visegrad region for applying this concept, where the integration of
digital and physical channels is not merely complementary but systematically redefines the
role of the brick -and-mortar store, transforming it into the physical interface of the
organisation’s digital infrastructure.
Particularly important is the use of 3D foot-scanning technology with the esize.me system. It
is available both as devices in the brand’s physical stores and via the app (GoMobi, 2020). This
solution addresses one of the key challenges in the footwear e-commerce industry, namely the
high rate of poor product fit. The scanner generates a three- dimensional model of the user’s
foot, converting it into a set of biometric s, which are stored in the cloud and assigned to the
customer’s individual profile (Nowy Marketing, 2020). This enables a highly accurate shoe fit,
reducing returns and increasing user comfort. Besides in physical retail stores, the data are
used in the mobile app and on the e -commerce platform. Additionally, the system allows
users to store footwear information about other family members (Newsweek, 2019).
Equally significant for the eobuwie.pl brand is the reorganisation of the shop space by
eliminating traditional shelves and replacing them with interactive touchscreens. The interior
design is minimalist, and product displays have been kept to a minimum. The primary way
of presenting the assortment is through a digital catalogue. As a result, customers can browse
the brand’s full range and check footwear availability in real time, both in the back-of-house
warehouse and at the logistics centre (Business Insider, 2018).

Let's get to the point!

healthcare, the automotive industry, finance, and retail (Zhang, Li, 2021)  because they
streamline decision-making and automate tasks efficiently (Jelonek et al., 2019; Jarosz, 2023).
Artificial intelligence is used in organisations to process vast amounts of customer data,
thereby enabling personalised marketing communications and closer alignment with
consumer needs and expectations (Błażewicz, 2021; Pathak, Sharma, 2022).
The global artificial intelligence market has been growing rapidly (Statista, 2026). Intelligent
systems utilising AI have the potential to transform existing business models (Singh,
Chouhan, 2021). As a result, ongoing technological development is driving  changes in the
modern business environment (Ebert, Duarte, 2020; Vial, 2021), whilst the scope of
applications for information and communication technologies is expanding at a rapidly
accelerating pace (Mazurek, 2019). Emerging innovations and transformations are also
driving changes in the way marketing communication is conducted (Nesterenko, Miskiewicz,
Abazov, 2023). T he use of artificial intelligence to create content —including text, images,
music, video, visualisations and other forms of design—appears particularly relevant (Muller
et al., 2022).
Furthermore, AI enables organisations to automate promotional activities based on consumer
characteristics and behaviour (Błażewicz, 2021). In line with the omnichannel model, which
involves the use of diverse, integrated channels to deliver promotional messages  and

continuous, consistent marketing communication based on a recipient’s individual
preferences. This approach is intended to foster a lasting relationship with the buyer

content that is both visually appealing and tailored to individual consumer needs. Algorithms
for natural language processing, machine learning, and recommendation systems can provide
significant support in this regard (Mondal, Das, Vrana, 2023).
Trends related to engaging consumers in co -creating the brand, encouraging them to share
values and motivating them to advocate for the market offering are also significant (Kotler,

Artificial intelligence can support these processes not only through subset  analysis but also
by discovering relationships in raw data, proposing  solutions, and creat ing new
functionalities (Garnerelo, Shanahan, 2019). Another important area of application for
artificial intelligence is monitoring the effectiveness of promotional activities and optimising
communication.
Four core functions of artificial intelligence in digital marketing can be identified:
• Supporting data analysis (Ahmed, Das, Smola, 2014; Sharakhina et al., 2023),
• Enabling process automation (Diwanji, Lee, Cortese, 2022),
• Facilitating content creation (Molares-Cardoso, Badenes-Plá, Maiz-Bar, 2024),
• Enhancing process optimisation (Wen, Lin, Guo, 2022).

The use of artificial intelligence not only streamlines marketing activities but also transforms
the overall approach to marketing decision-making. Data analysis is no longer used solely to
respond appropriately to past consumer decisions; it also enables the prediction of future
behaviour (Ma, Sun, 2020; Kumar et al., 2024). As a result, organisations can influence
consumer needs in real time, utilising predictive models and machine learning to optimise
their activities (Libai et al., 2020; Błażewicz, 2021). In this context, AI’s ability to integrate data
from numerous customer touchpoints is of key importance. This allows for the creation of
a coherent yet dynamic profile of the individual consumer, and this knowledge, in turn, forms
the basis for advanced personalisation of marketing communications (Błażewicz, 2021; Al
Prince et al., 2025).
A significant development in the field of personalisation is the growing role of artificial
intelligence in automating marketing communications and content creation. The automatic
generation of content significant ly increases the scale and pace of customer relationship
building. On the other hand, the effectiveness of such activities depends not only on the
quality of the AI software, but also on the human factor responsible for supervision,
interpretation, and ensuring that communication remains consistent with the brand identity
and cultural context (Raisch, Krakowski, 2021; Vlačić et al., 2021).
It should be emphasised, however, that the development of artificial intelligence and its
application in digital marketing presents significant ethical and legal challenges. In particular,
these relate to audience privacy and transparency (Kumar, Dayal, 2025; Benimma, Bennouna,
2026). A high level of personalisation requires access to large amounts of consumer data,
which increases the risk of privacy breaches and may negatively impact consumer trust
(Błażewicz, 2021; Al Prince et al., 2025). Furthermore, e xcessive automation can lead to
information overload and a decline in consumer goodwill. Another issue concerns algorithmic
bias, which may affect the fairness of promotional activities and the inclusivity of
communication (Akter et al., 2022; van Basshuysen, 2023).
Artificial intelligence in digital marketing should not be viewed merely as a technological tool,
but as part of a broader decision -making infrastructure, in which data resources, analytical
capabilities, and management and control mechanisms remain equally important (Shrestha et
al., 2019; Hicham, Nassera, Karim, 2023). Thus, the effectiveness of initiatives implemented
using artificial intelligence depends on the organisation’s resources, its ability to integrate IT
systems, its capacity for learning, and level of digital maturity.

Company Profile

authenticity, and courage. It considers fashion to be a form of expression and a means for the
wearer to convey their emotions (NAOKO Studio, 2026). NAOKO uses modern technology to
engage customers in the fashion design process, inviting them to co-create the brand. Its focus
is on combining creativity, technology, and a sustainable approach to clothing production

Overview

intelligence can be  fully realised when the technology support s human work, rather than
being treated as a substitute for it.
