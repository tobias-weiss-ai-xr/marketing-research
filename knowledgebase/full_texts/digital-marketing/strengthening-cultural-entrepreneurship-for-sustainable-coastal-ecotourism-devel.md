Abstract
Purpose: Coastal villages in Indonesia continue to depend on extractive fisheries, and ecotourism initiatives
introduced to diversify their livelihoods frequently stall after the initial intervention. This study examines how an
entrepreneurial culture rooted in loc al values and social capital is formed at community level, and how it
generates innovation in community -based coastal ecotourism.  Design and methods: A qualitative single -case
study was conducted in Tiku V Jorong Village, Tanjung Mutiara District. Agam Regency, West Sumatra. Twenty -
six informants such as traders, stall owners, river and seasonal fishermen, housewives, village officials and
plantation wo rkers were selected purposively and interviewed in depth between April and August 2025,
supported by participant observation and document analysis. Data were analysed thematically following the six-
phase procedure of Braun and Clarke, with source and metho d triangulation.  Findings: Four themes were
identified. Local wisdom like gotong royong, musyawarah, the authority of Ninik Mamak, and indigenous
ecological knowledge that operates as an informal institution rather than as heritage alone and regulates
economic conduct. Social capital mediates interest-free borrowing within kinship networks substitutes for absent
formal credit, and kinship channels carry market information at negligible cost. Cultural entrepreneurship then
reframes beaches, mangroves and seafood as tourism assets, producing product diversification and collaborative
services. These outcomes are, however, held back by limited capital, weak digital marketing capability,
inadequate tourism infrastructure and uneven policy support. Ritual practice, notably pre -voyage ceremonies,
has largely lapsed, while cooperative norms persist , indicating that the operative cultural resource is normative
rather than ceremonial. Originality: The study advances a process model in which local wisdom is the institutional
antecedent, social capital the mediating mechanism, cultural entrepreneurship the driving force, innovation the
immediate outcome, community -based ecotourism the development outc ome, and sustainable coastal
development the ultimate impact. The model offers policymakers an ordered sequence for intervention rather
than an undifferentiated list of success factors.

Introduction
The development of coastal villages in Indonesia is shaped by a combination of economic precarity, exposure to
climate variability, and persistent poverty among traditional fishing households (Adhuri & Visser, 2007; Versleijen
& Hoorweg, 2008). Development  models oriented towards resource extraction alone have repeatedly proved
unable to generate durable prosperity, since income remains tied to catch volumes that are themselves declining
(Busilacchi et al., 2013; Van Ginkel, 2007; Zamzami, 2024). Ecotourism  has emerged as a plausible alternative,
offering livelihood diversification, additional household income, and incentives for environmental and cultural
conservation (Clifton & Benson, 2006; Eriksson et al., 2019; Stem et al., 2003). Yet its potential rema ins largely
unrealised, because ecotourism is typically introduced as a discrete project rather than integrated with the
capacities, institutions and priorities of the community expected to operate it.

One condition that distinguishes ecotourism initiatives that endure from those that lapse is the presence of an
entrepreneurial culture at community level (Ratten et al., 2019; Zamzami & Effendi, 2023). Entrepreneurship in
this sense is not confined to the founding of enterprises; it denotes an innovative orientation, adaptive capacity,

and a willingness to bear risk in order to create value from local potential (Fritsch & Wyrwich, 2018). Coastal
villages possess considerable local knowledge and traditional skill, but these assets are rarely institutionalised in
a form that supports enter prise. Ecotourism initiatives therefore tend to operate in isolation from one another,
compete weakly, and innovate little, which makes it difficult for them to consolidate into a stable economic pillar
(Rojas-Sánchez et al., 2023; Zamzami, 2024).

Innovation is equally indispensable. It extends beyond new products or services to processes, business models
and social arrangements capable of resolving collective problems and opening new opportunities (Teixeira &
Ferreira, 2019). In ecotourism it may t ake the form of culturally distinctive tour packages, digital marketing, or
higher-value processed seafood. Without an entrepreneurial culture to absorb and sustain them, however, such
innovations rarely survive the withdrawal of external support. What is required is a model that connects the
strengthening of entrepreneurial culture to the innovation process explicitly, rather than assuming that one
follows from the other (Zamzami, 2024).

The literature on ecotourism and entrepreneurship has expanded rapidly, yet three limitations persist in the
coastal Indonesian context. First, studies of tourism entrepreneurship tend to isolate economic determinants
which is access to capital, marketing capability, training and give limited attention to how cultural institutions
shape entrepreneurial disposition in the first place (Nordin et al., 2014; Ratten et al., 2019; Rojas -Sánchez et al.,
2023). Second, research on coastal ecotourism concentrates on natural assets and their conservation, and rarely
traces how social and business innovation is generated within the community itself (Clifton & Benson, 2006; Stem
et al., 2003). Third, culture, entrepreneurship and innovation are generally examined as sep arate objects:
(Nurhayati et al., 2019) analyse the integration of conservation and community in marine ecotourism, while
(Kiper, 2013) addresses ecotourism strategy at the sectoral level. The consequence is that no framework specifies
the mechanism by whi ch local wisdom becomes entrepreneurial behaviour, and entrepreneurial behaviour
becomes innovation.

This study addresses that gap. Rather than combining the concepts, it seeks to explain the sequence connecting
them, and organises the analysis around three questions:
RQ1. What factors support and inhibit entrepreneurial activity among households in a coastal village of
West Sumatra?
RQ2. Through what mechanism do local wisdom and social capital shape entrepreneurial culture in that
setting?
RQ3. How does entrepreneurial culture generate innovation in community -based coastal ecotourism,
and under what conditions is that generation constrained?
Three contributions follow. First, local wisdom is reconceptualised as an informal institution rather than as
heritage: values such as gotong royong, mutual trust, the customary authority of Ninik Mamak, and indigenous
ecological knowledge reduce uncertain ty, enforce collective norms, and lower the cost of participation in
economic activity (Ostrom, 1990; Scott, 2014). Second, social capital is positioned as the mediating mechanism
rather than as one determinant among many: networks, reciprocity, trust and customary institutions actively
convert cultural values into collective entrepreneurial action (Prayitno et al., 2024; Putnam, 2000). Third, cultural
entrepreneurship is treated not as an outcome but as a driving force that produces innovation in tourism
products, local enterprise and community participation, which in turn strengthens community-based ecotourism
and sustains economic welfare, cultural continuity and ecosystem integrity.

The argument is developed through a single -case study of Tiku V Jorong Village, Tanjung Mutiara District, Agam
Regency. Indonesia's coastal villages differ markedly in their local institutions and resource endowments, so a
model of entrepreneurial culture is useful only if it is contextual and adaptive; the analysis is accordingly directed
at the mechanism rather than at generalisable magnitudes. The remainder of the article reviews the relevant
literature, sets out the research design, presents the four th emes derived from the data, discusses their
theoretical positioning, and concludes with implications and limitations.

Literature Review
Cultural entrepreneurship
Cultural entrepreneurship denotes the values, beliefs and behaviours through which cultural resources are
converted into economic opportunity. (Klamer, 2011) frames it as the capacity of individuals to realise cultural
value in economic form, while (Lounsbury & Glynn, 2001) emphasise the narrative work by which entrepreneurs
secure legitimacy and thereby access to resources. (Ratten et al., 2019) extend the concept to artisan and tourism

contexts, arguing that culturally embedded enterprise raises destination competitiveness through creativity and
differentiation. Common to these accounts is an individual unit of analysis: the entrepreneur mobilises cultural
material, and legitimacy is conferred by external audiences.

A second stream locates entrepreneurial culture at regional or community level. (Fritsch & Wyrwich, 2018) show
that regional entrepreneurial culture persists across long periods and correlates with subsequent start -up
activity, indicating that entrepreneurial disposition is a collective property that outlives particular firms. (Nordin
et al., 2014) similarly report that tourism enterprise among micro-entrepreneurs depends less on capital transfer
than on whether programmes cultivate entrepreneurial capabili ty. The implication for coastal villages is that
interventions supplying capital without addressing collective disposition are unlikely to produce durable
enterprise.

Social capital and local wisdom as institutional resources
Social capital comprises the networks, norms and trust that enable coordinated action (Putnam, 2000). Its
economic significance lies in the reduction of transaction costs: where trust is dense, agreements need less
formal enforcement, information circulate s without brokers, and credit can be extended without collateral.
(Ostrom, 1990) demonstrates that communities governing common -pool resources devise durable rules
precisely where such relational infrastructure exists, and (Scott, 2014) provides the broade r institutional
vocabulary as regulative, normative and cultural -cognitive pillars for treating customary norms as institutions
rather than as residues of tradition.

Empirical work in Indonesia supports this reading. (Prayitno et al., 2024) find that social capital underpins
sustainable tourism development across Indonesian villages, with trust and participation mediating the
relationship between programme design and o utcome. (Zielinski et al., 2020) show, from a synthesis of
community-based tourism cases in developing countries, that cohesion, leadership and prior collective
experience distinguish initiatives that succeed from those that fail more sharply than natural endowment does.
Local wisdom contributes a complementary asset: tested practical knowledge and a value framework that can
be embedded in product and service design, raising distinctiveness in markets where differentiation is otherwise
difficult.

Innovation and community-based coastal ecotourism
Coastal villages are exposed to climate variability and price volatility, so innovation in products, processes and
business models is a condition of sustainability rather than an optional improvement. (Teixeira & Ferreira, 2019)
show that artisan productio n functions as a competitiveness resource for regional tourism when it is organised
entrepreneurially. (Nurhayati et al., 2019) argue that sustainable marine ecotourism requires simultaneous
attention to conservation, community participation and economic r eturn, and (Butarbutar & Soemarno, 2013)
document the environmental consequences that follow when the conservation element is neglected in
Indonesian settings. (Sproule & Suhandi, 1998) established early on that community -based programmes in
Indonesia depend on local ownership of both decisions and revenue.

What this literature describes well is the configuration of conditions under which community -based ecotourism
performs. What it describes less well is the process by which a community arrives at that configuration and how
cultural resources become entrepreneurial behaviour, and how entrepreneurial behaviour becomes innovation.
(Zielinski et al., 2020) note this directly, observing that inventories of success factors offer limited guidance on
sequencing. The present study takes that process as its object.

Analytical framework
Drawing these strands together, the study adopts a process framework in which local wisdom functions as an
institutional antecedent, social capital as a mediating mechanism, cultural entrepreneurship as a driving force,
and innovation as the immediate outc ome that strengthens community -based ecotourism and, ultimately,
sustainable coastal development. Structural conditions such as capital, digital capability, infrastructure and policy
are treated not as parallel causes but as constraints that modulate the r ate at which each transition occurs. The
framework is stated here as an orienting device; its empirically grounded form is presented in Section 5.2.

Materials and Methods
Research design and case selection

The study employs a qualitative single-case design (Creswell & Poth, 2018; Yin, 2018). A case study is appropriate
because the object of inquiry is a contemporary social process that cannot be separated from its context, and
because the research questions are of the how and why type for which case -based inference is suited. The unit
of analysis is the village as an entrepreneurial community, not the individual enterprise.

Tiku V Jorong Village was selected on three criteria: it combines fishing, trade, plantation labour and nascent
beach tourism in a single settlement, so that the interaction between livelihood diversification and tourism is
observable; Minangkabau customar y institutions, including the authority of Ninik Mamak and customary land
tenure, remain operative in economic decisions; and its beach, lagoon and mangrove areas give it realistic
ecotourism potential that is as yet only partially developed. The case is t herefore revelatory rather than typical:
it exhibits the mechanism of interest in an unusually legible form.

Research site
Tiku V Jorong Village lies in Tanjung Mutiara District, Agam Regency, West Sumatra Province, on the western
coast of Sumatra facing the Indian Ocean. The settlement is arranged along the shoreline and the river mouth,
with oil-palm plantations inland and mangrove stands to the south. Land use, principal facilities and the tourism-
relevant features of the village are shown in Figure 1.

Data collection
Fieldwork was conducted between April and August 2025. Three instruments were used. In -depth interviews,
lasting between 45 and 90 minutes, were held at informants' homes or places of business and followed a semi -
structured guide covering enterprise histor y, sources of capital, marketing practice, the role of custom and
customary leaders, perceptions of tourism, and perceived barriers. Interviews were conducted in Minangkabau
and Indonesian, audio -recorded with permission, and transcribed verbatim; excerpts  reported below were
translated into English by the authors and checked against the source transcript. Participant observation covered
market days, landing and processing activity, beach-front trading, and village deliberations, and was recorded in
field notes. Document analysis drew on village development plans, village budget documents and district tourism
records to corroborate accounts of institutional support.

Data analysis
Data were analysed thematically using the six -phase procedure of (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Familiarisation
involved repeated reading of transcripts alongside field notes. Initial coding proceeded inductively, generating
first-order codes expressed as far as possible in informants' own terms. Codes were then collated into second -
order categories, and categories into candidate themes, which were reviewed against the coded extracts and the
full data set, defined, and named. Two authors coded independently and re conciled differences through
discussion; coding was managed manually with a shared codebook rather than with software, given the corpus
size. The resulting structure is displayed in Figure 2.

Trustworthiness
Credibility was addressed through source triangulation, comparing accounts across fishermen, traders,
housewives, customary leaders, village officials and tourism operators, and through methodological triangulation
across interviews, observation and docume nts (Lincoln & Guba, 1985; Nowell et al., 2017). Preliminary
interpretations were returned to six informants for comment, and two discrepancies concerning the current
status of pre-voyage ritual were resolved by further interviewing. Dependability was supp orted by an audit trail
comprising the interview guide, transcripts, codebook and analytic memos. Transferability is supported by thick
description of the setting; as with any single case, statistical generalisation is not claimed, and the model
presented in Section 5.2 is offered as an analytic proposition to be tested elsewhere.

Research ethics
Participation was voluntary and informed. The purpose of the research, the intended use of the data, and the
right to withdraw were explained to each informant before the interview, and oral consent was recorded; oral
rather than written consent was used b ecause several informants had limited literacy. Permission to conduct
fieldwork was obtained from the village administration and from customary leaders. Informants are identified in
this article only by gender, occupation and age, and no personally identif ying details are reported.

Results
Local wisdom as the institutional foundation of entrepreneurship
Local wisdom in Tiku V Jorong operates as more than a marker of identity. Values such as gotong royong,
collective responsibility, indigenous ecological knowledge and the leadership of Ninik Mamak constitute a
normative environment in which cooperation is expected rather than negotiated, and in which economic
decisions are subject to deliberation. Entrepreneurs do not build businesses in isolation from these
arrangements; they build them through reciprocal relationships that customary institutions define an d enforce.
Informants described assistance to new enterprises as an obligation rather than a favour:
“If someone wants to start a business, neighbours usually help without asking for payment. We believe
that if one family succeeds, the village will also benefit.”
(Male, fisherman, 54 years)
“Ninik Mamak always encourages us to work together. When there is a problem about land or business,
we discuss it first before making decisions.”
(Village elder, 68 years)
Customary authority extends into the material conditions of enterprise. Ninik Mamak and Datuk Mandaro
allocate customary land for business use, mediate disputes between traders, provide advice and occasionally
personal capital, and prohibit interest-bearing lending within the village. The last of these has a direct economic
effect: by closing off usurious credit, customary authority channels borrowing into kinship networks on interest -
free terms, which is the arrangement described in Section 4.2.
A significant qualification emerged. Most informants reported that ritual practice has largely lapsed: ceremonies
formerly performed before a boat put to sea, and rites associated with warding off misfortune, are no longer
routinely observed. Cooperative n orms, however, remain robust in-house building, in weddings, and in joint
activity at sea. The cultural resource that supports enterprise in this village is therefore normative and
organisational rather than ceremonial, and this distinction matters for policy, since programmes that seek to
revive ritual as a tourism attraction address a different asset from the one that actually sustains local enterprise.
Gender structures participation. Men predominate in fishing and plantation work; women predominate in trade,
stall keeping and home processing. Sixteen of twenty-six informants were women, and the enterprises described
most frequently, such AS seafood processing, beach -front stalls, and daily necessities, are principally operated
by women, which suggests that the entrepreneurial layer of the village economy is substantially female even
where the fishing economy is not.

Social capital as the mediating mechanism
Social capital is the mechanism through which the normative environment described above becomes economic
action. Three channels were evident in the data.  First, trust substitutes for collateral. Informants consistently
described obtaining start -up capital from relatives without interest or written agreement, in a setting where
formal credit is limited to a small number of BMT (Islamic microfinance) faciliti es and government capital
assistance is minimal and unevenly distributed:
“I borrowed money from my older brother without interest. Without family support, I could not start
selling seafood.”
(Female trader, 43 years)

Second, kinship networks carry market information. Traders and food sellers exchange observations about the

“We usually tell each other where tourists come from and what products they like.”

Third, reciprocal norms reduce the cost of labour. Informants described sharing labour in processing seafood,
preparing for market days and maintaining stalls, on the understanding that the assistance will be returned.
Word-of-mouth promotion through family and neighbourhood ties extends customer reach at no monetary cost.
The cumulative effect is that entry into enterprise is possible for households with negligible financial resources.
Capital, information and labour, the three inputs that formal institutions supply expensively or not at all in this
setting which are supplied instead through relational channels. Entrepreneurship in Tiku V Jorong is consequently
collective in its foundations even where the enterprise itself is individually owned.

Cultural entrepreneurship and ecotourism innovation
Entrepreneurial culture manifests first as a change in how coastal resources are perceived. Marine products are
increasingly treated not only as commodities for subsistence and sale but as inputs to tourism experiences;
beaches, the lagoon and mangrove sta nds are treated not only as fishing ground and buffer but as attractions.
This reframing is the point at which cultural entrepreneurship becomes visible in the data.

The innovations that follow are modest in scale but consistent in direction. Informants described diversifying
processed seafood beyond salted fish; establishing culinary businesses oriented to visitors rather than to
residents; developing beach -front facilities and services; and, in a small number of cases, promoting products
through social media. Lokan (estuarine shellfish) illustrates the pattern: a subsistence and local -market product,
sought by buyers from Lubuk Basung and Padang Pariaman, it is increasingly presented as a district speciality and
a reason to visit, which raises its value without requiring additional extraction.  Collaboration is characteristic of
these initiatives. Tourism services are frequently provided jointly with stall keepers coordinating with boat
owners, food sellers sharing preparation space or rather than through competing individual ventures. This
pattern is consistent with the collective foundation described in Section 4.2 and distinguishes the setting from
entrepreneurship models premised on individual opportunity capture.

Discussion

Theoretical positioning
The findings both support and qualify existing accounts of cultural entrepreneurship. (Ratten et al., 2019) argue
that culturally embedded enterprise raises tourism competitiveness through creativity and innovation, and the
innovations documented in Sectio n 4.3 are consistent with that claim. Their analysis, however, locates cultural
entrepreneurship in individual entrepreneurs and creative industries. In Tiku V Jorong the entrepreneurial act is
rarely individual: it is authorised by customary institutions,  financed through kinship, and executed
collaboratively. Innovation is initiated less by individual creativity than by culturally sanctioned collective action,
which extends the cultural entrepreneurship perspective towards informal institutions in traditi onal fishing
communities.

The findings depart more sharply from (Klamer, 2011; Lounsbury & Glynn, 2001). Both accounts locate the
generative act in the individual to the transformation of cultural assets into economic value, or the narrative
construction of legitimacy before extern al audiences. In this setting, economic value arises less from individual
transformation or storytelling than from the mobilisation of community -based social capital, and legitimacy is
conferred internally by customary authority rather than externally by i nvestors or markets. Social capital is
therefore not a supporting condition but the central mechanism connecting culture to enterprise.

In relation to ecotourism, the findings are broadly consistent with (Nurhayati et al., 2019), who show that
sustainable marine ecotourism requires the integration of conservation, participation and economic return, and
with (Zielinski et al., 2020), who id entify cohesion and leadership as decisive conditions in developing -country
cases. The present study refines both by specifying sequence: innovation is preceded by the formation of cultural
entrepreneurship on a base of local wisdom and social capital, so that ecotourism outcomes are the terminus of
a socio-cultural process rather than the direct product of managerial integration. It likewise extends (Zamzami &
Effendi, 2023), who established the relationship between cultural values and entrepreneurial atti tudes among
West Sumatran fishermen, by treating entrepreneurial attitude as an intermediate state rather than as the
outcome of interest. Table 3 sets out this positioning.

A process model of cultural entrepreneurship in coastal ecotourism
The four themes can be assembled into the process model shown in Figure 3. Local wisdom is the institutional
antecedent: customary values and indigenous ecological knowledge establish the norms that make cooperation
an obligation and deliberation a default . Social capital is the mediating mechanism: trust, kinship networks,
reciprocity and customary authority convert those norms into interest -free finance, low -cost information and
shared labour. Cultural entrepreneurship is the driving force: opportunity re cognition, collective initiative and
community commitment reframe coastal resources as tourism assets. Community innovation is the immediate
outcome: seafood diversification, culturally grounded tour offerings and emerging digital promotion.
Community-based coastal ecotourism is the development outcome, and sustainable coastal development to
economic welfare, social resilience, cultural continuity and ecosystem integrity as the ultimate impact.

Two features of the model warrant emphasis. First, structural constraints are represented as moderators rather
than as parallel causes. Limited capital, weak digital capability, inadequate infrastructure and uneven policy
support do not halt the process; t hey slow the transitions from social capital to entrepreneurship, and from
entrepreneurship to innovation. This distinction has practical consequences: because the constraint is on rate
rather than on direction, targeted support at these transitions should  yield disproportionate returns.

Second, the model includes a feedback path. Successful community -based enterprise reinforces the collective
norms that produced it, since visible returns to cooperation strengthen the expectation of cooperation. This
closes the loop between outcome and ant ecedent and helps explain why entrepreneurial culture, once
established, exhibits the persistence that (Fritsch & Wyrwich, 2018) document at regional scale.

Practical implications
Three implications follow for village government, district tourism agencies and supporting institutions. First,
sequencing matters: programmes that supply capital or facilities before collective norms and customary
endorsement are in place forgo the mechan ism that makes them productive, whereas in villages where that
mechanism is already present the appropriate intervention is the direct provision of missing structural inputs.

Conclusions
This study set out to explain how entrepreneurial culture is formed in a coastal village and how it generates
innovation in community-based ecotourism. Drawing on twenty-six in-depth interviews, participant observation
and document analysis in Tiku V Jorong Village, West Sumatra, it finds that local wisdom such as gotong royong,
musyawarah, indigenous ecological knowledge and the authority of Ninik Mamak was functions as an informal
institution that regulates economic conduct, and that social capital conver ts this normative resource into
interest-free finance, low-cost market information and shared labour. Cultural entrepreneurship formed on this
base reframes coastal resources as tourism assets and produces innovation in seafood products, culinary and
beach services, and emerging digital promotion, which in turn strengthens community-based coastal ecotourism.
In answer to RQ1, supporting factors are predominantly socio-cultural and locational, while inhibiting factors are
predominantly structural. In answer to RQ2, social capital is the mediating mechanism, operating through trust
that substitutes for collatera l, kinship networks that carry market information, and reciprocal norms that lower
labour costs. In answer to RQ3, entrepreneurial culture generates innovation by changing the perceived function
of coastal resources, and this generation is constrained in rate rather than in direction which is by limited capital,
weak digital capability, inadequate tourism infrastructure and uneven policy support.

The principal theoretical contribution is a process model that positions local wisdom as institutional antecedent,
social capital as mediating mechanism, cultural entrepreneurship as driving force, innovation as immediate
outcome, community -based ecotouris m as development outcome, and sustainable coastal development as
ultimate impact, with structural conditions as moderators and a feedback path from outcome to norm. Where
prior research has catalogued the conditions under which community -based tourism succ eeds, this study
specifies the order in which they operate. A secondary finding of practical importance is that ceremonial practice
has largely lapsed while cooperative norms persist, indicating that the cultural asset supporting enterprise is
normative rather than ritual.

Three limitations qualify these conclusions. The study examines a single village, so the model is offered as an
analytic proposition rather than as a generalisable finding, and its transferability to coastal communities with
weaker customary institutions, or with matrilineal arrangements different from those of Minangkabau society as
remains to be established. The evidence is cross -sectional, so the sequence proposed in the model is inferred
from informants' accounts of change rather than observed longitudi nally. And the innovations documented are
at an early stage, so their durability and their economic magnitude cannot yet be assessed.

Three directions for further research follow. Comparative case studies across coastal villages with contrasting
customary institutions would test whether the sequence holds where local wisdom is weaker. Longitudinal
fieldwork would allow the transitions to  be observed rather than reconstructed. And a quantitative study
operationalising the constructs of the model local wisdom, social capital, entrepreneurial culture and innovation
as across a sample of coastal villages would permit the mediating role of soc ial capital to be tested directly.
