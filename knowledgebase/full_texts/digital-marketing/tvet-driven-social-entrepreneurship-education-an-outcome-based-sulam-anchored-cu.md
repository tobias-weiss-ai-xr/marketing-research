Abstract
Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) programmes are under mounting
pressure to prove that graduates leave with more than a transcript; they need to leave with
community-tested competence. Social entrepreneurship modules are one of the few  places
in a technopreneurship  degree where that proof can actually be generated, yet published
accounts rarely show, in enough operational detail, how a TVET-based course turns a stack of
curriculum documents into hours of real fieldwork with vulnerable communities. This paper
works t hrough that gap using a single, closely documented case: BTMT 3383 Social
Entrepreneurship, a compulsory course inside the Bachelor of Technopreneurship with
Honours (BTEC) programme at a Malaysian technical university. Drawing on the institution's
official Summary of Course Information, its approved Service-Learning Malaysia-University for

Introduction
Ask a TVET graduate what they actually did in their final year, and the honest answer is often
"assignments." Social Entrepreneurship and Social Innovation (SESI) education was meant to
change that answer: students were supposed to build ventures that balance financial survival
against measurable social or environmental impact, not just read case studies about someone
else doing it (Roslan et al., 2022; Portales, 2019; Weaver, 2022). Universities are under
pressure to show graduate readiness for real proble ms, and they have leaned on social
entrepreneurship courses as a vehicle for smuggling the Sustainable Development Goals
(SDGs), Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) thinking, and circular -economy logic
into otherwise conventional business curricula  (Roslan et al., 2022; Magraner et al., 2025).
However, lecture-based delivery alone rarely produces the empathy, ethical judgement, or
stakeholder-management stamina that social entrepreneurship in practice actually demands
(Ministry of Education Malaysia, 2019; Hallinger & Kon gpiwatana Narong, 2024). That
shortfall is what has pushed curriculum designers toward outcome -based structures paired
with sustained, community-embedded learning instead of classroom simulation.

Malaysia has its own mechanism for forcing this pairing: the Service -Learning Malaysia -
University for Society (SULAM) policy, which requires public and private higher education
institutions to build sustained, credit -bearing community engagement into selec ted courses
(Ministry of Education Malaysia, 2019). Evaluations of SULAM in practice are admittedly a
mixed bag. They generally report gains in experiential learning, civic attitude, and knowledge
transfer to community partners, but they also flag resourci ng and sustainability headaches
that fall squarely on the lecturer running the course (Corrêa et al., 2022; González García et
al., 2020; Sharipah Ruzaina et al., 2025). What most of these evaluations do not do is show
SULAM fieldwork wired into the actual mechanics of a course, including its Student Learning
Time allocation, its weekly teaching plan, and its Table of Test Specifications. It  tends to sit
alongside the course as an add-on activity instead.

That gap matters more, not less, in TVET settings, where the entire premise of the
qualification is that competence gets demonstrated, not merely described. TVET systems
worldwide are being asked to widen their notion of competence beyond employer -facing
technical skill to include graduate attributes, adaptability, ethical reasoning, and digital
fluency, none of which was ever designed to be certified by a purely occupational curriculum
(Halik Bassah et al., 2025; Zahedi et al., 2023; Yusop et al., 2022). Malaysian TVET research in
particular has begun documenting how technical and vocational learners experience
entrepreneurship education, and it often finds that students value the hands-on components
far more than the theory components. Digital -economy competencies are also becoming as
important to TVET employability as the traditional trade skill itself (Zahedi et al., 2023; Rosdi
& Adnan, 2025). A social entrepreneurship course sitting inside a Bachelor of
Technopreneurship programme is, in that sense, a useful test case: it shows whether TVET
curricula can operationalise "soft" graduate outcomes with the same rigour they app ly to
technical ones.

Outcome-based education (OBE) theory supplies the underlying grammar for that rigour.
Curriculum content, teaching method, and assessment instrument should all trace back,
explicitly and checkably, to a small set of intended learning outcomes across Knowledge, Skill,
and Attitude (KSA) domains (Gruppen, 2012). Inside TVET systems specifically, competency -
based reform has been shown to sharpen the transparency of assessment once learning
outcomes are mapped against teaching methods, mark weightings, and the cognitive-process
levels of Bloom's Revised Taxonomy (Yusop et al., 2022; Magraner  et al., 2025). What case
studies in this space rarely disclose, though, is the underlying arithmetic: how Student
Learning Time (SLT) hours actually convert into percentage weightings, and how a Table of
Test Specifications gets built to balance lower -order and higher -order cognitive demand
across an examination.

This paper works through that arithmetic using BTMT 3383 Social Entrepreneurship, a
compulsory course in the Bachelor of Technopreneurship with Honours (BTEC) programme at
Universiti Teknikal Malaysia Melaka (UTeM). The course was chosen because its paper trail is
unusually complete: an approved Summary of Course Information, a Curriculum Design Plan,
a fourteen-week Teaching Plan, a full assessment package, and a Table of Test Specifications.
Newly available for this analysis is the official approval working paper for its Semester 2,
Session 2025/2026 SULAM component, which lays out the programme's  exact budget,
beneficiary counts, and delivery model. Together these documents make it possible to trace,
without guesswork, how intended learning outcomes become enacted teaching, continuous
assessment, and a final examination. The paper also looks close ly at the course's flagship
performance task, the Social Business Model Canvas (S -BMC) paired with a SULAM
Implementation Journal, which serves as a worked example of authentic assessment design
for social entrepreneurship competencies within a TVET degree.

Significance of the Study
This study matters because the stakes attached to TVET credentials in Malaysia are no longer
abstract. National figures put TVET graduate employability at 95.1 percent, with more than
430,000 students enrolled nationwide and 53.56 percent of 2024 secondary -school leavers
choosing a TVET pathway as their first option (Bernama, 2025). That headline number sits
alongside a persistent skills-mismatch problem: national labour-force data show 2.06 million

The study is significant for four distinct audiences, each of whom stands to gain something
specific and usable from it. For curriculum committees and quality -assurance panels, the
paper supplies a template for demonstrating constructive alignment with aud itable
arithmetic instead of narrative assertion. This is precisely the kind of evidence accreditation
review increasingly demands (Derouich, 2025). For TVET and technopreneurship  lecturers
designing their own service-learning components, it offers a worked example of how Student
Learning Time can be converted into percentage weighting and how a Table of Test
Specifications can be built around Bloom's Revised Taxonomy. This removes  much of the

themselves, including the disability and special -needs sites documented in this case, the
paper makes visible a mechanism by which their inclusion in higher -education service -

how consistently such partnerships are resourced and sustained. For policymakers overseeing
SULAM and similar service -learning mandates nationally,  the case offers a rare worked
example of how a compliance requirement can be embedded into a course's actual grading
architecture, rather than layered on top of it as a reporting obligation.

The need for this specific inquiry follows from a gap the wider literature has left open. Existing
scholarship on TVET employability and social entrepreneurship education establishes, at a
policy level, that experiential and community -embedded learning improves outcomes (Halik
Bassah et al., 2025; Al Issa et al., 2025). What that literature has not supplied is the operational
detail a lecturer, curriculum designer, or accreditation panel could actually replicate: the exact
arithmetic connecting budget, beneficiary count, contact hours, and grade weighting. Absent
that detail, calls to adopt service -learning risk remaining aspirational. This paper addresses
that need directly by documenting one course's complete design record rather than
summarising its outcomes from a distance.

Literature Review
Social Entrepreneurship and Social Innovation in Higher Education
Social entrepreneurship education asks learners to do three things at once. They must
diagnose a genuine community problem and design a venture that holds People, Planet, and
Profit in some kind of balance, and they must then convince sceptical stakeholder s the idea
will actually work (Roslan et al., 2022; Magraner et al., 2025; Portales, 2019; Weaver, 2022).
Portales (2019) treats social innovation and social entrepreneurship as two sides of the same
coin: the novel idea sits on one side, and the organisational vehicle, non-profit, hybrid, or
socially responsible for-profit, that carries it forward sits on the other. Weaver (2022) pushes
this further, arguing that curricula which only teach the non -profit model or only teach the
for-profit model shortchange students. Real practitioners, Weaver (2022) notes, move fluidly
across the whole spectrum of social business forms. A foundational critical review by Peredo
and McLean (2006) traces how the concept of social entrepreneurship itself has been
stretched across disciplines and definitions. Curricula that skip a clear conceptual grounding,
they argue, end up teaching a moving target instead of a coherent field, which is a large part
of why the curriculum examined here starts from an explicit conceptual framework bef ore it
moves to practice. A curriculum analysis of university -based social innovation and
entrepreneurship education in Hong Kong by Wang and Horta (2025) reaches a comparable
conclusion from a different higher -education system: it finds that programmes anchored in
authentic, university-community partnerships produce more coherent curricula than those
where social entrepreneurship content is scattered across unrelated electives. Two more
recent studies sharpen this picture further. Al Issa, Thai, and Nguyen (2024), mapping the field
systematically, call for tighter research frameworks and clearer ethical grounding in how
social entrepreneurship education is taught and evaluated. Al Issa, Thai, and Saad (2025) show
that experiential, field -based learning s trengthens students' social -entrepreneurial self -
efficacy far more reliably than classroom instruction alone. Shahid and Alarifi (2021)
corroborate this finding in their own conceptual review of the field.

TVET, Employability, and the Case for Community-Embedded Learning
TVET research has long wrestled with a credibility problem. Employers say they want
graduates with initiative, communication, and problem -solving skills, but conventional TVET
assessment is built to certify technical competence, not those broader attribute s. A
systematic review by Halik Bassah, Mohd Noor, and Ahmad (2025) concludes that Malaysian
TVET providers still lack a consistent, holistic mechanism for developing and verifying the
employability skills employers actually ask for. This gap persists desp ite years of policy
language calling for exactly that. A related review of the Malaysian TVET sustainability
framework, using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta -Analyses
(PRISMA) protocol, by Che Rus and colleagues (2023) reaches  a similar verdict: it finds that
graduate marketability still hinges on employability attributes that current TVET frameworks
describe in policy but rarely operationalise into assessable curriculum design. Zahedi, Jaffer,
Bryant, and Bada (2023) examine a service -learning programme in India. They find that
sustained community placement measurably strengthens students' civic engagement and
sense of connection to the community they serve. This pattern maps neatly onto why a

Service-Learning and the SULAM Framework in Malaysian Higher Education
SULAM was introduced by Malaysia's Ministry of Higher Education as a structured way of
forcing sustained, credit -bearing community engagement into existing courses; the
alternative, leaving it to extracurricular goodwill, was judged too unreliable (Ministr y of
Education Malaysia, 2019). Corrêa, Brito, Lima, and Queiroz (2022) synthesise  a decade of
research on female entrepreneurship in emerging and developing economies. They find that
women entrepreneurs consistently face compounded structural barriers, particularly
financing, networks, and skills gaps. A SULAM curriculum that wants to serve women -led
micro-enterprises well needs to design for those specific constraints; a one -size-fits-all
community-engagement format simply will not do. González García, Colomo Magaña, and
Cívico Ariza (2020) offer a bibliometric analysis of SDG 4 (Quality Education) research within
the 2030 Agenda. They show that quality -education scholarship increasingly foregrounds
community-linked, experiential models of delivery over classroom -only instructio n, which
lends broader empirical weight to framing SULAM implementation as a direct institutional
contribution toward that same goal. Sharipah Ruzaina  and colleagues (2025), working from
the lecturer's side of the desk, find that the long -term durability of SULAM projects depends
heavily on institutional support and the stability of community partnerships. This finding has
obvious relevance to a course running multi-site attachments across successive semesters.
Outside Malaysia, Compare and Albanesi (2023) review service -learning literature more
broadly. They find that programmes  genuinely built around social -justice engagement
produce measurably stronger shifts in student belief and critical understanding than a token
community visit ever could, which reinforces the case for depth over breadth in fieldwork
design. A broader syste matic review of service -learning in higher education by Salam and
colleagues (2019) situates these Malaysian findings within a wider evidence base. It concludes
that the strongest programmes  consistently pair structured community placement with
credit-bearing assessment instead of leaving engagement voluntary.

Outcome-Based Education and Competency-Based Curriculum Design
The basic requirement of outcome -based education is easy to state and hard to actually
deliver. Curriculum content, teaching method, and assessment instrument all have to trace
back explicitly to a defined set of intended learning outcomes (Gruppen, 2012).  Gruppen
(2012) makes this point from medical education, but it travels well beyond that field. Gruppen
(2012) argues that outcome -based curricula shift the whole locus of design from covering
content to certifying competency. Assessment instruments then h ave to test authentic
performance, and declarative recall alone will not do. Applied to sustainability specifically,
Magraner, Gil -Garcia, and Fernandez -Guillamon (2025) propose a tool for mapping SDGs,
competencies, and learning outcomes together, so that sustainability content stops feeling

bolted onto an otherwise unrelated curriculum. Dutta, Moganti , and Kundu (2026) echo this
caution in their broader review of how higher -education institutions handle the Sustainable
Development Goals: SDG content works best folded into existing learning outcomes, not
added as a stand -alone module. There is also a re sourcing angle here. Goyal, Gupta, and
Gupta (2022) use a fuzzy -inference meta -analytic approach and find that project -based
learning outcomes correlate positively with programme -level outcome attainment. This
correlation mainly holds when assessment weighting is proportionate to the instructional
time actually invested in a competency. That is the same principle this paper's Student -
Learning-Time-derived weighting returns to late r. Derouich (2025) takes a slightly different
angle, arguing that outcome-based curricula only really hold together when Course Learning
Outcomes and Programme Learning Outcomes stay aligned through iterative feedback loops.
A one-off mapping exercise done at curriculum -approval stage and then forgotten will not
sustain that alignment. And on the assessment side, Shvets and colleagues (2026) show that
tagging as sessment questions explicitly against Bloom's Revised Taxonomy levels supports
more targeted, level-appropriate feedback than an untagged question bank ever could, much
as this course's Table of Test Specifications does.

Authentic and Reflective Assessment in Competency-Based Training
Sluijsmans, Straetmans, and van Merrienboer (2008) were among the earlier voices arguing
that vocational education needs assessment instruments capable of scoring authentic
performance across an entire competency portfolio, not just a single graded artefac t. This
protocol-portfolio approach anticipates much of what current TVET competency frameworks
now require. More recent systematic work by Huerta Rosales, Taramona Ruiz, and Camones
Bazan (2026) confirms that authentic assessment strategies in competency -based training
remain unevenly implemented. Reflective and portfolio -style instruments are still the
exception in many programmes despite strong evidence of their value. Reflective w riting
specifically has its own evidence base: Alt, Raichel, and Naamati -Schneider (2022) find that
structured reflective journaling meaningfully strengthens lifelong -learning skills among
higher-education students. This finding is directly relevant to the implementation -journal
component examined later in this paper.

Digital Marketing, Community Knowledge Transfer, and Inclusive Beneficiary Groups
A recurring theme in Malaysian social-enterprise and rural-entrepreneurship literature is that
beneficiary groups, including rural women, persons with disabilities, and special -needs
learners, are usually the ones with the least access to the digital marke ting skills that would
actually grow their income. Aggarwal and Johal (2021) offer a systematic review of rural
women's entrepreneurship. They find that basic digital and marketing literacy gaps, not lack
of product quality, are consistently the binding co nstraint on rural women entrepreneurs'
income growth. Noorhaslinda Kulub Abd Rashid and colleagues (2024), studying a Terengganu
fishing community, show that structured knowledge -transfer programmes from universities
can measurably improve community-level social innovation outcomes, provided the transfer
is s ustained rather than a single workshop. Mastam and Zaharudin  (2024) turn attention
specifically to learners with disabilities inside vocational special -education schools. They
propose a conceptual framework for work-readiness skills that stresses hands-on, mentored
exposure; classroom instruction on its own, in their framing, does not get learners there. This
matches closely with how the present case study's special -needs community sites were
approached. This concern is echoed at policy level: a recent International Labour Organization

The Social Business Model Canvas as an Authentic Assessment Instrument
The Social Business Model Canvas (S-BMC) adapts the commercial Business Model Canvas. It
explicitly folds in beneficiary segments and social -impact metrics alongside the usual value -
proposition and revenue -stream boxes (Sparviero, 2019). Sparviero  (2019) argues that a
socially oriented canvas format resolves what he calls the mission -measurement paradox.
Beneficiary segments and impact metrics become explicit design boxes, not afterthoughts,
and the tool forces entrepreneurs to keep reconciling com mercial viability against social
mission as new stakeholder information comes in. Feor, Clarke, and Dougherty (2023) review
social-impact measurement approaches more broadly. They note a persistent problem with
static planning artefacts: these capture intent well but rarely capture whether anything was
actually tested against reality. Pairing the canvas with a sustained implementation journal, as
examined later in this paper, is one direct response to that limitation. It converts a desk-based
planning exercise into a longitudinal record of iteration.

Methodology
Research Design
This study is a single -case, document-based curriculum analysis. It treats BTMT 3383 Social
Entrepreneurship as an information-rich, bounded case of outcome-based curriculum design
integrated with TVET -anchored service -learning pedagogy. The unit of analys is is the
complete curriculum documentation package for Semester 2, Session 2025/2026, made up of
five primary artefacts. The first is the official Summary of Course Information, approved by
the Faculty of Technology Management and Technopreneurship  (FPTT). The second is the
Curriculum Design Plan, specifying the course's core competency, work and learning activities,
and KSA domains. The third is the fourteen -week Teaching Plan, mapping weekly topics to
Course Learning Outcomes, teaching methods, an d learning activities. The fourth is the full
assessment package. It comprises the weekly competency assessment plan, the course
assessment plan, the justification tables for assessment method and percentage weighting,
and the Table of Test Specifications for the Final Examination. The fifth is the official SULAM
programme approval working paper,which documents the programme's approved budget,
community-site allocation, beneficiary counts, and implementation committee.

Curriculum Analysis Procedure
The analysis itself unfolded in five stages, though not always in a straight line. It started with
the three Course Learning Outcomes (CLOs), which were pulled out and classified by KSA
domain. These were then mapped against Programme  Learning Outcomes (PLOs) and the
relevant Malaysian Qualifications Framework (MQF) learning -outcome clusters. From there,
the fourteen -week Teaching Plan was coded week by week, noting which CLO each week
addressed, which teaching method was used, and wha t the students were actually asked to
do. This essentially traced the course's pedagogical arc from environmental scanning through
venture design to the fieldwork weeks and the final storytelling pitch. The weekly competency
assessment plan and course asse ssment plan were then cross -tabulated so the percentage
weighting attached to each CLO could be quantified. This also showed how much of the final
grade sits in knowledge-based versus performance-based work, and in group versus individual
submissions. Next, the Table of Test Specifications for both examination sections was broken
down to see how marks distribute across the six cognitive processes of Bloom's Revised
Taxonomy and the four knowledge dimensions. Finally, the SULAM approval working paper
was checked against the teaching plan itself. This was mainly to confirm that the approved
scope, budget, and beneficiary profile actually match what the curriculum documents claim.

Findings
The four analytical themes developed below are read through two complementary

inclusion focus is deliberate, not acci dental, and an assessment design whose authenticity is
built in, not merely claimed.

Discussion
Constructive Alignment: Coherence as a Design Achievement, Not a Default
The alignment coherence documented in Section 4.1 deserves interrogation, not just
confirmation. Constructive alignment is frequently invoked in curriculum papers as a checklist
item and rarely demonstrated as an achieved property of a working system (Grup pen, 2012;
Biggs, 1996). Biggs's original formulation is explicit that alignment fails whenever any one of
the three elements, outcome, activity, or assessment, is designed in isolation from the other
two. That is exactly the failure mode the wider literat ure keeps documenting in TVET and
social-entrepreneurship curricula. One example is Programme Learning Outcomes stated at
approval stage but never operationalised into assessment weighting (Che Rus et al., 2023;
Halik Bassah et al., 2025). Another is sustainability content folded on top of an unrelated
syllabus instead of integrated into it (Magraner  et al., 2025; Dutta et al., 2026). What
distinguishes the present case is not that alignment was intended, since most curricula intend
it, but that it is auditable. The CLO -to-PLO mapping in Table 1, the SLT -derived weighting in
Table 2, and the cognitive -process blueprint in Table 4 are all traceable to the same three -
way split, and there is no single point where a design layer breaks from the outcomes above
it. That auditability is the practical difference between alignment as rhetoric and alignment
as a n engineering property of a curriculum. It is also the reason the finding in 4.1 travels
beyond this one course. Any programme that wants to claim constructive alignment should
be able to produce the equivalent of Tables 1, 2, and 4, rather than simply asserting that its
assessment "maps to" its outcomes.

Experiential Sequencing and the Limits of a Linear Reading of Kolb
The fourteen-week structure documented in this course, three weeks of CLO1 theory, four
weeks of CLO2 prototyping, and seven weeks of CLO3 fieldwork, maps at first glance onto
Kolb's experiential learning cycle. That cycle comprises concrete experience, re flective
observation, abstract conceptualisation, and active experimentation, cycling students from
theory through structured practice into unsupervised real-world judgement (Tomkins & Ulus,
2016). Read that way, the design simply operationalises a well-established model, and the fit
is genuinely close. But a more critical reading, consistent with recent re -examinations of
Kolb's cycle, complicates that fit, and the complication is worth stating plainly. Kolb's model
is a cycle, not a sequence: it assumes repeated, iterative passes through all four modes, not a
single linear pass from classroom exposure to graded fieldwork (Tomkins & Ulus, 2016). The
fourteen-week plan analysed here largely delivers a single pass instead. Theory runs in Weeks
1 to 3, structured practice in Weeks 4 to 7, and unsupervised fieldwork in Weeks 8 to 14.
Reflection is concentrated at the end (the SULAM Implementation Journal and final reporting)
instead of distributed as a recurring checkpoint across the semester. That is not so much a
design flaw as a design trade -off the findings make visible. A fourteen -week, credit-bearing
course cannot realistically run several full Kolb cycles without either compr essing fieldwork
depth or extending the course beyond one semester. This course therefore opts for depth
over cyclical repetition. The theoretical contribution here is therefore not that the course
"implements experiential learning" in the abstract. Rather , it shows, with documentary
specificity, where a single-semester TVET capstone must depart from the fully iterative form
of Kolb's cycle. That departure is a defensible response to a fixed credit -hour constraint, and
not an oversight.

Resourcing Transparency as an Answer to a Specific Literature Gap
The SLT-proportional weighting finding (4.2) speaks directly and specifically to a gap in the
literature, not to a general point about good practice. Goyal, Gupta, and Gupta (2022) link
stronger programme -level attainment to weighting schemes that track in structional time
investment. Yusop and colleagues (2022) separately note that authentic, performance-based
assessment remains the exception, not the rule, in vocational education. Neither study, nor
the wider competency-based-design literature reviewed in Section 2.4, shows the arithmetic

of that proportionality in a real course. The present case does: SCL -1's 20% weighting
corresponds to an exact, documented 24 of 120 SLT hours, not an approximated or rounded
figure. That specificity matters because it converts a principle the literature e ndorses in the
abstract, weight assessment proportionally to time invested, into a checkable design
procedure. Other programme committees could replicate this procedure when justifying
weighting decisions to accreditation panels. That is a materially stronger contribution than
restating the principle would be.

Inclusion by Site Selection: A Structural Answer, Not a Programmatic One
The inclusion pattern identified in 4.3, three of five community sites serving persons with
disabilities or special -needs learners, deserves a more pointed reading than the simple
observation that the course "includes" disability -serving partners. The disa bility-inclusive
TVET literature, including a 2024 International Labour  Organization brief, consistently finds
that skills systems remain inconsistently inclusive of persons with disabilities in principle, even
where policy language commits to inclusion (International Labour Organization, 2024). What
this course demonstrates is a structural route around that policy -practice gap. Inclusion was
achieved through site selection written into the SULAM approval paper itself, not through a
bolt-on disability module, differentiated c urriculum, or additional teaching hours. That is a
materially different, and arguably more scalable, mechanism than the curriculum -level
interventions the literature typically proposes. It does not require redesigning course content
at all, only redesigning which community partners a service-learning course chooses to work
with. The same standardised digital-marketing syllabus was delivered without adaptation
across both disability-serving and conventional sites. This finding suggests the mechanism has
a boundary condition worth naming honestly: site -selection inclusion secures access and
contact hours, but it does not, by itself, verify that the content delivered was pedagogically
appropriate for participants with additional support needs. No differentiated instructional
adaptation is documented in the curriculum package. Site -selection inclusion and content-
level inclusion are therefore separable design choices, and this course has demonstrably
achieved the first without yet demonstrating the second.

Authenticity by Construction: Extending the Canvas-Journal Logic to Examination Design
The fourth finding is that this course manufactures assessment authenticity through paired
instruments, canvas plus journal, examination blueprint concentrated in Analysing and
Evaluating, instead of asserting it through a single artefact. This is where the paper's clearest
theoretical contribution sits. Gulikers, Bastiaens , and Kirschner's five -dimensional
authenticity framework specifies that assessment authenticity requires alignment across task,
physical context, social context, form, and criteria. It is not enough for a task to merely
resemble real-world work on the surface (Gulikers et al., 2006). Sluijsmans, Straetmans, and
van Merrienboer (2008) anticipated part of this, arguing that vocational assessment needs
portfolio-style, protocol-driven scoring instead of a single graded artefact. Sparviero  (2019)
shows canvas-based tools force continuous reconciliation between planning assumptions and
field-generated evidence. The present case adds a worked demonstration to that lineage.
Authenticity has to be engineered at two separate assessment layers si multaneously: the
continuous performance layer (canvas plus dated journal) and the summative examination
layer (a Table of Test Specifications concentrated in Analysing and Evaluating). Each layer can
independently fail to be authentic even if the other succeeds. A canvas without a journal
degrades into a desk exercise regardless of how well the examination is designed. Conversely,

a well -designed canvas -journal pairing cannot rescue an examination that tests

assessment layer independently, not just at the level of the course as a whole, and that is  a

Implications for TVET and Technopreneurship Curriculum Design
Four transferable principles follow from the analysis above,  each pitched at the level of a
design procedure and not a general recommendation. First, service -learning components
should carry a percentage weighting derived arithmetically from their documented share of
Student Learning Time, with the derivation kept auditable for accreditation review, instead of
assigned by convention and justified narratively after the fact. Second, curriculum designers
claiming experiential-learning grounding should state explicitly whether their course design
delivers a single linear pass through Kolb's cycle or genuine iterative repetition. The two carry
different pedagogical claims, and a single -semester capstone will typically only be able to
defend the former. Third, authenticity should be engineered independently at the
continuous-assessment layer, a structured tool plus longitudinal, dated evidence of revision,
and at the summative -examination layer, a cognitive -process blueprint that shifts weight to
Analysing and Evaluating once foundational knowledge has already been assessed. Securing
authenticity at one layer simply does not transfer to the other. Fourth, where a service -
learning course serves community partners with additional support needs, designers shou ld
distinguish, and separately document, structural inclusion achieved through site selection
from content -level inclusion achieved through curriculum adaptation. The present case
demonstrates the former without yet demonstrating the latter, and conflating  the two
overstates what site selection alone can guarantee. A fifth, more exploratory, implication
follows from the digital -marketing content documented here. TVET programmes built on
technical or vocational competencies appear well positioned to fold basic digital -marketing
literacy into service-learning components, precisely because that skill set addresses a digital
divide affecting rural, disabled, and special-needs communities directly. This remains a design
opportunity this case illustrates, though, and not a causal claim this case can support (Halik
Bassah et al., 2025; Rosdi & Adnan, 2025; Aggarwal & Johal, 2021).

Limitations
Three limitations bound what these findings can support, and each maps onto a specific claim
made above, not the study in general. This is a single-case document analysis. The alignment-
coherence finding (5.1) therefore describes designed coherence, not verified coherence, since
no student learning-outcome attainment data are analysed here to confirm that CLO-aligned
design actually produced CLO-level competence. The inclusion finding (5.4) is bounded in the
same way. What is demonstrated is structural access, meaning site selection, contact hours,
and standardised  content delivery, not community -partner satisfaction or beneficiary -
reported outcome data. Claims about the quality or adequacy of the coaching received by
disability-serving sites specifically therefore remain untested by this analysis. The Kolb -cycle
reading (5.2) is bounded by genre: a single -semester, single-pass course design cannot be
judged against a fully iterative implementation of experiential learning theory. This paper's
claim is accordingly limited to showing where and why a single pass departs from the full
cycle, not to evaluating whether that departure costs students measurabl e learning. Future

research should therefore triangulate this curriculum-design analysis against quantitative CLO

here generalise beyond this single case.

Conclusion
This paper set out to answer a narrower question than "does service-learning work in TVET,"
a question the literature has already answered in the affirmative many times over. It asked
instead how a single Malaysian TVET course converts outcome -based curriculum theory into
an auditable set of design decisions. These are decisions a curriculum committee, an
accreditation panel, or another lecturer could actually inspect and replicate. The answer,
demonstrated through documentary analysis and not merely assert ed, is that BTMT 3383
closes the constructive-alignment loop from competency to grade without a single traceable
break (Section 5.1). It derives its SULAM -anchored 60% grade weighting from an exact,
auditable Student Learning Time calculation instead of convention (5.3). It achieves disability
and special-needs inclusion as a structural property of community-site selection instead of as
a curriculum add-on (5.4). And it secures assessment authenticity independently at both the
continuous-performance layer and the summative -examination layer instead of at only one
(5.5).

The paper's original contribution to the literature is threefold. First, it supplies the empirical
arithmetic: SLT hours to percentage weighting, site count to beneficiary profile, and cognitive-
process marks to examination blueprint. Constructive -alignment and competency -based-
design scholarship generally recommends this kind of arithmetic, but rarely documents it at
this level of operational detail (Gruppen, 2012; Goyal et al., 2022; Yusop et al., 2022). Second,
it identifies a boundary condition in the a pplication of Kolb's experiential learning cycle to
single-semester TVET capstones. Such courses can defend a single, deep linear pass through
concrete experience, reflection, and re -application, but not the fully iterative cycle Kolb's
theory assumes. Curriculum designers claiming experiential grounding should therefore say
explicitly which version of the model they are implementing (5.2). Third, it demonstrates that
authentic assessment, per Gulikers, Bastiaens , and Kirschner's five -dimensional framework,
has to be engineered separately at the performance -task layer and the examination layer.
Neither layer's authenticity substitutes for the other. This is a distinction the assessment -
authenticity literature has not previously stated this explicitly (5.5) (Gulikers et al., 2006;
Sparviero, 2019; Sluijsmans et al., 2008). Taken together, these three contributions reposition
the Social Business Model Canvas and SULAM Implementation Journal pairing, and the
course's wider design, as a transferable engineering template for TVET and
technopreneurship programmes. They are not to be read as a single successful case to admire.

named in 5.7, and neither is a generic call for "more research." The first is a quasi -
experimental or matched-comparison study. It would track whether courses that a dopt SLT-
proportional weighting and paired canvas-journal assessment show measurably different CLO
attainment than courses using conventional, non-derived weighting schemes. This would test
whether the auditability documented here actually improves learning outcomes and not
merely design transparency. The second is a qualitative study, conducted directl y with the

Vol. 1 6 , No. 9, 2026, E-ISSN: 2222 -6990 © 2026
98
disability and special-needs community partners named in this paper's SULAM sites. It would
examine whether the standardised digital-marketing curriculum these beneficiaries received
was pedagogically adequate for their support needs. This would test the boundary condition
this paper identified but could not itself resolve, namely that structural inclusion through site
selection is not yet evidence of content -level inclusion. Answering either question would

its design choices causally matter.
