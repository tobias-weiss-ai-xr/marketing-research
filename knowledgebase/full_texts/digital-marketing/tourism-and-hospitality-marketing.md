# Tourism and Hospitality Marketing

This book provides a comprehensive overview of marketing for the tourism
and hospitality industry. With a focus on the Indian subcontinent, it explores
various frameworks and marketing strategies and their application in diverse
areas and markets.
The volume discusses the dynamics of the tourism and hospitality industries
and provides an overview of conventional and non-conventional marketing
strategies for better decision making, service, and promotion. It looks at tools
and techniques for businesses to understand the customer’s needs, customer-
behaviour, building partnerships with other service providers, using digital
and social media marketing, and using a marketing mix to design an overall
marketing strategy which will serve the business’s objectives. With various
case studies, the book analyses the essential marketing tenets for the hospitality
and tourism industry and the importance of implementing a marketing plan
which prioritizes the customer relationship.
This book will be an indispensable resource for students and  academics, as well
as industry professionals working in tourism, hospitality, travel management,
marketing, communication, digital marketing, and public relations.

Tourism has seen unprecedented growth in contemporary times. It is one of
the fastest-growing sectors in the global economy. For several countries and
many states within India, it is one of the key contributors to the economy.
Not only does it contribute to the region’s wealth, but it is also a great equal-
iser. Revenue is evenly distributed in a tourism-based economy. This means
that generated wealth is not concentrated in only a few hands or restricted to
certain urban areas only; it reaches remote villages, small entrepreneurs, and
the last man out there. Besides being an instrument for cross-cultural under-
standing that contributes to global peace, tourism contributes to achieving
most sustainable development goals (SDGs). Thus, as tourism grows, the
number of enterprises in the sector grows manifold. The companies try to
outsmart each other in fierce competition. With the absorption of modern
technology, the tastes, preferences, and consumption patterns of the new-age
consumer have undergone a sea change. It is a new game, it has new rules.
Tourism and hospitality firms need to master the craft of marketing to
win hearts and markets. However, there are also destinations that report
over-tourism. With growing competition, it is difficult for the firms to remain
visible, capture customers’ attention, and create value to satisfy them. While
it appears simple, marketing is nuanced. The nature of tourism and hospital-
ity products and the new world of digital transactions complicate marketing
management for firms.
Businesses have been discussing marketing for businesses that transacted
goods for over a century. As competition has matured, the nature of products
has changed to include intangible components. Products as a combination
of goods and services were to be marketed considering the salient charac -
teristics of services. In India, universities and institutions started discussing
services marketing in the early 1990s. Tourism was noticed in the 1980s but
was thought of more as a product of history and heritage. It was primarily
considered a product of luxury consumption. There was mainly a scattered
and sporadic approach to promoting tourism until 2002, when a concerted
effort helped shape the “Incredible India” campaign. India became aggressive
in its marketing efforts, which affected private enterprises in the sector. Both
the government and the private players joined hands to market tourism and
hospitality products.

Preface
While the first schools of tourism surfaced in the early 1990s, they focused
more on history and geography as resources for tourism products. Tourism
as a business took a back seat, and so did tourism marketing. At the turn of
the century, some tourism schools in India started talking about marketing
based on service paradigms. Undoubtedly, tourism and hospitality products
are mainly services, but they have their nuances that merit attention. The
early authors of tourism marketing authored books that mainly discussed
service paradigms. Even today, many teachers refer to service marketing
books for teaching tourism and hospitality marketing. While many market -
ing principles apply to this industry, the industry has matured and has ways
and means to conduct business.
The COVID-19 pandemic has brought the world to a standstill. With
complete lockdowns in most countries and countries wanting checks on
people travelling and intermingling, the tourism and hospitality sector was
among the first casualties. It is estimated that the sector will be impacted by
60 to 80%. Nevertheless, the world is expected to emerge post-COVID-19 to
a new normal. Whether it is marketing to promote tourism at the destination,
demarketing overcrowded destinations, or re-marketing the destinations that
have lost their patrons, companies will need a thorough understanding of
marketing concepts and principles as applied to tourism and hospitality sec -
tors. In addition, companies will need executives and managers who can
market products with dexterity.
Personal Motivation to Write This Book
We have been teaching marketing of services for almost 20 years. Together
and independently, we have authored several books on services, including
marketing and management. For more than 12 years, we have been teaching
tourism marketing. We have been consulting tourism and hospitality compa-
nies and have always felt the need for teaching material that is more tourism
and hospitality specific. We have referred to many books to use in our classes
but were not satisfied with the present works on tourism and hospitality
marketing. We always wanted to use simple examples from the Indian con -
text to explain tourism and hospitality marketing. We collected examples,
stories, and caselets and used them during our classroom discussions. Many
colleagues and students have requested that we do a book on the subject.
Though we planned this book for some time, other engagements kept us
away from this project. The period of lockdown during the COVID-19 pan-
demic allowed us to put our thoughts together.

Conventional wisdom has generally followed the 4P framework of market -
ing, which is constructed around product, price, promotion, and place – but
not necessarily in any sequence. Academics and practitioners have always
questioned why only these four elements, and why not more or less? Then
we arrived at the 4C framework, which was more or less old wine in a new
bottle. While the 4P framework looked at marketing from the suppliers’
point of view, the 4C framework was more customer-orientated. Later, the
idea to market services evolved. These four were referred to as traditional
marketing-mix elements. A shared understanding was that the context in
which services are created and delivered is also essential, so three contextual
cues were introduced to take the marketing mix tally to 7Ps, which added
physical evidence, people, and process. While this conceptual framework is
still followed, conventionally, the traditional elements are first discussed, fol-
lowed by the additional three components. Many individuals have added to
this list in overexcitement, making it an 8P or 10P or even 14P assortment
of elements. However, there is little correlation or order (sequencing) among
these mixed elements.
Also, in research on service quality, Parasuraman, Zeithaml, and Berry
(1985) developed the gaps model of service quality. Quality Gap 5, the cus -
tomer gap, is a function of the four other gaps – the company gap, the design
gap, the delivery gap, and the communication gap. Like any other service
provider, a tourism and hospitality business enterprise must ensure bridging
these four gaps. The necessary steps to bridge these gaps are the marketing-
mix elements. Besides this, the book has chapters, each taking up a princi -
pal issue. These simple-to-read chapters are discussed in a shorter, easier to
consume manner. Each concept will also be explained with the help of brief
examples picked up during our practice.
Another critical issue in tourism is to determine what is to be marketed:
the destination as a whole or the independent service providers such as travel
companies, accommodation providers (hotels, guesthouses, etc.), food ser -
vice providers (restaurants, etc.), transporters, merchandisers, event organis-
ers, etc. The answer lies somewhere in between. The book, therefore, delves
into the marketing of tourism and hospitality companies on the one hand and
destinations on the other.

Kutch Nahi Dekha Toh Kuchh Nahi Dekha! – the story of Rann Utsav
(If you have not seen Kutch, you have not seen anything!)
Months before the infamous Operation Gibraltar by Pakistan in 1965,
our neighbour, in January of the same year, had designed another mili-
tary misadventure, Operation Desert Hawk, in the Rann of Kutch in
the state of Gujarat. The Pakistani army started patrolling the area
under Indian control, and Indians did not even know about it. It was a
barren land left unattended. The Rann of Kutch is a series of extensive
salt marshes that spans the border between Pakistan and India. The
Rann of Kutch is part of the Thar Desert and is located mainly in the
Gujarat state of India and some parts of Sindh, Pakistan. It is in the
delta of the mighty Indus River. Thus, it has the sea on one side and the
desert on the other.
Some time back, there was a political controversy alleging that the
Gujarat government allotted nearly 15,000 acres of land in the Kutch
district to the Adani port and Special Economic Zone (SEZ) in Mundra
for a price between INR 1 and INRs 32 per acre. The Adani group
bought mostly non-agricultural, barren, and uninhabitable land. A
large chunk, located close to the sea, was salty with very little meaning-
ful cultivation. It was long considered useless.
Today, when the sun is not harsh during the latter part of the year,
almost 500,000 visitors throng Rann, which is miles and miles of arid
land interspersed with salt flats – for long believed to be worthless,
for a spectacular extravaganza called the Rann Utsav (festival). Rann
Utsav is a festival of epic proportions, beginning on the day of the full
moon in November/December and lasting for more than two months.
It brings together art, music, culture, and several other state attrac -
tions. It also attracts artisans, including master weavers, musicians, folk
dancers, and Gujarat's best cuisine. Moreover, it attracts travellers and
culture enthusiasts from all over the world, making it one of the most
visited fairs in the country. Visitors can put up in the grand tent city

Tourism Demand Concepts
One of the basic concepts in tourism dynamics is that of tourism demand.
We will spend some time discussing the concept of tourism demand. Hall
and Page ( 2002) suggest that understanding tourism demand is a starting
point for analysing why tourism develops, who patronises specific destina -
tions, and what appeals to the client market. Understanding the concept of
demand is the basic building block that determines how visitors choose and
pursue a range of opportunities in their leisure time. The concept of demand
in relation to tourism helps understand motivation, needs and experiences
and indicates changing trends.
However, there are different perspectives of looking at the concept of
demand. Economist, for example, is concerned with the tourists' propensity
to purchase tourism products or services, which means the willingness and
ability to purchase at a specific price during a given period. Geographers
usually look at the determinants of demand other than price, which will
influence participation in tourism. They look at tourism demand as the total
number of persons who travel, wish to travel, and wish to use tourist facili -
ties and services at places away from their place of work or residence. On the
other hand, psychologists consider personality traits and external influences
that affect the willingness to buy a tourism product. They, therefore, bring in
the notion of motivation and behaviour of tourists in tourism demand.
According to Mathieson and Wall ( 1982), tourism demand is the total
number of persons who travel or wish to travel to use tourist facilities away
from their places of work and residence.
Primarily, the concept of tourist demand can be approached in three dif-
ferent ways:
• Actual or effective demand
• Suppressed demand
• No demand
Actual demand is the actual number of participants in tourism or those
travelling, i.e., de facto tourists. This demand is commonly measured, which
constitutes the bulk of tourism statistics.
Suppressed demand is the number of people who want to travel but are
not travelling for one reason or another. They have the willingness to travel
but are not able to travel, this can be broken into two subcategories:

24 Introduction
Potential demand is a suppressed demand that refers to those who would
travel at some later time if circumstances change, e.g., political situation
changes, their purchasing power increases or when they have an entitlement.
Deferred demand is a demand suppressed due to challenges in the supply
environment, peak season, or unavailability of seats on a flight.
No demand refers to that proportion of the market that is either unwilling
to travel or unable to travel.
There are two related concepts:
Substitution of demand  refers to a case when demand for one activity
(say a self-catering holiday) is substituted by another (staying in serviced
accommodation)
Redirection of demand  is where the geographical location of demand is
changed, e.g., a trip to Ooty is redirected to Darjeeling.
A new tourism supply may redirect demand from a similar facility or sub-
stitute demand from another facility (tourism product). New supply may
alternatively generate new demand altogether.
