ABSTRACT
The healthcare sector has undergone substantial transformation with the increasing expansion of corporate hospitals,
growing patient expectations, technological advancement, and intensified competition among healthcare service
providers. In this changing environment, effective promotion of healthcare services and systematic hospital brand
building have become important for developing patient awareness, trust, satisfaction, and long-term relationships.
Unlike conventional consumer markets, healthcare marketing operates within a context characterized by service
intangibility, information asymmetry, professional responsibility, patient vulnerability, and ethical considerations.
The present review examines the major strategies adopted by corporate hospitals for healthcare service promotion
and brand building, with particular emphasis on patient-centred approaches. The review synthesizes key themes
including traditional healthcare promotion, digital marketing, social media communication, patient education,
community outreach, service quality, healthcare professional interactions, patient experience, online reviews,
electronic word-of-mouth, trust, reputation, and ethical considerations. The literature indicates that promotional
activities can create awareness and influence patient expectations, but sustainable hospital branding depends
substantially on the consistency between promotional communication and the actual healthcare experience. Service
quality dimensions such as reliability, responsiveness, assurance, empathy, and tangibles contribute to patient
satisfaction and perceived hospital image, while professional competence, communication, transparency, and
empathy strengthen trust. Digital platforms and patient-generated online content have further increased the visibility
and influence of hospital reputation. At the same time, concerns related to misleading claims, patient privacy, data
security, misinformation, commercialization, and responsible use of testimonials require careful consideration. The
review proposes an integrated perspective in which healthcare promotion influences patient expectations, while
service quality and patient experience shape satisfaction and trust, ultimately contributing to hospital brand image,
reputation, loyalty, and positive word-of-mouth. The review emphasizes that sustainable corporate hospital branding
requires an integrated, ethical, evidence-based, and patient-centred approach that aligns promotional strategies with
actual healthcare service delivery.

1. Introduction
In the last few decades, healthcare has experienced
significant change, as medical technology is
progressing, consumers' expectations continue to rise,
the private healthcare system is expanding,
digitalisation has changed the landscape, and
healthcare providers are competing with each other.
Patients today aren't just assessing a healthcare
organization based on clinical knowledge or medical
facilities; they're taking into account the general
quality of service, availability, communication,
responsiveness, physical setting, reputation, and their
engagement with healthcare professionals. This has
led to the introduction of service management,
patient engagement, communication and relationship

findings suggest that the dimensions of interaction

furthered by the growth of corporate and private
hospitals. The environment of a corporate hospital
generally offers a wide array of specialized and
multispecialty services and patients who can make
their own choices based on their perception of the
level of services offered, the competence and
expertise of their doctors, facilities, accessibility,
cost, reputation and experience from previous visits.

2. Concept of Healthcare Service Marketing
There has been a significant shift in the approach to
healthcare service marketing from the traditional
provider to a more comprehensive patient-centered
approach that takes into account patient needs,
expectations, experiences, satisfaction, and long-term
relationships with healthcare organizations.
Healthcare marketing literature has grown
significantly and includes topics like the strategy of
services, advertising, brand, consumer behaviour, and
healthcare relationship management. The extent and
growing maturity of the research field was shown by
a bibliometric review of almost 2,000 healthcare
marketing publications. In today's health care
delivery systems, marketing is no longer just about
attracting patients to the organization; it is about
communicating health care value, building patient
relationships, managing the delivery of service
experiences, and creating a health care organizations
reputation (Corbin, 2001; Thomas, 2024).
Onthe Service Marketing in The Health Care Sector
Definition and Characteristics
Healthcare service marketing is an organized
approach to the planning, development, deployment,
maintenance, and enhancement of a healthcare
organization's service and product mix to meet the
evolving needs of patients, their families, and other
stakeholders, while establishing a reputation that will
result in long-term success. Healthcare marketing is
not like product marketing; it is a multifaceted
process that includes the movement of professional
knowledge, the provision of services, patient
engagement, organizational procedures, and health
outcomes. Healthcare services have a number of
attributes that make them different from goods. The
traditional services-marketing literature has outlined
four key characteristics of services: intangibility,
inseparability, heterogeneity/variability, and
perishability. In the healthcare sector, these qualities
are important as patients typically do not have the
ability to fully assess the technical aspects of a
healthcare service before utilizing it.  Intangibility is
the fact that healthcare services do not have a
physical appearance which can be inspected before
being consumed. Despite all of the information
hospitals can provide about their infrastructure, their
equipment, their specialists, their accreditation and
their treatment facilities, diagnosis, treatment,
communication and experience are subject to a
limited extent of assessment after the service
encounter. Inseparability refers to the intimate
connection between the production and use of
services. Patients normally interact directly with
doctors, nurses, technicians, administration and other
healthcare workers. As such, employees are not only
responsible for the service product they provide but
also for the way they interact with it. The behaviour
and the communication of employees are part of the
service (Corbin, 2001; Thomas, 2024).
Variability or heterogeneity occurs because of
differences between patients, healthcare workers,
clinical circumstances, and/or organizational
procedures and processes, as well as service
encounters. Despite having protocols in place, each
patient's needs and interactions can result in a
different experience. Variability in healthcare services
is an important focus of research because managing
variation in service encounters is vital, but
opportunities for patients to participate and co-create
are also highlighted. Perishability is the fact that
unused service capacity cannot be stored for later use.
For instance, an empty appointment slot, an unused
operating room, a diagnostic appointment, or an
unused inpatient bed cannot be merely stored and
sold on later, as a manufactured item. Healthcare
organizations need to carefully oversee and
coordinate capacity, demand, staffing and patient
flow. The aforementioned characteristics have

2.2 Intangibility and variability of healthcare
services
Because health is intangible, there is an information
imbalance between provider and patient. Patients can
see certain elements of hospital, like cleanliness,
buildings, technology, staff appearance, etc., but can
struggle to evaluate technical aspects of the diagnosis
or treatment on their own. As a result, patients often
rely on other factors like hospital reputation,
professional qualifications, accreditation,
recommendations, experiences, online reviews,
communication quality and perceived service quality
to assess a healthcare provider. This is a quality that
is especially crucial in the healthcare marketing. In
situations like a complicated diagnosis or a medical
emergency, patients might need to make choices
without sufficient information. Thus, the
trustworthiness of promotion messages and
reputation of the organization are significant elements
of perceived value. Secondly, there is variability.
Healthcare experiences may vary based on the
patient's clinical condition, providers' behavior, wait
time, communication, treatment pathway,
administrative process and o ther organizational
factors. This has led to the research interest in service
quality to focus on the patient's perception of quality
in addition to the professional view of quality. A large
survey of the research on service quality in the
healthcare sector has uncovered an emerging interest
in user-defined service quality. In the case of
corporate hospitals, variability has specific
implications in terms of brand building. Branding a
hospital through advertising and digital
communication can be very strong, but inconsistent
experiences by patients will defeat the branding. On
the other hand, a regular and patient interaction with
the service will strengthen the credibility of the
hospital brand. Therefore, service delivery becomes a
crucial element of healthcare marketing (Mehta,
2022).
2.3 Patient as consumer
In addition to patient empowerment, increasing
choice, private health care markets, online
information and active patient involvement in
decision-making, the 'patient as a consumer' has
become a focal point. Patients are seeking out
information about the hospital, the doctor, treatment
options, facilities, costs, access, and what other
patients have to say about you before choosing a
healthcare provider. But the patient is just another
consumer has conceptual and ethical flaws. Decision
making about healthcare is often carried out in a
vulnerable situation, in an uncertain or stressful
environment, with asymmetrical information and
where the consequences are likely to be significant.
Thus, patients are n ot always informed and
autonomous consumers like ordinary consumers of
commercial products. The patient-as-consumer idea
has been discussed by scholars who point to a
conflict between the empowerment of the patient and
the possible commercialization of health care. It is
more accurate to think of patients as active partners
in their healthcare and see the professional and
ethical duty of healthcare providers as well. Patient-
centred health care practices are based on patient
participation, communication, respecting patients'
preferences, and responding to patients' needs. A
study of patients as customers also shows that
healthcare delivery is complex, co -produced and
intangible in nature while highlighting the role of
patient centred practices in enhancing satisfaction
and perceived service quality.  This change in
marketing perspective, from “attract patients” to
“create patient value,” underscores that marketing is
a process aimed at generating value for the patient.
Patient value could come in the form of clinical
effectiveness, access, convenience, communication,
empathy, safety, transparency, continuity of care, and
positive service experience. Evaluating healthcare
marketing success should not only be done based on
patient acquisition or revenue metrics, but also
patient experience, satisfaction, trust, engagement,
and long-term relationships (Berkowitz, 2021;
Thomas, 2024).

2.5 Ethical issues in health promotion practice
One of the primary differences between healthcare
marketing and most commercial marketing scenarios
is ethics. Making health care decisions can have
significant physical, psychological, social and
financial implications, and patients may be especially
vulnerable due to illness, anxiety, lack of medical
knowledge and/or the need for immediate treatment.
Thus, the promotion of health in health organizations
must carry out a balance between the organization
interests and the responsibility to the patient as a
professional. Principles of ethically responsible
healthcare promotion include truthfulness, accuracy,
transparency, evidence, respect for patient autonomy,
confidentiality, fairness, and refraining from making
misleading claims. Promotional communication
should not overstate treatment results, guarantee
results where they cannot be guaranteed, fail to
disclose pertinent information, set up false
expectations, or take advantage of patient
vulnerabilities.
Ethical issues in healthcare advertising have been
well documented. Schenker and colleagues pointed
out the ethical issues that arise in healthcare-service
advertising due to the informational asymmetry, the
high stakes decisions and the vulnerability of th e
patient, which might not be covered by traditional
advertising concepts. Likewise, studies of direct to
consumer (D2C) health care products have come up
with concerns such as misleading claims, weak
regulation, dubious quality and effectiveness, safety,
privacy, financial costs, and targeting vulnerable
populations. This becomes an important
consideration if hospitals feature patient testimonials,
treatment success rates, before and after photos,
celebrity endorsements or any statements about
specialized procedures. Promotional material should
give enough background to patients for them to
understand the information. When promoting high-
risk or complex treatments, evidence -based
communication is particularly important. The ethical

2.6 Integrated perspective
The marketing of healthcare services can thus be
regarded as a complex process that involves the
design of services, the needs of patients,
communication, service delivery, service experiences,
trust and the reputation of the healthcare
organization. Healthcare services are unique, and
despite the fact that they can be promoted, there is no
way to create a sustainable hospital brand. Rather,
promotional communication is only credible if the
message sent by the hospital is received in the same
way by patients (Thomas, 2024; Rochford et al.,
2026).
3. Hospital Brand Building
With the proliferation of hospital and health care
providers and information sources available to
patients, hospital brand building has become an
essential element in the health care management
process. In the past, hospital reputation was primarily
linked to clinical skills, doctors' reputations, facility,
and treatment results. Today, however, hospital
branding is about more than just the quality of
services, it's also about patient's experience,
communication, availability, technological capacity,
trust, values of the organization, and reputation.
Hospital branding is not just a marketing campaign; it
is the creation of a unique and believable brand that
is reinforced by the patients' multiple healthcare
experiences. The literature review following a
systematical procedure yielded traditional hospital
brand dimensions like brand loyalty, perceived
quality, brand associations, brand awareness, and
brand image as well as some hospital-specific ones
like patient satisfaction, service quality, treatment
processes and the performance of the medical staff.
This illustrates the importance of traditional
marketing concepts as well as the nature of
healthcare provision (Thomas, 2024; Rochford et al.,
2026).
3.1 Concept of hospital branding
Hospital branding is the process of systematically
building and nurturing an organization's brand,
reputation, perceived value, and relationships with
patients and other stakeholders. A hospital brand sets
expectations to the patient about the nature and
quality of services he/she can expect to receive at the
hospital. The nature of healthcare services is
intangible and technically complex, and the branding
can help to minimize some of the uncertainty
associated with the delivery of this service by
offering patients more recognizable signals of the
competence, reliability, facilities, professionalism and
quality of service of the healthcare provider. But
healthcare branding is more than just visual branding
or marketing. When any organizational claims are
backed by actual service quality, the potential role of
branding for transparency, patient choice,
communication, and patient retention is acceptable.
Some studies have suggested that branding can help
enhance technical quality and service quality levels,
as well as to improve communication and the
appropriateness of care, by making consumers'
decisions about care more informed (Thomas, 2024;
Rochford et al., 2026).
3.2 Brand identity and brand image
Brand identity is the manner in which a hospital
wants to be identified and communicated,
encompassing the values, mission, clinical skills,
service philosophy, visual identity, communication
style and unique qualities of a hospital. Unlike that,
brand image is the actual perception of the hospital
by the patients and other stakeholders. The difference
is significant because the image a patient forms can
be advanced technology, compassionate care, clinical
excellence, patient-centredness—or something
entirely different. Patient choice is especially related
to the hospital brand image. A conceptual review of
healthcare branding suggested a relationship between
the hospital brand image, perceived service quality,
patient satisfaction and patient loyalty. The model
proposes patient loyalty can be affected by the
perception of service quality and satisfaction in terms
of brand image. So, in order to build a successful
brand, there is a need to have congruence between
the intended and experienced brand image. Patients
may feel disappointed when the reality differs from
the hospital's expectations, which can impact their
satisfaction and loyalty (Lexa and Berlin, 2006; Das
et al., 2021; Thomas, 2024; Rochford et al., 2026).

for patients when they are making decisions. Patients
may not have the technical expertise to evaluate the
quality of diagnosis, treatment or clinical decision
making on their own. As a result, credibility of the
organization, professional reputation, transparent
communication and previous experiences can be

3.4 Brand reputation
A patient's perception of a hospital's reputation is
built over time from the experiences of all those who
come into contact with it, including patients and their
families, healthcare providers, referring physicians,
hospital staff, other community members, media
outlets, and others. While clinical results and
professional experience are significant, reputation
can also be shaped by communication, infrastructure,
patient services, responsiveness, a digital footprint,
public relations, social responsibility, and what
patients say about them. With the rise in the amount
of information readily available online, hospital
reputation is becoming more apparent and possibly
more malleable. Positive or negative experiences can
be shared quickly and easily digitally so reputation
management is an ongoing organizational
responsibility. Thus, the reputation must not be
considered as a standalone promotional good. It is an
outcome of the organization's clinical performance,
service quality, communication practices, patient
experience and their relationships with stakeholders
(Thomas, 2024).
3.5 Patient experience and brand loyalty
Patient experience relates to the spectrum of
interactions that patients may have with a health-care
organization from beginning to end. These
interactions may involve appointment scheduling,
registration, waiting, consultation, treatment, and
nursing care, billing, discharge, follow -up, and
communication after treatment. The literature
suggests there is a strong association between patient
experience and communication and patient
centredness. A systematic review on patient and
provider perspectives found that communication was
an important element of enhancing patient experience
and patient centred care. Brand loyalty in healthcare
can manifest as patients' intentions to return to the
hospital, recommending it to others, using its services
and spreading positive feedback. An integrative
review resulted in the identification of determinants
of patient loyalty which include satisfaction, quality,
value, hospital brand image, trust, commitment, and
complaint related factors. So, loyalty should not be
viewed as just a matter of repeat buying. In the
healthcare sector, loyalty may demonstrate trust in
the healthcare provider, continuity of care,
satisfaction, trust, and recommending the healthcare
organization to others (Thomas, 2024).
3.6 Relationship between service quality and
hospital brand equity
Hospital brand equity is the extra value that the
hospital brand brings to a patient and other
stakeholders due to their recognition of the brand.
This is especially crucial since the quality of service
that patients perceive is an important factor in
determining the credibility of the brand in their eyes,
as they judge the quality of the service they receive
during actual service interactions. A systematic
review of hospital brand equity revealed the
following traditional factors as critical: perceived
quality, brand loyalty, brand associations, brand
awareness, and brand image, and the following
healthcare-specific factors: patient satisfaction,
treatment processes, and medical staff performance.
Indian private health care industry has also found
correlations between perceived service quality,
satisfaction and loyalty in the field of research.
Among the 475 patients included in the study from
five private hospitals in India, relationship with the
service providers, facility quality and interactions
with the supporting personnel influenced the
perceptions and loyalty-related outcomes. The
significance of service quality and trust is further
upheld by recent evidence in India (Monika et al.,
2026). There was positive correlation between the
perceived quality of hospital services and trust and
loyalty among patients in the two hospitals
(government and private hospitals) in Chhattisgarh
and the association between satisfaction and loyalty
was different in the two hospitals (Monika et al.,
2026).

Traditional advertising remains relevant for

The effectiveness of advertising, however, depends
on credibility and relevance. Advertising can create
awareness, but the patient's subsequent experience
determines whether the communicated brand promise
is reinforced or weakened (Thomas, 2024).
4.2 Print and television media
The media, including newspapers and magazines, TV ,
radio and mass media, can achieve wide geographic
coverage. Articles on health, public-health messages,
hospital announcements, profiles of specialists and
awareness campaigns can be printed. The television
can be used to better convey a message visually and
emotionally, whether in the form of an interview, an
expert discussion, a health programme or an
institutional advert. These types of communications
may be especially applicable for hospitals that are
trying to build a name for themselves outside of the
local area. The information, however, should be
based on evidence and should not include
exaggerated claims for treatment (Thomas, 2024).
4.3 Outdoor advertising
Outdoor advertising refers to billboards, banners,
transit advertising, signage and other forms of
advertising through location. Hospitals are allowed to
broadcast on television, radio and other media about
the location of the hospital, emergency services,
specialties, health camps, and major public-health
campaigns. The advantage is that the populations are
geographically relevant and repeat exposures occur.
However, it is generally not possible to communicate
a complex clinical claim responsibly in an outdoor
context, as a communication is usually short. So, it's
better to do that communication in the context of
awareness and identification of services rather than in
detail for medical decision making (Thomas, 2024).
4.4 Public relations
Public relations is a more comprehensive way of
communicating about the relationships between
hospitals, patients, communities, professionals,
media, and others. These can range from press
releases, expert interviews, public health events,
institutional reports, academic conferences,
community programmes, and disseminated health
information that's based on evidence. Public relations
can be a factor in building the reputation of a hospital
by allowing organizations to use it to show they are
experts and are being helpful rather than just
advertising individual services (Thomas, 2024).
4.5 Health camps and screening programmes
Health camps and screening programmes are
valuable tools for community involvement and
preventive-health promotion. Corporate hospitals can
arrange programmes for diabetes, hypertension,
cardiovascular diseases, cancer screening, women
health, eye health, oral health, occupational health
and more. These programmes could provide
opportunities for ea rly detection and health
education, and raise awareness of hospital services
within the community. In the case of the ethical
implementation, however, there are certain steps that
need to be taken, including the appropriate referral
pathways, informed participation, confidentiality, and
the avoidance of unnecessary diagnostic or treatment
recommendations (Thomas, 2024).
4.6 Medical awareness programmes
Medical awareness programmes involve the use of
educational communication to enhance awareness of
public understanding of diseases, disease prevention,
symptoms, available treatment options and healthy
behaviors. The programmes can be delivered in
seminars, workshops, public talks, webinars, expert
talks and awareness days. Educational
communication can help a hospital's branding to
convey credible health information. When the
information is correctly provided, transparent and
focused on the patient's well-being, and not only on
promoting the organisation, this can help build trust
(Alhomoud et al, 2025).

There is an increasing trend for corporate hospitals to

directions for drug use, measures to avoid, and

mobile apps, webinars, discharge information, and
face-to-face with health care professionals. The
educational function of the hospital is especially
significant, since the quality of the information on the

5. Digital Marketing Strategies in Healthcare
Digital transformation has significantly altered
patient search behaviour and expectations for health
information, their perception of health services, their
interactions with hospital staff and systems, their
appointment booking experiences and how they share
their experiences. This makes digital marketing a
vital part of the modern communication and
reputation management strategy for hospitals
(Vettriselvan, 2025).
5.1 Hospital websites
The hospital website frequently functions as the
central digital information platform. It can provide
information concerning:
 medical departments;
 physicians;
 clinical specialties;
 diagnostic services;
 hospital facilities;
 locations;
 appointment procedures;
 health education;
 emergency services;
 insurance information; and
 patient support services.
A professionally designed website can reduce
information barriers and strengthen perceived
organizational credibility. However, website content
should be accurate, updated, accessible, and
transparent (Bach et al., 2019).
5.2 Search-engine marketing
Patients increasingly use search engines to locate
hospitals, physicians, sp ecialties, symptoms,
treatment options, and healthcare information.
Search-engine optimization and paid search
advertising can therefore increase hospital visibility
when patients actively seek relevant services. Search
visibility, however, should not substitute for the
credibility of the underlying information. Hospitals
should ensure that search-oriented content directs
patients toward reliable and evidence -based
information (Zenone et al., 2024).
5.3 Social media marketing
Social media provides hospitals with opportunities
for two-way communication rather than one-way
advertising. Hospitals can use social platforms to
disseminate health education, public -health
messages, physician interviews, event information,
patient-support resources, and institutional updates.
Social media can also facilitate interaction and
feedback. Nevertheless, hospitals must manage
patient confidentiality, professional boundaries,
misinformation, and the potential misuse of patient-
generated content (Bikowski, 2026).
5.4 Content marketing
Healthcare content marketing involves producing
informative material designed to address patient
information needs. Examples include:
 disease-awareness articles;
 expert interviews;
 FAQs;
 preventive-health information;
 treatment explanations;
 rehabilitation guidance;
 nutrition information; and
 patient education videos.
Content marketing can contribute to hospital
credibility when information is clinically accurate,
understandable, appropriately referenced, and
regularly updated (Şenyapar and Nurgül, 2024).

online environment. Reviews can be useful for
patients when they are deciding on a hospital or
doctor. The findings, from a systematic review of 32

inclusion need to be taken into account (Oldenburg et

5.8 Telemedicine promotion
With the advent of telemedicine, hospitals have new
options for consultations and follow-up care. Digital
consultation platforms can make it easier for patients
who might have geographical, mobility or time
related issues. The promotion of telemedicine should
explicitly identify services suitable for
teleconsultation and when in -person clinical
assessment is required. This is crucial for patient
safety and reasonable expectations (Ezeamii et al.,
2024).
5.9 Online appointment systems
Online appointment systems make it easier for people
to access it, enabling them to choose the department,
doctors, date and time slots. Reminders and digital
registration and follow up communication can
enhance convenience. Accessibility is a component of
the patient experience, so appointment technology
can have an indirect impact on the perception of the
hospital's brand. Even as the hospital promotes its
brand, it may have a bad experience getting an

communication, but has bad appointment systems

Chatbots, automated appointment reminders,
personalized reminders, recommendation systems,
patient-navigation tools, and the analysis of patient
feedback are all examples of ways that artificial
intelligence is relevant in health care communication.
When properly designed, AI-driven communication
can enhance responsiveness and personalization. But
healthcare organizations have to differentiate between
administrative or information AI from clinical
decision making. When AI systems are designed for
patient interactions, there is a need for privacy
protection, transparency, proper human oversight
mechanisms, escalation procedures, and measures to
prevent the dissemination of inaccurate or misleading
information (Li et al., 2024).
6. Patient-Centric Branding Strategies
It is a change from an organization-centred to a one
that incorporates the needs, expectations,
experiences, participation, and well-being of patients
into the organization's strategy: patient -centric
branding. This is the approach that involves branding
not only in the communication activities but
throughout the patient journey (Aguirre -Duarte,
2025).
6.1 Patient experience
Patient experience is the sum of perceptions that a
patient has as a result of his or her experiences with
the health care organization. It encompasses
accessibility, communication, waiting, clinical, staff
behaviour, physical environment, coordination,
discharge and follow -up. Communication is
particularly crucial. In a systematic review of patient
trust and physician communication conducted in
2026, the authors made the following observations:
Clear information delivery, empathy, active listening,
shared decision-making, and supportive non-verbal
behaviours were generally linked to greater patient
trust with differing effects across clinical and
technological circumstances. Therefore, patient
experience should be treated as a key element of
brand building instead of just a measure for quality
assurance (Zhang et al., 2016; Kumar, 2026).
6.2 Communication and transparency
Transparent communication is the giving of
understandable and timely information about the
diagnosis, treatment options, likely treatment
pathway, risks, costs, waiting time, follow up and
other elements of care. Transparency helps to

6.3 Accessibility and convenience
Accessibility encompasses physical location,

mitigate administrative challenges (Fortney et al.,

Personalization is about modifying communication
and services to the patient's situational context,
preferences, clinical needs and healthcare journey.
This can include appointment reminders,
communication in multiple languages, patie nt
education tailored to the individual, follow up
(personalized) and navigation support.
Personalization is possible, but there needs to be a
balance between personalization and privacy, and
between personalization and inappropriate automated
recommendations, in hospitals.
6.5 Continuity of care
Continuity is about coordinating health care between
consultations, departments, professionals and at
different stages of care. Especially critical for patients
with chronic and/or complex conditions. Continuity
can boost confidence from a branding point of view
because the patient feels this is a coordinated health
care system rather than a series of independent
services. The procedure for providing feedback and
raising complaints is good. Feedback from patients
can help hospitals understand the strengths and
weaknesses of their services, as well as what they
need to improve and what issues are occurring
repeatedly. Feedback systems could include
complaints management systems, suggestion
systems, patient surveys, digital feedback platforms
and post-discharge communication. A good grievance
procedure should not only gather grievances, it
should be designed to accept, investigate a grievance,
respond to it, provide for corrective action and for
learning from the grievance by the organisation
(Mirzoev and Kane, 2018).
6.7 Patient engagement
Patient engagement is when patients are more
involved in decisions about their health and
healthcare. Shared decision-making is an important
aspect of engagement. Shared decision-making, as a
form of participatory approach to healthcare
relationships, was the focus of a recent scoping
review of interventions to strengthen these
relationships and those that build trust and
trustworthiness. Patients' engagement can enhance
the brand relationship as they feel more engaged,
respected and owned with the healthcare system
(Vahdat et al., 2014).
6.8 Trust and emotional connection
Healthcare decisions frequently involve uncertainty,
vulnerability, fear, and emotional stress.
Consequently, a hospital's relationship with patients
is not purely transactional. Empathy, compassion,
respectful communication, responsiveness, and
continuity can contribute to an emotional connection
between patients and healthcare providers. However,
emotional branding in healthcare must remain
ethically grounded. Emotional communication should
support patients rather than exploit fear, anxiety, or
vulnerability. Sustainable emotional connection is
more appropriately developed through authentic
patient-centred care than t hrough promotional
messaging alone (Berry et al., 2017).

7. Service Quality and Hospital Brand Perception
Patient satisfaction with service quality is one of the
most critical factors that patients use to gauge the
healthcare organization and is now a key element of a
hospital's branding strategy. Healthcare services are
assessed in many different ways, such as clinical
encounters, interpersonal interactions, organizational
processes, communication, accessibility, and physical
environments, compared to tangible products.
Patients might therefore judge the quality of the
hospital treatment and care services not just by the
outcome of care but also by the reliability,
responsiveness, professionalism, empathic and
physical aspects of the service experience.
Parasuraman et al. (1985) offered a framework that
was widely accepted for measuring the dimensions of
perceived service quality: reliability, responsiveness,
assurance, empathy, and tangibles. While the
dimensions are specific to healthcare services, they
can be applied to assessing patient evaluations of
service interactions and eventually forming
perceptions toward the healthcare organization ( Wu,
2011).
7.1 Reliability
Reliability is the capacity of a healthcare organisation
to provide the services it has guaranteed, accurately,
consistently and dependably. Reliability in the
hospital setting could be measured in the accuracy of
appointments, diagnostic information, treatment
processes, communication, documentation, billing,
and follow-up information. The services
communicated to patients by the hospital are
expected to be consistent and the second failure in the
administrative or clinical procedure may affect the
perception of the competence of the hospital.
Therefore, reliability is one of the factors that will
enhance the credibility of the hospital brand since the
promotion of the product based on quality, advanced
facilities, patient centred care or the special service
that a hospital offers will only be meaningful if the
service delivery is reliable. When a hospital
consistently delivers on promises, it has a better
chance of building a consistent and reliable
reputation with patients and other stakeholders.
7.2 Responsiveness
Responsiveness is the readiness and capacity of
health care providers and organisations to respond
quickly and meet the needs of patients. Examples of
responsiveness in hospitals include appointment
management, waiting times, emergency
communication, availability of information,
responding to patients' inquiries, and responding to
complaints/grievances. Quick and courteous
responses can be seen as a sign of the organization's
respect for the patient's time, concerns and well-
being.Rapid and courteous responses can be seen as
caring for the patient's time, concerns and well-being.
On the other hand, a lack of communication, delays

7.3 Assurance
Assurance is the knowledge, competence, courtesy,
professionalism and credibility of health care
workers. The dimension is especially relevant in
healthcare where patients may not necessarily
possess the technical information to assess the
clinical appropriateness of diagnosis and treatment on
their own. Therefore, professional qualifications,
communication, confidence, ethical behaviors and
explaining medical information to patients/patients'
safety can have an impact on the patients' perception
of competence and safety. Assurance can thus directly
influence patient trust, which is one of the most
important factors of the hospital's brand perception. A
patients' trust in the organisation may grow if they
feel confident in the knowledge, respect,
transparency and competence of healthcare
professionals. So, professional assurance isn't just
about the individual health care professional; it's
about the whole hospital brand.
7.4 Empathy
Empathy is giving care and attention in a
personalized, understanding, compassionate and
sensitive way to the needs and circumstances of the
patient. Interpersonal behaviour is especially
significant in healthcare settings, where patients are
often anxious, vulnerable and sick. Patients can
appreciate the listening ear, the willingness to take
the time to listen to their concerns, the ability to
communicate in terms understandable to them, and
sensitivity to their personal situation from the part of
the health care provider. Empathy can thus play an
important role in the patient experience and the
image of the hospital. The key to a patient-centred
spirit in a hospital is the consistent approach of
doctors, nurses, administration, and other personnel.
Previous studies in the area of physician
communication have shown that patients tend to feel
better trusting their doctors when they are given clear
information, when doctors are empathetic to the
patient, when they listen to the patient, and when the
doctor and the patient make decisions together,
although these relationships are not always as robust
as other settings and technologies.
7.5 Tangibles
Tangibles are all the physical and visible elements of
healthcare service delivery such as the hospital
infrastructure, cleanliness, waiting areas, equipment,
signage, staff appearance, patient rooms, diagnostic
facilities, and digital interfaces. Healthcare is an
intangible product and so the initial impression of
organizational quality can be perceived through
factors that are observable in the physical product.
Improved infrastructure, hospital facilities,
cleanliness, adequate signage, easy accessibility, and
sophisticated equipment, therefore, can help to
enhance hospital image. However, a sustainable
hospital brand can't be achieved solely through
physical infrastructure. While advanced facilities
might grab the attention of patients initially, the
experience they have during the patient journey will
ultimately shape their perception of the clinic.
7.6 Service quality, patient satisfaction, trust and
hospital brand perception
The five dimensions of service quality can be linked
with a chain of patient reactions that can include how
they perceive reliability, responsiveness, assurance,
empathy, and tangibles, which can lead to their
perception of service quality and finally to levels of
patient satisfaction and trust. Satisfaction is the
judgment the patient makes about the healthcare
experience, whether it met or exceeded expectations,
while trust is the patient's confidence in the
competence, reliability, honesty and intentions of the
healthcare organization and practitioners. These
factors can help build a positive hospital image and
can then affect patient loyalty, desire to return to the
hospital and referral. There are some studies related
to hospital brand equity that have identified some
interrelated components of hospital branding such as
perceived quality, hospital image, patient satisfaction,
trust, and loyalty. Here, these relationships must not
be considered as linear in all patients, as severity of
the disease, clinical outcomes, expectations, cost,
availability, past experiences, and other factors may
influence patient responses. However, the literature
confirms that service quality plays a central role in
creating "credible and patient -centred hospital
brands".

credibility. Patients may not be able to evaluate all
the aspects of clinical competence, making
professional behaviour and communication a reliable
sign of reliability and expertise.
Healthcare providers and patients are not just one
time service providers and consumers, then. These
principles of continuity, empathy, respect, shared
decision-making and responsiveness can enhance the
patient–provider relationship, especially in chronic
disease management, long -term treatment,
rehabilitation and repeated patient –provider
interactions. As far as branding, these relationships
can elevate the hospital from being just a service
provider to a trusted health care partner. Healthcare
practitioners should thus be actively involved in
brand creation, as they are seen in their daily
interaction with patients to either reinforce or
challenge the organisational brand identity sent out
by formal marketing efforts.
9. Role of Patient Reviews and Word-of-Mouth
Traditionally, word of mouth has played a significant
role in shaping consumer perceptions and purchasing
behaviours, but in the healthcare context, the
importance of word of mouth has increased in
relevance due to patients' ability to share their
experiences via digital platforms. Electronic word-of-
mouth is a form of digital communication that refers
to online reviews and ratings, social media
discussions, patient comments and testimonials,
discussion forums, and other interactions about
patient experiences. Patient-produced data differs
from the formal advertising of the hospital, as that is
the hospital's communication, but rather it is
information about the hospital from the patient's
perspective. Thus, eWOM can impact hospital
awareness, expectations, perceived credibility,
provider selection, reputation, trust, and behavioural
intentions.
There is a huge visibility of patient reviews,
especially via search engines and review sites online.
Hospitals' ratings, written comments, photos and
responses can be a consideration for patients when
assessing healthcare providers. In a systematic review
of studies that investigated the relationship between
online patient reviews and patient experience
measures, the online reviews were consistently
associated with increased measures of patient
experience, while the correlation between online
reviews and objective measures of healthcare quality
varied. This distinction is crucial since an online
review is often based on a personal experience or
perception and does not necessarily indicate clinical
performance. However, reviews can give hospitals
valuable feedback on issues that concern patients and
areas which may be shortcomings in the provision of
services.
Social media has also helped to speed up and increase
patient communications. Reputations can be
improved by positive experiences and can help to
attract potential patients, while negative experiences
can be very contagious and can work against the
organization beyond the individual patient
experience. Therefore, reputation management is now
an integral part of hospital brand management. Good
reputation management shouldn't just be about trying
to squash negative information. Rather, hospitals

10. Ethical and Regulatory Issues
In the context of health care, ethics is a key factor to
consider because choices made in health care affect
human well-being, vulnerability, uncertainty, and
possible physical, psychological, and/or financial
consequences. Some marketing tactics that are
acceptable in generic commercial markets may offer
increased exposure in a healthcare environment.
Patients might not be able to assess the quality of
care, their clinical results, or the specific expertise of
the healthcare providers they are receiving, and could
thus be especially reliant on the information that they
are given by the healthcare organization. Ethical
hospital marketing should therefore be founded on
the principles of truthfulness, transparency, evidence,
patient autonomy, confidentiality, fairness and
professional responsibility.
It is essential that hospitals be able to distinguish
between factual information, evidence-based claims,
professional opinions, promotional claims, and
individual patient experiences when developing the
content of their healthcare advertisements. Fear,
anxiety, sickness or vulnerability cannot be used as a
means of communication in a promotional campaign
to induce a change in health-care programs. Any
statements of treatment success, cure rates, specialist
skills or advanced technology, or claims for
superiority should be supported by appropriate
evidence. Overstated claims or promises regarding a
treatment's results can lead to false expectations and
potentially cause harm to an organization's reputation
if the reality of the treatment is not what is being
advertised. Ethical communication also needs to be
accurate as well as the right way to show uncertainty
and limitations.
As the healthcare marketing landscape becomes
increasingly digital, patient privacy is a major
concern. Sensitive personal and medical information
is frequently handled by hospitals, and the presence
of websites, social media, mobile apps, online
appointment booking systems, patient reviews and
online communication mechanisms adds to privacy
concerns. Patient photographs, videos, treatment
information, testimonials and other identifiable
information should only be used with the proper
consent and protection. It is especially critical to
protect privacy since a breach of confidentiality can
have a impact both on individual patients and on the
reputation of the healthcare enterprise.
There is a need for the same care in handling patient
testimonials. While testimonials can lend credibility
to a hospital's communication, patients should be free
to give them and should be made aware of how their
information, images, or words will be used.
Testimonials should not suggest a particular result or
make it appear that an extraordinary experience is a
typical result, without providing context. Hospitals
should also refrain from communication tactics that
might encourage patients to endorse good things.
Another one of the key points is the
commercialisation of healthcare. Healthcare is also in
a competitive market, and requires sustainable
models that are financially viable, but the decision to
buy a healthcare service is fundamentally different
from a normal consumer purchase. If an
overcommercial orientation leads to excessive use of
the healthcare system, the decisions made by
healthcare professionals, or the interests of the
organization are influenced, then this can raise
questions. It is therefore essential to strike the right
balance between commercial and professional
responsibilities in healthcare marketing.
Patients are also more likely to trust a provider when
there is transparency about costs and information
regarding services. Clear information regarding
consultation fees, diagnostic costs, treatment

11. Challenges in Hospital Brand Building
Corporate hospitals operate in increasingly
competitive healthcare environments in which
multiple organizations compete through clinical
specialization, infrastructure, technology, physician
expertise, accessibility, patient experience, digital
services, and reputation. High competition creates
difficulties in developing distinctive and sustainable
brand identities because similar hospitals may offer
comparable technologies, specialties, facilities, and
promotional campaigns. Differentiation based solely
on advertising or infrastructure may therefore provide
limited long -term advantage. Sustainable
differentiation increasingly depends on the
consistency and quality of the patient experience.
Increasing patient expectations represent another
major challenge. Patients have greater access to
health information and increasingly expect
convenient appointments, shorter waiting times,
transparent pricing, digital reports, responsive
communication, telemedicine, personalized services,
and continuous follow-up. These expectations have
expanded the meaning of healthcare quality beyond
clinical treatment alone. Hospitals must therefore
continually adapt their service systems while
maintaining appropriate standards  of safety,
professional competence, and ethical practice.
The growth of online reviews has created additional
challenges for reputation management. A negative
experience can become publicly visible and may
influence prospective patients who encounter the
review during their search for healthcare services.
However, the appropriate response to negative
reviews should not simply be the removal or
suppression of criticism. Instead, hospitals should
determine whether complaints identify genuine
organizational weaknesses and use such information
as an opportunity for service improvement. At the
same time, individual online reviews should be
interpreted cautiously because they may not represent
the experiences of the wider patient population.
Maintaining consistent service quality across large
corporate hospitals is also challenging because these
organizations may involve multiple departments,
specialists, branches, administrative units, and
service processes. A hospital may develop a strong
promotional identity around advanced technology,
compassionate care, or patient -centredness, but
inconsistent service encounters can create a gap
between the intended brand identity and the patient's
actual brand experience. Consistency therefore
requires organizational coordination, employee
training, service-quality monitoring, and effective
internal communication.
Digital misinformation presents another emerging
challenge. Patients increasingly use search engines
and social-media platforms to obtain health
information, but the digital environment contains
information of varying accuracy and credibility.
Hospitals must therefore compete for visibility while
ensuring that their own communication remains
evidence-based, understandable, and transparent.
This requires appropriate clinical oversight of digital
content, responsible use of health information, and
clear differentiation between general educational
information and individualized medical advice.
Privacy and data-security concerns have also become
more significant as hospitals increasingly rely on
digital platforms, mobile applications, telemedicine
systems, online appointment services, patient portals,
and AI-enabled communication. Personalization can
improve convenience and engagement, but it may
require the collection and processing of sensitive
information. Hospitals therefore need appropriate
data-governance mechanisms to ensure that
technological innovation does not compromise
patient confidentiality or trust.
A further challenge is the potential erosion of trust
when patients perceive inconsistencies between
promotional claims and actual experiences. Trust may
be weakened by unclear communication, unexpected
costs, long waiting times, poor coordination,
perceived conflicts of interest, or dissatisfaction with
service delivery. Because trust develops gradually
through repeated interactions but can be damaged by
individual negative experiences, hospitals must
approach trust as a continuous organizational
responsibility rather than as an outcome that can be
achieved through advertising alone.
Finally, corporate hospitals must balance commercial
objectives with patient welfare. Financial
sustainability and competitive positioning are
legitimate organizational considerations, but they
should not compromise patient autonomy, informed
decision-making, confidentiality, clinical
appropriateness, or professional ethics. The long-term
sustainability of hospital branding therefore depends
on the organization's ability to integrate commercial
objectives with responsible healthcare delivery.
Overall, the literature suggests that hospital brand
building is fundamentally an experience-based and
relationship-oriented process. Promotional
communication can generate awareness and shape
initial expectations, but actual service quality,
professional interactions, patient experience, trust,

12. Conclusion
The present review highlights that hospital brand
building has evolved considerably from conventional
promotional activities toward a broader, patient -
centred approach involving service quality, healthcare
professionals, patient experience, trust, digital
engagement, and organizational reputation. The
increasing competition among corporate hospitals,
changing patient expectations, expansion of private
healthcare services, and rapid growth of digital
communication have made effective healthcare
promotion an important component of organizational
strategy. However, the literature indicates that
promotion alone is insufficient to establish a
sustainable hospital brand. Advertising and
communication activities may create awareness and
shape initial expectations, but the long -term
perception of a hospital depends largely on the
quality and consistency of the services experienced
by patients. Service quality emerges as  a central
foundation of hospital brand perception. Reliability,
responsiveness, assurance, empathy, and tangibles
influence how patients evaluate healthcare
organizations and contribute to satisfaction, trust,
perceived hospital image, loyalty, and
recommendation behaviour. The role of healthcare
professionals is equally important because
physicians, nurses, administrative personnel, and
other staff members represent the hospital through
their everyday interactions with patients. Professional
expertise, effective communication, empathy,
responsiveness, and credibility can strengthen the
relationship between patients and healthcare
organizations and thereby contribute to institutional
reputation and brand equity.
The review also demonstrates the increasing
importance of digital technologies in healthcare
promotion and brand management. Hospital websites,
search engines, social media, online reviews,
educational videos, mobile applications, telemedicine
platforms, online appointment systems, and AI -
enabled communication have expanded the ways
through which hospitals interact with existing and
prospective patients. These technologies provide
opportunities to improve accessibility, information
delivery, engagement, and personalization, but they
also introduce challenges related to misinformation,
privacy, cybersecurity, digital inequality, and the
responsible use of patient information. Online patient
reviews and electronic word -of-mouth have
particularly strengthened the influence of patient
experiences on hospital reputation, making service
quality and reputation management increasingly
interconnected.
Ethical responsibility remains fundamental to
sustainable healthcare promotion. Accuracy of
promotional claims, transparency in pricing and
services, protection of patient privacy, responsible
use of testimonials, evidence-based communication,
and avoidance of misleading or exaggerated claims
are essential for maintaining credibility. Corporate
hospitals must therefore balance commercial and
competitive objectives with professional obligations
and patient welfare. A hospital brand that is strongly
promoted but inconsistently experienced may
generate short-term awareness without establishing
long-term trust. In contrast, a brand supported by
reliable service delivery, professional competence,
transparent communication, positive patient
experiences, and ethical organizational practices can
contribute to stronger and more sustainable patient
relationships.
Future research should increasingly adopt patient-
centred and evidence-based approaches to examine
how traditional promotion, digital marketing, service
quality, professional interactions, patient reviews,
trust, and brand loyalty operate together. Greater
empirical attention is also required to understand
differences across regions, hospital types, patient
groups, and healthcare specialties. In particular,
context-specific studies involving corporate hospitals
and patients in regional healthcare markets can
provide valuable evidence regarding the relative
importance of promotional strategies, service quality,
digital reputation, and patient experience in hospital
brand development. Such research can assist
healthcare organizations in designing promotional
strategies that are not only commercially effective but
also ethically responsible, patient -centred, and
aligned with the long-term objective of delivering
trustworthy and high-quality healthcare services.
