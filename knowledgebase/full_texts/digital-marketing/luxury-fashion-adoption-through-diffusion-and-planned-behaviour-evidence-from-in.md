Abstract:
Background: Luxury-fashion adoption can no longer be explained by a single direction of diffusion because elite emulation,
street-originated innovation, peer transmission, and digital marketing operate concurrently. The aim of the study: To test a
hybrid model that integrates trickle-down, trickle-up, and trickle-across fashion diffusion with the Theory of Planned Behaviour
(TPB) for consumers in India’s National Capital Region (NCR). Materials and Methods: Survey responses from 800 luxury -
fashion consumers were analysed through confirmatory factor analysis and structural equation modelling using the robust
weighted least -squares mean - and variance -adjusted estimator for ordinal five -point items. Reliability, convergent and
discriminant validity, global fit,  standardized paths, explained variance, and bootstrapped mediation effects were evaluated.
Results: The model demonstrated excellent fit ( χ²/df=1.49, CFI=.961, TLI=.957, RMSEA=.032, SRMR=.038). Attitude
toward luxury fashion was explained strongly (R²=.72), and purchase intention was explained at R²=.63. Fashion involvement
had the largest antecedent association with attitude ( β=.27), followed by social media marketing ( β=.21). Attitude predicted
purchase intention (β=.56), while subjective norm (β=.17), perceived behavioural control (β=.16), and the direct social-media
path (β=.17) were also significant. All three diffusion mechanisms significantly predicted attitude. Conclusions: Luxury-fashion
adoption in NCR is a hybrid process in which diffusion routes  shape attitudes and TPB mechanisms convert those attitudes
and social conditions into purchase intention.

Keywords: attitude; luxury fashion; purchase intention; social media marketing; structural equation modelling; theory of
planned behaviour; triple-trickle diffusion

1. INTRODUCTION

Luxury-fashion markets combine symbolic status, aesthetic
involvement, identity expression, peer visibility, and practical
purchase considerations. These mechanisms are particularly
salient in India’s National Capital Region (NCR), where
established affluent consumers, mobile professional groups,
youth cultures, designer retail, global brands, and social-media
communities coexist. Luxury consumption in this setting is
therefore unlikely to follow one uniform route. It can descend
from high-status reference groups, rise from youth or street
contexts, and move horizontally across peer groups at the
same time.  Classical trickle -down explanations describe
fashion as a cycle of differentiation and imitation: elite groups
adopt distinctive styles, broader groups emulate them, and
elites then seek new distinctions (Simmel, 1904; Veblen,
1899). Trickle -across theory challenged an exclusively
hierarchical account by proposing simultaneous diffusion
among social strata and peer networks (King, 1963). Trickle-
up or status -float accounts further recognise that styles can
originate in youth, occupational, street, or subcultural groups
before being appropriated by mainstream and luxury markets
(Field, 1970; Polhemus, 1994). Contemporary fashion
systems make these directions complementary rather than
mutually exclusive. Mohr et al. (2022) accordingly proposed a
triple-trickle perspective in which several diffusion routes
interact.

Individual differences determine which cues become
meaningful. Fashion innovativeness reflects a tendency to
adopt fashion earlier than others, whereas fashion opinion
leadership concerns interpersonal influence over others’
choices. Fashion involvement captures the personal relevance
and cognitive -emotional importance of fashion. Consumer
innovativeness reflects a broader openness to new products,
and brand consciousness indicates attention to recognised
and prestigious brands (Beaudoin et al., 2003; Golds mith &
Hofacker, 1991; Workman & Lee, 2017b). Status
consumption makes luxury products valuable as publicly
legible signs of achievement and position (Wiedmann et al.,
2009). These orientations can form favourable evaluations of
luxury fashion through different psychological routes.

Social media compresses the time and distance separating
diffusion routes. Luxury brands use entertainment,
interaction, trendiness, customisation, and electronic word of
mouth to create brand experience and customer equity (Kim
& Ko, 2012). The same envir onment exposes consumers to
celebrity and elite cues, emerging street styles, peer
endorsements, product information, and accessible purchase
interfaces. Social media marketing may therefore influence
attitude directly and may also retain a direct relationship with
purchase intention through repeated exposure, convenience
cues, and calls to action.

The Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) provides the
conversion layer of the proposed model. Behavioural
intention is shaped by attitude toward the behaviour,
subjective norm, and perceived behavioural control (Ajzen,
1991). Attitude captures the overall fav ourable or
unfavourable evaluation of purchasing luxury fashion.
Subjective norm reflects perceived social approval, while
perceived behavioural control reflects the perceived
resources, opportunities, and capacity to complete the
purchase. Luxury-fashion studies have repeatedly linked self-
and brand-related evaluations with purchase intentions, but
diffusion direction and TPB mechanisms are seldom
estimated within one model (Bian & Forsythe, 2012;
Giovannini et al., 2015; Zhang & Kim, 2013).

The model addresses this gap by treating fashion
innovativeness, opinion leadership, involvement, status
consumption, consumer innovativeness, brand
consciousness, social media marketing, and the three trickle
mechanisms as antecedents of attitude. Attitud e, subjective
norm, and perceived behavioural control then predict
purchase intention, while social media marketing retains a
direct path to purchase intention. This arrangement tests
whether multiple fashion -diffusion mechanisms help form
evaluation and w hether TPB variables translate evaluation
and social feasibility into intention.

The aim of the study. The study aimed to develop and test a
reliable and valid hybrid structural model of luxury -fashion
adoption among NCR consumers by integrating triple-trickle
diffusion, consumer orientations, social media marketing, and
TPB. Specifica lly, it examined measurement quality, model
fit, direct structural paths, variance explained in attitude and
purchase intention, and attitude-mediated effects on purchase
intention.

2. MATERIAL AND METHODS

2.1 Research Design and Participants

2.2.1 Sampling and Recruitment

A cross -sectional quantitative survey design was used. The
unit of analysis was the individual luxury-fashion consumer in
India’s National Capital Region (NCR). Because no complete
and auditable sampling frame of NCR luxury -fashion
purchasers was available , probability sampling was not
claimed. Recruitment therefore used a non -probability
combination of purposive outreach, respondent referral and
location-based procedures. Eligibility was defined
behaviourally and geographically: participants had to be NCR
residents and to have previously purchased luxury -fashion
clothing. Fieldwork extended for approximately six months.
Roughly 5,000 potential respondents were approached and
about one -fifth declined participation. Willing participants
were screened first for prior luxury -fashion purchase.
Respondents who had never  purchased luxury -fashion
clothing were excluded. Completed questionnaires were then
checked for NCR residency, completeness and duplicate or
replicated participation; tourists and short -term business
visitors who were not NCR residents were also excluded.
After these sequential eligibility and quality checks, 800 usable
responses remained for analysis. Because category -specific
exclusion counts were not retained consistently, they are not
reconstructed retrospectively. The final usable yield was 800
of approximately 5,000 persons approached (16%), while the
initial participation rate before eligibility and quality screening
was approximately 80%. Findings are therefore interpreted as
relationships within a large NCR luxury -consumer sample
rather than as population prevalence estimates.

2.2.2 Data Screening and Common-Method Bias

Data screening applied the study’s documented eligibility and
quality checks: prior luxury-fashion purchase, NCR residency,
questionnaire completeness and duplicate or replicated
participation. Non-resident tourists and short -term business
visitors were excluded. Because all principal constructs were
collected in one self-report survey, common-method variance
was assessed diagnostically rather than assumed absent.
Harman’s unrotated single-factor check showed that the first
factor accounted for 19.22% of total item variance, below the
conventional 50% warning threshold. This result indicates
that no single general factor dominated the covariance
structure, but it is treated only as a diagnostic because
Harman’s test cannot rule out common-method bias.

The analytical sample comprised Delhi (n=291, 36.4%),
Gurugram (n=159, 19.9%), Noida/Greater Noida (n=151,
18.9%), Ghaziabad (n=100, 12.5%) and Faridabad (n=99,
12.4%). Women represented 51.4% and men 48.6% of the
sample. The largest age groups were 26–30 years (26.0%) and
31–35 years (22.9%). Luxury-purchase frequency was 1/year
for 27.9%, 2 –3/year for 39.8%, 4 –6/year for 24.8% and
7+/year for 7.6%. Preferred channels included luxury
boutiques/stores (31.4%), mixed/omnichannel (30.4%),
brand websites/apps (13.1%), luxury e -commerce (13.1%)
and department stores/malls (12.0%).

2.2.3 Measures

The instrument contained 68 observed items measuring 14
constructs: Fashion Innovativeness (FIN, 3 items), Fashion
Opinion Leadership (FOL, 3), Fashion Involvement (FI, 5),
Status Consumption (STC, 4), Consumer Innovativeness
(CIN, 6), Brand Consciousness (BC, 6), Social Media
Marketing (SMM, 8), Trickle-Down Fashion (TD, 6), Trickle-
Up Fashion (TU, 6), Trickle -Across Fashion (TA, 6),
Subjective Norm (SN, 4), Perceived Behavioural Control
(PBC, 4), Attitude toward Luxury Fashion (AT, 4), and
Purchase Intention (PI, 3). Responses used a five-point Likert
format. CIN1 was negatively worded and reverse -coded
before analysis. Construct selection was grounded in
diffusion research, domain -specific innovativeness, luxury -
value research, social -media marketing, and  TPB (Ajzen,
1991; Goldsmith & Hofacker, 1991; Kim & Ko, 2012;
Rogers, 2003; Wiedmann et al., 2009).

2.2.4 Hypotheses

H1-H7 proposed that FIN, FOL, FI, STC, CIN, BC, and
SMM positively influence attitude toward luxury fashion,
respectively. H8 -H10 proposed that TD, TU, and TA
positively influence attitude. H11-H13 proposed that attitude,
subjective norm, and perceived behavioural control positively
influence purchase intention. H14 proposed a positive direct
effect of SMM on purchase intention. In addition, attitude
was expected to mediate the effects of FIN, FOL, FI, STC,
CIN, BC, SMM, TD, TU, and TA on purchase intention.

2.2 Analytical Strategy

2.2.1 Collinearity, Parsimony and Robustness
Diagnostics

Because ten conceptually related constructs predict Attitude
in parallel, model parsimony and multicollinearity were
treated as explicit robustness issues. Potential
multicollinearity was examined using the full fourteen -factor
CFA latent-correlation matrix. The largest latent correlation
among the ten Attitude antecedents was .418 (SMM with TA),
and the largest correlation in the complete fourteen-construct
matrix was .482 (AT with PI). Latent-predictor VIFs derived
from the ten-predictor CFA correlation m atrix ranged from
1.07 to 1.40; an independent construct -score check ranged
from 1.06 to 1.33. These values do not indicate problematic
multicollinearity or construct redundancy. A higher -order
factor was considered as a possible parsimony alternative but
was not imposed because the empirical overlap was low and
the constructs represent theoretically distinct mechanisms:
domain-specific early adoption (FIN), interpersonal influence
(FOL), personal relevance (FI), status signalling (STC),
general novelty orientation (CIN), reliance on prestige-brand
cues (BC), digital marketing exposure (SMM), and three
different directions of fashion diffusion (TD, TU and TA).

The complete latent correlation/Fornell –Larcker matrix is
reported in Supplementary Table S2. Supplementary Table S1
reports the latent -predictor VIFs together with a construct -
score incremental effect -size sensitivity analysis. These
diagnostics supplement, but do not replace, the primary latent
WLSMV estimates.

2.2.2 Practical Significance and Effect Sizes

Because N=800 can make small coefficients statistically
significant, structural interpretation distinguishes statistical
significance from substantive magnitude. Incremental
Cohen-type f² values were obtained from construct -score
regressions by comparing t he full Attitude model with
reduced models omitting one predictor at a time. Sensitivity
f² values were FIN=.022, FOL=.000, FI=.017, STC=.062,
CIN=.004, BC=.016, SMM=.050, TD=.005, TU=.024 and
TA=.005. Using conventional benchmarks of approximately
.02, .15 and .35 for small, medium and large effects, TU, FIN,
STC and SMM showed small incremental effects, whereas the
remaining effects were negligible to very small. These
construct-score diagnostics are used only to qualify practical
magnitude and do not rep lace the latent WLSMV path
estimates.

Structural equation modelling was specified in JASP -
compatible lavaan syntax. Because the indicators were ordinal
five-point responses, estimation used robust weighted least
squares with mean - and variance -adjusted test statistics
(WLSMV). The analysis ass essed standardized factor
loadings, Cronbach’s alpha, composite reliability (CR),
average variance extracted (AVE), heterotrait -monotrait
ratios (HTMT), global model fit, standardized structural
coefficients, R² values and indirect effects. Internal
consistency was considered acceptable at .70 or higher,
convergent validity at AVE=.50 or higher, and discriminant
validity at HTMT below .85. Global fit was interpreted jointly
using χ²/df, CFI, TLI, RMSEA and SRMR.

Standardized structural coefficients, robust standard errors, z
values and p values were reported. Explained variance was
assessed for Attitude and Purchase Intention. Indirect effects
through Attitude were estimated with bootstrap standard
errors and 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance
was evaluated at p<.05. The structural specification is
recursive: the antecedent and diffusion constructs predict
Attitude; Attitude, Subjective Norm, Perceived Behavioural
Control and Social Media Marketing predict Purchase
Intention. All estimates are interpreted as cross -sectional
associational evidence rather than causal effects.

3. RESULTS

3.1 Measurement Quality
All standardized factor loadings were statistically significant
at p<.001 and ranged from .70 to .88 in the displayed model.
Cronbach’s alpha ranged from .86 to .93 and composite
reliability from .88 to .93. AVE values ranged from .57 to .75,
exceeding the  recommended .50 threshold for every
construct. Maximum HTMT values ranged from .71 to .79,
below the conservative .85 criterion. The measures therefore
demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, convergent
validity, and discriminant validity (Table 1).

3.2 Global Model Fit

The hybrid latent model fitted the ordinal data well. The
robust chi-square was 3185.47 with 2143 degrees of freedom,
yielding χ²/df=1.49. CFI=.961 and TLI=.957 exceeded .95,
while RMSEA=.032 (90% CI [.029, .035]) and SRMR=.038
were comfortably below conve ntional upper limits. Taken
together, the indices indicate excellent fit rather than reliance
on any single statistic (Table 2).

3.3 Structural Paths and Hypothesis Tests

Every hypothesized structural path was positive and
statistically significant (Table 3). Fashion Involvement was
the strongest consumer -orientation predictor of Attitude
(β=.27), followed by Social Media Marketing ( β=.21),
Fashion Opinion Leadership ( β=.18), Brand Consciousness
(β=.16), Fashion Innovativeness ( β=.15), Status
Consumption ( β=.14) and Consumer Innovativeness
(β=.13). The three diffusion routes also predicted Attitude:
Trickle-Down ( β=.11), Trickle-Up ( β=.09) and Trickle -
Across (β=.08).

3.4 Mediation Through Attitude

All ten tested indirect effects on Purchase Intention through
Attitude were statistically significant because their 95%
confidence intervals excluded zero (Table 4). Fashion
Involvement produced the largest indirect effect ( β=.151),
followed by Social Medi a Marketing ( β=.118), Fashion
Opinion Leadership (β=.101), Brand Consciousness (β=.090)
and Fashion Innovativeness ( β=.084). The three diffusion
routes also influenced intention indirectly through Attitude:
TD β=.062, TU β=.050 and TA β=.045. These finding s
reinforce Attitude as the principal behavioural bridge
between fashion diffusion and purchase intention.

The direct effect of SMM on purchase intention was .170
(bootstrap SE=.042, 95% CI [.088, .257]). Its indirect effect
through attitude was .118 (bootstrap SE=.027, 95% CI [.070,
.170]). The resulting total effect was .288 (bootstrap SE=.054,

through partial mediation: it improves attitude while also

4. DISCUSSION

The results support a hybrid account of luxury -fashion
adoption. The excellent fit indices indicate that the
measurement and structural components reproduce the
observed covariance structure well. More importantly, the
model explains substantial variance i n both attitude (72%)
and purchase intention (63%). These values show that luxury
adoption in NCR is not reducible to status display, novelty
seeking, peer influence, or digital exposure alone. It emerges
from the simultaneous operation of personal orienta tions,
multidirectional diffusion, planned -behaviour mechanisms,
and social-media influence.

4.1 Multiple Diffusion Directions

The three diffusion routes should not be interpreted as three
mutually exclusive theories simultaneously ‘winning.’ Rather,
they describe different sources and directions of fashion
influence that can reach the same consumer at different
moments within the same product category. A consumer may
encounter elite or celebrity cues that operate in a Trickle -
Down direction, observe an emerging street - or youth -
originated style that later enters luxury collections through
Trickle-Up movement, and receive peer vali dation or
imitation cues through Trickle -Across diffusion.
Contemporary digital platforms compress these processes by
placing luxury houses, celebrities, creators, subcultures and
peer communities in the same information environment. The
same consumer can therefore be exposed to multiple
directional cues, while Attitude represents the evaluative
mechanism through which those cues are accepted, rejected
or integrated. This coexistence logic explains why TD, TU
and TA can remain distinct without being concept ually
contradictory.

The ordering of the diffusion coefficients—TD=.11, TU=.09
and TA=.08 —suggests only a modest advantage for
hierarchical diffusion in the surveyed luxury context. Their
statistical significance should not obscure their small practical
magnitude. The construct-score f² sensitivity values were .005
for TD, .024 for TU and .005 for TA. Their theoretical value
therefore lies mainly in showing that elite imitation is not the
sole route through which fashion meaning is formed.
Segment heterogeneity is a plausible b oundary condition:
younger or digitally embedded consumers may be more
receptive to Trickle -Up and Trickle -Across cues, whereas
older, status-oriented or strongly brand-conscious consumers
may rely more heavily on Trickle-Down cues. These segment
differences were not directly tested in the present cross -
sectional model and are therefore treated as future multigroup
or moderation hypotheses rather than established findings.

Trickle-down, trickle-up, and trickle -across fashion all have
significant positive paths to attitude. The ordering of their
coefficients—TD=.11, TU=.09, and TA=.08—suggests that
hierarchical diffusion remains slightly more influential in the
surveyed luxur y context, yet bottom -up and horizontal
transmission add unique explanatory value. This finding
aligns with the view that contemporary fashion systems
contain several simultaneous pathways rather than a universal
replacement of one diffusion theory by another (Mohr et al.,
2022). Elite symbolism remains relevant because luxury
brands preserve scarcity and prestige, but street culture,
emerging creators, and peer networks continually supply new
meanings and styles.

The diffusion effects are mediated through attitude rather
than directly specified to purchase intention. This provides a
psychologically coherent interpretation: exposure to a style’s
direction of movement does not automatically produce
intended purchase. Instead, consumers evaluate whether the
style is attractive, desirable, self -congruent, and worthwhile.
The significant indirect effects for TD, TU, and TA confirm
this evaluative mechanism.
4.2 Consumer Orientations and Attitude Formation

Fashion involvement is the largest antecedent of attitude.
Consumers for whom fashion is personally relevant are more
likely to attend to products, process fashion information, and
derive identity value from luxury offerings. Fashion opinion
leadership is the next strongest personal predictor, indicating
that consumers who influence others also form more
favourable attitudes toward luxury fashion. Brand
consciousness, fashion innovativeness, status consumption,
and consumer innovativeness all add significan t
contributions. The pattern is consistent with earlier findings
that luxury intention depends on both symbolic value and
consumer self-concept (Giovannini et al., 2015; Wiedmann et
al., 2009; Zhang & Kim, 2013).

The significance of all these paths should not be interpreted
as interchangeability. Fashion innovativeness concerns early
adoption within the domain; consumer innovativeness
reflects a broader novelty orientation; opinion leadership
concerns interpersonal  influence; involvement concerns
personal relevance; status consumption concerns visible
social value; and brand consciousness concerns reliance on
recognised brand cues. Their concurrent significance shows
that favourable luxury -fashion attitudes are mult iply
determined.
4.3 Social Media as Direct and Mediated Influence

Social media marketing has both direct and indirect effects on
purchase intention. Its total standardized effect of .288 is
meaningful because it combines an attitude-forming pathway
with a direct intention pathway. The indirect component is
consistent with digital content shaping evaluations through
entertainment, trend information, interaction, customisation,
and electronic word of mouth (Kim & Ko, 2012). The
remaining direct effect may capture calls to action, product
access, price and availability information, shoppable content,
repeated exposure, and other mechanisms not fully
represented by attitude.

For managers, this means that reach and impressions are
incomplete goals. Social content should deliberately support

the relevant diffusion route. Prestige storytelling, heritage,
celebrity association, and invitation -only experiences can
strengthen trickle -down cues. Collaborations with street
communities and emerging creators can activate trickle -up
movement. Peer test imonials, community styling, creator
conversations, and social proof can strengthen trickle -across
diffusion. Each content stream should also reduce
uncertainty and make evaluation easier.
4.4 Planned-Behaviour Conversion

Attitude is by far the strongest proximal predictor of purchase
intention, but subjective norm and perceived behavioural
control remain independently significant. Social approval is
relevant because luxury products are often publicly consumed
and evaluated . Perceived control is relevant because
favourable evaluation cannot become intention when
affordability, access, availability, payment options,
authenticity concerns, or channel friction make purchase
seem difficult. The result is consistent with TPB: motivation,
social expectation, and perceived feasibility jointly form
intention (Ajzen, 1991).

The practical implication is that luxury brands should
combine symbolic persuasion with enabling conditions.
Favourable attitude can be strengthened through
craftsmanship narratives, styling relevance, authenticity,
experience, and emotional value. Subject ive norms can be
influenced through credible communities and socially
respected endorsers. Perceived behavioural control can be
improved through transparent product information,
authenticated channels, assisted selling, availability visibility,
convenient appointments, delivery reliability, and payment
flexibility that does not dilute prestige.

4.5 Theoretical Contributions

Subgroup Heterogeneity as a Boundary Condition

The relative strength of TD, TU and TA may differ across
consumer segments, but the present model does not estimate
those path differences directly. Future multigroup analysis
should first establish measurement invariance and then
compare structural paths across age, gender, income, city,
purchase frequency and shopping channel. A particularly
relevant test is whether younger and digitally embedded
consumers show stronger TU/TA effects while older or
more status-oriented consumers show stronger TD effects.
Until such tests are completed, these differences remain
theoretically motivated boundary conditions rather than
empirical findings of the present study.

The study makes four contributions. First, it places three
directional diffusion mechanisms in a single latent model and
shows that each contributes uniquely to attitude. Second, it
joins fashion -diffusion theory to TPB, distinguishing the
social movement of fashion cues from their conversion into
purchase intention. Third, it demonstrates that social media
marketing works partly through attitude and partly through a
direct route, positioning digital communication as both an
evaluative and transactional infrastructure. Fourth, it provides
an ordinal -data SEM specification with strong reliability,
convergent validity, discriminant validity, and model fit,
offering a replicable basis for testing luxury-fashion adoption
in other metropolitan markets.

4.6 Limitations and Future Research

Robustness Qualification

Excellent global fit alone is not treated as evidence that every
direct path is substantively necessary. Additional robustness
diagnostics were therefore used to assess whether the ten -
predictor Attitude block was distorted by latent overlap. The
observed latent correlations were modest, the highest
predictor correlation was .418, and latent VIFs ranged from
1.07 to 1.40. These results do not indicate problematic
multicollinearity. A higher -order orientation model was
considered as a parsimony alternative, but it was not imposed
because the first -order constructs remain conceptually
distinct and empirically distinguishable. Small paths are
interpreted cautiously using the supplemental f² sensitivity
results rather than p-values alone.

The cross -sectional design does not establish temporal
causality or observe actual fashion -diffusion sequences, and
Purchase Intention is not equivalent to purchase behaviour.
Recruitment was non -probability based, combining
purposive, referral and locatio n-based procedures, so
probability-based population inference cannot be claimed. All
principal variables were measured by self -report in one
survey; although Harman’s first factor accounted for only
19.22% of variance, common -method bias cannot be ruled
out. Future studies should incorporate stronger procedural
separation, marker variables or latent -method-factor
approaches where feasible, as well as independent replication
and longitudinal purchase outcomes.

Future research should include actual purchase behaviour,
longitudinal observation around product launches, social -
network measures, influencer -source characteristics,
perceived authenticity, affordability, scarcity and channel
access. Multigroup or modera ted SEM can test whether
Trickle-Down is stronger among older or status -oriented
segments and whether Trickle -Up/Trickle-Across are
stronger among younger or digitally embedded consumers.
Experimental manipulation of the source and direction of
fashion cues would further strengthen causal inference.

5. CONCLUSIONS

Luxury-fashion adoption in India’s NCR is best understood
as a diffusion–TPB hybrid. Fashion innovativeness, opinion
leadership, involvement, status consumption, consumer
innovativeness, brand consciousness, social media marketing
and all three trickle mechanisms contribute to Attitude, while
Attitude, Subjective Norm, Perceived Behavioural Control
and Social Media Marketing explain Purchase Intention. The
model fits the ordinal data well and explains 72% of Attitude
and 63% of Purchase Intention. Fashion I nvolvement and
Social Media Marketing are the strongest antecedents of
Attitude, Attitude is the dominant predictor of intention, and
SMM operates through both direct and attitude -mediated
routes. Reviewer-requested robustness diagnostics show low
multicollinearity, while effect-size sensitivity analysis indicates
that the weakest diffusion paths are statistically distinct but

practically modest. The results therefore support a
multidirectional account of luxury -fashion adoption without
overstating the magnitude of every individual path.
