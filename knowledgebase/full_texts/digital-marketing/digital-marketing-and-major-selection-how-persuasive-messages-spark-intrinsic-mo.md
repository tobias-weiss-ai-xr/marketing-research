Abstract
This study investigates the impact of digital marketing persuasive messages on high school
students' intrinsic motivation in major selection. While higher education institutions utilize
aggressive digital campaigns focusing on extrinsic rewards, this research positions digital
marketing as a psychological variable influencing career orientation stability. Using PLS-SEM,
data were collected from 855 social-media-active students in Yogyakarta. The structural model
demonstrates robust predictive power, explaining a substantial variance in decision paralysis (R2
= 0.615) and a moderate variance in intrinsic motivation (R2 = 0.428). The results reveal that high
intensity of persuasive content and algorithmic exposure significantly trigger cognitive overload
(β = 0.425 and β = 0.380, respectively), which directly precipitates decision paralysis (β =
0.680). This mental fatigue subsequently erodes intrinsic motivation (β = -0.310; direct path β =
-0.150) and increases cognitive dissonance (β = 0.520). Critically, the findings demonstrate a
significant negative overall effect ( p<0.001), confirming that excessive, uniform digital
marketing produces a destructive psychological cycle, replacing pure interest with anxiety and
dependence on digital trends. The study concludes that universities must shift from quantity-
focused strategies to more authentic, curated information to foster genuine student interest and
reduce the risk of future academic burnout or dropout.
Keywords: cognitive overload, decision paralysis, digital marketing, intrinsic motivation, major
selection

INTRODUCTION
Higher education institutions increasingly use digital content to drive rapid
student enrollment through extrinsic promises, such as lucrative salaries and luxurious
facilities (Monazam-Tabrizi et al., 2026). This promotional trend fosters a transactional
perception, motivating prospective students by "what they will get" rather than "what
they want to become" through their education (Eom & Ashill, 2016; Satria et al., 2025).
Psychologically, selecting a university major should be driven by intrinsic motivation,
an internal impulse rooted in curiosity, learning enjoyment, and personal values (Kaloko
et al., 2025). However, constant exposure to extrinsic digital stimuli often overshadows
genuine student interest, leading individuals to choose financially prestigious majors
that mismatch their natural talents or intellectual capabilities (Nguyen et al., 2023; Orji
et al., 2024). This disparity between superficial marketing promises and the demanding
reality of the academic process poses severe l ong-term risks. When the actual
coursework falls short of stylized social media depictions, students lacking intrinsic
motivation easily experience boredom, disorientation, and academic decline.
Ultimately, homogeneous marketing that strictly equates major selection with financial
gain narrows the definition of success and undermines student perseverance.
The problem is that these persuasive messages often fail to accommodate the
diversity of individual potential. Digital campaigns that focus too much on ex trinsic
stimuli seem to suggest that "good" majors are only those that promise quick financial
security (Akgun et al., 2017; Dolinšek et al., 2024). This prevents students from
engaging in honest and in-depth self-exploration before making decisions. However,
according to Alannasir (2020) and Thilavongsa et al (2020), each student has unique
potential, talents, and characteristics. They should be able to maximize their abilities by
choosing a major that suits them.
Today, students find themselves in a situation where each platform has its own
persuasive qualities. TikTok boasts music trends, Instagram boasts aesthetic visuals, and
YouTube boasts alumni success stories. The problem is, when these persuasive
messages arrive simultaneously and incessantly, the frequency of information exceeds
students' cognitive capacity to process them deeply (Braca & Dondio, 2023). As a
result, intrinsic motivation or the desire that stems from deep interests and talents is
often drowned out by the noise of content that prioritizes visual appeal over academic
substance (Green et al., 2019). Rather than helping students make informed choices, the
abundance of persuasive messages often triggers anxiety. Psychologically, when people
are faced with too many choices and persuasive arguments, they tend to experience
decision-making paralysis. This is in line with the opinion of Misuraca et al (2024), who
stated that too many choices can make individuals fear making the wrong decision,
often causing them to struggle or even fail in making decisions. Furthermore, students
become uncertain whether they chose a major because they truly enjoyed it, or simply
because they frequently saw advertisements for it in their feeds (Khan et al., 2021).
The quality of decision-making declines because the selection process is no
longer based on critical, independent research, but rather on who has the loudest voice
on social media. Digital persuasive messages often employ FOMO (Fear of Missing
Out) techniques or promises of instant success. This creates a distortion where students
feel compelled to follow trending majors to be considered relevant. The main problem
is a shift in orientation: from "Who am I and what are my talents?" to "What content is
most compelling today?" (Braca & Dondio, 2023). This information saturation

In today's digital ecosystem, universities are no longer simply  educational
institutions but have transformed into brands that must compete for attention on
students' social media feeds. This competition has given rise to a campaign trend that
heavily emphasizes visual aspects or aesthetic branding (Siregar et al., 2025).
Persuasive messages are often packaged in slick cinematography, showcasing the ideal
student lifestyle, futuristic campus facilities, and seemingly instant testimonials of
success. However, behind this captivating content curation, a worrying gap emerges
digital marketing strategies tend to oversimplify the complexities of a major (Dibda &
Ahmad, 2025). For example, an Informatics Engineering major might be marketed with
sleek, modern-looking coding visuals, but rarely showcases the reality of mind-numbing
algorithmic logic or the hours of downtime lost to bug fixes. When persuasive messages
only highlight the beautiful result without showing the grueling process, students
develop unrealistic expectations.
A serious problem arises when students lured by  such content eventually
become university students. This is where cognitive dissonance occurs, a state of mental
discomfort when faced with reality (heavy study load, abstract theories, or exhausting
lab work). This is clearly in stark contrast to their initial beliefs formed by marketing
content. Students begin to question their decisions: "Did I choose the wrong major?" or
"Why isn't the reality as beautiful as it appears on Instagram?" Rather than fostering a
deep interest in science (intrinsic motivation), campaigns that focus too much on
extrinsic factors risk creating brittle motivation (Aldabbas et al., 2025). If a student's
initial motivation is driven solely by digitally enhanced aesthetics or prestige, then when
academic pressures mount, that motivation will quickly fade. The impact is not only felt
in declining academic performance but also in high burnout rates, potentially leading to
first-year dropout. In the long term, this imbalance creates a crisis of trust. Students who
feel trapped by the sweet promises of digital campaigns will become negative
ambassadors for the university. In an era of information transparency, negative
testimonials from disappointed students are far more powerful than any paid advertising
campaign (Mironica et al., 2024).
Generation Z grew up surrounded by massive digital information, which
indirectly created a natural radar for all forms of manipulative content. This
phenomenon presents a significant challenge for educational institutions in developing
marketing communication strategies. Gen Z tends to ignore content that initially appears
to be advertising (hard selling). Problems arise when universities use overly rigid
persuasive messages or excessive formality. For them, overly self -aggrandizing
narratives are often perceived as digital noise rather than useful information (Utami &
Kurniawan, 2026). This results in messages that should be motivating being ignored. A
closer look reveals a discrepancy between expectations and reality. When a campus
portrays a seemingly perfect and instant student lifestyle in its digital campaigns, Gen Z
tends to feel it's too good to be true. This skepticism prevents the message from
penetrating their intrinsic motivational system. Instead of feeling drawn to their interests
and talents, they feel lured, which then triggers their cognitive defense mechanisms to
reject the information. Effective persuasive messages should be directional, but they
often become coercive (Widyasari et al., 2019). In the context of choosing a major, the
choice is personal. The key challenge today is how educational institutions can craft

In today's digital ecosystem, social media algorithms work precisely to maintain
users' attention by presenting content relevant to their interaction history. For
prospective students, this mechanism indirectly creates a digital "echo chamber." When
a student begins to show interest in trending content about a particular major, the
algorithm automatically bombards their feed with similar content. Problems arise when
this "echo chamber" is no longer informative but instead becomes a barrier to insight.
Students who should have the freedom to explore their broad interests and talents are
instead trapped by the popularity of a particular major. The content presented by the
algorithm tends to glorify certain majors with high visual appeal or social prestige,
while other majors that may be more aligned with the student's hidden talents or
intelligence are marginalized from their knowledge (Prasetyoaji et al., 2026).
As a result, decision-making regarding major selection is no longer based on in-
depth self-reflection, but rather on digital social validation. Most students tend to adopt
the major choices that appear most frequently in algorithms because they feel that is the
prevailing standard of success (Prasetyoaji et al., 2022). Unconsciously, students' ability
to recognize their internal potential is reduced by content curation that favors
popularity, not personal abilities. This situation creates a paradox: despite today's open
and broad access to information, algorithms narrow students' perspectives. According to
Putri and Prasetyoaji (2025), this can certainly trigger the risk of mismatching major
choices with students' intrinsic abilities, potentially decreasing learning motivation,
increasing future dropout rates, or triggering career dissatisfaction because they choose
majors not based on their own desires, but rather due to the influence of the algorithm
bubble that continuously validates popular choices.

values (Prasetyoaji & Widyatmoko, 2021). Furthermore, there is a disparity between the
"aesthetics of content" on social media and the challenging academic reality. This

Self-Determination Theory (SDT) into marketing communication practices to offer
strategic solutions for higher education institutions to interact with Gen Z more

METHOD
This quantitative study utilizes Partial Least Squares Structural Equation
Modeling (PLS-SEM) to examine complex, latent psychological constructs. The model
includes six variables: Digital Persuasive Content (X1) and Algorithm Exposure (X2) as
exogenous variables; Cognitive Overload (M1) and Decision Paralysis (M2) as
mediators; and Intrinsic Motivation (Y1) and Cognitive Dissonance (Y2) as endogenous
variables (Hair et al., 2021; Rahadi, 2021). Because the study relies on a cross-sectional
design, the structural paths reflect associational relationships at a single point in time
rather than definitive causal links. The target population comprises 12th-grade students
in the Special Region of Yogyakarta. A sample of N= 855 was collected via online
convenience sampling. While efficient for large -scale data collection, this non -
probability technique introduces potential selection bias, as highly active digital media
users may be overrepresented.
To ensure data relevance, inclusion criteria required participants to be: (1) 12th-
grade students in Yogyakarta; (2) active on TikTok, Instagram, or YouTube within the
past 6 months; (3) engaged with higher education digital marketing content; and (4)
actively selecting a university major. Conversely, exclusion criteria ruled out students
who had already finalized binding university enrollments or who did not intend to
pursue tertiary education. Data were collected through a self-administered electronic
questionnaire. Measurement items were adapted from established literature: persuasive
design frameworks (Braca & Dondio, 2023), choice overload scales (Misuraca et al.,
2024), and Self-Determination Theory (SDT) metrics for intrinsic motivation. To align
with the higher education recruitment context, the initial draft underwent content
validation via expert judgment by a panel specializing in educational psychology,

To address potential Common Method Bias from single-source self-reporting,

confidentiality were guaranteed to reduce social desirability bias. Additionally, survey
items were structurally counterbalanced and separated into distinct thematic sections to
prevent patterned responses. Statistically, inner Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) were
reviewed during structural modeling under a full collinearity approach to ensure the
data remained uncorrupted by CMB. Based on an a priori power analysis using the "10-
times rule" and Cohen's guidelines, a model with a maximum of 4 structural predictors
and a standard expected effect size ƒ2=0.15 at α=0.05 requires a minimum sample size
of approximately 100 observations to achieve 80% statistical power. With N=855, this
study vastly exceeds the required threshold, eliminating Type II error risks, ensuring
stable parameter estimations, and confirming robust predictive power.
This study adhered to strict ethical protocols to protect all participants. Ethical
clearance was formally obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Universitas
PGRI Yogyakarta under approval number No. A.007/KEPK-UPY/V/2026, valid from
May 30, 2026, to May 30, 2027. Data were collected from 855 high school students in
Yogyakarta via self-administered questionnaires, and the investigators were obligated to
maintain the privacy and confidentiality of all participants' personal data in accordance
with research ethics principles. To further minimize bias and protect respondents,
procedural controls such as guaranteeing respondent anonymity and strict data
confidentiality were implemented during the survey process to reduce social desirability
pressure. Furthermore, the investigators were required to report any adverse events or
significant protocol changes to the research ethics committee and to submit a final
report upon completion of the study.

Discussion
The structural model reveals an R2 value of 0.428 for Intrinsic Motivation (Y1),
indicating that the combined effects of digital persuasive content, algorithm exposure,
cognitive overload, and decision paralysis explain 42.8% of the variance in prospective
students' internal academic drive. Under standard PLS-SEM criteria (Hair et al., 2021),
this explanatory power is classified as moderate, yet it represents a highly substantive
effect size in educational and behavioral research. When contrasted with related
quantitative literature, these findings equal or exceed established baselines . Studies
modeling the impact of social media visual campaigns on Gen Z consumer behavior
typically yield lower R2 values, ranging from 0.35 to 0.40 (Siregar et al., 2025). Our
higher value suggests that digital marketing does more than drive brand awareness;
during high-stakes choices like major selection, it serves as a potent force reshaping

To minimize student Cognitive Overload (M1), protect Intrinsic Motivation
(Y1), and mitigate Cognitive Dissonance (Y2), universities should adopt cognitively
sustainable communication strategies through three practical measures. First, marketing
divisions should enforce micro-information bundling through the "One Post, One Core
Message" Rule by abandoning dense, all-in-one promotional graphics or long scripts.
Information must be separated into brief, single-theme video clips, such as a 30-second
reel highlighting one lab project or a single core competency, to respect the working
memory limits of digital users. This approach aligns with the understanding that
persuasive messages must be carefully designed to avoid overwhelming recipients, as
excessive information can trigger cognitive overload and reduce message effectiveness
(Braca & Dondio, 2023). Additionally, complex data such as administrative
accreditation details or deep tuition structures should be removed from top -of-funnel
social media ads and introduced gradually through automated direct -message auto-
replies or email campaigns only after an initial inquiry is made, as research suggests that
too much choice presented simultaneously can lead to decision paralysis and reduced
satisfaction (Misuraca et al., 2024; Khan et al., 2021).
To counter chaotic, algorithm-driven social feeds, universities should direct
traffic to a clean, mobile-optimized landing page featuring an interactive comparison
tool. This feature should allow prospective applicants to compare up to three majors
side-by-side using standardized criteria such as weekly contact hours, core skills,
prerequisites, and realistic career outcomes, thereby eliminating manual data filtering
fatigue. The design of such persuasive systems must consider the interplay between
persuasion techniques and consumer traits, ensuring that the presentation of choices
facilitates rather than hinders decision-making (Braca & Dondio, 2023). Moreover,
universities should shift budgets from overly stylized corporate tours to unscripted,
documentary-style student diaries that focus on real senior students discussing their
academic journeys, including intellectual hurdles and the intrinsic satisfaction found in
mastering the subject. Content creators should partner with alumni and faculty to create
day-in-the-life video series, as visual imagery plays a crucial role in building authentic
brand image and shaping consumer purchase intention in the social media era (Dibda &
Ahmad, 2025). By showing the true problem-solving processes of careers linked to a
major rather than over-emphasizing generic financial rewards, universities can help
prospective students align their choices with their natural talents and core values,
thereby supporting their intrinsic motivation and reducing post -decision dissonance
(Aldabbas et al., 2025).
Because social media feeds are inherently unstructured and dynamic, they
worsen information chaos. Universities should direct traffic from social media to
mobile-optimized web landing pages featuring an interactive, clean Comparison Matrix
Tool. This tool should allow prospective students to side-by-side evaluate up to three
majors based on clear, standardized parameters such as core competencies, real
workload expectations, and elective pathways, minimizing the mental weight of manual
information filtering. Research on choice overload indicates that providing structured

peer-led, unpolished documentary-style narratives where senior students share their
actual academic journeys, including the intellectual hurdles, study habits required, and
the intrinsic fulfillment found in mastering the subject matter. This approach is
consistent with findings that persuasive health and educational messages are more
effective when they address mental well-being and authentic experiences rather than
superficial rewards (Dolinšek et al., 2024). Content creators should work alongside
faculty and alumni to produce realistic video journals detailing the day-to-day realities
of careers linked to specific majors. Showing the actual process of solving problems in a
particular profession, rather than just the end financial reward, helps students align their
major choice with their natural talents and intellectual curiosity, which has been shown
to enhance intrinsic motivation and reduce cognitive dissonance (Aldabbas et al., 2025;
Widyasari et al., 2019).
High school guidance counselors can utilize these findings to build proactive
digital literacy and career counseling frameworks. Since algorithm exposure (X2) locks
students into high-intensity information loops, counselors should train 12th-grade
students on how to intentionally reset their social media feeds, use targeted keyword
searches instead of relying entirely on passive algorithmic recommendations, and use
structured offline career assessment tools to anchor their personal interests before
engaging with campus digital ads. The role of digital learning platforms in shaping
student motivation and emotional responses has been well-documented, with research
emphasizing the importance of structured feedback mechanisms and intentional content
consumption (Monazam-Tabrizi et al., 2026). Counselors should lead reflective sessions
where students analyze university advertisements, helping them separate superficial
extrinsic hooks such as luxurious facilities and prestige branding from core educational
variables such as curriculum alignment and professional values, thereby protecting their
intrinsic decision-making autonomy. This practice is supported by studies
demonstrating that self-efficacy and assertiveness play dominant roles in predicting
learning motivation and career decision-making, suggesting that proactive counseling
interventions can buffer against the negative effects of algorithm -driven content
exposure (Kaloko et al., 2025; Asriyana et al., 2026).
The findings of this study reveal a rather alarming digital reality: the
combination of Digital Persuasive Content and the intensity of Algorithm Exposure
have become a major trigger for significant Cognitive Overload. Based on data from
855 respondents, this study captures a phenomenon in which prospective students are no
longer merely consuming information but are instead caught in a situation of massive
information bombardment. This pattern aligns with the thesis by Suherman et al (2026),
which states that when the influx of data exceeds the brain’s processing capacity, an
individual’s cognitive functions begin to face serious impairments.
In this context, social media algorithms act as aggressive curators. Persuasive
content that repetitively highlights narratives of instant success, such as the lure of high
salaries and luxurious amenities, is no longer perceived as positive inspiration. Instead,
this constant exposure transforms into a mentally taxing burden. Prospective students

Theoretically, these findings underscore the urgency for educational institution
administrators to shift from quantity-focused strategies toward more curated, high-
quality information. Linking these findings to the work of Misuraca et al. (2024) and
Pratiwi et al. (2025), it becomes clear that the greatest challenge in modern educational
marketing is no longer how to "stand out," but how to convey a message without adding
to cognitive load. Success in attracting new students now heavily depends on a
university's ability to present information that is simple, relevant, and capable of
reducing anxiety about making a choice amidst the digital noise (Widyasari et al.,
2019).
Research data reveals a significant negative correlation between decision
paralysis and intrinsic motivation, a phenomenon that raises serious concerns yet has a
strong logical foundation. When faced with an overwhelming flood of information,
people's decision-making mechanisms often experience functional paralysis. This
paralysis is not merely an inability to choose, but a psychological erosion in which
external noise begins to drown out the inner voice. As a result, individuals lose the
ability to hear their own intuition, creating a widening gap between their actions and the
source of their authentic passion (Aldabbas et al., 2025; Kaloko et al., 2025; Asriyana et
al., 2026).
This condition is exacerbated by the aggressiveness of marketing content in the
digital space, which constantly dictates standards of success. Social media is no longer
merely a window to information but a curator of reality that floods users with narratives
of financial success and luxurious lifestyles. Constant exposure to content about “high
salaries” and “the most promising professions” creates sociocultural pressure that
distorts individuals’ perceptions of a career’s value. Within this algorithmic siege, a
genuine interest in a field of study is often viewed as an unrealistic luxury or even an
unnecessary risk to take (Asriyana et al., 2026; Suherman et al., 2026).

phase, namely the emergence of a significant positive influence of Decision Paralysis on
Cognitive Dissonance, with a value of 0.520. This value indicates an acute crisis of self-
confidence among the respondents. In this state, prospective students are no longer able
to choose based on pure interest but are trapped in a vortex of doubt about their own
judgment. The inability to decide with certainty creates internal tension (dissonance),
where individuals begin to question whether the choice ultimately made truly represents
their desires or is merely an escape from the exhaustion caused by the lengthy selection
process. Furthermore, this study highlights the wide gap between digital expectations

CONCLUSION
In conclusion, this study demonstrates that intensive exposure to digital
persuasive messages and social media algorithms triggers significant cognitive overload
and decision paralysis among prospective students, which subsequently compromises
their intrinsic motivation and fuels pre-enrollment cognitive dissonance. These findings
offer important theoretical implications by extending Self-Determination Theory into
the digital higher education marketing landscape, proving that aggressive algorithmic
environments can directly subvert an adolescent's psychological autonomy. Practically,
the study highlights an urgent need for universities to shift from high-volume, attention-
grabbing recruitment tactics toward more authentic, simplified, and well -curated
information architectures that respect a user's mental capacity. However, certain
methodological limitations must be acknowledged, as the research relies on a cross -
sectional design and self-reported survey data from a single regional sample, which
restricts the immediate generalization of the causal trends. To address these boundaries,
a robust future research agenda should prioritize longitudinal and experimental designs
alongside cross-cultural studies to track how these technology -mediated cognitive
pressures evolve across diverse global student cohorts.
