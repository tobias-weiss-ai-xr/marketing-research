Introduction
Human nutrition is a multidimensional phenomenon shaped not only by biological
requirements but also by social, cultural, economic, psychological, and environmental
conditions. What people eat reﬂects the interaction of individual preferences and
capabilities with income, food prices, social relationships, cultural norms, gender roles,
migration, psychological wellbeing, physical activity, commercial inﬂuences, and food
environments. Dietary behavior is therefore embedded within broader social and economic
contexts that determine the opportunities, constraints, and meanings associated with
food (1–3).
This perspective is increasingly important as populations experience interconnected
challenges including undernutrition, micronutrient deﬁciencies, overweight and obesity,
diet-related non-communicable diseases, food insecurity, and environmental pressures
on food systems. These challenges frequently share common social and commercial
determinants and may interact across the life course ( 4, 5). Globalization, urbanization,
migration, changing employment patterns, and digitalization are also transforming food
environments and dietary practices. Contemporary nutrition research must therefore
examine not only what people eat, but why particular dietary behaviors emerge, under
what circumstances they change, and how social and cultural environments inﬂuence their
health consequences.
The contributions within this Research Topic, “ Exploring Socio-Cultural Factors
in Human Nutrition: The Importance of Multidimensional Approaches , ” collectively
demonstrate the limitations of approaches that interpret nutrition primarily through
individual choice. Across diverse populations and settings, the studies illustrate
how dietary practices are produced through interactions among social position,

From individual choice to the social
production of dietary behavior
Nutrition knowledge is important, but knowledge alone
does not guarantee healthy dietary behavior. Food choices
are also inﬂuenced by aﬀordability, availability, convenience,
social expectations, taste, identity, household responsibilities,
and perceived behavioral control. Behavioral theories such as
the Theory of Planned Behavior highlight the importance of
attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived control in shaping
dietary intentions (2).
This has important implications for nutrition education.
Interventions based primarily on information provision may have
limited eﬀects when healthy foods are unaﬀordable, inaccessible,
culturally unfamiliar, or incompatible with time and household
constraints. Socioeconomic inequalities in diet are partly related
to food prices and the resources available to households ( 6). Thus,
nutrition education should be combined with approaches that
improve the conditions in which dietary decisions are made.
Culture, identity, and dietary change
Culture should not be treated merely as a demographic variable.
Food is closely connected to identity, family, social belonging,
tradition, religion, memory, and gender. Dietary change may
therefore represent broader processes of social transformation.
Research on dietary acculturation demonstrates that migration
and exposure to new food environments can produce complex
combinations of retained, modiﬁed, and newly adopted dietary
practices rather than a simple replacement of traditional diets ( 7).
Similarly, culturally adapted interventions suggest that cultural
context should be incorporated into intervention design rather than
treated as a barrier to healthy eating ( 8). Traditional foods should
consequently not be classiﬁed as inherently healthy or unhealthy
without considering preparation methods, portion sizes, frequency,
cultural meaning, and changing lifestyles.
Socioeconomic position, food
insecurity, and nutritional inequality
A multidimensional perspective also requires distinguishing
dietary preference from dietary constraint. Individuals may value
healthy eating but lack suﬃcient income, time, transportation,
cooking facilities, or access to nutritious foods. Socioeconomic
position therefore inﬂuences not only dietary behavior but also the
range of feasible choices.
Food insecurity provides a particularly important example.
It can contribute to dietary compromise, psychological stress,
competing ﬁnancial priorities, and diﬃculties in managing chronic
diseases ( 9). Consequently, nutritional inequalities should not
automatically be interpreted as failures of individual behavior.
Programs such as medically tailored meals and other food-support
interventions reﬂect an emerging recognition that improving
nutrition may require addressing material and social conditions
alongside individual behavior.
Gender, psychological wellbeing, and
food environments
Dietary practices are also embedded within gendered and
psychological experiences. Household responsibilities, food
preparation roles, workplace conditions, social support, stress,
and perceived control can inﬂuence dietary and feeding practices.
Psychological factors should therefore not be considered solely
as individual characteristics; they may themselves be shaped by
socioeconomic and social environments.
At the population level, food environments further inﬂuence
what foods are accessible, aﬀordable, promoted, and socially
normalized ( 3). Digital technologies have expanded these
environments through social media, targeted advertising, online
food delivery, and digital marketing. Consequently, nutrition
research should consider food environments as dynamic systems in
which price, promotion, availability, working schedules, transport,
digital exposure, and household resources interact.
Toward an integrated biocultural and
systems perspective
Taken together, the evidence supports an integrated model
in which biological processes are embedded within behavioral,
psychological, interpersonal, cultural, economic, institutional, and
environmental systems. Such a perspective is consistent with
ecological and systems approaches to nutrition and is particularly
relevant to understanding the coexistence of undernutrition,
micronutrient deﬁciencies, obesity, non-communicable diseases,
and environmental pressures—the so-called double or triple
burden of malnutrition (4, 5).
Environmental sustainability adds another dimension. Healthy
dietary recommendations increasingly need to consider the
environmental consequences of food production and consumption.
The EAT–Lancet Commission emphasized the need for healthy
diets within sustainable food systems ( 10). However, sustainability
recommendations must remain sensitive to cultural, economic,
and geographical contexts. A dietary pattern that is nutritionally
desirable but inaccessible or culturally inappropriate is unlikely to
generate equitable population-level improvements.

For policy, responsibility for healthy eating should not

structural determinants while improving physical and economic

In clinical and public-health practice, nutritional assessment
should incorporate relevant contextual factors such as food
insecurity, social barriers, psychological stress, cultural
preferences, and access to appropriate foods. Culturally
responsive interventions should be developed collaboratively
with communities and, where appropriate, linked with social
services and other forms of support.
Conclusion
The central contribution of a multidimensional approach
to nutrition is the recognition that social and cultural factors
are not merely associated with dietary behavior; they are part
of the processes through which nutritional health is produced.
Food practices emerge from interactions among biological needs,
social relationships, cultural meanings, economic resources,
psychological experiences, and changing food environments.
Improving nutritional health therefore requires more than
providing individuals with information. It requires understanding
why people eat what they eat, what makes particular choices
possible or impossible, and how nutritional opportunities and risks
are distributed across populations. Integrating biological, social,
cultural, economic, and environmental perspectives can provide a
stronger foundation for nutrition policies and interventions that
are eﬀective, equitable, culturally meaningful, and sustainable.
