Abstract
This study revisits the privacy paradox in data -driven marketing by
examining whether privacy concerns constrain the effectiveness of
behavioural analytics and whether transparency plays a more
influential role in shaping consumer purchase decisions. Drawi ng on
the Theory of Planned Behaviour, Privacy Calculus Theory, and
research on algorithm aversion, behavioural analytics is
conceptualized as a digital choice architecture that influences
consumer decision -making. Survey data from 228 digitally active
consumers were analysed using Partial Least Squares Structural
Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM). The results indicate that behavioural
analytics positively influences purchase decisions, while privacy
concerns do not significantly moderate this relationship, supp orting
the persistence of the privacy paradox. In contrast, transparency
significantly strengthens the effect of behavioural analytics by
enhancing consumer trust and perceived procedural fairness. These
findings suggest that transparency, rather than privacy concern, is the
key mechanism supporting effective personalization. From a
managerial perspective, organizations should complement
behavioural analytics with transparent data governance, explainable
algorithmic practices, and clear communication regarding data use to
strengthen consumer trust. This study advances digital marketing
research by reframing the privacy paradox as a question of legitimacy
and trust in responsible data-driven marketing.

Keywords
behavioural analytics , privacy paradox , transparency, algorithmic
transparency, personalized marketing, purchase decisions.

INTRODUCTION
The rapid advancement of digital technologies has transformed data -driven marketing
into a cornerstone of contemporary business strategy. Through artificial intelligence, machine
learning, and behavioural analytics, organizations increasingly analyse consu mers' digital
footprints to deliver personalized recommendations, targeted advertising, and optimized
customer experiences (Wedel & Kannan, 2016). By processing clickstream data, browsing
histories, and purchase behaviours, behavioural analytics enables fi rms to anticipate consumer
preferences and support more effective marketing decisions. Consequently, behavioural analytics
has evolved beyond a descriptive analytical tool into a form of digital choice architecture  that
influences how consumers search for information, evaluate alternatives, and make purchasing
decisions. Previous studies show that personalized marketing enhances perceived relevance,
reduces information search costs, and improves consumer decision -making when
recommendations align with users' preferences (Bleier & Eisenbeiss, 2018; Goldfarb & Tucker,
2019). As organizations increasingly depend on behavioural analytics to strengthen customer
engagement and competitive advantage, understanding the facto rs that influence consumers'
acceptance of personalized marketing has become an important concern for both researchers and
practitioners. Among these factors, consumer trust and perceptions of data use have emerged as
critical determinants of the long-term effectiveness of analytics-driven marketing strategies.
Despite the growing effectiveness of behavioural analytics, its reliance on extensive
consumer data collection has intensified concerns about digital surveillance, privacy intrusion,
and algorithmic opacity. Consumers frequently express discomfort with personalized advertising
and behavioural targeting, particularly when recommendations appear overl y intrusive or
difficult to explain (Jerath & Miller, 2024). Paradoxically, however, these concerns rarely
discourage continued participation in digital platforms or online purchasing. This inconsistency,
commonly referred to as the privacy paradox, descri bes the gap between individuals' stated
privacy concerns and their actual digital behaviour (Norberg et al., 2007; Baruh et al., 2017).
Privacy Calculus Theory suggests that consumers evaluate the benefits of personalization,
convenience, and efficiency ag ainst the perceived risks of data disclosure when engaging with
digital services (Martin & Murphy, 2017). Recent evidence further indicates that many consumers
continue to accept personalized marketing because its functional benefits outweigh perceived
privacy risks (Jerath & Miller, 2024; Liu, 2024). Although the privacy paradox has been widely
documented, it remains unclear why privacy concerns often fail to reduce the effectiveness of
behavioural analytics in influencing purchase decisions and what facto rs enable consumers to
continue trusting personalized marketing despite persistent concerns about data privacy.
One explanation for the persistence of the privacy paradox lies in the growing importance
of transparency and trust within algorithmic marketing systems. Behavioural analytics relies on

However, research on algorithm aversion suggests that resistance to algorithmic systems
declines when consumers are provided with meaningful explanat ions of how recommendations
are generated and perceive these processes as transparent and fair (Castelo et al., 2019; Dietvorst
et al., 2018). Transparency therefore extends beyond regulatory disclosure by reducing
information asymmetry, enhancing explaina bility, and strengthening procedural fairness, all of
which contribute to greater consumer trust (Binns et al., 2018). Rather than eliminating privacy
concerns, transparency may reassure consumers that their data are handled responsibly, thereby
increasing their willingness to engage with personalized marketing. These findings suggest that
transparency may play a more influential role than privacy concerns in determining the
effectiveness of behavioural analytics, although this relationship has not yet been fully examined
within an integrated theoretical framework.
Since the introduction of the privacy paradox by Norberg et al. (2007), extensive research
has examined the inconsistency between consumers' stated privacy concerns and their actual
online behaviour (Baruh et al., 2017). More recently, studies have also in vestigated behavioural
analytics as a driver of personalized marketing (Wedel & Kannan, 2016; Bleier & Eisenbeiss,
2018) and algorithmic transparency as a determinant of trust, fairness, and the acceptance of
automated decision-making (Burrell, 2016; Binns et al., 2018). However, these research streams
have largely developed independently, providing only a partial explanation of consumer
behaviour in data -driven marketing environments. Consequently, limited attention has been
given to understanding how beha vioural analytics, privacy concerns, and transparency jointly
influence consumers' purchase decisions. In particular, it remains unclear whether privacy
concerns genuinely weaken the effectiveness of behavioural analytics or whether transparency
serves as the more influential mechanism through which trust is established and personalized
marketing becomes effective. Addressing this gap, the present study develops an integrated
theoretical framework that combines behavioural analytics, Privacy Calculus Theory, the Theory
of Planned Behaviour, and algorithmic transparency to explain consumer purchase decisions in
digital marketing.
To address the identified research gap, this study adopts an integrated theoretical
framework combining the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB), Privacy Calculus Theory, and
insights from algorithmic transparency and algorithm aversion research. TPB explains  how
attitudes, perceived usefulness, and perceived behavioural control influence consumers'
intentions and purchasing behaviour in digital environments (Ajzen, 1991). Complementing this

the privacy paradox suggests that privacy -risk evaluation alone is insufficient to explain why

By integrating these comp lementary perspectives, the proposed framework extends existing

of how behavioural analytics, privacy concerns, and transparency jointly influence consumer
purchase decisions in data-driven marketing environments.
Building on the proposed theoretical framework, this study revisits the privacy paradox
by examining whether privacy concerns genuinely constrain the effectiveness of behavioural
analytics or whether transparency plays a more decisive role in shaping consu mer purchase
decisions in digital marketing environments. Specifically, the study investigates the direct effect
of behavioural analytics on purchase decisions and examines the moderating roles of privacy
concerns and transparency in this relationship. Beh avioural analytics is expected to positively
influence purchase decisions because personalized recommendations enhance perceived
relevance, reduce information search costs, and improve consumers' decision-making efficiency
(Bleier & Eisenbeiss, 2018; Wedel & Kannan, 2016). Privacy concerns, however, may weaken this
relationship by increasing perceived risk and reducing consumers' willingness to rely on
personalized marketing (Baruh et al., 2017; Martin & Murphy, 2017). Conversely, transparency is
expected to strengthen the effectiveness of behavioural analytics by enhancing trust, reducing
uncertainty, and increasing the perceived fairness and legitimacy of algorithmic
recommendations (Castelo et al., 2019; Dietvorst et al., 2018). Accordingly, the following
hypotheses are proposed:
• H1: Behavioural Analytics positively influences Purchase Decisions.
• H2: Privacy Concerns negatively moderate the relationship between Behavioural Analytics
and Purchase Decisions.
• H3: Transparency positively moderates the relationship between Behavioural Analytics and
Purchase Decisions.

This study contributes to the digital marketing literature in several important ways. First,
it develops an integrated theoretical framework that combines the Theory of Planned Behaviour,
Privacy Calculus Theory, and algorithmic transparency perspectives t o explain consumer

To examine the proposed relationships, this study employs a quantitative research design
using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS -SEM) to analyse the effects of
behavioural analytics on purchase decisions and the moderating roles of p rivacy concerns and
transparency. Survey data were collected from digitally active consumers with recent online
purchasing experience, enabling empirical evaluation of the integrated theoretical framework.
The following section describes the research desig n, sampling procedures, measurement
development, data collection process, and analytical techniques used to test the proposed
hypotheses.

METHODS
This study employed a quantitative, cross -sectional, explanatory research design to
examine the relationships among behavioural analytics, privacy concerns, transparency, and
consumer purchase decisions in digital marketing. A quantitative approach was ado pted to test
the proposed hypotheses and estimate the structural relationships among latent constructs using
empirical data, while the cross -sectional design enabled the collection of respondents'
perceptions at a single point in time. The study is explana tory because it seeks to explain the
direct effect of behavioural analytics on purchase decisions and the moderating roles of privacy
concerns and transparency within an integrated theoretical framework. The hypotheses were
tested using Structural Equation  Modelling–Partial Least Squares (PLS -SEM) with WarpPLS
software. PLS -SEM was selected because it is well suited for predictive research involving
complex structural relationships, latent variables, and moderation analysis, while

The questionnaire was developed by adapting measurement items from previously
validated scales in the literature on behavioural analytics, privacy, algorithmic transparency, and
consumer purchase behaviour to ensure content validity and conceptual consiste ncy (Ajzen,
1991; Martin & Murphy, 2017; Hair et al., 2021). The instrument comprised four constructs:
Behavioural Analytics, Privacy Concerns, Transparency, and Purchase Decisions. All items were
measured using a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree,
which is widely used to capture respondents' perceptions and behavioural intentions in
marketing research (Sekaran & Bougie, 2020). Prior to the main survey, the questionnaire was
pre-tested with a small group of digitally active consumers and reviewed by two academics with
expertise in digital marketing and consumer behaviour to evaluate the clarity, relevance, and
comprehensibility of the measurement items. Based on their feedback, minor revisions were
made to improve wording, eliminate ambiguity, and enhance the readability of the questionnaire
while preserving the conceptual meaning of each construct (Boateng et al., 2018; DeVellis &
Thorpe, 2022).
The target population comprised digitally active consumers who had made at least one
online purchase within the preceding three months, ensuring that respondents possessed recent
experience with personalized digital marketing and behavioural analytics. A purposive sampling
technique was employed because the study required respondents who met predefined eligibility
criteria relevant to the research objectives (Sekaran & Bougie, 2020). Data were collected through
a structured online questionnaire administered  via Google Forms, with the survey link
distributed through social media platforms and digital communication networks to maximize
participation from eligible respondents. A total of 228 valid responses were obtained and
included in the analysis. The sample  size was considered adequate based on current PLS -SEM
guidelines, which recommend that sample adequacy be evaluated according to statistical power,
model complexity, and the number of structural relationships rather than solely relying on the
traditional "10-times rule" (Hair et al., 2021; Sarstedt et al., 2022). With 228 observations, the
sample exceeded the minimum requirements for estimating the proposed structural model with
moderation effects, providing sufficient statistical power and stable paramete r estimates for
hypothesis testing.
The study examined four latent constructs: Behavioural Analytics, Privacy Concerns,
Transparency, and Purchase Decisions. Behavioural Analytics was operationalized through
indicators reflecting consumers' perceptions of personalization, recommendation rele vance,
targeting accuracy, and the predictive capability of analytics-driven marketing systems (Wedel &
Kannan, 2016; Bleier & Eisenbeiss, 2018). Privacy Concerns captured respondents' perceptions

measured using indicators related to the clarity of organizational data practices, explainability of

The proposed hypotheses were analysed using Structural Equation Modelling –Partial
Least Squares (PLS -SEM) with WarpPLS software. WarpPLS was selected because it is
particularly suitable for predictive research involving complex structural relationships, la tent
constructs, and moderation analysis, while accommodating relatively moderate sample sizes and
fewer distributional assumptions than covariance -based SEM (Hair et al., 2021; Sarstedt et al.,
2022). The analysis followed the recommended two -stage PLS -SEM procedure. First, the
measurement model (outer model) was evaluated by assessing indicator reliability, internal
consistency reliability using Cronbach's alpha and Composite Reliability (CR), convergent validity
using the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) , and discriminant validity based on the Fornell –
Larcker criterion and cross -loadings (Hair et al., 2021). Second, the structural model (inner
model) was assessed by examining path coefficients, coefficients of determination (R²), predictive
relevance (Q²), effect sizes (f²), and the significance of direct and moderating relationships using
bootstrapping procedures. This analytical approach enabled a comprehensive evaluation of both
the measurement properties and the structural relationships proposed in the integrated research
model (Sarstedt et al., 2022).

DISCUSSION
The findings support H1, demonstrating that Behavioural Analytics has a positive and
significant effect on Purchase Decisions, highlighting the important role of analytics -driven
personalization in influencing consumer behaviour. Consistent with the Theory  of Planned
Behaviour (TPB), personalized recommendations enhance consumers' perceptions of relevance,
usefulness, and confidence, thereby facilitating the translation of purchase intentions into actual
buying behaviour (Ajzen, 1991). By analysing consumer s' browsing behaviour, purchase
histories, and interaction patterns, behavioural analytics enables organizations to deliver timely
and relevant recommendations that reduce information search costs and improve decision
efficiency (Bleier & Eisenbeiss, 2018; Wedel & Kannan, 2016). These findings further support the
concept of digital choice architecture, in which algorithmic recommendation systems subtly
shape consumers' decision environments without limiting their freedom of choice (Sunstein,
2021; Caraban e t al., 2019). Rather than functioning solely as a predictive tool, behavioural
analytics actively guides consumers through personalized decision -making processes.
Consequently, the findings reinforce previous research on personalization while highlighting

behavioural analytics as a strategic capability that enhances consumer engagement and
purchasing behaviour in data-driven digital marketing.
Contrary to expectations, Privacy Concerns did not significantly moderate the
relationship between Behavioural Analytics and Purchase Decisions, indicating that concerns
about personal data privacy were insufficient to reduce consumers' responsiveness to
personalized marketing. This finding provides empirical support for the persistence of the
privacy paradox, whereby consumers continue to engage in digital transactions despite
expressing concerns about privacy (Baruh et al., 2017). A possible explanation i s offered by
Privacy Calculus Theory, which suggests that consumers evaluate the perceived benefits of
personalization, convenience, and decision efficiency against the potential risks of personal data
disclosure (Martin & Murphy, 2017). In increasingly ma ture digital ecosystems, where data
collection and personalized recommendations have become commonplace, consumers may
perceive the benefits of analytics -driven services as outweighing the associated privacy risks
(Jerath & Miller, 2024; Liu, 2024). Consequently, privacy concerns may function more as a general
attitudinal orientation than as a direct behavioural constraint. These findings extend previous
research by suggesting that the effectiveness of behavioural analytics depends less on the
presence of p rivacy concerns than on the conditions that foster consumer trust in digital
marketing environments.
The findings support H3, indicating that Transparency positively moderates the
relationship between Behavioural Analytics and Purchase Decisions, although the effect size is
relatively small. This result suggests that transparency strengthens the effective ness of
personalized marketing by fostering consumer trust and reducing uncertainty surrounding
algorithmic decision -making. Consistent with research on algorithmic transparency and
algorithm aversion, consumers are more likely to accept analytics -driven r ecommendations
when they understand how personal data are collected, processed, and used to generate
personalized content (Burrell, 2016; Castelo et al., 2019; Dietvorst et al., 2018). Transparency
therefore functions not merely as a regulatory requirement  but as a trust -enabling mechanism
that enhances perceptions of fairness, accountability, and organizational credibility (Binns et al.,
2018). From a managerial perspective, organizations can strengthen consumer trust by
implementing clear privacy notices,  explainable recommendation systems, transparent consent
mechanisms, and accessible communication regarding data practices. Although the moderating
effect is modest, the findings demonstrate that transparent data governance enhances the
persuasive power of  behavioural analytics and supports more effective and sustainable
personalization in digital marketing.
The present study makes several theoretical contributions to the digital marketing
literature. First, it extends the privacy paradox literature by demonstrating that privacy concerns

findings identify transparency as a trust-enabling mechanism that strengthens the effectiveness
of behavioural analytics, shifting theoreti cal attention from privacy risk as the primary
determinant of consumer behaviour toward the role of trust, explainability, and perceived
fairness in algorithmic marketing. Finally, by integrating the Theory of Planned Behaviour,
Privacy Calculus Theory, an d algorithmic transparency into a single empirical framework, this
study provides a more comprehensive explanation of consumer purchase decisions than previous
studies that examined these perspectives independently. Collectively, these findings advance the
understanding of responsible data -driven marketing by demonstrating that the effectiveness of
behavioural analytics depends not only on predictive capability but also on transparent and
trustworthy data governance.
The findings offer several practical implications for organizations seeking to leverage
behavioural analytics in digital marketing. First, firms should recognize that the effectiveness of
personalized marketing depends not only on predictive accuracy but also on consumers' trust in
how personal data are collected and used. Organizations can strengthen this trust by
implementing transparent data governance through clear privacy notices, user -friendly consent
mechanisms, explainable recommendation systems, an d accessible communication regarding
data practices. Such initiatives reduce information asymmetry, enhance perceptions of fairness,
and encourage greater acceptance of analytics-driven personalization (Binns et al., 2018; Castelo
et al., 2019). Second, al though privacy concerns did not significantly weaken the influence of
behavioural analytics in this study, organizations should not interpret this finding as justification
for excessive data collection. Responsible data management remains essential for mai ntaining
regulatory compliance, organizational legitimacy, and long-term customer relationships (Martin
& Murphy, 2017). Consequently, transparency should be viewed not merely as a compliance
requirement but as a strategic capability that reinforces consum er trust, strengthens the
effectiveness of behavioural analytics, and supports sustainable digital marketing in increasingly
data-driven business environments.

CONCLUSION
This study revisited the privacy paradox by examining whether privacy concerns
constrain the effectiveness of behavioural analytics and whether transparency plays a more
influential role in shaping consumer purchase decisions in digital marketing. The find ings
demonstrate that behavioural analytics significantly enhances purchase decisions, while privacy
