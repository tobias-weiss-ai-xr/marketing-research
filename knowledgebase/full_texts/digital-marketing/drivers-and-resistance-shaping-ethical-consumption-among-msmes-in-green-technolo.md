Abstract
Purpose: This study examines the effects of digital marketing
strategies–mobile marketing, gamification, customer reviews, and
fintech–on ethical consumption, with particular emphasis on the
mediating role of customer resistance.
Research Methodology: A mixed-methods approach combining
quantitative and qualitative techniques was employed.  The
quantitative phase involved survey data collected from 200
consumers of MSME -based digital businesses, while the
qualitative phase included 15 in -depth interviews with consumers
and MSME practitioners. A mediation analysis was conducted to
assess the indirect effects of digital marketing strategies on ethical
consumption through customer resistance, and qualitative findings
were used to support triangulation and strengthen the
interpretation.
Results: The findings reveal that digital marketing strategies
influence ethical consumption primarily through indirect
mechanisms. Fintech does not exert a significant direct effect on
ethical consumption; however, its impact becomes significant
when it is mediated by customer resistance. These results indicate
that ethical consumption is driven more by consumers’ cognitive
and affective evaluations than by technological convenience alone
Conclusions: Drawing on the Stimulus–organism–response (S-O-
R) theory, this study concludes that customer resistance serves as
a constructive psychological mechanism that transforms digital
marketing stimuli into ethical consumption behavior within
MSME digital ecosystems.
Limitations: This study is limited by its cross -sectional design,
focus on Indonesian digitally oriented MSMEs, and a sample that
may not fully represent consumer diversity across regions and
sectors.
Contributions: This study reconceptualizes customer resistance as
a key mediating mechanism in ethical consumption and clarifies
the indirect role of fintech in consumers’ psychological evaluation
processes.
Keywords: Customer Reviews, Customer Resistance, Digital
Sustainability, Ethical Consumption, Fintech

1. Introduction
The rapid expansion of digital technology has fundamentally transformed the competitive landscape for
Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs), particularly in emerging economies such as Indonesia.
Digital marketing strategies enable MSMEs to expand their market reach, enhance operational efficiency,
and engage consumers beyond geographical boundaries (Arridho, Sinaga, Hartono, & Syahchari, 2025 ;
Probohudono, Suhardjanto, Aligarh, Chayati, & Putra, 2025 ). This transformation aligns with broader
global shifts toward green technology and ethical consumption, where businesses are increasingly expected
to integrate sustainability, transparency, and responsibility into their value -creation processes. By 2023,
approximately 22 million MSMEs, representing 33.6% of all MSMEs in Indonesia, had entered the digital
economy (INDEF, 2024), signaling substantial progress toward digital inclusion.  Figure 1 illustrates the
rapid growth of digital adoption among Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs).

Figure 1. The speed and scale of digital adoption among MSMEs

Despite rapid digital growth, ethical and sustainable practices remain inconsistent. Many MSMEs still face
structural, technological, and behavioral barriers that limit their effective use of digital marketing tools for
environmental and ethical objectives (Hidayat, Alifah, Astuti, Cahya, & Lutfia, 2025 ; Izzah, 2025 ;
Sudiantini, Sefita, Maharani, Maharani, & Febianti, 2024 ; Yelfiarita, Darwanto, Waluyati, & Masyhuri,
2025). This gap highlights that digital adoption does not automatically produce ethical consumption
outcomes (Izzah, 2025).

Among digital strategies, mobile marketing is the most widely used because of high smartphone
penetration; however, its implementation remains suboptimal because of low digital literacy, resource
constraints, and limited capacity to create sustainability-oriented content (Ochs & Schmitt, 2021; Pratama
et al., 2024; Sucandrawati, Suartini, Yaqin, Kaharuddin, & Yuni T, 2024). Gamification has gained traction
for enhancing engagement Ismaliyanto, Sari, Rahakbauw, Andoyo, and Andriani (2025), but its adoption is
limited by technical and managerial constraints, and its use is predominantly internal rather than directed
at promoting ethical consumption(Ahmad, Kirmani, & Haque, 2024; Saputra & Rahmatia, 2021). Customer
reviews represent another critical digital mechanism that influences trust and purchase decisions. However,
many MSMEs lack transparent and well -managed review systems that could guide consumers toward
responsible and sustainable choices (Sihura, 2025).

Crucially, customer resistance remains a major barrier to these digital initiatives.  Resistance is driven by
trust deficits, preferences for conventional transactions, data privacy concerns, and limited awareness of
sustainability benefits (Kuncara, 2022; PwC, 2023; VIDA, 2024). Although ethical consumption is gaining
prominence, its practical adoption remains fragmented due to information asymmetry and skepticism
toward sustainability claims (Sekardhani, 2024). Against this backdrop, this study aims to examine the
influence of mobile marketing, gamification, customer reviews, and fintech on ethical consumption among
MSMEs in the green technology era. Specifically, this study places customer resistance at the center of the
analysis by investigating its mediating role in shaping the effectiveness of digital marketing strategies in
fostering ethical consumption behavior (Apriadi, Adelin, Mutumanikam, & Lanjarsih, 2025 ; Serang,
Kalsum, Pasagi, & Putri, 2025; Sumidartiny & Laela, 2025).

The existing literature has extensively examined digital transformation and sustainability in MSMEs,
highlighting the positive role of social media in sustainable marketing Dash, Sharma, and Sharma (2023),
digital marketing in enhancing visibility despite infrastructure barriers (Setiawan, Prasetyo, & Yuniawan,
2025) and White, Cakanlar, Sethi, and Trudel (2025),  green marketing in strengthening customer loyalty
Mendrofa (2024), and digital literacy in supporting sustainability outcomes (Surjadi, Hakki, & Zulkifli,
2024; Suttharattanagul, Santipiriyapon, & Daengrasmisopon, 2025 ). Other studies emphasize the

contribution of digitalization to lean -green practices Queiroz, Alves Junior, and Costa Melo (2022) ,
Martínez-Peláez et al. (2023)  and identify ethical concerns regarding emerging technologies such as

However, prior research has tended to examine these digital tools in isolation, offering limited insight into

Almeida, Kalinowski, Uchôa, and Feijó (2023) and Bangun, Suhara, and Husin (2023), the role of customer
resistance remains under-theorized and under-tested within an integrated ethical consumption framework.

Prior studies have applied theoretical lenses such as the Theory of Planned Behavior, Technology
Acceptance Model, and Sustainability Marketing Theory separately.  The Theory of Planned Behavior
explains consumers’ ethical purchase intentions White et al. (2025) , while the Technology Acceptance
Model accounts for digital payment and e -commerce adoption through perceived usefulness and ease of
use (Tran & Vu, 2025 ; White et al., 2025 ). Sustainability Marketing Theory focuses on how firms
communicate eco-friendly attributes to encourage responsible consumption (Tran & Vu, 2025; White et al.,
2025). However, limited empirical work integrates these perspectives to explain how digital marketing
strategies interact with behavioral resistance to shape ethical consumption within MSME contexts (Tran &
Vu, 2025; White et al., 2025).

This study offers several contributions to the ethical consumption and MSME digitalization literature. First,
it develops an empirical framework that simultaneously examines mobile marketing, gamification,
customer reviews, and fintech as strategic drivers fof ethical consumption an integrative approach largely
absent in prior MSME studies that have examined these mechanisms in isolation.  Second, it
reconceptualizes customer resistance not as a peripheral barrier but as a mediating mechanism that explains
how behavioral friction weakens digital sustainability strategies, thereby advancing consumer behavior
theory by emphasizing psychologica l dissonance, bounded rationality, and perceived effort in ethical
decision-making. Third, by situating the model in Indonesia’s emerging green technology landscape, this
study synthesizes the Technology Acceptance Model and Sustainability Marketing Theory to generate
empirically grounded insights into digital sustainability in contexts c haracterized by high consumer
skepticism and weak regulatory enforcement.  Collectively, these contributions extend the theoretical and
practical understanding of how digital interventions support ethical consumption transitions among
MSMEs in emerging economies.

Finally, by embedding this model within the emerging green technology era, this study responds to urgent
global and regional sustainability challenges, where only 30% of MSMEs in Southeast Asia apply
integrated digital sustainability strategies, gamification remains underutilized, transparent review systems
are limited, fintech adoption for green -related activities reaches only 18.6%, and consumer skepticism
toward digital sustainability claims remains high (Almeida et al., 2023 ; Deloitte, 2023 ; Pranoto &
Hwihanus, 2023; R. Setiawan et al., 2025). Against this backdrop, the present study aims to examine how
mobile marketing, gamification, customer reviews, and FinTech influence ethical consumption among
MSMEs, with customer resistance acting as a mediating mechanism.  By bridging digital marketing
innovation and ethical consumption through the lens of behavioral friction, this study advances the
theoretical understanding of ethical digital transformation while offering actionable insights for MSMEs
navigating sustainable digital transitions.

2. Literature Review and Hypotheses Development
2.1 Mobile Marketing and Ethical Consumption
Mobile marketing provides real -time sustainability information and eco -label transparency, thereby
strengthening ethical evaluations during purchasing.  Studies grounded in TAM and TPB show that
perceived usefulness, subjective norms, trust, and personalization increase engagement with mobile
promotions and stimulate green purchasing intentions. Mobile cues also increase the willingness to pay for
environmentally responsible products. Thus, mobile marketing functions as a communication channel and
behavioral stimulus that encourages ethical consumption.  Accordingly, H1 predicts a positive effect on
ethical consumption, and H5 predicts a positive effect on customer resistance to greenwashing.

2.2 Gamification and Ethical Consumption
Gamification leverages intrinsic motivation through autonomy, competence, and relatedness, as
emphasized in Self -Determination Theory (SDT), to enhance engagement and self -efficacy in digital
environments (Gao, 2024; Gunawan, 2023; D. Kumar & Manjula, 2025 ; Prayoga, 2025). The Octalysis
framework identifies motivational drivers that guide gamification design, while the Theory of Gamified
Instruction shows that gamification influences performance and reflective evaluation  (Rojas, 2024 ).
Gamified interactions can stimulate ethical awareness by prompting consumers to critically evaluate their
choices. Thus, H2 hypothesizes that gamification positively affects ethical consumption, and H6 proposes
that it positively influences customer resistance.

2.3 Customer Reviews and Ethical Consumption
Customer reviews function as social proof and informational cues that shape consumer attitudes and
intentions through central and peripheral processing, as explained by the Elaboration Likelihood Model
(ELM). Reviews influence expectations, satisfaction, and loyalty, with accuracy and visibility enhancing
ethical decision making. Social Proof Theory also highlights the persuasive role of peer-generated content.
Accordingly, H3 predicts that customer reviews positively affect ethical consumption, and H7 proposes a
positive effect on customer review resistance.

2.4 Fintech and Ethical Consumption
Fintech represents disruptive innovation that shifts financial services toward consumer-facing applications,
such as mobile payments and digital banking (Gratton, 2024 ). MSME adoption is explained through
technological benefits (TOE) and performance -related determinants (UTAUT). While fintech offers
functional efficiency, ethical consumption may occur when consumers assess its security, transparency, and
ethical implications. Thus, H4 proposes a positive effect of fintech on ethical consumption, and H8 predicts
a positive influence on customer resistance.

2.5 Customer Resistance
Customer resistance reflects functional and psychological barriers, including perceived risk, low value,
attachment to tradition, discomfort, and insecurity, as explained by Innovation Resistance Theory (IRT),
Status Quo Bias, and Technology Readiness Inde x (TRI) (Musyaffi et al., 2022 ). In this study, customer
resistance is treated as a reflective and evaluative process through which digital marketing strategies
influence consumers’ ethical consumption. H9 proposes that customer resistance positively affects ethical
consumption.

2.6 Ethical Consumption
Ethical consumption is shaped by attitudes, norms, perceived control, personal values and social influences.
The TPB explains that these factors drive purchasing intentions Nurzaman and Herdiani (2023), while the
Value-Belief-Norm (VBN) Theory highlights the role of personal and social values in green consumption
decisions (Laksmana & Hendriana, 2023). The Ethical Decision-Making Model emphasizes the evaluation
of alternatives, with mindful consumption increasing sustainability values and purchase intentions, and
social influence playing a significant role (Djafarova & Foots, 2022; Suttharattanagul et al., 2025).

2.7 Mediation Hypotheses
Based on the literature and theoretical frameworks, customer resistance is hypothesized to be a mediating
mechanism between digital strategies and ethical consumption. H10 posits that customer resistance mediates
the relationship between mobile marketing and ethical consumption. H11 proposes that the effect of
gamification is mediated by customer resistance. H12 suggests that customer reviews influence ethical
consumption through resistance. H13: Fintech affects ethical consumption through customer resistance.

2.8 Theoretical Framework
The theoretical framework integrates mobile marketing, gamification, customer reviews, and fintech as
stimuli that influence ethical consumption both directly (H1–H4) and indirectly through customer resistance
(H10–H13), while also shaping customer resistance itself (H5–H8), which, in turn, affects ethical consumption
(H9). This framework synthesizes prior empirical evidence and aligns with TAM, TPB, SDT, ELM, IRT,
and other relevant theories, providing a coherent basis for testing H1–H13 in the context of digital
consumption.

3. Methodology
3.1 Research Approach and Design
This study adopted a mixed -methods approach with a sequential explanatory design Toyon (2021), as
follows. A quantitative analysis was first conducted to examine the relationship between mobile marketing
strategies, customer resistance, and ethical consumption, followed by a qualitative phase to explain the
behavioral mechanisms and resistance dyn amics underlying these statistical findings.  This design is
particularly appropriate given the complex interplay between technology acceptance, ethical values, and
consumer resistance in MSMEs operating in a green technology context, where numerical patterns do not
fully capture the contextual meanin gs, trust issues, and sustainability perceptions that shape ethical
consumption behaviors.

3.2 Population and Sample
This study used a sequential explanatory mixed -methods design involving MSME consumers and
practitioners. In the quantitative phase, purposive sampling targeted consumers with experience in mobile
marketing, gamification, customer reviews, and fintech. This ensured relevance to the TPB–TAM–MMAM
constructs and ethical consumption measures.  A power-based PLS-SEM criterion indicated a minimum
sample size of 150; therefore, 200 valid responses were collected.  The qualitative phase recruited 10 –15
MSME practitioners through snowball sampling until data saturation was reached.  Qualitative insights
contextualized PLS-SEM mechanisms, particularly customer resistance, thereby enhancing interpretation
and cross-phase integration.

3.3.2 Qualitative Data
Qualitative data were collected through semi-structured interviews with MSME practitioners to explain and
contextualize quantitative findings.  The interview protocol focused on perceived challenges in
implementing mobile marketing, gamification, review systems, and fintech, as well as practical experiences
of customer resistance in promoting ethical consumption.  In line with the sequential explanatory design,
themes emerging from the quantitative phase, particularly significant or unexpected relationships, were
used to guide the qualitative inquiry, enabling explanation building and a deeper understanding of the
mechanisms underlying customer resistance and ethical consumption behavior.

3.4 Data Analysis Techniques
This study employs a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design, in which quantitative findings provide
the primary evidence and qualitative inquiry is subsequently used to elucidate the underlying mechanisms,
particularly customer resistance, that shape ethical consumption within MSME digital marketing contexts.

3.4.1 Quantitative Analysis (PLS-SEM)
Quantitative data were analyzed using Partial Least Squares structural equation modeling (PLS -SEM) to
test the hypothesized relationships derived from the TPB–TAM–MMAM framework. The analysis followed
a two-stage procedure that was consistent with the established PLS-SEM guidelines. First, the measurement
model was assessed for validity and reliability using outer loadings (>0.70), average variance extracted
(A VE) (>0.50), composite reliability (>0.70), and discriminant validity via HTMT (<0.90).  Second, the
structural model examined the direct effects of mobile marketing, gamification, customer reviews, and
fintech on ethical consumption and the mediating role of customer resistance using bootstrapping.  Model
explanatory power and effect sizes were evaluated using R² and f² to determine predictive adequacy and
effect magnitude (Sarstedt, Hair Jr, & Ringle, 2023).

3.4.2 Qualitative Analysis
Qualitative data from semi -structured interviews were analyzed using thematic analysis.  Interview
transcripts were systematically coded to identify themes related to digital strategy implementation,
technology acceptance challenges and manifestations of customer resistance.  In line with the sequential
explanatory design, the qualitative analysis focused on explaining significant, weak, or unexpected
quantitative relationships, thereby supporting the explanation building.  Method triangulation was applied
by comparing the interview insights with the quantitative findings to enhance credibility and interpretive
validity (Sugiyono, 2017).

Overall, MSME actors demonstrate an emerging awareness of sustainable consumption and production,

4.2 Discussion
4.2.1 H1: Mobile Marketing has a Positive Effect on Ethical Consumption
H1 is supported, indicating that mobile marketing positively influences ethical consumption. From the S–
O–R perspective, mobile marketing acts as a digital stimulus that delivers persuasive and informational
cues and activates cognitive evaluation processes that lead to ethical decision -making responses.  This
finding suggests that ethical consumption is shaped not only by product availability but also by the
internalization of ethical information conveyed through digital channels.  This result aligns with prior
literature showing that transparent messaging and value communication enhance ethical purchase intentions
through awareness and normative reinforcement (Alwonais & Al Mubarak, 2025; Hawkins & Horst, 2020).
Similar research in digital marketing has also shown that personalized and interactive mobile
communication encourages responsible consumption. Accordingly, the confirmation of H1 reinforces that
digital persuasive strategies help form ethical buying behaviors.

4.2.2 H2: Gamification has a Positive Effect on Ethical Consumption
H2 is supported, showing that gamification positively influences ethical consumption. From the S –O–R
perspective, gamification acts as a stimulus that enhances engagement through rewards and challenges,
prompting consumers to reflect on their social and environmental values before purchasing.  This process
fosters moral deliberation and experiential involvement.  This finding aligns with prior studies indicating
that gamified environments boost awareness, self-monitoring, and pro-social motivation (Hawkins & Horst,
2020). Compared to the functional utility of fintech, gamification provides psychological and normative
reinforcement, supporting ethical consumption through reflective and motivational pathways rather than
mere transactional efficiency.

4.2.3 H3: Customer Reviews have a Positive Effect on Ethical Consumption
H3 is supported, confirming that customer reviews positively influence ethical consumption. Within the S–
O–R framework, customer reviews operate as informational and social stimuli that communicate credibility,
usage experiences, and perceived ethical values.  These cues activate cognitive processes such as trust
formation, risk assessment, and social validation, which subsequently promote responsible consumption.
This finding aligns with prior research emphasizing the influence of e -WOM and user-generated content
on sustainable and ethical purchasing behavior, as reviews enhance transparency, reduce uncertainty, and
reinforce normative motivations (Alwonais & Al Mubarak, 2025; Hawkins & Horst, 2020). Compared with
fintech, which emphasizes transactional efficiency, customer reviews emphasize peer validation and value
communication, making them more effective in shaping ethical orientations.  Thus, H3 supports the view
that ethical consumption emerges from informational persuasion embedded in digital social contexts.

4.2.4 H4: Fintech has a Positive Effect on Ethical Consumption (Not Supported)
H4 is not supported, indicating that fintech does not directly influence ethical consumption. Within the S–
O–R framework, fintech operates as a utilitarian stimulus, emphasizing speed, convenience, and
transactional efficiency, which is insufficient to trigger the organism -level moral cognition required for
value-driven ethical consumption.  This aligns with prior findings showing that fintech primarily affects
usefulness, inclusion, and behavioral intention rather than ethical or normative behaviors  (B. Setiawan et
al., 2025; Vardari & Hameli, 2025). Fintech tools generally function within transactional domains and do
not embed persuasive ethical cues, which explains the absence of a direct pathway.  Thus, ethical
consumption is not produced through technological enablement alone but requires value interpretation, as
evidenced by the significant mediation effect observed in H13.

4.2.5 H5: Mobile Marketing has a positive effect on Customer Resistance (Supported)
H5 is supported, indicating that mobile marketing influences customer resistance positively. In the S–O–R
framework, mobile marketing functions as a stimulus that increases exposure while activating cognitive
evaluations related to privacy, persuasion, and message credibility.  These reactions generate resistance,
which, in this context, reflects reflective screening rather than outright rejection.  From the MMAM
perspective, personalization and real -time targeting enhance relevance but also intensify scrutiny of
persuasive attempts. This result aligns with studies showing that push notifications and location -based
offers can trigger privacy concerns, perceived intrusiveness, and persuasion knowledge, thereby increasing
resistance (Bernritter, Ketelaar, & Sotgiu, 2021 ). Thus, resistance operates as a constructive evaluative
mechanism that precedes ethical decision-making in mediated pathways.

4.2.6 H6: Gamification has a Positive Effect on Customer Resistance (Supported)
H6 is supported, indicating that gamification positively influences customer resistance to change.  Within
the S–O–R framework, gamification features (badges, challenges, and rewards) act as stimuli that enhance
engagement while activating reflection on persuasion intent, fairness, and data transparency.  Consistent
with persuasion knowledge theory, consumers become more resistant when they detect strategic motives
that are embedded in gamified tactics.  In this context, resistance reflects evaluative caution, rather than
disengagement. Prior studies have also shown that gamified marketing triggers assessments of trust,
autonomy, and privacy, thereby increasing resistance responses (Hofacker, De Ruyter, Lurie, Manchanda,
& Donaldson, 2016). Thus, the confirmation of H6 reinforces customer resistance as an organismic filtering
mechanism that precedes ethical consumption in a mediated pathway.

4.2.7 H7: Customer Reviews have a Positive Effect on Customer Resistance (Supported)
H7 is supported, indicating that customer reviews significantly increase customer resistance to change.
Within the S–O–R framework, reviews act as informational stimuli that prompt reflective processing related
to credibility, authenticity, and perceived risk. Instead of merely informing choices, they foster cautious and
selective behaviors that represent constructive resistance rather than rejection.  This is consistent with
persuasion knowledge theory, which states that exposure to user-generated information heightens consumer

awareness of persuasive tactics, increasing evaluative scrutiny.  Prior studies show that reviews stimulate
critical thinking and skepticism and reduce impulsive decision -making due to higher accountability and
transparency (Filieri, 2015). Thus, the confirmation of H7 suggests that resistance arises not only from
digital design features but also from peer -generated knowledge structures that shape ethical decision -
making processes.

4.2.8 H8: Customer Reviews have a Positive Effect on Fintech Adoption
The results confirm H8, indicating that customer reviews positively and significantly influenced fintech
adoption. This finding aligns with the Information Adoption Model (IAM), which states that information
credibility and argument quality shape users’ perceived usefulness and intention to adopt digital
technologies. In fintech contexts, reviews reduce perceived uncertainty related to security and transaction
risk. This outcome is consistent with prior research Lee and Shin (2018) that found that review-based social
information increases trust and accelerates the adoption of financial technologies. The mechanism can also
be explained through Social Proof Theory, where peer evaluations act as social validation, encouraging
users to engage with fintech platforms.

4.2.9 H9: Customer Reviews have a positive effect on Ethical Consumption
H9 is supported, indicating that customer reviews positively and significantly influence ethical consumption
behavior. Within the S –O–R framework, reviews act as informational stimuli that trigger cognitive
evaluations of transparency, product quality, and social or environmental implications before purchase
decisions. This mechanism aligns with the Social Proof Theory, in which individuals rely on collective
evaluations under conditions of uncertainty, validating responsible consumption choices and reducing
perceived risk. Empirical studies have similarly shown that online reviews enhance consumer trust, reduce
ambiguity, and reinforce ethical or sustainability-oriented purchasing behaviors (P . Kumar & Ghodeswar,
2015). Thus, reviews not only inform but also legitimize the ethical considerations embedded in
consumption practices.

4.2.10 H 10: Customer Resistance Mediates the Relationship between Mobile Marketing and Ethical
Consumption
H10 is supported, indicating that customer resistance significantly mediates the effect of mobile marketing
on ethical consumerism.  Although mobile marketing directly encourages ethical considerations by
providing accessible product information, mediation underscores an additional reflective evaluation
process. In this context, customer resistance does not denote rejection but rather a deliberate scrutiny of
persuasive content and value alignment.  This confirms that ethical consumption emerges from conscious
deliberation rather than from mere marketing exposure.  Consistent with prior findings Nguyen, Tran, and
Nguyen (2025) and Kapoor and Vij (2018), mobile marketing triggers ethical reflection when messages are
actively interpreted.  Within the S –O–R framework, mobile marketing acts as a stimulus, resistance
represents organismic appraisal, and ethical consumption constitutes the response.  This result also aligns
with the TPB and MMAM, which emphasize cognitive filtering in shaping value -driven behavioral
outcomes.

H11 is supported, indicating that gamification indirectly influences ethical consumption through customer
resistance. Although gamified features such as rewards and challenges initially drive engagement, ethical
consumption emerges only when consumers critically assess the incentives and content provided.  In this
mechanism, customer resistance acts as a cognitive filter, allowing consumers to judge whether gamified
stimuli align with their moral values and responsible consumption goals.  This aligns with prior studies
Alwonais and Al Mubarak (2025)  and Vardari and Hameli (2025) that show that gamification promotes
ethical outcomes when accompanied by reflective evaluation.  Within the SOR framework, gamification
functions as a stimulus, resistance represents organismic appraisal, and ethical consumption constitutes the

4.2.12 H 12: Customer Resistance Mediates the Relationship between Customer Reviews and Ethical

H12 is supported, indicating that customer reviews indirectly influence ethical consumption through

H13 is supported, showing that fintech affects ethical consumption indirectly through customer resistance.
Although fintech is adopted mainly for functional benefits such as convenience and transactional efficiency,

5. Conclusions
5.1 Conclusion
This study concludes that digital marketing strategies such as mobile marketing, gamification, customer
reviews, and fintech shape ethical consumption predominantly through indirect mechanisms.  Ethical
consumption in digital environments does not arise from technological convenience alone but also from
consumers’ internal evaluative processes. Consistent with the S–O–R framework, digital strategies operate
as stimuli whose influence materializes only after cognitive and affective processing. Customer resistance
emerges as a central mediator that transforms digital exposure into ethical consumpt ion. Rather than
signaling rejection, this form of resistance reflects a mechanism through which consumers assess trust,
transparency, and value alignment.

The results also show that fintech has no significant direct effect on ethical consumption but becomes
influential when mediated by customer resistance.  This clarifies previous inconsistencies in the fintech
literature by indicating that ethical outcomes require a reflective appraisal of security, social implications,
and moral considerations rather than transactional efficiency alone.  Qualitative triangulation further
reinforces that consumers do not adopt digital innovations passively but engage in deliberate judgment,
particularly in ethically sensitive contexts. Overall, this study contributes to the literature by
reconceptualizing customer resistance as a key explanatory mechanism in ethical consumption. Practically,
firms seeking to promote ethical behavior should address consumer concerns, build trust, and enable value-
based decision-making, rather than relying solely on technological performance.

5.2 Research Limitations
Despite its contributions, this study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design restricts the ability
to observe changes in ethical consumption and customer resistance over time; longitudinal approaches may
better capture behavioral dynamics as digital familiarity increases. Second, although customer resistance
was identified as the main mediator, other psychological and contextual factors, such as moral identity,
trust, perceived risk, and digital literacy, were not included, which may constrain the model’s explanatory
scope. Third, the quantitative phase relied on self -reported measures, raising the potential for social
desirability bias in ethically sensitive contexts. Fourth, the qualitative component involved a limited number
of MSME actors, which, while adequate for triangulation, may not reflect the heterogeneity of consumer
and business experiences across sectors. Finally, the study was conducted within a specific geographic and
cultural setting, which may limit its generalizability to environments with different institutional, cultural,
or digital infrastructures.

5.3 Suggestions and Directions for Future Research
Future research should adopt longitudinal or experimental designs to examine how customer resistance and
ethical consumption evolve with sustained exposure to digital marketing and fintech technologies. Building
on the central mediating role of customer resistance identified in this study, subsequent models could
incorporate complementary psychological constructs such as trust, moral identity, perceived risk, and digital
literacy to better explain ethical  decision-making in digital contexts.  Cross-cultural and multi -country
studies are also recommended to test the robustness of the model across different regulatory, technological,
and cultural environments, particularly in emerging versus developed digital economies, thereby enhancing
its external validity. From a practical perspective, MSMEs and digital platform providers should move
beyond emphasizing technological efficiency and design strategies that prioritize transparency, data
security, and ethical value communication, transforming consumer resistance from a potential barrier into
a constructive evaluative process that supports ethical consumption.
