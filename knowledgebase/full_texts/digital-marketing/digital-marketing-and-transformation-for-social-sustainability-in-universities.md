Abstract

This paper seeks to explore the relationship between digital marketing, digital transformation,
and social sustainability in universities, with green technology innovation as a mediator. Digital
strategies have been associated with sustainability in higher  education in previous studies, yet
little evidence is available from the Middle East. Data were obtained from 213 senior executives
at the University of Anbar (Iraq) and analyzed using structural equation modeling with SPSS 26
and AMOS 26. The results sho w that affect and significantly social sustainability and that
innovation partly mediates these relationships and their total effects. The study extends this
research to a university in a conflict region by positioning it as the bridge between organizational
capacities and social sustainability. It is restricted to top managers; future research should involve
students and teachers.
Keywords: Digital Marketing; Digital Transformation; Social Sustainability; Universities.

Resumen
En este artículo se analiza la relación entre marketing digital, transformación digital y
sostenibilidad social en las universidades, siendo la innovación en tecnología verde la variable
mediadora. Si bien las investigaciones anteriores relacionan las estr ategias digitales con la
sostenibilidad de la educación superior, la evidencia del Medio Oriente es escasa. Se recogieron
datos de 213 altos ejecutivos de la Universidad de Anbar (Irak), los cuales se analizaron mediante
ecuaciones estructurales a través d el SPSS 26 y AMOS 26. Los resultados indican que ambos
influyen de forma significativa en la innovación y la sostenibilidad social, que media parcialmente
en estas relaciones y sus efectos totales. Situándola como puente entre capacidades organizativas
y sostenibilidad social, el estudio extiende esta investigación a una universidad de una región en
conflicto. Solo está dirigido a altos directivos; en futuras investigaciones deberían ser incluidos
estudiantes y profesorado.
Palabras clave:  Marketing Digital; Transformación Digital; Sostenibilidad Social;
Universidades.

Introduction
Universities and other higher education institutions are expected to promote a social orientation
and contribute to balanced social sustainability (Aleixo et al., 2018). In practice, this expectation
takes the form of sustainability reporting, curricula fo r education for sustainable development
(Aleixo et al., 2018), and awareness initiatives that generate interest in climate change and in local
and global problems of poverty, food, energy, and water scarcity (Disterheft et al., 2015; Amran
et al., 2016; Sullivan & Gouldson, 2017). The social role of universities is increasingly linked to
their other missions (Benn & Dunphy, 2009; Figueiró & Raufflet, 2015; Holm et al., 2015;
O’Byrne et al., 2015). Nevertheless, few studi es reflect on how new technological ideas  and
digital marketing techniques can support the social dimension of sustainability. The greatest
attention has been paid to economic and environmental aspects, while relatively little has been
studied on digital and marketing processes that can strengthe n the social component. The
present study aims to fill this gap.
The digital revolution, technological change, and globalization have exposed the limits of higher-
education models constructed in the second half of the twentieth century, many of which are
now obsolete (Kleiner, 2019). One of the consequences is the press ure for universities to
fundamentally change in the next two decades (Timokhova  et al., 2022). Meanwhile, threats on
campus and online have increased, and models that rely heavily on human supervision have not
kept up, making it harder to keep students and staff safe. The adoption of digital technology is
crucial for universities to be competitive (Norton et al., 2020). Two factors determine the
eventual result of competitiveness for social sustainability: the rate of adoption of new

Digital marketing is ubiquitous in higher education now. Digital media is a major communication
channel worldwide (Kannan & Li, 2017). Many organizations report intensive use of digital
marketing. Recent evidence from the Middle East is pointing in the sam e direction. According
to Alshaketheep et al. (2024), digital marketing at Jordanian universities boosted knowledge of
the Sustainable Development Goals among students, faculty, and administrators. This finding
suggests that digital marketing can serve the university’s social mission, not just recruitment and
reputation.
Social sustainability is also part of the broader agenda of digital transformation. Digital
technology is reshaping the distribution of economic activity’s benefits. The social dimension of
this agenda is related to fair sharing of the benefits of digitali zation, use of technology for well-
being, and prevention of widening inequality. It is also connected to adverse effects of
technology on employment and privacy (Iqbal, 2021). The present study builds on an integrated
framework of three concepts: social su stainability, which relates to well -being and equitable
distribution of benefits; digital transformation, which relates to how organizations embed
technology in their processes; and digital marketing, which relates to how digital resources
support engagement with the university community. These concepts together allow the question
of how universities may use digital means to enhance social sustainability.
The case studied here is the University of Anbar, one of the more established universities in Iraq,
which appears in several international rankings and maintains an active program of technological
development. These developments will be integrated into the university’s digital transformation
and the use of technology in marketing to promote social sustainability. It also has real
constraints, including low awareness of technological methods, insufficient funds for advanced
equipment, and the organizational structure that does not fully support technological change.
These conditions render the university a useful critical case, since the development of digital
infrastructure alongside the difficulty of putting it into practice provides a concrete basis for
studying the relationship between digital strategies and social sustainability.
This setting also speaks to a larger divide. Most literature on digital transformation and
sustainability in higher education has been developed in politically and economically stable
contexts with little evidence from conflict -affected areas. Al -Shaer et al. (2025), in their case
study of An-Najah National University in Palestine, demonstrate how digital transformation can
enhance access, resilience, and administrative efficiency in times of instability, albeit with some
financial and structural constraints. A second context in which to explore the operation of digital
strategies amid limited resources is the University of Anbar, in a province that has undergone
similar turmoil. The analysis also speaks to the wider role of universities beyond teaching and
research, often referred to as the third mission and located within the Education 4.0 agenda,
where digital capability is linked to social and economic contribution.

These threads come together in what scholars have recently described as the twin transition, the
simultaneous and interrelated transition to digital and green transformation (Bulut & Oğuz
Haçat, 2026). Conceptually and bibliometrically , the twin transition has been largely studied
within the framework of higher education, with limited empirical evidence from developing and
resource-constrained systems (Omar & Abdullahi, 2024). This frame is directly addressed by the
present study. On th e digital side we have digital transformation and digital marketing; on the
green side we have green technology innovation, and the outcome is social sustainability. The
study is also related to the Sustainable Development Goals, namely quality education ( SDG 4),
industry, innovation, and infrastructure (SDG 9), and climate action (SDG 13), which
universities are increasingly expected to advance by making green technology innovation the link
between the two sides.
The main problem is the uncertainty of the University of Anbar and other universities in similar
situations in benefiting from digital marketing and digital transformation for social sustainability
and the gap between the digital resources available and th e extent of their use. This requires a
quantitative analysis able to identify the relations involved and their main drivers. The study has
two objectives. First, it is to determine the scope of universities to orient their digital marketing
and digital transformation to social sustainability by the senior administrators who have control
over these decisions. Secondly, to examine whether digital capability affects social sustainability
via green technology innovation. As the respondents are senior administrators, the goals are at
the institutional level. Future work on student data can explore behavior at the student level.
The hypotheses are based on three theoretical frameworks. Universities can use digital
technologies to sense changes in the environment, seize opportunities for innovation, and
reconfigure their capabilities for social and economic sustainability, in line with the dynamic
capabilities theory (Teece, 2007). Institutional theory (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983) accounts for
the regulatory and normative pressures on universities to implement digital and green practices,
such as accreditation requirements, funding conditions, and stakeholder expe ctations. The
adoption of green technology innovation and digital marketing is viewed as a strategic response
to the expectations of stakeholders (Freeman, 1984), including students, staff members, the local
community, and funders. The three perspectives complement the suggested model and indicate
a gap in the relationship between green technological advances and social sustainability in higher
education.
Digital marketing is the use of electronic tools such as mobile devices and computers by
organizations to market their products or services (Montgomery et al., 2012). The focus is on
the link between digital marketing and social sustainability. Digital mar keting is a fast and
effective way of reaching an audience through digital media and offers that audience various
ways in which to interact with what is being offered (Royle & Laing, 2014). It enables
organizations to build awareness, understand preference s, develop relationships, and earn
revenue (Kannan & Li, 2017). Many universities have incorporated sustainable development in
their mission statements (Cortese, 2003), but the implementation is uneven, especially for social
sustainability (del Mar Alonso -Almeida et al., 2015). Many institutions continue to rely on

From this reasoning follows the first hypothesis.
H1: Digital marketing is positively related to social sustainability.
The same reasoning is valid for the relationship between digital transformation and social
sustainability. The digital transformation in universities is a move away from traditional teaching
approaches to designs that use the present technology to improve the quality and efficiency of
education, respond to a fast -changing labor market, and compete for students (Mühlburger et
al., 2019). Common barriers are weak leadership support, resistance to new technology, lack of
skills, poor analysis, rigid existing structures, misalignment between strategy and objectives, lack
of clear technology vision, and limited shared understanding between stakeholders (Maltese,
2018). Digital transformation has a strong social effect, and technological development should
be aimed at improving quality of life and supporting human values (Felt, 2022). If social effects
of digital transformation are not considered, it can deepen the digital divide, limit access, threaten
jobs, and jeopardize data (Reggi & Gil -Garcia, 2021). However,  if they are considered, it can
support social progress. From the dynamic capabilities perspective, digital transformation is not
only a technological process but also a way to reconfigure organizational capabilities to generate
social value and to meet stakeholders’ demands for environmentally friendly practices. This
becomes the second hypothesis.
H2: Digital transformation is positively related to social sustainability.
As environmental problems are worsening and resources are becoming scarce, green technology
innovation has drawn more and more attention. This allows organizations to create sustainable
products and processes and achieve their sustainable development goals (Figueiró & Raufflet ,
2015; Ahmed et al., 2022). Higher education contributes to this innovation in various ways, such
as by improving the quality of the workforce, contributing to green development and
employment (Geng et al., 2023), and by maintaining a knowledge base that encourages entry into
technology-intensive fields (Liu & Wang, 2022). However, much of the evidence for the green
innovation-sustainability relationship has been drawn from industry (Song et al., 2020; Shahzad
et al., 2020; Li et al., 2025), so recent high er education studies are valuable in avoiding over -
generalizing industry findings to universities. Nusraningrum et al. (2024) show that green
innovation and green technology mediate the impact of e -learning on green management
practice among lecturers. Sutawaidjaya et al. (2024) show that green innovation mediates the
impact of green management and orga nizational culture on the sustainability of private

H3: Green technology innovation is positively related to social sustainability.
Digital marketing can promote innovation in green technologies. It enhances communication

technologies that improve stakeholder satisfaction (Kohtamäki  et al., 2019). It promotes
networking, content management, and collaboration. New channels are opened for staff and

technologies, and digital marketing can be useful to sustain a shift toward green technology. The
higher-education evidence mentioned above points to the same channel but via universities'
pedagogical and inst itutional services rather than production processes ( Nusraningrum et al.,

H4: Digital marketing is positively related to green technology innovation.
There is a large body of evidence on how digital transformation affects green technology
innovation. Several studies report the positive effects of digital transformation on green
innovation (El-Kassar & Singh, 2019; Yin et al., 2023; Sui & Yao, 2023; Li e t al., 2025). Digital
transformation can boost absorptive capacity, enhance human capital, increase investment in
innovation, improve internal control of green technologies, and reduce the related costs (Sun et
al., 2023). Institutional theory accounts for  the role of regulatory and funding pressures in
prompting universities to develop green innovation as part of a larger digital transformation.
This leads to the fifth hypothesis.
H5: Digital transformation is positively related to green technology innovation.
Finally, green technology innovation may play the mediating role. In several studies, it plays the
role of a mechanism connecting digital and non -digital antecedents to outcomes. Sui and Yao
(2023) find it mediates the relationship between digital transfor mation and corporate
financialization; Wang et al. (2022) find mediation between environmental regulation and
sustainability performance; Xu et al. (2022) find mediation between social responsibility and
financial and environmental performance; and Feng et  al. (2021) find mediation between
environmental information disclosure and pollution. Much of this evidence comes from
manufacturing and corporate settings and should be applied to universities with caution. The
above-mentioned studies of higher education  (Nusraningrum et al., 2024; Sutawaidjaya et al.,
2024) confirm the same mediating role within the framework of educational services, which
forms the sector-specific basis for the last two hypotheses. Green technology innovation is the
transforming mechanism  between digital capability and social sustainability from a dynamic
capabilities perspective.
H6: Green technology innovation mediates the relationship between digital marketing and social sustainability.

Method
The study used a quantitative cross -sectional survey design, and structural equation modelling
was used to test the hypothesised  relationships. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS
version 26.0 and AMOS version 26.0. The University of Anbar was chosen as a critical case due
to its recent progress in the international rankings and early implementation of the digital
transformation initiatives, making it an appropriate context in which to study the research
variables.

The sample consisted of 213 senior administrators at various levels, including university
presidents, college deans, department heads, and directors of university units, divisions, and
centres. A non -probability purposive method was used, as senior administrative leaders are
knowledgeable about and directly involved in decisions on digital transformation and are
therefore well placed to report on the study variables. Questionnaires were administered in paper
rather than electronic form, allowing the research team to monitor completion, clarify items
where necessary and respond to respondents’ questions. The data were collected for a period of
about three months with periodic collection and face -to-face follow-up. The response rate was
100%. The study was approved by the ethical committee of the University of Anbar. The first

was assessed through 6 items adapted from De Pelsmacker et al. (2018). Digital transformation
was measured by 11 items taken from Chu et al. (2019) and Jafari -Sadeghi et al. (2021). Green

instrument was translated into Arabic and then back translated into English to make sure of the
meaning of eac h item. Face and content validity was checked by an academic panel prior to
distribution, and the wording of the questions for paper questionnaires could be clarified during
data collection and therefore reduce the chance of misinterpretation.

The analysis was done in several steps. Reliability was tested using Cronbach’s alpha, composite
reliability and McDonald’s omega. Convergent validity was measured using average variance
extracted and the Fornell and Larcker (1981) criterion. Discriminant validity was evaluated on
the basis of the Fornell–Larcker criterion and the heterotrait–monotrait (HTMT) ratio. To test
the measurement model for each of the constructs separately and the four -factor model
collectively, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted. Then, the structural model was
estimated by using structural equation modelling (SE M). Hierarchical regression and the Sobel
test were used to test for mediation, and bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals based on
5,000 resamples (Preacher & Hayes, 2008) were used to further examine mediation. The fit of
the model was assessed us ing the chi -square statistic, normed chi -square (CMIN/DF), GFI,
AGFI, CFI, TLI, IFI, NFI, RMSEA, and SRMR using the threshold values from Hu and Bentler
(1999), Hair et al. (2010), and Kline (2016).

Before hypothesis testing, some assumptions were checked. Harman’s single -factor test
(Podsakoff et al., 2003) was used to test for common method bias. The first unrotated factor
accounted for 32.4% of the variance. This was below the 50% cut -off and indic ated that

for the items (all below ±2) to assess multivariate normality. Mardia’s coefficient was OK. The
Mahalanobis distance identified no multivariate outliers. Variance inflation factors for all
variables were less than 3, suggesting multicollinearity is not a concern. The sample size (N =
213) was above the commonly accepted minimum for structural equation modelling , and the

the power was > 0.80 for the tested paths. The sample size was determined by the size of the

Discussion
All seven hypotheses were confirmed. The results show that green technology innovation
positively mediates the relationship between digital marketing, digital transformation and social
sustainability at the University of Anbar. Much of the evidence on green technology innovation
is to be found in manufacturing and corporate settings and cannot be transferred to a university
context without adaptation. The interpretation given here is therefore cast within higher
education.

This positive relationship between digital marketing and social sustainability is consistent with
the growing use of digital marketing tools by university centres , units and departments. The
effect may be more pronounced in less digitally mature environments, as Iraqi universities move
from rudimentary automation to a more robust digital transformation, and institutions with
limited resources depend more on tools that connect more directly to stakeholders. This finding
is in line with Kannan and Li (2017) and the overall literature on the role of digital marketing in
promoting sustainability (Dumitriu et al., 2019; Saura et al., 2020; Baltaci, 2021; Arantes, 2022).
This is also consistent with recent evidence from the Middle East, where digital marketing has
heightened sustainability awareness in Jordanian universities ( Alshaketheep et al., 2024).
However, the evidence base is still emerging, and this finding should be read as part of an
emerging body of work rather than an established consensus.
Also, social sustainability was positively related to digital transformation. This is consistent with
the work of Nosratabadi et al. (2023) who emphasised the need for digital transformation for
social sustainability, and with the evidence that organisatio ns that report higher levels of digital
transformation report stronger sustainability performance (Feroz et al., 2021; Esses et al., 2021;
Guandalini, 2022; Costa et al., 2022; Ji et al., 2023). The pattern supports a dynamic capabilities
interpretation where institutions achieve sustainable outcomes through constant reconfiguring
of their digital capabilities and is consistent with the largely top-driven nature of change in Iraqi
universities.
Although the structural model shows a greater direct effect for digital marketing than digital
transformation ( β = 0.278 vs. β = 0.191; see Table 5), it is misleading to compare the two
capabilities only based on their direct effects because a large part of each capability’s effect is
transmitted indirectly through green technology innovation. The total effects of digital marketing
and digital transformation are similar in mediation models (Table 8): 0.462 and 0.503,
respectively, with green technology innovation accounting for slightly more than half of each
(56.8%, 52.2%). When indirect effects are taken into account , the view that digital marketing
contributes more to social sustainability than digital transformation is not supported; rather, the
total effect of digital transformation is slightly larger. One likely explanation is that the building
of digital infrastr ucture has a longer -lasting influence than the mere communication activities,
although the relatively recent introduction of digital transformation at the university may also
limit the direct effect seen here.
The two digital capabilities were linked to green technology innovation for social sustainability.
In a university this mechanism functions less through production processes, as in much of the
industrial literature, and more through pedagogical and institu tional services and campus
operations. This reading is bolstered by the literature on higher education. Green innovation and
green technology are the green management practices of lecturers (Nusraningrum  et al., 2024).
Green innovation mediates the effect of green management and organisational culture on
university sustainability. Sutawaidjaya et al. (2024) The result is also consistent with the broader
literature linking green innovation to sustainabilit y (Galindo-Martín et al., 2019; Vimala et al.,
2022; Wang, 2022; Islam, 2023; Rahman & Shah, 2023; Batool & Mohsin, 2024).

The association between digital marketing and green technology innovation is indicative of the

to a wider audience, a finding consistent with Dewi and Sudhiksa (2022). Digital transformation
and green technology innovation are linked by increased environmental consciousness and top
management support for digital transformation (Xue et al., 2022; Ren & Li, 2022; Zhang et al.,
2023; Tang et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). For instance, Ahmed et al. (2022)
illustrated the mediating role of green technology innovation via digital pathways in a university
setting.
Theoretical Contributions of This Study 1. First, it enriches the research on the relationship
between digital transformation and sustainability by providing green technology innovation as a
mediating mechanism instead of a side effect, which provides a cl earer pathway for digital
capabilities to generate social value rather than presuming that they do. Second, it provides an
empirical test of the twin transition at the level of institutions, showing how digital and green
efforts come together to produce a social outcome with green innovation acting as the bridge;
this adds to a twin -transition literature in higher education that is largely conceptual or
bibliometric (Bulut & Oğuz Haçat, 2026). Third, it integrates institutional and stakeholder
theories to interpret social value as a reflection of the university’s digital capabilities. Fourth, it
occurs in a context of conflict and paucity of evidence and resources (Omar & Abdullahi, 2024;
Al-Shaer et al., 2025). It addresses the extent to which such relationships are generalisable
beyond stable, well-resourced systems, which dominate the literature.
The findings have a number of implications for university decision -makers. Colleges need to
develop a formal strategy for digital communication with students and local communities. They
should improve their digital infrastructure, incorporate sustainability into their digital
transformation, and foster innovation in green technology at the college and department levels.
Where possible, investment in digital technology should be directed to environmentally
sustainable options so that increases in digital capacity also bring social benefits. These initiatives
should be grounded in  a digital governance framework to help ensure sustainability beyond
leadership changes.
Some limitations are presented which point the way forward for future work. Data were collected
from only senior administrators first. This group will give firsthand experience of decision -
making in digital transformation. One group of stakeholders is a hu ge disadvantage for a study
of social sustainability, because it limits diversity in perspectives. Future research needs to
incorporate the perspectives of students and faculty. Second, the results of this study should not
be over-generalised because it was limited to one institution. Frameworks will be tested across
the breadth of the Middle East and North Africa in multi -country, multi-institutional designs.
Third, the study is cross -sectional and non-probability, and causality cannot be con cluded, and
the results are presented as associations and not as causal effects. Future evidence needs to be
increased with longitudinal designs, qualitative additions and probability sampling.

Conclusion
The findings of this study revealed that social sustainability is linked with digital marketing and
digital transformation at the University of Anbar. Green technology innovation partially
mediated the two relationships, however. Digital marketing helps to build the university’s image
and trust in the community and equitable service delivery; digital transformation allows the
university assets to serve the social mission of the university. In both cases, digital capabilities
are used in the name of environmental and social equity, through innovation in green technology.
The findings of the twin transition imply that a university should tackle the digital and green
transition together, not in isolation, and can serve as a basis for integrating the digital str ategy
into sustainability goals in comparable settings. More research is needed with a wider range of
stakeholders and institutions to confirm and build on these findings.
