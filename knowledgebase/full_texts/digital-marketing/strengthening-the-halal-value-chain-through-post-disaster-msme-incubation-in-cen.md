 This study evaluates how the PABETA incubation program strengthened the halal value
chain of post-disaster MSMEs in Central Sulawesi, Indonesia. Using an evaluative case
study design and the Context –Input–Process–Product (CIPP) framework, the study
examines program relevance, implementation quality, and early outcomes. Data were
collected retrospectively from program documents, participant outputs, activity records,
and stakeholder feedback, and were analyzed using descriptive statistics and qualitative
content analysis. The findings show that the program addressed four interrelated areas:
business readiness, certification and compliance, digital marketing and distribution, and
external market linkage. All 25 participating MSMEs completed Business Model
Canvases and established professional social media accounts, while 18 adopted e -
commerce platforms. Ten MSMEs initiated PIRT or halal-related certification processes,
seven obtained PIRT certification, and nine reached investment readiness, with six
attracting investor interests. These results suggest that integrated incubation can
strengthen the upgrading of halal -oriented MSMEs, although longer -term mentoring is
still needed to support sustained market performance.

INTRODUCTION
Micro, small, and medium enterprises (MSMEs) play a strategic role in regional economic development
by supporting employment, supporting household income, and strengthening community resilience. (Aisya &
Syamsu, 2024; Sofyan et al., 2022). However, from th e perspective of Islamic economics, the development of
MSMEs is not only related to increasing productivity or increasing income. It is also closely related to
strengthening the halal ecosystem that ensures product integrity, consumer trust, business legit imacy, and
broader market participation. (Makhasi et al., 2025; Sofyan et al., 2023; Syamsu et al., 2025). Recent literature
shows that the halal ecosystem is shaped by interactions between producers, regulators, certification bodies,
financial institutions, consumers, and supporting institutions, all of which must work in coordination to maintain
halal integrity and business sustainability. (Maulana et al., 2025; Muflihin & Fatchan, 2025; Nusran et al., 2025).
Therefore, the empowerment of MSMEs in the context of the Muslim majority should be understood not only as
an increase in business, but as part of a broader effort to strengthen a halal-oriented local economic system.
The halal value chain ranges from production and processing to certification, distribution, marketing, and
governance. Therefore, the halal ecosystem requires not only religious compliance but also institutional
coordination, traceability, and technological support. Comparative studies show that regulatory approaches
differ across countri es, with centralized and decentralized models resulting in different implications for the
efficiency, inclusivity, and participation of MSMEs. (Zhang et al., 2026). In Indonesia, the halal economy has
grown rapidly. However, the integration of business actors into the halal ecosystem remains uneven, especially
among small businesses that often struggle to meet certification requirements, adopt digital tools, and respond
to increasingly formal market standards. (Ansori et al., 2025; Fathoni et al., 2025). As  a result, strengthening
halal participation among MSMEs requires more than just normative awareness; This demands a practical
support system that links regulation, training, certification, and market access.
This issue is very important for Indonesian MSM Es, many of which continue to face structural barriers
to entering the halal-oriented market. Recent studies show that the intention to pursue halal certification is not
only shaped by religiosity or awareness but also by administrative readiness, financial capacity, digital literacy,
and perceived business benefits. (Fianto et al., 2025; Wahab et al., 2024). Other studies show that policy
change alone is not enough when small businesses lack operational guidance, institutional support, and
accessible certification pathways. (Putro et al., 2024; Rofiah et al., 2024). In practical terms, this means that
halal certification for MSMEs should not be seen only as a legal obligation but as a process that requires literacy,
facilitation, and institutional assistance. Without such support, many micro and small businesses remain outside
the formal halal ecosystem even as they operate in a predominantly Muslim market.
At the same time, digitalization is becoming increasingly important in the development of halal -oriented
MSMEs. Digital technologies support traceability, certification management, branding, customer
communication, and broader market access. The halal supply chain literature highlights that digital tools,
including platform -based marketing, traceability sy stems, and advanced technologies such as AI and
blockchain, can strengthen operational trust and transparency. However, adoption remains uneven among
small businesses. (Harsanto et al., 2024; Khan et al., 2025). Therefore, for MSMEs, digitalization is not only a
technical problem but also a strategic driver of competitiveness in the halal economy. This argument is
strengthened by recent evidence showing that halal certification, branding, and digital innovation work together
to increase market legitimacy an d strengthen consumer trust, especially in an increasingly competitive food
market. (Dhakirah & Hidayatinnisa, 2026; Mohd Salleh et al., 2026).
From the business side, halal participation can also contribute to stronger MSME performance. Recent
research in Indonesia shows that halal certification is associated with increased business credibility, stronger
PIRT atau sertifikasi halal; tujuh memperoleh sertifikat PIRT, dan sembilan mencapai
kesiapan investasi, dengan enam di antaranya berhasil menarik minat investor. Temuan
ini menunjukkan bahwa inkubasi terintegrasi dapat memperkuat peningkatan kapasitas
UMKM berorientasi halal, meskipun pendampingan jangka panjang masih di perlukan
untuk mendukung keberlanjutan kinerja pasar.

These concerns are particularly relevant in Central Sulawesi, where post-disaster recovery requires not

the PABETA Business Incubation Program is implemented to support MSMEs through t raining, mentoring,

support. Although the program was initially framed as a general incubation intervention, its structure also reflects
key elements of strengthening the halal ecosystem, especially for food-based MSMEs that require certification

Islamic economic perspective, as it offers a practica l example of how post -disaster MSME support can
contribute to more formal, trusted, and market-ready participation in the halal economy.
 Despite the growing literature on halal certification, halal value chains, and MSME empowerment, there
are still important gaps. Most halal value chains include a series of value -creation activities from the selection
of raw materials, processing, storage, packaging, distribution, marketing, to products received by consumers
while maintaining halal integrity (Mohd, 2024).  In this study, the strengthening of the halal value chain is
operationalized through seven dimensions: (1) raw materials and suppliers; (2) production processes and
facilities; (3) traceability; (4) Halal assurance system; (5) legality and certification; (6) storage and distribution;
and (7) product information and halal market access (Muhlizoh & Noer, 2025).
 Beyond the halal value chain, the Islamic economic perspective is employed to assess program quality
through the principles of halal–tayyib, maṣlaḥah, al-‘adl, al-ta‘āwun, and amanah (Mayang et al., 2025). Halal–
tayyib requires that products be not only halal in terms of their ingredients but also safe, clean, and processed
responsibly. Ma ṣlaḥah directs the program toward tangible and sustainable ben efits. Al -‘adl demands
proportional access to assistance, including for participants with limitations. Al-ta‘āwun emphasises partnership
among MSME actors, facilitators, the government, certification bodies, and the market. Amanah pertains to
honesty of information, consistency of processes, and consumer protection (Sofiana et al., 2024).
 A variety of literature, however, has recently focused on governance, certification barriers, digital
technologies, or consumer behavior . At the same time, incubation stu dies often emphasize entrepreneurial
development without explicitly linking it to participation in the halal ecosystem. (Fathoni et al., 2025; Harsanto
et al., 2024; Nusran et al., 2025). As a result, there is still limited empirical research on how post -disaster
incubation programs can strengthen business capacity while supporting halal readiness, certification
orientation, and market legitimacy among MSMEs. This gap is important because, in regions recovering from
disasters, business support must not only  address survival and income recovery, but also long -term inclusion
in more formal and competitive value chains.
This research overcomes these gaps by evaluating the Central Sulawesi PABETA Business Incubation
Program through the Context-Input-Process-Product (CIPP) framework. The study positions incubation not only
as a business development activity in general but as a strategic intervention that can strengthen participation in
the halal ecosystem through improved business planning, digital capabilities, c ertification support, and market
access. In particular, this study examines: (1) the context that shaped the program in Central Sulawesi after the
disaster; (2) the adequacy of the input, including the composition of participants, mentors, and program design;
(3) the process of implementing training, mentoring, digital support, and certification facilitation; and (4) outputs
generated in business model development, digital presence, certification progress, and investment readiness.
Thus, this study contribu tes to the literature on Islamic economics and MSME development by showing how
post-disaster incubation can serve as a practical mechanism to strengthen local halal ecosystems.

RESEARCH METHODS
This study uses an evaluative case study design, using the Context –Input–Process–Product (CIPP)
framework, to assess the Central Sulawesi PABETA Business Incubation Program systematically. The CIPP
model was chosen because it provides a structured framework for evaluating program relevance, resource
adequacy, implementation quality, and observable outcomes. At the same time, the evaluative case study
approach allows for an in -depth examination of rea l interventions in their actual context. This design is
appropriate because this study does not aim to test cause -effect relationships, but rather to assess the
advantages, implementation, and initial results of incubation programs for MSMEs in post -disaster settings, as
is commonly recommended in program evaluation research. (Stufflebeam & Coryn, 2014; Stufflebeam & Zhang,
2017; Yin, K., Robert & Campbell, T. Donald, 2018).
 The researcher acts as an independent evaluator and is part of the program assista nce implementation
team. The study was conducted retrospectively from December 2025 to January 2026, following the completion
of the main incubation cycle. The case studied is the PABETA Business Incubation Program which is carried
out in Central Sulawesi, Indonesia, especially in Palu City and surrounding districts that are affected. The unit
of analysis is the program as a whole, including its design, implementation, and direct output. This program
involves 25 MSMEs, through a selection process from the o rganizing team, namely by looking at business
profiles, business proposals, products and commitments, then completing intensive training for five days,
followed by mentoring and business matching activities. These MSMEs represent the food processing and
agricultural sectors, including businesses that produce fried onions, coffee, chocolate, and agriculture. This
composition makes the case relevant not only for the analysis of MSME development but also for examining
business readiness in food-related sectors, where certification, branding, and market legitimacy are critical.

To ensure triangulation and strengthen the credibility of the evaluation. The first source consists of
program documentation, including official reports, training modules, mentoring guidelines, administrative
records, participant registration data, and monitoring forms. The second source consists of participant output,
especially 25 Business Model Canvases, pitch decks, digital marketing content, and certification -related
documents produced during or after the incubatio n process. Third sources include program activity records,
such as attendance data, mentoring logs, schedules, and business matching documentation. The fourth source
involves stakeholder feedback, including mentor notes, program manager observations, and p articipant
responses collected after the training phase. These sources were chosen because they provide evidence not
only of program delivery, but also of participant readiness, digital adaptation, certification progress, and market
linkages.
Data analysis was carried out in two stages. First, descriptive quantitative analysis is used to summarize
measurable program output, including training completion, Business Model Canvas completion, digital platform
adoption, certification initiation, and investment readiness. Frequency and percentage are used to present these
results clearly. Second, qualitative content analysis is applied to participant outputs and program records to
assess the quality of business planning, digital communication, certification progress, and evidence of capability
development. The Business Model Canvas was examined for completeness, specificity, coherence, and
innovation, while the digital output was reviewed for branding consistency, professionalism, and use of customer
communication features.

To improve trust, this study used data triangulation across documents, participant outputs, activity
records, and stakeholder feedback. However, some limitations must be acknowledged. The study was
retrospective, did not include a comparison group, and focused primarily on immediate and short-term outcomes
rather than long -term business performance. As a result, findings should be interpreted as an evaluation of
program relevance, implementation quality, and initial outcomes, rather than as conclusive evidence of long -
term causal impact. Even so, this design is still appropriate to explain how the incubation program can
strengthen the readiness of MSMEs and support the gradual entry into a more structured and halal -oriented
value chain in a post-disaster context.

Strengthening the Halal Value Chain through Incubation
Product evaluation shows that the program strengthens the halal value chain of participating MSMEs
through four interrelated dimensions: upstream business readiness, certification and compliance support, digital
marketing and distribution capabilities, and external market linkage. This interpretation is important because the
halal value chain is not limited to certification alone; it encomp asses interrelated production, certification,
distribution, and marketing processes within the broader institutional ecosystem (Sochimin et al., 2025;
Harsanto et al., 2024).
This program improves business readiness through the development and mentoring of  the Business
Model Canvas. All 25 MSMEs completed the Business Model Canvases, with strong results in completeness
(100%), specificity (92%), and coherence (84%), although innovation remained more moderate (56%). These
findings suggest that incubation is effective in strengthening internal business organization and planning
disciplines. The improvement is important because structured business preparation forms the basis for
compliance, branding, and market engagement later in the day. Previous studies have  also shown that
incubation often improves the execution capabilities and structure of the business faster than deeper innovation
capacity. (Luo, 2024; Moroz et al., 2024; Osterwalder & Pigneur, 2010).
At the compliance level, certification support is one of the most significant program outcomes. Ten
participants initiated the certification process related to PIRT or halal, and seven obtained PIRT certification
during the observation period. Although certification advances are more limited than digital adop tion, these
outcomes remain important because formal compliance generally requires stronger administrative, technical,
and procedural readiness. The results are consistent with previous findings that MSMEs often struggle with
certification due to cost, doc umentation burden, literacy limitations, and institutional complexity, especially in
decentralized regulatory settings. (Putro et al., 2024; Rofiah et al., 2024; Siriwardane et al., 2025; Solfanelli et
al., 2021). In this study, certification facilitation helped move some participants from general awareness to
tangible compliance actions, a central step in participation in the halal value chain.
The level of marketing and distribution, digital results is very substantial. All participants built a
professional social media presence, 22 upgraded to WhatsApp Business, and 18 entered e -commerce
platforms. These results show that the program moves participants beyond basic digital awareness towards
practical digital communication and wider market access. For MSME s, especially in the food sector, digital
presence is not solely promotional; It also supports credibility, customer interaction, and market visibility. This
is in line with broader evidence showing that digital technologies are increasingly shaping SME competitiveness
and consumer trust, especially when used for product presentation, branding, and communication. (Camps et
al., 2026; Esposito et al., 2022; Hein et al., 2026). In the context of the halal market, digital communication also
contributes to branding, transparency, and building trust. (Dhakirah & Hidayatinnisa, 2026; Mohd Salleh et al.,
2026).
At the level of ecosystem linkage, this program produces selective but meaningful investment results
and market access. Nine MSMEs are considered ready to p itch, six attract investor interest, and two receive
initial funding commitments. This suggests that the program does not benefit all participants equally, but does
manage to prepare some of the efforts for broader external involvement. Such selectivity makes sense because
investment readiness typically depends on the maturity of the business, growth potential, and the ability of the
entrepreneur to present a coherent value proposition, not just on participation in training. (Gupta, 2025; Moroz
et al., 2024). These results show that strengthening the halal value chain also requires external linkages with
market participants, investors, and supporting institutions.

Discussion
Overall, the results of the study show that the PABETA Program plays a role more than just
conventional entrepreneurship training. This program functions as a business incubation mechanism that is
able to gradually increase the readiness of MSMEs in various important aspects, such as the preparation of
business plans, business digitalization, halal certification, market acce ss, and readiness to obtain investment.
Based on the results of the evaluation, the highest achievements were in the aspects of business planning and
digital orientation, while the indicators of halal certification and investor involvement showed relativel y lower
achievements. These findings indicate that internal capacity building can be achieved in the short term, while
achievements that require external validation and collaboration with others require longer mentoring time.
The findings are in line with the concept of business incubation which emphasizes that MSME
development requires an integrated approach, not partial interventions. Business incubators not only provide
training, but also provide mentoring, network access, consulting, and facilitati on of the resources needed by
business actors. The results of this study are consistent with (Lenihan, 2011; Moroz et al., 2024; Stufflebeam &
Zhang, 2017) who explained that the success of incubation is determined by the integration of various support
services so that it is able to increase business capacity in a sustainable manner. This shows that for food-based
MSMEs, digital capabilities and certifications should not be treated as peripheral elements. They are centers
for business improvement because th ey directly affect market trust, compliance, and access to a wider range
of commercial channels. (Camps et al., 2026; Esposito et al., 2022; Mohd Salleh et al., 2026; Nur Azizah et al.,
2025).
When compared to the MSME incubation program in Malaysia, the a chievements of the PABETA
Program show a relatively similar pattern. Various studies report that improving managerial skills, developing
business models, and adopting digital technologies is generally faster than indicators that require the fulfillment
of administrative requirements, such as product certification, access to formal financing, and investment
(Hackett & Dilts, 2004, Fithri et al., 2025). Thus, the results of the PABETA Program show that internal capacity

The results also reveal the limitations of short-cycle incubation. Certification is progressing slower than
digital adoption, and only a fraction of the group is achieving investme nt readiness. This suggests that while
intensive training and mentoring are effective in initiating change, they are not enough to ensure a full transition
to formal compliance or broader market expansion. In practical terms, future programs should expand post-
training support, especially for certification completion, conversion of digital presence into actual sales, and
long-term business relationships. These implications are also consistent with recent research on the halal
ecosystem, which emphasizes tha t MSME participation depends on sustainable growth, institutional
coordination, and support to navigate regulatory complexities. (Fathoni et al., 2025; Nusran et al., 2025; Zhang
et al., 2026).
These findings yield three practical implications. First, MSME  incubation in post -disaster settings
should be designed as an integrated model that connects business planning, digitalization, certification, and
market linkage, rather than as an isolated training module. Second, mentoring must continue beyond the
intensive phase as the transition from awareness to implementation, especially in certification and digital market
utilization, requires ongoing support. Third, future incubation programs should adopt a phased approach,
recognizing that not all MSMEs are equall y ready for certification or investor involvement at the same time. In
this sense, the program is effective in generating initial capability development and market preparation, but
long-term mentoring is still needed to achieve deeper and more sustainable business transformation.

CONCLUSION
This study concludes that the Central Sulawesi PABETA Business Incubation Program plays an important
role in strengthening the readiness of MSMEs in the post -disaster context. Through an integrated incubation
model, the  program addresses interconnected constraints related to business planning, digital capabilities,
certification support, and market linkage. These findings show that all 25 participating MSMEs completed
Business Model Canvases and built a professional soci al media presence. At the same time, 18 adopted e -
commerce platforms, 10 started the PIRT -related or halal certification process, seven obtained PIRT
certification, and nine achieved investment readiness. These results show that the program is effective in
promoting the improvement of start-up businesses and in increasing the readiness of MSMEs to participate in
a broader and more formal market environment. In this sense, the findings suggest that strengthening MSMEs
in post-disaster areas can be attributed to broader efforts to increase market legitimacy, consumer confidence,
and formal participation in halal -oriented business systems. Overall, this study contributes to the literature on
Islamic economics and MSME development by showing that post -disaster incubation can function not only as
a recovery intervention but also as a pathway to strengthen halal value chains. Therefore, future programs
should adopt a gradual and integrated approach that combines business planning, digitalization, certification
assistance, and market linkage, so that early improvements can translate into long-term business resilience and
inclusive economic development.
However, the study also shows that business improvement does not develop evenly across all dimensions.
Business planning and digital onboarding are achieved more broadly, while the completion of external market
certifications and validation remains more limited. This suggests that formal compliance and ecosystem linkages
require more time, stronger institutional support , and ongoing assistance beyond intensive training periods.
Therefore, while the program succeeds in generating direct output and supporting the development of initial
capabilities, a deeper and more sustainable transformation will depend on expanded mento ring, stronger
relationships with certification bodies, and follow -up support for digital market conversion and business
expansion.
