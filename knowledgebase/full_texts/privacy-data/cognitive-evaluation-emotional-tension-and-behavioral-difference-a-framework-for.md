Abstract. As artificial intelligence(AI) becomes deeply integrated into a dvertising, personalization
has become both effective and controversial. Targeted advertisi ng can improve relevance and
efficiency, but it also raises concerns about privacy and user autonomy. This paper proposes a
integration of cognition, emotion, and behavior framework to ex plain how users perceive
personalization in intelligent advertising. User perceptions are complex instead of one-dimensional.
At the cognitive level, users evaluate personalized advertising in terms of perceived value, perceived
risk, and perceived autonomy. At the emotional level, they may feel both trust and caution, reliance
and discomfort. These cognitive and emotional responses shape t hree main behavioral patterns:
positive engagement, active resistance, and conditional acceptance.
Keywords: intelligent advertising; perceived personalization; user persp ective; intelligent
technology.
1. Background
Over the past decade, artificial intelligence and big data have  significantly reshaped advertising.
Through recommendation systems, machine learning, and user profiling, advertisers can now deliver
ads that adjust in real time to users’ behavior and context. Intelligent advertising has become common
across social media platforms, search engines, and e-commerce s ites. At the same time, Chinese
governments have introduced regul ations addressing data protect ion, algorithmic transparency, and
user consent, and this action in turn reflects growing concerns about privacy and fairness.
Hence, personalization has become central to both advertising p ractice and academic research. It
is often described as a way to improve ad relevance and reduce information overload to enhance user
experience. However, user reactions are not always positive. Re search shows that while relevant
advertising can increase engagement, it can also create feelings of manipulation[1,2].
Although scholars have studied int elligent advertising from per spectives such as privacy
protection and algorithmic fairness, the concept of perceived p ersonalization remains insufficiently
developed. Adopting a critical path, this study focuses on the heterogeneous and paradoxical
experiences of users in intelligent advertising contexts. By proposing a dynamic model that integrates
cognition, emotion, and behavior, we elucidate the manifest cha racteristics and theoretical
implications of perceived personalization, thereby refining its analytical boundaries in contemporary
advertising practice.
2. From Intelligent Advertising to Perceived Personalization
2.1 The Conceptual Development of Intelligent Advertising
With the rapid development of artificial intelligence(AI), intelligent advertising has emerged from
the combination of AI technology and advertising. It has gradua lly become an important topic in
digital marketing research. However, there is still no unified definition of intelligent advertising.
Scholars define it from different perspectives, such as technology use, value orientation, and practical
context.
For Starters, research on intelligent advertising began earlier  in Western academia. Adams first
introduced the concept of “intelligent advertising” in academic  research. He described it as a new

form of advertising that adjusts content and communication meth ods based on user behavior within
an AI and digital media environment[3]. In China, one of the earliest and most widely cited definitions
was proposed by Long Yi [4]. He defined intelligent advertising as a series of new advertising forms
supported by artificial intelligence and based on Web 3.0 technology.
As AI technologies and big data algorithms became more deeply i ntegrated into advertising
practice and digital marketing, re search on intelligent adverti sing increased. At the 2019 American
Advertising Conference, Hairong Li defined intel ligent advertis ing as consumer-centered, data-
driven, and algorithm-mediated brand communication[5]. This definition attracted wide attention and
discussion.
From these studies, intelligent advertising shows three main ch aracteristics. First, it relies on
algorithm-driven interaction. Base d on large-scale user data, m achine learning and algorithm
prediction are used to automate advertising decisions[6]. Second, it emphasizes contextual interaction.
Time, location, and other situati onal factors are included in t he matching process to improve the
accuracy of advertising delivery in specific scenarios[7]. Third, it works through feedback loops. User
behavior data flows back into th e system in real time, allowing  continuous adjustment and
optimization of advertising content and delivery strategies[8,9].
Based on the above discussion of the definitions and features of intelligent advertising, this paper
focuses on how intelligent advert ising matches user needs throu gh personalization. It further
examines intelligent advertising as a new form of communication  that uses algorithms as
intermediaries, scenarios as context, and feedback as a closed loop to achieve personalized interaction
between advertising and users through data-driven processes.
2.2 Perceived Personalization in Intelligent Advertising
Early studies generally understood personalized advertising as a form of advertising that delivers
different content based on user  information. Yuan & Tsao argued  that personalize d advertising
provides customized information at  specific times and in specif ic contexts to meet individual
needs[10]. Vesanen and Raulas approached it from a process perspective and described personalized
advertising as a dynamic marketi ng process centered on user dat a collection, analysis, and
interaction[11].
W i t h  t h e  d e v e l o p m e n t  o f  A I ,  t r aditional personalized advertisin g has evolved in to intelligent
advertising personalization. Compared with traditional rule-bas ed targeted advertising, intelligent
advertising personalization relies more on continuous learning and dynamic prediction through
algorithmic models. Its personalized effect is not a fixed outcome but a process that constantly adjusts
as user data is updated[7]. Research also shows that personaliz ed recommendation uses big data
analysis and AI technologies to provide customized advertising content based on consumers’ past
behavior, interests, and needs[2] . From this perspective, intel ligent advertising personalization has
three main characteristics: it is data-driven, mediated by algo rithms, and dynamically optimized.
Overall, it represents a deep a lignment between technology and user needs, and it extends
personalization into the intelligent era.
Furthermore, personalization in intelligent advertising can be understood at two levels: objective
personalization and subjective perception. Scholars introduced the concept of perceived
personalization to distinguish bet ween actual personalization a nd the level of personalization users
feel. Aksoy et al. define perceived personalization as a multidimensional psychological construct, and
they argue that personalization is not an objective feature of advertising but depends on whether users
feel that the content understands their needs, interests, and c ontext[12]. Li found that personalized
advertising produces positive effects only when users perceive a match between the information and
their own characteristics[13]. If there is a mismatch between s ystem-level personalization and user
perception, trust may decrease and  resistance may arise. This h ighlights the mediating role of
perceived personalization in int elligent advertising practice a nd helps explain advertising
effectiveness.

Existing research on perceived personalization in intelligent a dvertising has explored cognitive
mechanisms, value evaluation, and ethical risks. It has examined why personalization is implemented,
how it is implemented, and what effects it may produce. However, the internal mechanisms that shape
user responses under perceived personalization still need further study, especially a more systematic
understanding of differences in user cognition, emotion, and behavior.
3. Cognitive Evaluation, Emotional Tension, and Behavioral Differentiation:
From Cognition to Behavior
Based on the previous research results, nowadays user perceptio ns of personalization do not
develop in a linear way. Instead, they result from the continuo us interaction of cognitive evaluation,
emotional response, and behavioral decision-making. This paper conceptualizes that process through
a cognition–emotion–behavior framework.
3.1 The Cognitive Level: Applications of Perceived Value, Risk, and Autonomy
In data-dense environments such as social media platforms, pers onalized advertisements are
frequently delivered with high re levance[14]. Upon perceiving s uch ad relevance, users form their
fundamental perceptions of pers onalized intelligent advertising  by engaging in a comprehensive
benefit–cost–controllability assessment, which revolves around three core concerns: whether the use
of personalized advertising is worthwhile, whether potential risks are involved, and whether users can
retain dominant control over the process.
3.1.1 Perceived Value: Usefulness and Efficiency
Perceived value refers to users’ cognitive evaluation of whethe r personalized advertising brings
actual benefits. It’s about whet her advertising improves inform ation acquisition efficiency, reduces
decision-making costs, and in turn effectively matches users’ current needs.
Technology acceptance theory identifies perceived usefulness as  a key prerequisite for the
acceptance of a technology or service[15]. In the context of in telligent advertising personalization,
this judgment means whether advertisements reduce information s earch burdens, provide useful
information at appropriate times and in appropriate situations,  and assist users in completing
consumption or decision-making tasks.
Research also finds that compared  with other factors, perceived  usefulness is the strongest
predictor of users’ willingness to click and buy. Personalized advertising leads to positive responses
only when users believe it is genuinely useful[14]. This sugges ts that the effectiveness of intelligent
advertising personalization does not inherently exist from its technical characteristics. Rather, it
depends on whether users cognitively recognize its practical value, and this evaluation forms the basis
for subsequent attitudes and behaviors.
3.1.2 Perceived Risk: Privacy Concerns and Feelings of Intrusion
Perceived risk refers to users’ cognitive assessment of potential privacy loss and reduced autonomy
resulting from personalized advertising. The key lies in whethe r the use of data crosses acceptable
personal boundaries. From the perspective of privacy calculus, users treat privacy risk as a potential
cost when evaluating personalized  services, and this assessment  becomes an important basis for
deciding whether to accept such  services[16,17]. In this case, perceived risk initi ally appears as a
rational expectation, a concern that personal information may be misused or become uncontrollable.
Intrusiveness theory further argues that privacy concern may ev olve into a subjective experience
of boundary violation[18]. Compared with long-term and potential privacy concerns, intrusion is an
immediate response occurring in specific advertising encounters . It directly affects individuals’
psychological boundaries and sense of control. Hence, privacy c oncern alone does not necessarily
make users avoid personalized ads. It only reduces click and pu rchase intentions when it becomes a
clear feeling of intrusion in a specific situation[14].

This shows that in personalized  intelligent advertising, users feel risk when highly relevant ads
seem to cross their personal in formation boundaries. In these c ases, privacy concern turns into a
feeling of intrusion, which becomes an important barrier to accepting personalized ads.
3.1.3 Perceived Autonomy: Agency and Controllability
Perceived autonomy refers to whether users feel they still have  control during personalized
interactions. Boundary management theory suggests that being able to adjust information boundaries
is the key to preventing personalization from being seen as man ipulation[19]. When users possess
options and control, data collection and use are more likely to be understood as helpful service rather
than a privacy intrusion. In p ersonalized advertising, this mea ns that users’ ability to adjust ad
frequency, recommendation content, or the level of personalization directly shapes how they evaluate
the experience. If users feel they are losing control, personal ization may no longer seem convenient
but instead manipulative. In that case, algorithmic accuracy deprives personal decision-making.
From a broader social perspective, autonomy in intelligent adve rtising can also be understood as
an issue of “attention governance. ” Intelligent advertising may  capture users’ attention through
technological advantages, blur the line between public and private space, and allow consumer culture
to dominate public attention, creating a form of collective unconsciousness[7]. Therefore, perceived
autonomy reflects users’ judgmen t about who holds control and d ecision-making power in
personalized advertising. Personalization can be accepted over time only when users feel they control
their information boundaries and attention.
Overall, cognitive evaluation of perceived personalization has three parts: perceived value,
perceived risk, and perceived autonomy. These correspond to users’ judgments about benefits, costs,
and control. This cognitive structure lays the foundation for later emotional reactions and behavioral
decisions.
3.2 Emotion: The Tension Between Positive and Negative Feelings in Perceived
Personalization
At the emotional level, perceived personalization creates mixed  feelings. Users may feel both
dislike and dependence, trust a nd vigilance. These conflicting emotions come from personal
experience, privacy concerns, and value judgments, and they strongly influence whether users accept
and interact with intelligent advertising.
3.2.1 Negative Emotions: Aversion and Vigilance
A sense of being monitored due to privacy intrusion is the main  source of aversion. When
intelligent advertising uses personal profiles, private posts, or multiple data sources for targeting,
users may feel that their lives are being observed. Liu shows that excessive personalization increases
privacy concerns and directly reduces trust in advertising. Hidden data collection can also make users
feel vulnerable, which further s trengthens aversion[2]. In addi tion, the loss of autonomy caused by
algorithmic manipulation is another important source of aversion[20].
Vigilance is a more subtle and long-term defensive emotion. It means that users remain
psychologically alert and emotionally cautious when facing intelligent advertising. Vigilance comes
from expected privacy risks and concerns about algorithm bias. Users understand that personalization
depends on data collection. They worry that their browsing hist ory may be used for excessive
marketing, that their preferences  may be fixed by algorithms, o r that sensitive information may be
misused.
Concerns about bias and unfairness further increase vigilance. For example, when users notice
gender or group stereotypes in advertisements, their defensive attitudes become stronger[20].
Similarly, when users believe that advertising is designed to push them to consume, they may activate
emotional defense mechanisms[21].
Overall, negative emotions towar d perceived personalization inc lude immediate aversion and
ongoing vigilance. Aversion comes from direct experiences of pr ivacy intrusion, disturbance, or
manipulation. Vigilance develops over time through continued interaction. Together, these emotions

3.2.2 Positive Emotions: Dependence and Trust
In contrast to negative emotions, positive emotions include dep endence and trust, which develop
through long-term exposure to personalized advertising.
Dependence reflects a stable positive emotional state formed th rough improved efficiency,
satisfaction of needs, and perceived value. Personalized advertisements that closely match user needs
are more likely to gain attention and positive attitudes[22]. W hen users feel that advertising content
is tailored to them, their sense of dependence increases. De Ke yzer finds that highly personalized
advertising can create emotional resonance, which leads users to use intelligent advertising as a tool
to understand themselves[23].
Moreover, trust is an emotional recognition built on consistent  value delivery. Its stability relies
on whether personalization is predictable and consistent. When intelligent advertising continuously
provides high-quality and relevant information over time, users’ trust increases[2]. Transparency and
privacy control can also help mai ntain trust. When platforms cl early explain how data is used and
provide personalization settings, trust is easier to establish[ 24]. In addition, brand reputation and
social endorsement strengthen trust and increase users’ willing ness to accept personalized
advertising[23,25].
The emotional conflict users possess toward intelligent advertising is thereby dynamic. It reflects
a deeper tension between technological value and privacy ethics , and between benefits and risks.
When advertising provides sustain ed value, ensures privacy cont rol, and maintains transparency,
dependence and trust become stronger. On the contrary, when these conditions are not met, aversion
and vigilance expand.
3.3 Behavioral Responses: Strategic Responses to Perceived Personalization
Cognitive evaluations and emotional tensions ultimately shape d istinct behavioral responses.
These responses can be categorized as positive, negative, or ne utral, and they are closely influenced
by the degree of personalization, how it is presented, and the context in which it appears.
3.3.1 Positive Responses: Clicking, Purchasing, and Continued Interaction
Positive responses include clicking on advertisements, purchasi ng recommended products,
voluntarily authorizing non-sensi tive personal data, continuing  to use personalized services, and
recommending them to others.
Research shows that a higher level of personalization is an imp ortant condition for positive
responses. Personalization increas es perceived relevance, which  improves click intention and
strengthens positive brand attitudes. Kang and Guo point out th at the stronger users perceive “need
responsiveness,” the higher their acceptance and purchase intention, which makes  them more likely
to interact deeply through clicking and purchasing[26].
Moreover, whether positive responses continue over time depends  on user experience. Zhang
argues that intelligent advertisi ng enhances users’ immersion b y matching personalized services to
users during media use[8]. This immersive experience increases enjoyment and promotes continued
interaction. Therefore, clicking and purchasing are not one-tim e results, but ongoing behaviors
supported by immersion and emotional engagement.
3.3.2 Negative Resistance: Advertising Avoidance and Defensive Behavior
Negative resistance refers to active avoidance and defensive be haviors taken after perceiving
personalized advertising. Users may reduce exposure, weaken influence, or cut off interaction. These
behaviors include skipping advertisements, using ad blockers, providing false or incomplete personal
information, turning off persona lization functio ns, uninstallin g applications, filing complaints, or
even boycotting brands.

Advertising avoidance is the bas ic form of negative resistance.  It refers to behaviors that reduce
exposure to advertising after contact[27]. This behavior often comes from sensitivity to privacy risk
and serves as a low-cost and imm ediate risk-avoidance strategy. Yang and Wen found that privacy
concern activates users’ intentio n to protect privacy, which le ads them to block ads, turn off
personalization, or provide incomplete information[28].
When users do not perceive clear value from personalized advertising, resistance becomes stronger.
Research on algorithm fatigue show s that when users feel that i nformation sources become narrow
and create “intellectual isolat ion,” personalization no longer motivates clicks but instead increases
deeper resistance. Users may r ebuild personal boundaries throug h technical tools or psychological
withdrawal[29].
However, many users choose a compromise strategy—privacy conces sion. Instead of simply
accepting or rejecting personalization, users weigh the practic al value against potential risks. With
some perceived control, they may give up part of their privacy in exchange for functional or efficiency
benefits[2].
This shows that behavioral responses to perceived personalization are fluid, which means that they
are contextual decisions based on cognitive evaluation and emot ional experience. Personalization
does not automatically lead to ac ceptance or rejection. Instead , users continuously adjust their
strategies between efficiency gains and privacy risk.
4. New Issues and New Value: Research Directions of Perceived
Personalization in Intelligent Advertising
4.1 A New Issues: The Practice Path of Intelligent Advertising from the Perspective of User
Agency
As algorithmic logic deeply resha pe media logic, media systems have shifted from a traditional
technology-centered orientation to a relationship-centered orientation[30]. Similarly, the practice of
intelligent advertising is moving  from a technology-centered mo del to one that emphasizes user
agency. Algorithms are not only tools for delivering advertisem ents, but also structures that form
users as reflective and active s ubjects. Therefore, examining u ser agency has become an important
new research field in intelligent advertising.
First, algorithm literacy is a key user capability that affects  how personalization is perceived. It
includes knowledge of how algorithms work, the ability to criti cally evaluate advertising outcomes,
and confidence in adjusting or influencing algorithmic systems.  Research shows that algorithm
literacy has a significant posit ive effect on users’ adoption o f personalized advertising[31]. Users
with higher algorithm literacy a re better able to understand th e logic behind adve rtising algorithms
and are more likely to recognize the value of personalization[32].
Second, algorithm literacy directly  shapes users’ acceptance of  personalization through
psychological evaluation. In intelligent advertising, users con stantly weigh risks and benefits. Zeng
et al. point out that perceived privacy risk and perceived privacy benefit interact with each other[33].
Users with higher algorithm literacy are able to be more rationally evaluating the convenience brought
by advertising and the potential privacy cost. As a result, they are more likely to accept personalized
services while maintaining a sense of control. In contrast, when personalization becomes too intrusive
and exceeds users’ cognitive boundaries, those with lower liter acy tend to experience algorithm
fatigue and cognitive dissonance. This may lead to resistance behaviors such as withdrawal, disabling
functions, or providing false f eedback[34]. If technological de velopment ignores users’ perceptual
abilities, it may result in psychological resistance and technological alienation.
Therefore, intelligent advertising should move beyond on algori thmic performance and instead
prioritize empowering users. Platforms should improve algorithm  literacy through educational and
knowledge-based interventions to anxiety caused by algorithmic monitoring. Giving users more
rights and control can transform  them from passive data provide rs into active collaborators in

4.2 New Value: Restoring Human Agency in Intelligent Advertising Personalization
Intelligent advertising personalization has improved how effici ently information is delivered and
how effectively sales are generated. At the same time, attentio n is shifting from making it more
precise to considering how it should be used responsibly. This shift encourages a more human-
centered and sustainable approach to intelligent advertising.
Personalization should move from simple “information matching” to “value matching.” Yu argues
that current algorithmic rationality mainly achieves information matching in specific contexts, but it
does not fully incorporate value judgment or social needs. This  limitation may weaken subjectivity
and create tension between technological power and humanistic v alues[35]. The new value of
perceived personalization lies in shifting intelligent advertising from a tool for commercial persuasion
to a mechanism that supports human agency. True personalization does not just understand consumer
preferences, but it respects user subjectivity, considers situa tional and psychological needs, and
supports individual decision-making rather than replacing it.
A human-centered approach to intelligent advertising also means  that its technological
development should clearly aim to ward ethical and socially resp onsible goals. Advertising systems
should pursue precision while mai ntaining humanistic concern. B y combining human wisdom with
artificial intelligence, intelligent advertising can evolve fro m a marketing technology into a media
form with social integration functions. Ultimately, this promot es harmonious collaboration between
humans and machines.
5. Conclusion
This study examines perceived per sonalization in intelligent ad vertising through a cognition–
emotion–behavior framework. At the cognitive level, perceived value, perceived risk, and perceived
autonomy form the basis of user judgment. At the emotional level, users experience tension between
aversion and dependence, vigilance and trust. These cognitive a nd emotional processes lead to
different behavioral outcomes: positive interaction, negative resistance, or neutral privacy concession.
Together, they reveal the internal logic of interaction between technology and users.
More importantly, the value of s tudying perceived personalizati on goes beyond explaining
differences in advertis ing effectiveness. It engages a central issue of intelligent communication:
technology does not operate in one direction alone, and users are not merely passive subjects shaped
by algorithms. Perceived persona lization provides an important perspective for understanding the
dynamic relationship between algorithmic media and human agency. The long-term development of
intelligent advertising depends not only on technological effic iency, but also on car eful attention to
ethical principles and user subjectivity. Only by finding a balance between efficiency and human care
can intelligent advertising pers onalization support healthy hum an–machine collaboration and bring
lasting human value to advertising in this digital age.
