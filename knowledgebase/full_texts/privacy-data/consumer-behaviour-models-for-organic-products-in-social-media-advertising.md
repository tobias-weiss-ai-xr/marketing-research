1. Introduction

Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs) are key pillars of economic growth in developed as well as developing
economies. MSMEs contribute to increasing national GDP, foster innovation, provide employment opportunities, and enhance
economic diversification. According to the International Labour Organization (ILO) estimates, MSMEs offer almost 70% of total
employment worldwide and contribute to some 60% of global GDP (Samaniego -Arias et al., 2025). In India alone, MSMEs
contribute to generating almost 30% of national GDP, a fact that proves the role played by them in encouraging local production
and redressing regional imbalances (Sharma et al., 2022). MSMEs are commonly classified on the basis of size, turnover
annually, and investment in p lant and machinery, although this varies marginally in different countries and institutions.
According to classification by the World Bank and the OECD, micro enterprises have fewer than 10 employees, small enterprises
fewer than 50 employees, and medium e nterprises fewer than 250 employees. Though the size and capital are small in these
companies, they are comparatively innovative and flexible, i.e., they have the capacity to react to changing market needs and
enhance technological progress (Chaudhary et al., 2021). Governments across the globe have understood the capability of such
companies and have given different schemes of financial assistance to develop and enhance the companies ( You et al., 2020).
Despite the growing number of government support financial schemes, the majority of recent studies have been focusing on a
single instrument or national perspective examination. In -depth, comparative studies covering multiple support financial
instruments in developed and emerging economies remai n in short supply. Moreover, few studies have examined the true

Abstract This review examines the intersection of consumer behaviour theory and social media advertising in the context
of organic product marketing. Drawing on established behavioural models including the Theory of Planned Behaviour,
Health Belief Model, and Value -Belief-Norm Theory, the paper explores how psychological and sociocultural factors
influence consumer attitudes, intentions, and decision-making processes. Key mechanisms of social media advertising, such
as influencer marketing, user-generated content, native advertising, and interactive formats, are analysed for their role in
shaping perception and engagement. The review also highlights demographic, psychographic, and behavioural

online environments. Practical implications are provided for marketers and policymakers seeking to design evidence-based
campaigns that align with consumer values of health, sustainability, and transparency. The paper also addresses ethical
concerns related to misinformation, privacy, and market access, offering recommendations for responsible
communication. Finally, gaps in the literature are identified, including the lack of integrated theoretical frameworks for
digital environments, the need for cross -cultural studies, longitudinal analysis, and exploration of emerging tools such as
VR and AI influencers. The paper concludes by emphasizing the importance of interdisciplinary approaches to better
understand and ethically manage the influence of social media on consumer adoption of organic products.

2. Background (Overview of Consumer Behaviour Theories Applicable to Organic Products)

This necessitates critical scrutiny of theoretical models for the explanation of decision -making, motivation, attitude
formation, and behavioural intention in an attempt to explain why consumers choose organic products (Azad et al., 2023).
Theories outlined below are frequently quoted when outlining health -conscious and environmentally conscious consumer
conduct and can be used to explain organic product consumption (Ahmad et al., 2023).

2.1. Theory of planned bahavior (TPB)

• The most widely used model of consumer behaviour is the Theory of Planned Behaviour (Ajzen, 1991). TPB holds that
the optimal predictor of the behaviour will be the intention to do it. Three intention determinants are:
• Attitude towards the behaviour: how much the person is positive or negative about purchasing organic goods.
• Subjective norms: others' pressure to purchase or not to purchase or ganic products.
• Perceived behavioural control: the individual’s perception of their ability to perform the behaviour, such as
affordability or product availability.

TPB has been frequently used to explain organic food consumption, especially when the choice involves deliberate
evaluation rather than impulse.

2.2. Health belief model (HBM)

The Health Belief Model (Rosenstock, 1974) was developed to explain health-related behaviours. It has also been applied
to food choices, particularly those associated with health outcomes. The core components are:

• Perceived susceptibility: belief about the risk of experiencing a health issue.
• Perceived severity: belief about the seriousness of the health issue.
• Perceived benefits: belief in the effectiveness of choosing organic products to avoid health risks.
• Perceived barriers: cost, inconvenience, or taste concerns.
• Cues to action: triggers that stimulate behaviour, such as social media campaigns.
• Self-efficacy: confidence in one’s ability to choose and maintain consumption of organic food.

2.3. Value-belief-norm (VBN) theory

Developed by Stern et  al. (1999), the VBN theory links values, beliefs, and personal norms to pro -environmental
behaviour. It suggests that individuals who hold biospheric or altruistic values are more likely to adopt behaviours like
purchasing organic products.
The model flows through:

• Values → Beliefs (awareness of consequences, ascription of responsibility) → Norms (personal obligation to act) →
Behaviour

This model is effective in explaining the behaviour of consumers motivated by environmental sustainability and ethical
concerns rather than just health or taste.

2.4. Theory of reasoned action (TRA)

The Theory of Reasoned Action (Fishbein  & Ajzen, 1975) is the precursor to TPB and shares a similar structure but
without the component of perceived behavioural control. In TRA:

• Attitude toward the behaviour and subjective norms form the intention to act.

Although TRA has more limited use when compared to TPB in modern contexts, it remains applicable when the
behaviour is entirely under volitional control. For organic food, TRA helps to explain choices based on values and social
influence, particularly among consumers who already have easy access to such products ( Johnstone & Lindh, 2022).

2.5. Stimulus-organism-response (S-O-R) framework

Originally rooted in environmental psychology (Mehrabian & Russell, 1974), the S-O-R framework is increasingly used in
consumer research. It explains behaviour as:

• Stimulus (S): external factors such as advertisements, product labels, or social media content.
• Organism (O): internal processes including emotional and cognitive responses.
• Response (R): resulting behaviours, such as purchasing or recommending organic products.

S-O-R is especially useful in analysing online environments. In social media advertising, for instance, visual appeal,
message framing, and influencer endorsements are stimuli that affect the consumer's internal state and, eventually, behaviour.

2.6. Consumer decision-making process (CDMP)

The Consumer Decision-Making Process model outlines the sequential stages a consumer goes through when making a
purchase:

1. Problem recognition: identifying the need (e.g., health, environmental concern)
2. Information search: seeking product and brand details
3. Evaluation of alternatives: comparing organic and non-organic options
4. Purchase decision
5. Post-purchase behaviour: satisfaction, repeat buying, or switching

CDMP offers a structured approach to understand how consumers move from awareness to action (Liao, 2024). It is
especially relevant when the decision to buy organic involves planned behaviour rather than impulse, which is often the case
in food and grocery categories. Table 2 below shows the Comparison of Consumer Behaviour Th eories.
Each of these theories contributes to the account of why consumers purchase organic goods in different ways. Some of
the model’s stress health motivation, some eco -values, some external influence through the new media. In social media
marketing, integrated or hybrid approaches may be required to best describe the intricacy of organic product consumption
(Ahmad et al., 2023).

3.1. Influencer advertising and user-generated contente (UGC)

Influencer marketing involves collaborations between firms and influencers with a large social media following such as
Instagram, YouTube, or TikTok  (Li & Shan, 2025). The influencers, in the majority of instances, are perceived to be genuine or
trustworthy and promote products on their respective websites. In the case of organic products, the influencers primarily
introduce themselves as champions of healthy living, wellness, or sustainable living (Amir et al., 2023).
User-generated content (UGC) is, nevertheless, unpaid content created by regular users, such as product reviews,
images, or testimonials (Khan et al., 2023). UGC creates social proof, which deters perceived risk and fosters trust in a product.
Both influencer content and UGC succeed because they mimic word -of-mouth communication, which is a potent behaviour
driver of organic product use (Pentina et al., 2018).

Native advertising is paid advertising that is identical in form and function to the platform it's being shown on. On social
media, this will be sponsored posts which are blended in with the user's stream. They are designed to look like normal content,
so are less in-your-face and more likely to be noticed.
For organic fruits and vegetables, native ads are likely to focus on simple food shots, behind -the-scenes farm work, or
short instances of product use. Their subtle nature c an also provoke more engagement and message recall, especially when it
aligns with users' values or lifestyle.

3.3. Emotional appeals and brand storytelling

Brand narrative employs narrative strategies to convey a firm's history, mission, or values. Such  stories, in the case of
the organic industry, typically involve tales of ethical sourcing, local production, environmental stewardship, or founder-driven
missions (Arora et al., 2025).
Emotional appeals are frequently embedded in these narratives to create stronger emotional connections with the
audience. These may involve fear of health risks from conventional food, hope for a healthier future, or guilt over unsustainable
practices. Emotional messages have been shown to increase message  effectiveness and product recall in organic food
advertising (Lee & Hong, 2016).

3.4. Interactive ads (polls, reels, AR filters)

Interactive advertising uses platform tools that allow users to engage directly with content. This includes:

• Polls and quizzes (e.g., on Instagram Stories) to gather user opinions or reinforce brand messages.
• Reels and short videos that highlight product use in real time.
• Augmented reality (AR) filters that let users “try” a product visually or interact with branded el ements.

Interactive formats increase the time users spend engaging with content, which may strengthen message retention and
improve brand perception (Arora et al., 2024).

3.6. Psychological and socio-cultural factors affecting organic product adoption

Consumer purchase of organic food is driven by both sociocultural environments and psychological attitudes. They go
beyond functional attributes like storage life or taste. They encompass other people's social norms, beliefs, values, and
perceptions, as well as self. The following section explains how health, environment, trust, social influence, and price influence
the purchase of organic food (Kumar et al., 2023).

3.7. Health and environmental consciousness

Health issues can be one of the most frequent reasons for going for organic products. Those who think that pesticides,
hormones, or synthetic additives are harmful to health would like to purchase organic ones (Al‐Swidi et al., 2014). They tend to
be individuals with certain dietary needs, parents shopping for children, or people with a history of health problems (Yadav &
Pathak, 2016).
Environmental concern is another good predictor. Consumers who are concerned about global warming, soil erosion, or
pollution will regard organic farming as a cleaner choice. Organic farming is perceived to be environmentally friendly becaus e
it uses fewer chemicals, focuses on biodiversity, and depends little on fossil fuel-derived inputs (Wier & Calverley, 2002).
Both of these forms of consciousness —health and environmental —tend to go hand -in-hand, and highest -scoring
customers in one will most assuredly have the other highly rated.

Consumers cannot verify directly if a product is organic or not. So, trust comes into play. Certification labels like USDA
Organic, EU Organic, or India Organic are signals of trust. Perceived credibility of the certifying bodies decides if consumers will
trust the claims that the brands are making. (Ahmad et al., 2024).
If consumers doubt the integrity of the certification process, they are less likely to pay a premium for organic goods.
Research shows that institutional tru st—in regulatory agencies and third -party certifiers—is a stronger predictor of organic

3.9. Social proof and peer influence

Social behaviour plays a significant  role in organic consumption. When people see friends, family members, or
influencers choosing organic products, they are more likely to follow suit. This is known as social proof—the idea that behaviour
is shaped by observing others, especially in uncertain or high-involvement decisions.
Peer influence is especially strong in younger demographics who spend more time on social media. Positive reviews,
shared experiences, or “haul videos” create a sense of collective approval that validates the choice to buy organic. Even offline,
being part of a social group, that values sustainability can lead individuals to make similar choices to maintain social cohe sion
or approval (Thøgersen, 2006).

3.10. Ethical consumption and identity signalling

For many consumers, buying organic is not only a functional decision —it is a moral and identity -driven choice. Ethical
consumption involves choosing products that align with personal values related to fairness, animal welfare, sustainability, a nd
global equity (Li & Shan, 2025).
This behaviour also supports identity signalling—the use of products to communicate one’s beliefs or lifestyle to others.
For instance, someone who buys organic food may do so in part to reflect their commitment to environmentalism, minimalism,
or health-conscious living. Organic products, in this context, function as symbolic goods that convey ethical or social identity
(Bartels & Reinders, 2016).

3.11. Perceived quality and price sensitivity

Organic products are often perceived as being of higher quality in terms of freshness, taste, and safety. This perception
is shaped by both branding and consumer experience. However, price remains one of the most important barriers to
widespread adoption ( Temesi et al., 2022 ). Organic goods ofte n cost significantly more than conventional ones due to higher
production costs and smaller-scale distribution.
Price sensitivity varies across consumer segments. For high-income or strongly motivated consumers, the price premium
is justified by perceived benefits. For some, price is a limitation despite the favourable attitudes towards organics (Hughner et
al., 2007). For others, consumers will engage in selective purchasing—buying only a limited number of organic items which they
feel are most essential (for example, baby foods or milk).

4. Social Media Role in Shaping Attitudes and Purchase Intentions

Social media is a strong driver of consumer beliefs and buying intention, especially for value-based products like organic
foods. Social media is unique compared to traditional media because it allows two -way communication, immediate feedback,
and peer involvement, all of which shape the way consumers form perceptions and buy. This chapter describes the specific way
social media affects consumer attitude toward organics.

4.1. Social media as a tool for persuasion and information

Social media is a mixed platform on which consumers are consuming both peer-generated and commercial information.
For natural products, Facebook, Instagram, and YouTube are channels of persuasion that deliver consumers information on the
benefits of products, methods of production, certification, and usage.
The advertising strength of social media lies in its interactivity, customized content delivery, and capacity for embedding
advertising in regular content. These features make promotional content less intrusive and more interactive. Moreover, visual
content such as short videos, stories, and infographics enables buyers to learn about comple x ideas such as organic farm
methods, environmental impacts, or food labels more conveniently.
Organic food businesses are using these places not just to inform, but also to situate their products within consumer
values of health, sustainability, and transparency, which are the most significant attitude and buying behaviours drivers (Appel
et al., 2020).

4.2. Electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM) review credibility

Electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM) is spontaneous product -related communication disseminated online in the form of
reviews, testimonials, postings, or recommendations. In organic products, where consumers' trust is the main issue,

• Backstage farm or processing facility video

• Before-and-after stories demonstrating health effects or home changes

4.4. Consumer interaction as behavioural indicators

Consumer engagement metrics —shares, saves, comments, and likes —are behaviour indicators of user interest and
brand engagement. While not direct measures of purchase, these will likely be indicative of purchase intent and attitude
strength.
For example: Likes may indicate low-effort congruence with the brand message.
Shares indicate greater engagement and intent to share the content with others.
Comments will be more thoughtful engagement, questions, or endorsement.
They are utilized by researchers and marketers to gauge which content works best and to predict buying behaviour
based on patterns of web activity (Voorveld et al., 2018).

5.1. Using behavioural data to tailor content

Social media platforms collect vast amounts of behavioural data, such as: Content viewed or liked, Frequency of visits,
Engagement with similar brands, Previous purchase or search history (Ahn & Shamim, 2022).
This data allows for personalization and retargeting, which increases relevance and effectiveness of advertising. For
example: A consumer who frequently interacts with wellness page s may receive ads for organic supplements. Someone who
abandoned a cart with organic groceries might be shown a retargeted ad offering a discount or reminder.
Behavioural segmentation enables:

• Dynamic content generation: Changing visuals or messaging based on consumer traits or behaviour.
• Adaptive scheduling: Showing ads at times when users are most likely to engage.
• Tailored incentives: Offering promotions that match a consumer’s past interests or needs.

These methods support the "perceived behavioural control" element of TPB by reducing barriers and improving
convenience.

5.2. AI and machine learning in predicting consumer responses

AI and machine learning models enhance campaign design by analyzing patterns across large datasets to: Predict
likelihood of engagement or purchase, Identify optimal content formats  (e.g., video vs. image), Segment audiences based on
behavioural and psychographic traits
In the context of organic product marketing, AI tools can:

• Detect high-propensity buyers using lookalike modeling based on previous organic shoppers
• Optimize ad placement by predicting user receptiveness to specific messaging (e.g., health vs. environmental)
• Conduct A/B testing automatically, adjusting campaigns in real time based on performance metrics

Machine learning also supports sentiment analysis, which c an assess how audiences react emotionally to campaign
elements. This is valuable when messages rely on trust, ethics, or health -related concerns.

Several empirical studies have already taken into account the effect of social media campaigns on the consumer behavior

Pentina et al. (2018) examined an international study of the impact that  social media has on attitudes towards organic
food. It was determined that eWOM, influencer credibility, and product expertise positively influenced purchase intentions
when consumers felt that the content was real and represented their personal values.
Marques et al. (2020) further found that organic product campaigns with narrative content and word -of-mouth
recommendations fared better in terms of achieving higher behavioural conversion rates than promotional language -based
campaigns alone. Their study r eaffirmed that ethical or environmental message -based campaigns boosted brand recall and
customer satisfaction too.
Lipsman et al. (2012) experimented with ROI for CPG brands on social media. They found that Facebook ad exposure
drove measurable  offline sales increases, particularly when campaigns included retargeting alongside high -frequency
messaging. While not explicitly focused on natural products, this supports the strategic digital investment argument.

7. Challenges and Ethical Considerations

Although the social media provides powerful tools for the promotion of organic products, it also poses some ethical and
pragmatic issues. These issues can undermine consumer trust, manipulate market forces, and create inequalities in access
(Nabivi, 2025). Four main issues are discussed in this section: disinformation and greenwashing, privacy in behaviour targeting,
equity of market access, and risk of over-commercialization of sustainability.

7.1. Greenwashing and misinformation risks

Perhaps the most pressing ethical concern in the organic products market is greenwashing the process of making a false
impression or misrepresentation about the environmental benefits of a product or business ( Thanki et al., 2022 ). On social
media, where information i s most likely to be visual, concise, and emotive, disinformation is most probable. Brands will use
vague or unsubstantiated claims (e.g., "all -natural," "eco-friendly") without official endorsement. Promote small sustainable
efforts while ignoring bigger d etrimental effects. Collaborate with influencers with no information or spread misinformation
(Ahmad et al., 2023).
This can erode consumer trust and dilute the meaning of legitimate labels like “USDA Organic” or “EU Organic.”
Regulatory oversight of digit al marketing is still evolving, and platforms often lack consistent enforcement mechanisms for
environmental or health claims (Delmas & Burbano, 2011).

7.2. Privacy concerns in behaviour tracking

Social media advertising heavily relies on user data to personalize and optimize content. This includes: Browsing history,
Engagement metrics (likes, shares, clicks), Location data, Purchase behaviour (Kufaine, 2024).
While data -driven strategies improve targeting efficiency, they raise serious privacy  concerns. Many consumers are
unaware of how their data is collected and used. In some cases, they may be subjected to persuasive tactics they did not
explicitly consent to.
There is also a broader concern about algorithmic manipulation, where platforms shape consumer exposure to content
based on predicted behaviour rather than informed choice. This undermines autonomy and may lead to overexposure to
specific messages, reducing the consumer’s ability to evaluate alternatives objectively (Martin & Murphy, 20 17).

7.3. Equity and access issues in organic marketing

Organic products are often marketed to consumers with higher income, greater access to health information, and more
digital literacy. This creates a segmentation bias that excludes low -income and marginalized groups from both the marketing
discourse and the market itself. Issues are:
Price barriers: Organic foods are pricier, and discounts are aimed at urban or affluent regions.
Digital divide: Internet campaigns may be out of reach to those who have no access to the internet or low social media
penetration.
Incompatibility of culture: Market data may not be capable of reflecting the multicultural society's food culture, values,
or language.
This reinforces social food access in equalities and has the potential to create sustainability narratives that are seen to
be elitist or exclusionary (Guthman, 2003). Moral marketing must consider how to address wider groups without reducing their
needs to oversimplified form or dragging them down to market segments.

7.4. Over-commercialization of sustainability

While social media has placed sustainability into mainstream parlance, increasingly there is concern that it is being
commodified against its very aim. Organic and sustainable enterprises increasingly resort to beauty -led campaigns that
emphasize life style imagery over structural change.
This has several consequences:

• Consumers will be able to link sustainability to self-image or fashion trend membership, not ethical membership.
• Brands may divert their focus from real environmental impacts to shallow branding projects.
• Long-term trust in organic labeling and environmental initiatives may decline as consumers become skeptical of brand
motives.

Over-commercialization risks turning sustainability into a commodity, weakening its potential to drive structural
improvements in agriculture and food systems (Carfagna et al., 2014).

8. Gaps in the Literature and Future Research Directions

Although the existing literature offers valuable insights into how social media influences consumer behaviour toward
organic products, several important gaps remain. These gaps limit both theoretical advancement and the practical effectiveness
of marketing strategies. This section outlines key areas where further research is needed, focusing on the integration of
behavioural models with digital environments, cultural variation in responses, long-term effects of exposure, and the emerging
influence of new technologies.

8.1. Need for integrated models combining social media in behavioural psychology

Most studies apply existing psychological models—such as the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB), Health Belief Model
(HBM), or Value -Belief-Norm (VBN) theory —without adapting them to the unique features of digital and interactive media.
These models were originally developed for offline behaviour and do not fully account for: Real -time interaction, Algorithm -
driven content exposure, Peer influence through eWOM and influencers, Visual storytelling and emotional engagement.
There is a need for integrated theoretical models that combine elements from behavioural psychology with media and
communication studies. Future research should explore how constructs like perceived behavioural control or subjective norms
are shaped or distorted by algorithmic curation, influencer credibility, and social metrics (e.g., likes, shares).
Such models should also incorporate variables related to media literacy, engagement behaviour, and trust  in digital
environments.

8.2. Cultural differences in consumer response

Much of the current literature is concentrated in Western and high -income markets, where consumer access to both
organic products and digital platforms is high. However, organic consumption is growing in diverse regions including Asia, Latin
America, and parts of Africa, where cultural norms, trust dynamics, and communication styles differ significantly.Key areas f or
investigation include: Cultural perceptions of health and natural food,  Trust in certification systems across different regulatory
environments, Social influence structures, such as family vs. peer -led norms, Attitude toward digital advertising, which varies
by region and age group.Cross -cultural studies are needed to test whether social media advertising strategies effective in one
region transfer successfully to others. More research is also needed in multilingual and multicultural advertising, particularly in
regions where organic labels are poorly understood or mistrusted.

8.3. Longitudinal impact of social media exposure

Most empirical studies in this field use cross-sectional designs, measuring attitudes or intentions at a single point in time.
However, behaviour related to organic consumption is often habitual, evolving through repeated exposure, trust-building, and
personal experience. Therefore, single-time-point studies may overestimate short-term intentions and fail to capture long-term
behaviour change. Future research should include:

• Longitudinal studies tracking changes in consumer perception, purchase behaviour, and brand loyalty over time
• Assessment of habituation effects, where overexposure may lead to decreased responsiveness
• Evaluation of how consistent messaging and engagement across platforms affect sustained organic consumption

This is especially important for understanding whether social media campaigns lead to repeat purchases, brand
switching, or lifestyle shifts related to food and sustainability.

8.4. Role of new technologies (e.g., VR, AI influencers)

Emerging technologies are increasingly being incorporated into social media advertising, but their effects on consumer
behaviour, particularly in the organic sector, remain underexplored.
Examples include:

• Virtual reality (VR): Allows immersive storytelling (e.g., virtual farm tours, production process walkthroughs). Potential
to increase transparency and trust, but empirical studies are limited.
• Augmented reality (AR): Enables consumers to interact with  products virtually (e.g., scanning labels for sourcing
information). Potential to enhance engagement but requires usability studies.
• AI influencers: Digital personas (e.g., Lil Miquela) with large followings now endorse products. Their credibility, impact
on trust, and influence on ethical consumption remain unclear.

These technologies could significantly reshape how organic values are communicated, but the psychological and ethical
implications need rigorous study.

9. Final Considerations

The increasing convergence of organic product marketing and social media communication has introduced new
dimensions to consumer behaviour that require both theoretical refinement and empirical investigation. This review has
examined major consumer behaviour models —including the Theory of Planned Behaviour, Health Belief Model, Value -Belief-
Norm Theory, and others —in relation to the specific features of social media advertising. These frameworks offer valuable
insights into how consumers for m attitudes, develop purchase intentions, and respond to ethical, health -related, and
environmental messaging.
The review further explored the mechanisms through which social media influences consumer behaviour, including
influencer marketing, user -generated content, platform -specific strategies, and interactive formats. These mechanisms not
only facilitate message dissemination but also shape psychological responses, such as perceived credibility, emotional
engagement, and social validation. Psychological and sociocultural factors—such as health consciousness, trust in certification,
