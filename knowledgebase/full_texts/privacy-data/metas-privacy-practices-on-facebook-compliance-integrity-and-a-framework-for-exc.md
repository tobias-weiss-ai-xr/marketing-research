1 Introduction
This paper explores Meta's privacy practices from three perspectives: compliance with
data protection regulations in the United States (US) and the European Union (EU), the
company's integrity in balancing stakeholders’ interests, and the standards these prac -
tices should meet for excellence.

Rights as a key aspect of individual dignity and freedom. The U.S. Supreme Court has
affirmed that privacy protects personal dignity from unwarranted state intrusion [ 133],

Abstract
This paper examines Meta’s privacy practices on its Facebook platform through
the lenses of compliance with data protection laws, the integrity of its stakeholder
relationships, and the pursuit of excellence based on a Neo-Aristotelian virtue
ethics framework. The paper highlights the tension between privacy protection and
business objectives by analyzing Meta’s reliance on user data for its advertising-driven
business model. It critiques the limitations of compliance with legal standards in the
US and the EU, showing that fines and regulations often fail to deter the company’s
invasive data practices. Integrity-based approaches are similarly inadequate, as
they are frequently co-opted for reputational management rather than promoting
genuine ethical behavior. This paper advocates for a virtue ethics approach grounded
in the pursuit of human flourishing and the common good. Facebook can advance
these aims by deepening its commitment to its MacIntyrean practice—namely,
enabling social connections—and realizing its internal good of facilitating “global
meaningful social interactions.” Achieving this requires not only embedding virtues
within the company’s operational culture but also fostering their cultivation
among stakeholders. The analysis concludes that reorienting Facebook toward
ethical excellence could both safeguard user privacy, mitigate associated harms,
and establish a new benchmark for corporate responsibility, thereby contributing
meaningfully to the common good.

To address these challenges, we contend that existing regulations and integrity-based
approaches are insufficient and propose a Neo-Aristotelian, MacIntyrean virtue ethics
framework for the following reasons.
First, virtue ethics is oriented toward eudaimonia—flourishing as the highest good for
individuals, involving the realization of their best selves [ 126], p. 26), and the common
good, defined as conditions that foster individual and community flourishing [ 140, 142],
p. 13). Virtue ethics recognizes privacy as essential for flourishing because it safeguards
dignity [52].
Second, Facebook's mission to foster social connections aligns with Aristotle's [6] view
that social bonds are essential for living well. Cultivating friendships within a community
enhances its stability and contributes to overall flourishing [ 134]. Given Facebook’s vast
reach and influence, Meta holds a significant opportunity to positively impact society.
Third, unlike compliance and integrity approaches—the dominant ethical models in
business—virtue ethics goes beyond rule-based deontology and utilitarianism. Deon -
tology focuses on universal rules, widely reflected in regulations and codes of conduct,
which set essential minimum standards. In contrast, utilitarianism aims to maximize
overall utility, making it particularly effective for resource allocation. However, both
frameworks have proven insufficient in practice, as evidenced by recurring ethical
breaches in corporate practice. Their limitations include an overreliance on rules or
outcomes, often neglecting the moral development of the agents involved and the situ -
ational complexities of ethical decision-making. Integrity programs attempt to balance
deontological and utilitarian principles but often become instrumentalized to pursue
short-term gains or appease external pressures.
Virtue ethics resolves these issues by emphasizing the cultivation of virtuous character.
While acknowledging the importance of rules and consequences, it focuses on the moral
agent [ 159]. Since the moral life is undeniably complex due to shifting circumstances
and competing priorities [ 21], it encourages actions done for the right reasons, in the
right way, and at the right time [6].
This paper examines the limitations of compliance-based and integrity-based
approaches within social media companies and proposes a virtue ethics framework to
address privacy issues. Our work differs from other virtue ethics approaches to busi -
ness, which typically focus on identifying virtues that individuals and companies should
cultivate to face ethical challenges posed by technology [ 21, 119, 126, 141, 159]. Instead,
this analysis is grounded in MacIntyre’s moral framework, which distinguishes between
practices, institutions, and the pursuit of internal and external goods. Applied to Face -
book, this framework allows us to interpret the platform not merely as a technologi -
cal product but as a social practice. Thus, Facebook’s core functionality—its telos, in
Aristotelian terms—is understood as facilitating global meaningful social interactions .
This claim draws on MacIntyre’s notion of a practice as a cooperative human activity

2 Compliance
Compliance involves systematically aligning actions and behavior with laws and regu -
lations [ 95]. To assess Meta's compliance with privacy and data protection laws, we
examine the penalties and settlements related to privacy breaches in the US and EU—
jurisdictions known for their stringent regulations, proactive enforcement, and acces -
sible public records.
Our analysis draws on diverse sources from Meta’s inception through 2023, includ -
ing public reports, academic articles, news coverage, and Meta’s financial statements
and public disclosures. While this paper primarily examines Facebook, isolating specific
fines from it is challenging. Therefore, our compliance analysis encompasses all privacy-
related fines imposed on Meta.
First, let’s examine the legal frameworks in the EU and the US The European Union’s
General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)2 is the world’s most comprehensive privacy
law, and exerts global influence through the so-called “Brussels effect” [ 18]. The GDPR
imposes strict obligations on data controllers, such as Meta, and grants individuals’
rights to access, correct, and erase personal data. Other relevant EU laws include the
Digital Services Act (DSA) 3 and the Digital Markets Act (DMA), 4 which, although not
focused on privacy, impact data practices. The DSA mandates transparent data handling
and the swift removal of illegal content, while the DMA prohibits combining personal
data without consent and promotes interoperability between platforms.
The GDPR allows fines up to €20 million or 4% of global revenue, whichever is higher.
The US does not impose such limits but, nonetheless, also lacks a comprehensive
data protection regulation. Instead, it relies on sector-specific regulations such as the

2.1 Meta’s privacy infringements
Meta's history with privacy and data protection violations reveals a notable pattern.
From its inception in 2004 until 2011, the company remarkably faced no penalties. This
stands in stark contrast to its current status as the most fined company for data protec -
tion infringements. Facebook's initial success can be attributed to its strategic emphasis
on strong privacy policies [ 37], an approach that distinguished it from competitors like
MySpace, which had a poor reputation for safeguarding privacy [41].
However, as Facebook solidified its market dominance and approached a quasi-
monopolistic status, its stance on user privacy shifted. The company gradually incor -
porated more invasive user behavior-tracking methods [ 147]. Some of these were the
Beacon program which posted users purchases without consent [72]; the Facebook Pixel,
a tracking code for behavior profiling [ 1], and attempts to manipulate user preferences
through deceptive design [ 73]. As a result, the company has been under increasingly
intense scrutiny regarding its commitment to privacy since 2011. Regulatory frame -
works have since evolved as well,the GDPR partly emerged as a response to Facebook’s
and other tech companies’ privacy neglect [153].

 Until 2023, Meta paid $8.8 billion for privacy violations. Additionally, Meta was sub -
ject to non-economic settlements, such as establishing oversight entities, implementing

to have allocated an additional five billion dollars to enhance its privacy practices [ 121

In Meta’s defense, it may be argued that rapid technological advancements often out -
pace legislative progress; some countries cannot but depend on companies' self-reg -
ulation efforts [ 50]. However, Meta has not only violated the law but its own internal
policies. For instance, although children under 13 are not allowed to open an account,
a 2011 survey found 7.5 million underage users on Facebook [ 32], violating COPPA and
the company’s Terms of Service. In 2023, a legal complaint alleged that “Meta does not
obtain—or even attempt to obtain—verifiable parental consent before collecting the per-
sonal information of children on Instagram and Facebook” [ 149]. The company claims
to try to detect and remove illegal accounts, but since this issue has persisted for over a
decade, this claim is questionable.
While some argue that given its size, Meta cannot always comply with regulations, no
matter how hard it tries [ 80], the company instead exploits loopholes in various regu -
latory frameworks. In Latin America, where data protection laws are weak, users can -
not prevent Meta from using their content to train AI algorithms, unlike in areas with
stricter regulations, such as the EU or the US [ 118]. Moreover, infringements in the EU
and US are systematic. Andrea Jelinek, Chair of the European Data Protection Board,
remarked, “Meta’s infringement is very serious since it concerns (data) transfers that are
systematic, repetitive, and continuous” [ 47]. Similarly, Samuel Levine, Director of the
FTC's Bureau of Consumer Protection, emphasized, “Facebook has repeatedly violated
its privacy promises” [58].
Regulation requires careful thought about unintended effects [ 85]. Poorly designed
laws can create loopholes and exacerbate the problems they aim to solve. For instance,
just before the GDPR took effect, Meta attempted to bypass it by shifting data process -
ing from consent to contractual obligation and embedding behavioral ads in its Terms
of Service. When the EU ruled this illegal and required Meta to offer a non-targeted
advertising option, Meta began charging users over €12.99 per month [132]. This "pay or
consent" model worsened the issue by removing users' options to limit data tracking and
was adopted by many others. Although it may be a legitimate business model, it is under
ongoing legal dispute. The EU has deemed it a breach of the DMA, arguing that it effec -
tively "forces" users to pay for privacy [46].
Most of Meta’s violations involve collecting information without obtaining full or
informed consent. These include concealed data collection, attempts to circumvent reg -
ulations, deceptive tactics, or outright lying and misleading users and authorities. Data
breaches result from security failures and increase the potential harm caused by other

Regulatory compliance is important but insufficient. First, even with full legal compli -
ance, some privacy risks remain unaddressed by current laws. Regulatory bodies often
struggle to anticipate all potential issues, especially in fast-emerging technologies. Sec -
ond, enforcement mechanisms are often slow, weak, or insufficient [ 84]. Agencies may
lack the expertise for complex tech issues and must balance enforcement with due pro -
cess. Penalties may also fail to deter well-funded tech behemoths. Third, compliance
approaches often fail to ensure that employees consistently adhere to both ethical and
legal standards [7]. This is because it focuses on following rules regardless of their moral
implications, and rules themselves can be flawed, containing loopholes or being outright
wrong. Consider, for example, a compliant Nazi concentration camp officer [144].
Regulation may be able to reduce certain kinds of aberrant behavior out of fear of
sanction or public retaliation, but it will not motivate people to become better or guar -
antee ethical behavior. Therefore. the penalizing “stick” approach (i.e., compliance) is not
enough; a rewarding “carrot” approach (i.e., integrity-based strategies) is also needed
[163].
3 Integrity
Effective compliance is fundamentally rooted in a corporate culture of integrity [ 62],
fostering a commitment to ethical principles and consistent moral behavior. However,
regulatory compliance represents the minimum standard—a baseline threshold. Suc -
cessful organizations thrive by nurturing good relationships with stakeholders whose
goals extend beyond profit maximization [ 163]. “To have integrity is to refuse to be…
one kind of person in one social context, while quite another in other contexts” [ 97],
p.192). Thus, a company with integrity always acts honestly, transparently, and respect -
fully toward stakeholders. Achieving this balance requires making trade-offs and manag-
ing competing interests.
Assessing Meta's integrity requires a thorough analysis of its interactions with stake -
holders—individuals and groups that influence or are influenced by the organization.
Stakeholder theory provides an effective framework by offering a holistic understanding
of the diverse interests and impacts associated with corporate actions.

Meta claims to operate with integrity and has addressed issues, often under public scru -

4.1 Meaningful global social interactions
With its vast global reach, Facebook enables users to connect across cultures, transcend-
ing barriers of time, distance, and language. These digital connections serve as chan -
nels for interaction, which become meaningful when they enhance individuals’ lives,
strengthen their relationships, or provide emotional, informational, or tangible ben -
efits [ 90]. In this sense, meaningful social interactions are not limited to face-to-face
encounters,research shows that technology-mediated interactions, including those on
social media platforms like Facebook, can be just as meaningful [ 90].
Through repeated and deepening interactions, users can form habits that promote
personal growth. The value of such relationships is often most apparent to those directly
involved, as they experience increased trust, empathy, and shared understanding. Inter -
action frequency may vary across one’s social connections—more regular with some
and less with others—but importantly, social relationships are not zero-sum. Building
a bond with one person does not prevent forming new ones; on the contrary, each con -
nection can broaden a user’s network, creating opportunities for further relationships
and strengthening the social fabric.
This internal good—the facilitation of meaningful global social interactions—is par -
ticularly salient in the context of social media. Facebook excels in fostering these con -
nections by offering a wide range of features, including personalized news feeds, groups,
events, messaging, video calls, stories, marketplace, live streaming, comments, tagging,
and third-party app integration. In contrast, other platforms often focus on narrower
forms of interaction: X (formerly Twitter), TikTok, and YouTube emphasize broad con -
tent sharing, while WhatsApp and Snapchat specialize in instant messaging.
A Neo-Aristotelian philosophical framework, enriched by contemporary research in
economics and social sciences on happiness, trust, prosperity, and well-being, provides
an effective lens for understanding how meaningful global social interactions foster vir -
tues, contribute to human flourishing, and advance the common good.

true friendship, characterized by mutual virtue, which Aristotle identifies as central to a
fulfilling and happy life [6]. Such friendships enhance moral character and provide emo -
tional support, thus playing a crucial role in achieving eudaimonia. Recent research sup-
ports this view, showing that meaningful connections between individuals are strongly
linked to higher levels of well-being and are fundamental to self-perceived happiness [ 8,
67, 86, 100, 137]. Although Facebook may rarely be used explicitly to make new friends,

Building on this, the Aristotelian notion of zoon politikon  further emphasizes that

of beneficial societal outcomes. Extensive research has demonstrated that higher levels
of social trust are associated with greater economic prosperity [ 16, 165, 171], improved

are crucial for sustaining systems like pensions, insurance, and long-term infrastruc -

activity by offering tools for businesses, such as Facebook Marketplace, targeted adver -

distraction, information overload, and superficial engagement, Facebook can neverthe -
less foster forms of modern contemplation by promoting intellectual dialogue, reflec -
tive learning, and the communal pursuit of knowledge through features such as groups,
pages, and interactive comments.

4.2 Meta and the virtues
Aristotle taught that virtues are essential for achieving excellence. In the same way, for
Meta to truly excel in its practice of facilitating social connections and to realize its
internal good of fostering meaningful global social interactions, it must be guided by the
cultivation of virtues. Virtues are stable dispositions to act in morally excellent ways,
such as honesty, courage, and generosity, each representing a mean between extremes
(e.g., courage as the mean between recklessness and cowardice).
Virtues are not innate; they are cultivated through deliberate practice, guided by rea -
son, and shaped into stable habits that form one’s character [ 139]. Much like learning
a new language, through consistent practice, one develops fluency until it becomes an
inherent ability. This does not mean that virtuous people cannot make mistakes, but
that there is a genuine commitment to live according to convictions and principles. An
individual—or by extension, an organization—that consistently practices virtue will
increasingly make ethical choices naturally, as a matter of character. While virtuous indi-
viduals may occasionally err, a virtuous organization benefits from collective guidance
and internal critique, reducing the risk of ethical failure, such as prioritizing external
over internal goods.
The Aristotelian concept of the unity of virtues suggests that virtues are intercon -
nected and mutually reinforcing [ 98], p.142). Accordingly, embedding particular virtues
within a company’s operations will inevitably cultivate others and produce ripple effects
across its operations and among its stakeholders. For example, by being transparent
about data collection and usage, Facebook not only promotes honesty but also reinforces
the virtues of justice, by ensuring fairness and protecting users' rights to make informed
decisions, and temperance, by restraining the pursuit of profit at the expense of deceiv -
ing users.
We suggest grounding Meta's approach in the four cardinal virtues: prudence, justice,
temperance, and courage.
Justice is the habitual disposition to give each person what is due to them [ 125]. It is
synonymous with fairness, honesty, equity, and righteousness. In the context of Meta’s
practices, it encompasses being truthful and transparent in policies, actions, and inten -
tions, recognizing the dignity of every individual. It involves the ethical and skillful com -
munication of information [ 159]. A commitment to justice would push Facebook to
implement “privacy by design” principles [23].
Courage involves strength, endurance, and the ability to confront fear, uncertainty,
and intimidation. These qualities are vital when facing challenges such as dealing with
authoritarian states that pressure Facebook to suppress dissident voices. For example, in
Vietnam, individuals have been imprisoned for their Facebook posts [ 124]. While that
reflects external political coercion, Facebook has also faced ethical challenges internally.
When NYU researchers developed a tool to study political advertising and disinforma -
tion on the platform, the company chose to cut off their access. A more courageous and

Moderation, or temperance, is the virtue that involves the ability to control one's

Practical wisdom is story-dependent and requires a teleological framework [ 117],
that is, a sense of purpose and an understanding of one's life as a coherent narrative.
It involves making morally sound decisions that guide a person toward their ultimate
good, eudaimonia. Without a clear understanding of the end goal (telos), decisions
become isolated and lack coherence, preventing a person from living a virtuous life. This
is where the contribution of this paper lies: in identifying Facebook’s underlying practice
and internal good, it offers a moral framework through which the company can reorient
its efforts toward meaningful and ethically grounded outcomes.
Facebook has the capacity to influence not only the short-term behaviors of its users,
employees, and stakeholders but also their long-term moral development by shaping
the practices in which they engage. Through these practices, Facebook can contribute
to the development of character, thereby influencing the life narratives of those involved
[81]. When Facebook fosters meaningful global connections and interactions that pro -
mote trust, understanding, and community, it enhances users' ability to cultivate the vir-
tues necessary for living well. However, if the platform prioritizes external goods at the
expense of excellence, it risks corrupting these practices and undermining the cultiva -
tion of virtues essential to human flourishing.
4.3 The challenge of corporate transformation
If adopting virtue ethics is so clearly beneficial, why hasn’t Meta already undergone this
moral transformation? The answer lies in the significant practical challenges and deeply
embedded incentive structures that make building and sustaining virtuous organizations
difficult to achieve. The strongest challenge is the relentless drive for profits. As a pub -
licly traded company, Meta is under constant pressure to deliver growth and returns on
investment. Practices like extensive personal data collection, micro-targeted advertis -
ing, and algorithmic amplification of engaging content have been enormously profitable.
Transforming these practices in accordance with virtue ethics might mean sacrificing

criticism or regulatory scrutiny. Internal incentive systems—employee evaluations,
promotions, and bonuses—have historically been tied to metrics like user growth and
engagement. In such an environment, even well-intentioned employees find it difficult
to prioritize excellence. While ethical behavior can lead to sustainable success, its ben -

Another challenge is the lack of strong external accountability. While privacy regula -

external pressure. Internally, Meta would need leaders of strong moral character—indi -

profit or power. A realignment of internal incentives would be necessary, complement -

trust, and social impact. This may require difficult decisions, including upholding ethi -

model, or investing significantly in content moderation and privacy protections that may
not yield immediate financial returns.
While such a shift is undoubtedly challenging, it is not beyond reach. Numerous stud -

Mozilla, which champions user autonomy through open-source tools, and Patagonia,
which integrates environmental and social responsibility into its core strategy, demon -
strate that aligning ethical purpose with business success is possible. Similarly, Proton—
the company behind Proton Mail and Proton VPN—embodies a virtue-driven approach
to technology. Founded by CERN scientists, Proton operates on the principle that pri -
vacy is a fundamental human right. This ethos reflects the virtue of justice by respecting
user autonomy, temperance by rejecting data-driven advertising, and practical wisdom
by designing secure, privacy-preserving technologies.
No individual or organization acts virtuously at all times; moments of weakness are
inevitable. Moreover, a virtuous organization depends on the character of its members,
who shape and sustain its ethical culture. Yet individuals come and go, and their val -
ues or priorities may change over time, leading to shifts in an organization’s ethical cul -
ture. Google’s early adoption of the motto “Don’t be evil” reflected an initial aspiration
to ethical leadership in tech. Facebook once differentiated itself by prioritizing user pri -
vacy compared to platforms like MySpace. For this reason, external forces—particularly
government regulation and civil society—are necessary as well. Legal frameworks that
enforce accountability, combined with public pressure that rewards responsible conduct,
help ensure that companies are held to consistent moral standards.

5 Conclusion
The main causes of Meta’s regulatory infringements and ethical lapses stem from its
extensive and aggressive data collection practices. Meta has exploited cognitive biases,
intentional ignorance, deceptive tactics, and other strategies to bypass data protection
laws and abuse users' trust. When exposed, Meta covers the fines and resorts to public
relations stunts, using integrity as a facade. This short-term, zero-sum approach relies on
manipulating users for immediate gain, but in the long term, it is not likely to succeed.
This challenge extends beyond Meta; transforming into a virtuous organization is
undoubtedly challenging and requires significant time, effort, and a willingness to make
trade-offs, including potential impacts on profitability. Thus, such a transformation is
highly unlikely for Meta and similar companies. However, history shows that systemic
change is possible, and we should not water down ethical ideals just because they are
difficult to achieve. Ethical inquiry should aim to inspire change, not merely guide indi -
vidual behavior within current constraints.
Addressing Meta's deep ethical challenges requires both internal moral commit -
ment and sustained external accountability. A shift toward a virtue ethics framework
is essential, not as a replacement for regulation and integrity measures but as a foun -
dation that reorients the company toward human flourishing and the common good.
Without intrinsic motivation grounded in virtue, and without the support of regulatory
and societal pressure, meaningful and lasting transformation remains unlikely. Even if
Meta resists this shift, the same principles can guide other organizations in cultivating a
new entrepreneurial ethos—one that values moral character alongside innovation, sets
new standards for technological platforms, and fosters a culture oriented toward human
flourishing.
Further research is needed to explore how the transition toward virtue-centered orga -
nizations could be practically implemented within companies like Meta. This includes
identifying strategies, leadership models, and institutional changes necessary to culti -
vate virtues internally while aligning business practices with the common good. Future
studies could also investigate how users themselves can engage with platforms like Face-
book in ways that foster personal virtue and promote societal well-being. Together, these
research avenues would help create a more comprehensive roadmap for transforming
digital environments into spaces that support human flourishing.
