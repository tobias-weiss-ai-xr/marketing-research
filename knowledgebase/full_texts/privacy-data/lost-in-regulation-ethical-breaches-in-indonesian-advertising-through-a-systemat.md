ABSTRACT

This article examines how criticism of advertising has been
discussed in Indonesian communication scholarship and how
these critiques can be mapped onto the Indonesian Advertising
Ethics (Etika Pariwara Indonesia/EPI). Using a systematic
literature review, this study analyzed 123 peer -reviewed journal
articles published between 2004 and 2023 in SINTA -indexed
Indonesian communication journals. The search used the
keywords “komunikasi pemasaran,” “marketing communication,”
“advertising,” “advertisement,” “periklanan,” and “iklan,” followed
by manual screening of titles, abstracts, and full texts. The
findings identify ten dominant themes: exploitation of gender and
sexuality, discrimination and social representation bias, power
domination and capitalism, adver tising camouflage and blurring
boundaries, commodification of religion, visual manipulation and
hyperreality, inappropriate advertising for children, misleading
information, exaggerated claims and puffery, and intrusive digital
advertising. These themes ca n be mapped onto EPI provisions
concerning women, gender, pornography and pornoaction,
religion and culture, halal claims, children, misleading language,
product benefits, hyperbole, embedded advertising, digital
media, privacy, and database practices. However, EPI has been
explicitly and systematically used as an analytical framework in
only a limited number of studies. Most articles criticize advertising
through semiotics, gender studies, cultural studies, political
economy, media sociology, or discourse analysis without
operationalizing EPI as the primary framework. Drawing on
Niklas Luhmann’s Social Systems Theory, this article interprets
this pattern as limited resonance between professional self -
regulation and academic observation.

INTRODUCTION
Advertising is the focal point of the modern communicative sphere (Dyer, 2008)—an economic activity
(Kopf et al., 2011)  and a social communicative code (Huth, 1995) —that, in an increasingly functionally
differentiated society such as Indonesia, can transform public perception and sociocultural identity (Bazarcı,
2022; McDonald et al., 2020). Advertising does not function solely on a denotative level by presenting product
information; rather, it delivers a broader message as a symbolic transfer that influences understandings of
gender (Kitsa & Mudra, 2019) , religion (Ansari & Hyder, 2023) , social strata (Livas, 2020) , and macro -
ideologies (Zhao & Belk, 2008) . Consequently, at the intersection of traditional and digital distribution,
advertising becomes an ethically controversial arena for the contestation of meaning.
The Indonesian advertising market grows in a complex, diverse, and religious socio -cultural
environment. In the context of consumption, advertising not only fosters economic activity  (Arens & Weigold,
2017; Yanti & Gusfa, 2022)  but also becomes a vehicle for reinstating gendered norms through the physical
representation of women (Wicaksono, 2012; Widiastuti et al., 2023; Fernandez, 2011; Yanti A. D., 2016) ,
religious beliefs through the promotion of halal goods (Arbak et al., 2019; Mandjusri, 2017; Karimah et al.,
2019), and nationalist agendas (Tikhomirova et al., 2015). Thus, regulations that go beyond the mere fairness
of information are urgently needed to provide ethical guidance concerning social rights, human dignity, and
cultural engagement (Perdana, 2022; Schauster, 2019; Feenstra & Esteban, 2019) .
In Indonesia, the primary instrument for advertising ethics regulation is the Indonesian Advertising Ethics
(Etika Pariwara Indonesia/EPI) (Dewan Periklanan Indonesia, 2020) . The latest edition of EPI has expanded
its scope to address developments in digital media, algorithmic advertising, social media, native advertising,
and the protection of vulnerable audiences (Astuti, 2017; Dinanti & Hadi, 2021; Pasandaran, 2018;
Ruckenstein & Granroth, 2019; Hotkar et al., 2023) . As a self -regulatory framework, EPI provides normative
guidance for advertising practice in Indonesia. However, the effectiveness of advertising self -regulation
depends not only on the existence of ethical codes, but also on how such codes are understood ,
institutionalized, and operationalized by relevant stakeholders, including practitioners, regulators, educators,
and scholars (Belch & Belch, 2021; Moitra et al., 2022; Floridi, 2021; Feenstra & Esteban, 2019; Schauster,
2019).
Despite the existence of EPI, ethical concerns in Indonesian advertising continue to be discussed across
different academic approaches. Relevant studies have examined advertising through semiotics (El Ghiffary &
Widana, 2023; Arnie et al., 2017) , gender theory (Yanti A. D., 2016) , sociology of media (Trijayanto, 2017),
and cultural studies (Mutahir et al., 2019). However, only a limited number of studies have explicitly used EPI
as a systematic analytical framework. This indicates a gap between professional advertising ethics and
academic research: Indonesian communication scholarship has critically examined adve rtising practices, but
has not always translated those critiques into the normative categories provided by the industry’s own ethical
code (Joaquin & Biana, 2020).
Therefore, this study aims to systematically review how criticism of advertising has been discussed in
Indonesian communication scholarship and how these critiques can be mapped onto the Indonesian
Advertising Ethics (Etika Pariwara Indonesia/EPI). Rather than directly measuring advertising industry
compliance, this article focuses on academic observation: how Indonesian communication studies identify,
classify, and interpret ethical issues in advertising. This focus is important because previous studies ha ve
criticized Indonesian advertising through semiotics, gender studies, cultural studies, media sociology, and
other approaches, yet only a limited number of studies have explicitly operationalized EPI as a systematic
analytical framework. Accordingly, this study addresses three research questions:  (1) What are the dominant
themes of advertising criticism in Indonesian communication scholarship from 2004 to 2023?  (2) How can
these themes be mapped onto the provisions of the 2020 Indonesian Advertising Ethics?  (3) To what extent
has EPI been explicitly referenced and operationalized as an analytical framework in Indonesian advertising
scholarship?
The contribution of this study is twofold. Empirically, it maps the dominant themes of advertising criticism
and their relationship to EPI provisions. Theoretically, it uses Niklas Luhmann’s Social Systems Theory (1995;
2013) to interpret the limited operationalization of EPI in academic communication as a problem of weak
resonance between professional self -regulation and scholarly observation, rather than simply as a matter of
individual ignorance, moral failure, or total regulatory breakdown.

LITERATURE REVIEW
The Dynamics and Limitations of Advertising Self-Regulation
Globally, self-regulation does not always work as effectively as expected. Perelló-Oliver & Muela-Molina
(2014) found differences between the UK and Spain when assessing institutional independence and regulatory
oversight to preserve industry independence. Furthermore, Campbell & Grimm (2018) noted that emerging
ethical challenges —such as native advertising —can blur the boundaries between informational and
persuasive intent, indicating the need for stronger mechanisms. Conversely, Farrell (2012) found that in the
UK, practitioners’ compliance is primarily driven by the institutional authority and public acceptance of ethical
standards. Taken together, these studies suggest that the effectiveness of advertising self-regulation depends
not only on the normative text itself, but also on how ethical standards are institutionally supported, publicly
recognized, and connected to industry interests.
Social Systems Theory, Self-Regulation, and Second-Order Observation
To interpret the relationship between advertising ethics, academic research, and professional self -
regulation, this study draws on Niklas Luhmann’s Social Systems Theory (Luhmann, 1995; 2000; 2013) . In
Luhmann’s theory, modern society is functionally differentiated into social systems that reproduce themselves
through communication. Society does not consist of individuals as its basic elements, but of communication.
Human beings participate in communication, but as psychic systems, they remain in the environment of social
systems rather than functioning as the elements of society itself (Luhmann, 1995; 2013) . This distinction is
important because the limited visibility of advertising ethics cannot be reduced merely to individual ignorance,
moral failure, or the unwillingness of particular actors to follow ethical rules.
From this perspective, EPI can be understood as a form of normative communication produced within
advertising self -regulation. It formulates expectations about what advertising should and should not do.
However, such expectations do not automatically become operative within academic communication, industry
practice, or public discourse. They must be reconstructed within the communicative operations of each system.
Therefore, when Indonesian advertising studies criticize advertisements through semiotics, gen der studies,
cultural studies, media sociology, or other frameworks without explicitly operationalizing EPI, this does not
necessarily indicate the total failure of EPI. Rather, it suggests limited resonance between professional self -
regulation and scholarly observation.
This argument is also relevant to discussions on advertising education and ethical competence. Ethical
regulation gains stronger social relevance when it is supported by academic research, professional education,
and empirical analysis (Andrews & Shimp, 2018; Jones et al., 2022). However, the academic system does not
simply reproduce professional codes as they are. It observes, selects, translates, and reconstructs them
through its own concepts, research problems, theories, and methods. For this reason, the limited use of EPI
in advertising scholarship should be examined not only as a regulatory issue but also as a problem of how
ethical norms become visible within academic communication.
This article uses Luhmann’s theory to interpret this pattern. The theory is not used as a coding instrument
for classifying the reviewed articles. The empirical classification remains based on thematic categories and the
provisions of the 2020 EPI. Luhmann’s theory is used to explain how the reviewed articles observe advertising
ethics and how this SLR, in turn, observes those academic observations. In this sense, the study operates as
a second-order observation of Indonesian communication scholarship on advertising ethics and self-regulation
(Luhmann, 2000; 2013).

METHOD
This study employed a systematic literature review (SLR) to examine how criticism of advertising has
been discussed in Indonesian communication scholarship and how these critiques can be mapped onto the
Indonesian Advertising Ethics (Etika Pariwara Indones ia/EPI). The SLR approach was selected because it
enables a transparent, replicable, and structured synthesis of existing academic literature (Page et al., 2021;
Petticrew & Roberts, 2006; Gough et al., 2017). In this study, the SLR was not used merely to collect previous
studies, but to identify recurring themes of advertising criticism, examine their relationship to EPI provisions,
and assess the extent to which EPI has been explicitly operationalized as an an alytical framework in
Indonesian communication scholarship.

Population and Sampling
The population for this study was drawn from communication journals indexed in SINTA (Science and
Technology Index). A total of 131 communication -related journals constituted the journal source base. These
journals consisted of 21 journals indexed in SINTA  2, 19 journals indexed in SINTA 3, 49 journals indexed in
SINTA 4, and 42 journals indexed in SINTA 5. The SINTA distribution is reported to clarify the publication
context of the reviewed literature, rather than to treat journal ranking as a direct measure of the methodological
quality of individual articles.
Article collection was conducted by exploring the website of each journal and using the available
keyword search functions. The search strategy began with broader terms and then narrowed to more specific
terminology in both Indonesian and English, following the logic of systematic searching proposed by Gough et
al. (2017). The keywords used were: “komunikasi pemasaran” OR “marketing communication*” OR
“advertising” OR “advertisement*” OR “periklanan” OR “iklan.” These keywords were selected to capture
articles discussing advertising, marketing communication, advertising cr iticism, and ethical issues in
advertising.
The year 2004 was selected as the starting point because this review aimed to trace scholarly
discussions published after the 2004 edition of the Indonesian Advertising Ethics. The 2004 –2023 period
allows this study to examine how advertising criticism and  EPI-related concerns have been discussed in
Indonesian communication scholarship over nearly two decades.
To ensure sample quality and minimize bias, inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied (Littell et al.,
2008; Jesson et al., 2011). The inclusion criteria comprised: (1) research articles published in SINTA -indexed
communication journals; (2) articles published between 2004 and 2023; (3) articles whose titles, abstracts, or
contents discussed advertising studies, marketing communicat ion, or critiques of advertising with explicit or
implicit ethical dimensions; (4) articles written in Indonesian or English; and (5) articles available in full text.
The exclusion criteria excluded opinion articles or non -academic essays, studies unrelate d to advertising in
Indonesia, articles lacking ethical relevance, duplicate articles, and articles with incomplete citation
information.
Research Procedures
The research procedure consisted of three main stages: article identification, article screening, and final
synthesis. In the identification stage, articles were collected from the websites of SINTA -indexed
communication journals using the predetermined ke ywords. This stage resulted in 126 articles related to
advertising criticism, marketing communication, and ethical issues in advertising.  In the screening stage, the
titles, abstracts, and full texts of the identified articles were reviewed against the inclusion and exclusion
criteria. Three articles were excluded because they did not sufficiently meet the criteria for final synthesis. As
a result, 123 articles were included in the final qualitative synthesis. The selection process is presented in
figure-1.

Data from the selected articles were extracted manually into a spreadsheet to ensure transparency and
auditability. The extraction sheet included the following information: (1) article title; (2) year of publication; (3)
journal name; (4) SINTA ranking of the journal; (5) research method; (6) main theme of advertising criticism;
(7) subtheme; (8) relevant EPI provision; (9) whether the article explicitly mentioned EPI; and (10) key findings
relevant to advertising ethics. This procedure follows the principl e that systematic reviews should make the
process of selection, extraction, and synthesis visible and traceable (Patole, 2021; Gough et al., 2017).
The extracted data were then analyzed using thematic narrative synthesis. First, the articles were
grouped according to the dominant themes of advertising criticism. Second, these themes were mapped onto
relevant provisions of the 2020 Indonesian Advertising Ethics (Dewan Periklanan Indonesia, 2020). Third, the
articles were assessed based on the extent to which they explicitly referenced and operationalized EPI as an
analytical framework. This classification was necessary because not all articles that criticized advertising
explicitly used EPI. Some  articles directly mentioned EPI and identified specific provisions, while others
discussed advertising ethics or criticized advertising practices through semiotics, gender studies, cultural
studies, media sociology, or other frameworks.
Accordingly, this study distinguishes between four levels of EPI visibility in the reviewed literature:
articles that explicitly mention EPI and identify relevant provisions; articles that discuss advertising ethics
without specifying EPI provisions; artic les that do not mention EPI but can be analytically related to EPI
provisions; and articles that discuss broader advertising criticism without sufficient basis for article -level EPI
classification. This distinction allows the study to examine the visibilit y of EPI in Indonesian communication
scholarship without assuming that every criticism of advertising automatically constitutes a formal EPI violation
or that every article in the corpus operationalizes EPI in the same way.
Theoretical Interpretation
Beyond the empirical synthesis, Niklas Luhmann’s Social Systems Theory (1995; 2013) provides the
theoretical foundation for interpreting the findings. In this study, Luhmann’s theory is not used as the primary
coding instrument for classifying the reviewed articles. The coding process remains based on thematic
categories of advertising c riticism and their analytical relationship to the provisions of the 2020 Indonesian
Advertising Ethics.
Instead, Luhmann’s theory is used as an interpretive framework to understand how EPI appears,
disappears, or becomes only partially visible within academic communication. In this sense, the reviewed
articles are treated as academic observations of advertis ing, while this SLR functions as a second -order
observation of how Indonesian communication scholarship observes advertising ethics and self -regulation.
This distinction is important because the limited use of EPI in the reviewed literature should not be
understood simply as individual ignorance, moral negligence, or the total failure of advertising self -regulation.
Rather, it can be interpreted as a probl em of limited resonance between professional self -regulation and
academic communication. EPI formulates normative expectations about advertising, but these expectations
become analytically visible only when they are reconstructed within scholarly communica tion as concepts,
categories, or analytical frameworks.
Ethical Considerations
Given that this study is a systematic literature review relying entirely on publicly available, peer-reviewed
academic journal articles, formal institutional ethical approval involving human participants was not required.
Nevertheless, this study complies with standard research ethics by applying transparent selection criteria,
documenting the review procedures, and reporting the findings accurately in accordance with PRISMA 2020
principles (Page et al., 2021).

Research Question-1: Dominant Themes of Advertising Criticism
To answer the first research question, the 123 reviewed articles were analyzed to identify the dominant
themes of advertising criticism in Indonesian communication scholarship from 2004 to 2023. The analysis
shows that advertising criticism is not concentr ated on a single issue but distributed across several recurring
themes. These themes include gender and stereotypes, discrimination and social representation bias,
domination and capitalism, advertising camouflage and blurred boundaries, commodification of religion, visual
manipulation, child-targeted advertising, misleading information, exaggerated claims, and digital intrusiveness.
The distribution of these themes is presented in figure-3.

The most dominant theme concerns the exploitation of gender and sexuality, which appears in 29
articles. This theme includes studies that criticize the representation of women’s bodies, sexualized visual
display, gender stereotypes, and the use of feminini ty or masculinity as persuasive advertising devices. In
these studies, advertising is often criticized for reproducing unequal gender relations and for reducing women
or gendered identities to objects of visual attraction, consumption, or symbolic exchange.
The second major theme is discrimination and social representation bias, identified in 18 articles. This
theme refers to studies that examine how advertising constructs unequal representations of class, ethnicity,
race, body image, social status, or margin alized identities. These articles show that advertising criticism in
Indonesian communication scholarship is not limited to product promotion, but also addresses broader
symbolic inequalities reproduced through media representation.

The third theme is power domination and capitalism, found in 14 articles. These studies criticize
advertising as part of a broader economic and ideological mechanism that promotes consumerism,
commodifies social values, and normalizes capitalist forms of d esire. Rather than treating advertisements as
isolated promotional texts, these studies situate advertising within wider relations of power, market ideology,
and consumer culture.
The fourth theme is advertising camouflage and blurred boundaries, identified in 13 articles. This theme
includes studies on native advertising, embedded advertising, product placement, advertorial forms, and
promotional content that appears similar to edi torial, entertainment, or user -generated content. The central
issue in this theme is the blurring of boundaries between information, entertainment, and persuasion.
The fifth theme is commodification of religion and religious values, appearing in 11 articles. These
studies examine how religious symbols, Islamic identity, halal values, piety, and moral narratives are used as
advertising resources. The criticism does no t necessarily reject the presence of religion in advertising, but
questions how religious meanings may be transformed into marketable signs, brand differentiation, or
consumer persuasion.
The sixth theme is visual manipulation and hyperreality, identified in 10 articles. This theme includes
studies that criticize exaggerated visual construction, idealized imagery, artificial beauty standards, and
symbolic representations that may create unr ealistic expectations. These articles show how advertising can
construct realities that are aesthetically persuasive but socially problematic.
The seventh theme is inappropriate advertising for children, found in 9 articles. These studies focus on
children as a vulnerable audience and criticize advertisements that may exploit children’s limited knowledge
of persuasion, encourage unhealthy consumption, or expose children to inappropriate persuasive appeals.
The eighth theme is lack of clarity or misleading information, identified in 7 articles. This theme refers to
studies that criticize unclear claims, incomplete product information, ambiguous messages, or promotional
statements that may mislead consumers. The issue here concerns the informational dimension of advertising
and the expectation that advertising should communicate claims in a clear and accountable manner.
The ninth theme is exaggerated claims and puffery, also found in 7 articles. These studies examine
promotional exaggeration, excessive superiority claims, and persuasive overstatement. Although puffery is
often treated as a conventional advertising techniq ue, these studies criticize how exaggeration may blur the
boundary between persuasive creativity and misleading communication.
The final theme is privacy and intrusive digital advertising, identified in 5 articles. This theme reflects
emerging concerns about data -driven advertising, digital tracking, algorithmic targeting, and intrusive
promotional practices. Although this theme a ppears less frequently than gender or representation issues, it
indicates a growing shift in Indonesian advertising criticism toward digital media environments.
Overall, the findings indicate that Indonesian communication scholarship has developed a broad range
of advertising criticism. The dominant concerns remain related to gender representation, social inequality,
capitalism, and religious commodification, while newer concerns such as digital intrusiveness and data-related
advertising practices are beginning to emerge. This finding answers the first resear ch question by showing
that advertising criticism in Indonesian communication scholarship is thematically diverse, but still concentrated
around issues of representation, commodification, persuasion, and consumer vulnerability.

advertising, and digital privacy can be directl y related to specific clauses in the EPI. This indicates that EPI
provides a sufficiently broad normative vocabulary for addressing many of the ethical concerns discussed in
Indonesian communication scholarship.
However, not all themes are equally codified in EPI. Themes such as power domination, capitalism,
consumerism, and the social construction of reality tend to operate at a broader theoretical level. These themes
criticize advertising as part of a wider syst em of market ideology, consumer culture, symbolic domination, or
social inequality. They can be related to EPI’s general principles of responsible advertising and consumer
protection, but they are not always reducible to one specific clause.
This distinction is important for interpreting the findings. The reviewed articles do not merely identify rule
violations; they also observe advertising as a cultural, ideological, and symbolic practice. Therefore, EPI
functions in this study as a normativ e mapping framework, while the articles themselves often use broader
theoretical lenses such as semiotics, gender studies, cultural studies, political economy, and media sociology.
Overall, the answer to the second research question is that the dominant themes of advertising criticism
can be mapped onto several EPI provisions, especially those concerning gender, women, religion and culture,
children, language, product claims, hyperbo le, embedded advertising, digital media, privacy, and database
practices. At the same time, the mapping also reveals that some academic criticisms exceed the clause-based
structure of EPI and require broader theoretical interpretation.

EPI provisions, this section focuses on whether the articles themselves explicitly mention, use, or

Discussion
This section provides a critical and theoretical interpretation of the empirical findings using Niklas
Luhmann’s Social Systems Theory. The high frequency of the ten dominant themes of advertising criticism
suggests that ethical issues in Indonesian advertising scholarship should not be understood merely as isolated
cases, historical accidents, or individual moral fa ilings. Rather, they can be interpreted as recurring
communicative patterns through which advertising is observed, criticized, and normatively evaluated within
Indonesian communication studies (Luhmann, 1995).
Luhmann (1995; 2013) argues that contemporary society is functionally differentiated into operationally
closed and autopoietic social systems, each reproducing itself through its own communicative operations.
From this perspective, law, ethics, academia, and advertising do not  necessarily process the same issue
through the same logic. While legal and ethical communication tend to formulate expectations in terms of
compliance, violation, responsibility, and normativity, advertising communication is strongly oriented toward
persuasive visibility, attention, memorability, and effectiveness. Therefore, the recurring themes identified in
this review should not be read simply as evidence that advertising actors deliberately reject ethical norms.
Rather, they indicate that ethical expe ctations require communicative translation before they can become
meaningful within advertising, academic, or regulatory communication.
This interpretation is consistent with advertising and marketing communication literature that has long
criticized gender bias, visual manipulation, cultural stereotyping, puffery, religious commodification, and
advertising directed at children. Belch & Belch (2021) and Clow & Baack (2022), for example, emphasize that
advertising strategies can reproduce gender bias, unrealistic representations, and persuasive distortions.
Similarly, Moriarty et al. (2019) and Andrews & Shimp (2018) show that cultural stereotyping, exaggerated
claims, and child-targeted persuasion raise ethical concerns because they may reproduce symbolic inequality,
exploit vulnerable audiences, or blur the boundary between creative persuasion and misleading
communication. From a Luhmannian perspective, these concerns become sociologically significant not
because advertising is outside ethics, but because ethical critique must be reconstructed within the
communicative logic of advertising before it can affect advertising practices.
The challenge becomes more complex in the context of digital transformation. The integration of big
data analytics, algorithms, and artificial intelligence (AI) in advertising intensifies the speed, personalization,
and opacity of persuasive communication (Gao et al., 2023; Darmawan & Perdana, 2026). The use of chatbots
based on large language models (LLMs) to insert unlabeled advertising content (Vukmirović et al., 2025) ,
together with real -time data tracking and algorithmic targeting, produces new problems of privacy,
transparency, consent, and accountability (Chuan et al., 2023; Judijanto et al., 2025; Singhal, 2024) . These
developments do not make ethical regulation impossible, but they complicate the translation of ethical norms
into the operational mechanisms of digital advertising. As Perdana (2022) suggests, regulatory and ethical
frameworks face difficulties when digital advertising practices operate through technical infrastructures that
are not easily visible to users, regulators, or even researchers.
In this context, the empirical finding that EPI has been explicitly and systematically operationalized only
in a limited number of studies should not be interpreted as the absolute failure of structural coupling between
law, academia, and advertising. A mo re precise interpretation is that the relationship between EPI and
Indonesian advertising scholarship demonstrates limited resonance. EPI exists as a professional self -
regulatory framework, and the 2020 document defines it as normative provisions concernin g the advertising
profession and business that are agreed upon for observance, compliance, and enforcement by its supporting
associations and institutions. EPI also states that advertising ethics are grounded in the principle of self -
regulation, where ethical norms are more effective when formulated, agreed upon, and enforced by advertising

The role of this study, therefore, is not to claim that EPI is merely symbolic or that advertising ethics
have completely failed. Instead, the contribution of this study is to show how EPI appears unevenly across
academic communication: explicitly in a sma ll number of studies, implicitly in studies that discuss advertising
ethics without specifying EPI provisions, and analytically in studies whose themes can be mapped onto EPI
even though they do not cite it directly. This is where Luhmann’s concept of second-order observation becomes
theoretically useful (Luhmann, 2000; 2013) . The reviewed articles function as first -order academic
observations of advertising, while this SLR observes how those studies observe advertising ethics, self -
regulation, and EPI.
Accordingly, the theoretical contribution of this study lies in reframing the marginal use of EPI not as
simple ignorance or total regulatory failure, but as a problem of limited communicative translation between
professional self-regulation, academic obse rvation, and advertising practice. To strengthen EPI’s relevance,
future research needs to use it not only as a normative background but also as a systematic analytical
framework. This would allow scholars, legal researchers, sociologists, advertising practitioners, and the public
to translate ethical expectations into categories that can be recognized within academic research, professional
evaluation, and industry practice.

CONCLUSION
This study examined how criticism of advertising has been discussed in Indonesian communication
scholarship and how these critiques can be mapped onto the Indonesian Advertising Ethics (Etika Pariwara
Indonesia/EPI). Based on a systematic literature review  of 123 journal articles published between 2004 and
2023, the findings show that advertising criticism in Indonesia is concentrated in ten dominant themes: gender
and sexuality, discrimination and social representation bias, power domination and capitalism , advertising
camouflage, religious commodification, visual manipulation, child-targeted advertising, misleading information,
exaggerated claims, and intrusive digital advertising.
These themes can be mapped onto several provisions of the 2020 EPI, particularly those concerning
women, gender, pornography and pornoaction, religion and culture, halal claims, children, misleading
language, product benefits, hyperbole, embedded advertising, digital media, privacy, and database practices.
However, the mapping also shows that not all academic criticisms are reducible to specific EPI clauses.
Themes such as capitalism, consumerism, domination, and social construction require broader theoreti cal
interpretation because they address advertising as part of wider cultural, ideological, and economic processes.
The study also shows that EPI has been explicitly and systematically operationalized only to a limited
extent in Indonesian advertising scholarship. EPI is not entirely absent, but its presence is uneven: it appears
explicitly in a small number of studies,  implicitly in studies discussing advertising ethics, and analytically in
studies whose themes can be related to EPI without directly citing it. Drawing on Niklas Luhmann’s Social
Systems Theory, this pattern is interpreted as limited resonance between pro fessional self -regulation and
academic observation, rather than as individual ignorance, moral failure, or the total failure of advertising ethics
(Luhmann, 1995; 2000; 2013).
A limitation of this study is that it analyzes academic articles, not direct advertising industry practice.
Therefore, the findings should be read as a reflection of how Indonesian communication scholarship observes
advertising ethics, not as a direct meas urement of EPI compliance in the industry. Future research should
examine how EPI is understood and used by advertising practitioners, regulators, educators, and audiences,
particularly in digital advertising, influencer marketing, native advertising, algo rithmic targeting, and AI -
mediated persuasion.
