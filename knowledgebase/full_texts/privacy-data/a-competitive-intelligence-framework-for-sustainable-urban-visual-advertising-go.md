1. INTRODUCTION
The urban visual advertising now consists of a variety of forms, including static billboards,
transport media, building wrapping, digital screens, projections, interactive street furniture to
sensor-enabled installations. Although they may facilitate patterns of commerce, wayfinding,
emergency communication, and place activation, they may also create conflicts over the aesthetics
of a visual environment, neighbourhood identity, energy consumption, artificial illumination, road
safety, pedestrian accessibility and privacy, and legitimacy of permitting by municipal regulations.
It is not how advertisements are supposed to advertise, but where, when and how they can be
accommodated within the objectives of the public space.
This is a dynamic issue with digital transformation. The displays that are driven by data will
be able to refresh the information fairly quickly, personalize the content to the crowd and the
place, and combine commercial information with traffic and emergency information. The biological
diversity of their work may be utilized to create social value, yet they may also lead to unclear
data cultures, excessive optimism, rapid animation, and biased effects in the residential, heritage,
school, healthcare and transport spheres. Studies on the topic of digital transformation and digital

Competitive Intelligence is the organizing logic which transforms these scattered signals to
relevant knowledge needed for decision-making. In the present work, the procedure of gathering
resident assessments is made into intelligence via constructs that are validated, merge with concrete
situations and distinct risks, and are turned into ranked governance interventions, shared with the
units who execute the interventions, and provided with feedback. This is a single consultation, but
incorporates the environmental scanning, analysis, decision support, implementation and monitoring.
The existing research has given limited insights for the issues of digital signage, visual clutter,
artificial light, data governance, smart city technology, public sector AI, sustainability and quality of
life in cities. Such streams in reality are not often found in a single, well-defined decision architecture
that connects positive design characteristics with perceived harms, transparency of governance,
legitimacy of the public, preference of the public, approval of the scenario, priority of intervention,
organizational implementation and feedback. So the non-existence of research on urban advertising
and digital governance per se is not the problem; it’s the lack of a new Research + Digital Governance
configuration that can be managed within a governed Competitive Intelligence Cycle that can support
repeated municipal decisions.

2. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
The theoretical framework integrates Competitive Intelligence, sustainable urban governance,
public value, and public preference. It does not transfer a private-sector market model unchanged
into municipal decision-making. Instead, it adapts Competitive Intelligence as an ethical and
evidence-based organizational capability in which the principal criteria are public value, legitimacy,
accountability, and sustainable decision quality.
2.1 Competitive Intelligence, Intelligence Governance, and Strategic Urban Decision
Support
Competitive Intelligence is defined in the present study as a controlled organizational process
that channels external signals, receives, validates, analyzes and disseminates results which are
then used and reevaluated for strategic decision making. It has intellectual roots in Jonathan Calof,
Craig Fleisher, John Prescott, Jan Herring, Leonard Fuld, Paul Dishman and René Pellissier. To keep
the manuscript’s boundary of recent references, that lineage is addressed through contemporary
syntheses of Competitive Intelligence as an organizational capability, which are modular, process-
based and analytics-driven (Madureira et al., 2021; Ranjan & Foropon, 2021; Talaoui & Kohtamäki, 2021).
This Competitive Intelligence Cycle consists six related stages. Intelligence direction specifies
the decisions that the municipality needs to be informed about, and the subject areas of the key
intelligence items. Resident evaluations, neighborhood conditions, ads, scenario responses,
governance priorities, complaints and contextual risks are gathered in environmental scanning and
collection. Validation and analysis evaluates information quality and transforms the evidence by
using the measurement validation, powerful modelling, mediation analysis, scenario comparison,
priority ranking and profile analysis. Dissemination is the process of converting statistical results
into useable products of intelligence such as scenario-approval estimates, intervention ranking,
importance-performance maps etc. These products are translated into permit decisions, sensitive-
site controls, brightness limits, safety assessment, privacy safeguards, the complaint process, and
enforcement actions in the organization as decisions and implementation. Outcomes of monitoring
and feedback are returned to the next intelligence cycle including everything from complaints to
compliance failures, appeals, and updated preferences.

Intelligence Governance is carried out throughout the cycle. It defines who can gather and have
access to information, how the provenance and data quality are recorded, what types of analyses
are acceptable, who is responsible for making the decisions, how to communicate uncertainty and
how to review and challenge the decisions. This governance layer will be particularly significant
when it comes to public space advertising with sensors and audience estimation, personalization and
automated content selection. Intelligence if not suitably structured can reduce governance visibility
and accountability, and introduce institutional opacity without proper decision rights, audit trails,
transparency and accountability requirements. This need is confirmed by recent organizational
research indicating that readiness, analysis ability and the interplay between automated and human
decision making influence the value creation of data intensive systems (Jöhnk et al., 2021; Mikalef &
Gupta, 2021; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021).
The framework also takes into account information asymmetry. People may be unaware of the
ownership of displays, the types of permits and associated data collection, complaint procedures and
municipal decision making criteria. Regulators may not have a systematic understanding of context-
specific preferences and of cumulative visual burdens. Advertisers may have poor intelligence about
design and governance of installations which will be accepted as acceptable. These asymmetries
can be mitigated by allowing disclosure, public notices, clear criteria, preference intelligence and
documented feedback, which provides information to those affected by decisions that is relevant
for the decision.
Sustainable competitive advantage is not something which one city can claim to be better than
another, but it is regarded as an enduring organizational capability. The benefit to public authorities
is the ongoing ability to identify emerging risks, gauge stakeholders’ expectations, pool evidence,
be able to fine-tune regulatory tools and not have to suffer from the same governance failures
again. For responsible advertisers and display operators, acceptance that is tied to the ability to
design contextually, demonstrate your environmental performance, provide transparency about
data usage and meet the regulations can be more sustainable than quick visibility gained through
intrusive installations.
This interpretation is given when it comes to Competitive Intelligence as compared to one-
off consultation. Consultation records perceptions at a specific time while intelligence is a system
that continually transforms the signals from the environment into organizational response and
assesses the outcomes of the response. The framework thus goes through a continuous sequence
of advertising attributes and perceived risks, public value and legitimacy, preference, scenario
evidence, governance decisions and implementation outcomes, and so on, providing feedback for
the next intelligence cycle.

Energy demand, material and operating hours, and artificial lighting are issues related to

There are two tendinous points for governance that are presented in the literature. A control
approach to advertising sees it primarily as a size and placement, illumination and legal issue. A
public -value approach involves considering the contribution of an installation to useful information,
consideration of place identity, equitable distribution of environmental burden, and accountability to
impacted communities. The first method is easier to administer, but may fail to consider cumulative,
data-related and legitimacy effects. The second is more detailed and more demanding in terms
of intelligence, interdepartmental cooperation, and open and neutral decisionmaking. The current
system is based on the public -value perspective, but still includes physical and safety restrictions
that are enforceable.
In literature, there are two conflicting governance trends. In a control oriented approach, there
is an emphasis on advertising as a size, place, light and legality matter. A public -value approach will
consider whether an installation provides relevant information, considers place identity, is equitable
in the distribution of environmental costs and is held accountable to those affected by it. The first
one is simpler to administer, but might neglect data related and cumulative effects, and legitimacy
effects. The second is more specific and requires that the intelligence functions are more powerful,
inter-departmental co-ordination and more open decision criteria. The current system is a public
value system that has physical and safety controls that are enforceable.
2.3 Public preference intelligence
Public Preference Intelligence understands that the residents make judgments about advertising
in the city on numerous criteria; not an all or none basis. Aesthetic Integration (AI) is an aesthetic
embrace of fit by architecture and streetscape. Contextual and Cultural Congruence (CCC) is an
appreciation of neighborhood identity and heritage. Informational Public Value (IPV) encompasses
a range of contributions to wayfinding, emergency, transport, community and truthful information.
Environmental Sustainability (ES) is a form of support for energy efficiency, reusability of materials
and the environmental considerations associated with permits.

Unlike a traditional satisfaction survey, Public Preference Intelligence is not just about the level
of satisfaction, but is about linking validated evaluations to a specific strategic decision. Preference
evidence is then intelligence when it is known and uncertainty is reported, contextual and scenario
information is added on, and findings are communicated to those who can take action based on them.
The framework, however, doesn’t assume that all expressed preferences are policy instructions;
it tests whether the patterns are reliable, are of practical importance, are analytically stable, are
legally, safely, environmentally and equitably feasible.
2.4 Public value, legitimacy, and governance support
Legitimacy is conceptually different from liking. Residents may prefer a particular design but
still consider its approval process opaque, or dislike a commercial message while accepting a fair
and consistent regulatory system. Public value and legitimacy therefore provide the institutional
interpretation through which design, sustainability, and transparency become broader preference.
This distinction also prevents the outcome from being defined by the same items used as predictors.
Transparency does not automatically create legitimacy, and legitimacy is not equivalent to
popularity. A transparent procedure may still be considered unfair if affected groups cannot contest
it, while a popular installation may remain institutionally problematic if authorization standards
are inconsistently applied. The model therefore treats governance transparency as an antecedent
and legitimacy as an evaluative mechanism. This distinction allows the analysis to test whether
information, sustainability, and governance attributes become acceptable because the overall
arrangement is interpreted as procedurally fair and publicly valuable.
The framework expects positive design and value attributes to strengthen preference directly or
through legitimacy, whereas perceived visual and safety harms should weaken preference. Privacy
concern is expected to matter, although its effect may depend on whether safeguards are concrete
and visible. Governance transparency should strengthen legitimacy and independently support policy
participation. Public preference should, in turn, increase support for enforceable governance action.

3. METHOD
The method was developed to ensure that the fixed title, constructs, scenarios and strategic
outputs remain aligned with each other. The study materials included a 600-complete-profile, wide-
format matrix and a long-format file of 2,400 evaluations of scenarios. The numerical data were
provided as a methodological demonstration data set and are not actual respondents gathered
and analyzed independently. Thus results are interpreted as an analytical proof of the proposed
framework instead of a population estimate on the basis of a probability sample.
3.1 Research design and analytical architecture
The design combines a cross-sectional latent-variable survey architecture with a repeated
scenario module. The cross-sectional component estimates associations among design attributes,
perceived risks, governance trust, legitimacy, preference, and policy support. The scenario component
compares stated acceptability and permit decisions across eight advertising configurations. Cross-
sectional coefficients are interpreted as standardized associations and predictive contributions;
stronger causal language is reserved for contrasts attributable to the assigned scenario conditions.
The analytical records were designed to reproduce the proposed measurement and decision-
support workflow and were not obtained through independent probability-based recruitment.
Consequently, statistical significance, confidence intervals, and predictive measures describe
relationships within the supplied analytical dataset; they do not establish prevalence, population
parameters, or subgroup distributions in a real municipal population. External validity is therefore
not established. Transfer to an actual city requires field administration, documented sampling,
ethics approval where applicable, nonresponse assessment, measurement-invariance testing, and
replication of the structural and scenario results.

and geographical distributions. Named city or district categories must not be treated as evidence

were retained, and no imputation was required.

SAR, DPMC, GTT, PVL, and PGC each contained four items, while PPE contained five. All used a seven-
point agreement scale. Ten additional items measured governance priorities, and each scenario was
evaluated for acceptability, public value, harm, trust, and permit decision. Positive and risk constructs
were retained separately; no global all-item score was calculated.

4.10 Integrated theoretical interpretation
The results provide a denial both to accepting and rejecting the urban visual advertising in
general. The more aesthetically integrated, culturally congruent, institutionally legitimate and related
to the value of the people, the greater the preference. Preference was down when installations
caused safety and accessibility issues, or were intrusive. Based on the scenario evidence, the
same conclusion was reached when considering concrete scenarios: regulated, safeguarded, and
temporary configurations were accepted, while high brightness residential, cluttered, and school
adjacent were rejected.
The main method for conversion was legitimacy. There was a higher association with PVL in
GTT compared to other variables and a higher direct association with PPE than in other variables.
The factor of informational value and environmental sustainability had an impact on preference
mostly via legitimacy. This means that design and governance attributes can be strategically helpful
when construed as testament that social benefits exceed visual and social costs. The outcome is
in line with the research into digital transformation which focuses on redesigning institutions and
organizations rather than just implementing technology (Kraus et al., 2022).
Immediate intervention priorities were Safety and Clutter. They both possessed negative
significant coefficients, a low level of good performance and a scenario counterpart. There is a shift

4.11 Practical governance framework
The combined evidence supports a sequenced governance strategy. First, sites should be
classified by sensitivity rather than governed by one citywide threshold. Second, high-impact
installations should be subject to dimming, operating-hour limits, safety and accessibility review,
and protected wayfinding. Third, permit ownership, authorization status, complaint channels, and
enforcement outcomes should be publicly visible. Fourth, transport, emergency, wayfinding, and
community information should be treated as public -value features. Fifth, data-enabled displays
should provide explicit notices, purpose limitation, opt-out procedures, and restrictions on biometric
or inferred targeting. Sixth, preference intelligence should be updated periodically so that governance
responds to technological and neighborhood change.

inputs into analyzed intelligence; priority rankings and the importance-performance map can be used
to disseminate information; and governance recommendations can be used to transform intelligence

process of permit assessment, the framework will facilitate a systematic comparison of proposed
installations. Feedback is given during monitoring in terms of the frequency of complaints, failures

capability, which is adaptive.

credible formats has the advantage of being more likely to maintain both the acceptance of the
people and the regulatory bodies in the long run. These are just suggestions that field validation
must be made, and are not assertions of long term performance seen.

congruence were also strongly related to preference, and the visual intrusiveness and the risk
of safety/accessibility had negative relationships. There was also a tendency of the scenario
comparisons to prefer integrated, secure, information-fomenting, time-restrained installations as
opposed to high-brightness, clutter-filled and sensitive-location setups.
The scientific advancement has been to bring together streams of streams which are usually
studied independently. The framework integrates the urban structure, sustainability, perceived
harm, legitimacy, institutional trust, scenario appraisal and governance prioritization on a single
analytical framework. It also clears up the difference between legitimacy and general preference
and demonstrates how the informational value, environmental sustainability, and transparency in
governance can impact preference via an ordinary institutional process.
Operationalization of complete and controlled intelligence cycle is the contribution to Competitive
Intelligence. The inputs of the environmental-scanning are resident evaluation and contextual
information; the inputs of measurement assessment are the quality of information; the outputs of
the interpreted intelligence are statistical and scenario analysis, the inputs of the priority maps and
scenario comparisons are the outputs of dissemination, and the inputs of the permit, design, privacy,
safety, and enforcement tools are the outputs of organizational action. The feedback loop is further
facilitated by monitoring, complaints, compliance information, appeals and repeated preference
measurement, and Intelligence Governance, which come with rules of provenance, access, ethics,
auditability, transparency, and accountability of decisions.
The implications to management involve how the city decision-making may be practically
organized. To ensure high-impact installations are approved through scenario based assessment,
the public managers ought to develop critical intelligence subjects on sensitive sites and emerging
technologies, have open permit and ownership registers, integrate planning and enforcement data
and apply scenario-based assessment. Controls on brightness and operating hours, safety and
access reviews, sensitive-site zoning, privacy notices, limitations on biometric or inferred targeting,
complaint process, and context-sensitive design requirements are not to be handled as individual
rules, but as a package of coordinated rules.
There are significant weaknesses in the evidence. The attitudinal associations are cross-sectional
and fail to build up temporal causality. Records that were provided were created to demonstrate
the methodology, and were not created by previously documented, probability-based recruitment.
The results thus have an analytical coherence and decision support potential although it cannot be
interpreted as population estimates of a particular city or country. Scenario outcomes are reported
acceptability, and allow decisions to be made not based on observed behavior, environment,
economic or safety impacts. A little separation was also present in the profile solution and it must
be approached in an exploratory manner.
