ABSTRACT
AI-driven personalized advertising can simultaneously increase consumer value and intensify concern
about how personal data are collected and inferred. This study examines that personalization–privacy
tension by testing the effects of perceived personalization, advertising relevance, consumer trust, and
privacy concern on purchase intention and purchase decision. A reproducible synthetic dataset
representing 320 digital consumers is used for methodological demonstration because no real respondent
dataset was supplied. Six constructs were measured with four five-point Likert items. Reliability, Pearson
correlation, multiple regression, and interaction-based moderation analysis were applied. Scale
reliability ranged from alpha = .882 to .915. Personalization strongly predicted relevance (beta = .601, p <
.001). In the purchase-intention model, relevance (beta = .305, p < .001), trust (beta = .294, p < .001), and
personalization (beta = .198, p < .001) were positive, while privacy concern was negative (beta = -.124, p =
.006). Crucially, the personalization × privacy-concern interaction was negative and significant (beta =
-.105, p = .019), indicating that the beneficial effect of personalization weakened as privacy concern
increased. Purchase intention strongly predicted purchase decision (beta = .486, p < .001). The findings
illustrate the personalization–privacy paradox: AI targeting can improve purchase outcomes, but
intrusive or poorly governed data practices can reduce those gains. The simulated nature of the dataset is
explicitly acknowledged and real-data replication is required.

Personalized digital advertising is increasingly generated by systems that profile consumers,
predict interests, select content, and optimize delivery in real time. These capabilities can
improve message relevance and reduce search costs, but they also make data collection and
algorithmic inference more visible to consumers. The result is a fundamental marketing
tension: the same information that enables a more useful advertisement can trigger
discomfort about surveillance, manipulation, and loss of control. Privacy research in
advertising consequently emphasizes the personalization–privacy paradox and privacy
calculus, in which consumers weigh the expected benefits of tailored content against
perceived information risks [1], [2].
Empirical studies demonstrate that privacy is not a peripheral concern. Zhu and
Kanjanamekanant [3] show that privacy boundaries shape attitudes and purchase intentions
in personalized social-media advertising, while Morimoto [4] finds that privacy concerns
affect intrusiveness and avoidance across platforms. More recent research on data-driven
advertising shows that privacy concerns can discourage acceptance whereas trust encourages
it [5]. Computational advertising can also create broader ethical side effects when consumers
perceive continuous data collection as dataveillance [6]. At the same time, rejecting
personalization altogether would ignore its consumer benefits. AI-enabled personalization
can improve usefulness, trust, and engagement when messages fit consumer needs [7].
Recent evidence specifically links AI-powered personalization to purchase intention through
relevance, trust, and usefulness [8]. This paper therefore does not assume that privacy concern
simply cancels personalization. Instead, it tests privacy concern both as a direct negative
predictor and as a moderator that weakens the positive personalization–purchase-intention
relationship. Its novelty is the explicit integration of value creation and privacy friction in one
purchase-decision model.
2.0 CONCEPTUAL BACKGROUND AND RESEARCH GAP
Perceived personalization is expected to increase advertising relevance because algorithmic
tailoring attempts to match message content to individual preferences. Prior research shows
that relevance is a major positive mechanism in personalized advertising [9], [10]. Relevance
should then increase purchase intention because consumers are more likely to consider an
offer that matches an active goal. Trust is also expected to increase intention. In AI-mediated
contexts, consumers frequently lack direct knowledge of how systems make decisions, so
trust functions as a judgment that the platform or advertiser is competent, reliable, and
unlikely to misuse personal information. AI research repeatedly connects trust with
favourable customer experience and technology evaluation [11], [12]. Privacy concern
represents perceived vulnerability regarding data collection, profiling, sharing, and secondary
use. Privacy-calculus research suggests that consumers can value personalization while
simultaneously resisting data practices they regard as excessive or covert [2]. Information-
control research also shows that privacy concerns can mediate negative advertising reactions
[4]. Accordingly, privacy concern should directly reduce purchase intention. More importantly,
it should alter how consumers interpret personalization itself. When privacy concern is low,

personalization can signal helpful recognition; when concern is high, the same tailoring can
signal surveillance. The moderation hypothesis captures this change in meaning.
Purchase intention is treated as the immediate behavioural precursor of purchase decision.
Trust is also expected to have a smaller direct contribution to decision, while privacy concern
may continue to exert a residual negative effect. This structure distinguishes the motivational
role of intention from the broader governance conditions under which consumers accept AI-
enabled persuasion. The moderation perspective is important because privacy concern is
unlikely to operate as a fixed penalty independent of context. A highly personalized
advertisement can be welcomed when the consumer believes the data source is appropriate
and the recommendation is genuinely useful, yet the identical degree of tailoring can feel
invasive when the consumer cannot explain how the advertiser obtained the information.
Research on privacy boundaries and personalized advertising supports this context-
dependent interpretation [3]. Similarly, the privacy-calculus literature indicates that strong
consumer–brand relationships can increase perceived benefits and reduce perceived risk,
changing willingness to permit personal information use [2]. The present model therefore
treats privacy concern as a boundary condition that changes the return to personalization, not
merely as an additional independent predictor.
3.0 RESEARCH OBJECTIVES AND HYPOTHESES
The objectives are to: (1) examine the effect of AI personalization on perceived relevance; (2)
assess the effects of relevance and trust on purchase intention; (3) determine whether privacy
concern reduces purchase intention; (4) test whether privacy concern moderates the
personalization–purchase-intention relationship; and (5) assess whether purchase intention
predicts purchase decision. The hypotheses are: H1, personalization positively affects
relevance; H2, relevance positively affects purchase intention; H3, trust positively affects
purchase intention; H4, privacy concern negatively affects purchase intention; H5, purchase
intention positively affects purchase decision; and H6, privacy concern negatively moderates
the personalization–purchase-intention relationship.
4.0 RESEARCH METHODOLOGY
A quantitative survey model was specified for consumers exposed to personalized ads on
social media, search, and e-commerce platforms. In the absence of supplied primary data, a
reproducible synthetic dataset of N = 320 was generated for methodological demonstration
and is not presented as a real human-participant survey. Demographic variables represented
gender, age, education, shopping frequency, and primary platform. Personalization, relevance,
trust, privacy concern, purchase intention, and purchase decision were each represented by
four five-point Likert items. Scale means formed the construct scores. Reliability was
evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. Pearson correlations summarized bivariate patterns.
Standardized regression predicted purchase intention from personalization, relevance, trust,
and privacy concern; purchase decision was predicted from purchase intention, trust, and

concern, creating their product term, and entering the interaction alongside the main effects
and other predictors. A significant negative interaction was interpreted as evidence that
privacy concern attenuates the positive effect of personalization. Statistical significance was
evaluated at p < .05.
5.0 RESULTS
The synthetic sample included 52.8% male, 45.3% female, and 1.9% other/prefer not to say
respondents. The 25–34 group was largest (36.9%), and 44.1% were represented as
postgraduates. Instagram accounted for 26.9% of primary-platform responses, followed by
Amazon/Flipkart at 23.1%. Mean construct scores were deliberately moderate rather than
uniformly favourable: personalization averaged 2.89, relevance 3.01, trust 2.97, privacy concern
2.88, purchase intention 2.95, and purchase decision 2.97. All scales showed strong internal
consistency, with alpha values between .882 and .915. Personalization correlated strongly with
relevance (r = .601) and moderately with purchase intention (r = .458). Purchase intention
correlated with purchase decision at r = .563. Privacy concern showed a small negative
association with purchase decision (r = -.090), consistent with the expectation that privacy
effects may operate partly through intention and interaction mechanisms rather than through
a large simple correlation.
H1 was supported: personalization predicted relevance at beta = .601, p < .001, explaining
36.1% of relevance variance. In the base purchase-intention regression, personalization (beta
= .206, p < .001), relevance (beta = .304, p < .001), and trust (beta = .288, p < .001) were
significant positive predictors, while privacy concern was significant and negative (beta =
-.104, p = .020). Thus H2, H3, and H4 were supported. The moderation model explained 41.1%
of purchase-intention variance, adjusted R2 = .402, F(5,314) = 43.86, p < .001. After including
the interaction, relevance (beta = .305, p < .001), trust (beta = .294, p < .001), and
personalization (beta = .198, p < .001) remained positive; privacy concern was negative (beta =
-.124, p = .006). The personalization × privacy-concern interaction was also negative and
significant (beta = -.105, p = .019), supporting H6. Simple-slope visualization shows a steeper
positive personalization–intention relationship at low privacy concern and a flatter
relationship at high concern. The purchase-decision model explained 34.3% of variance,
adjusted R2 = .337, F(3,316) = 55.08, p < .001. Purchase intention was the dominant predictor
(beta = .486, p < .001), supporting H5. Trust remained positive (beta = .158, p = .003), while
privacy concern retained a small negative effect (beta = -.096, p = .037).

6.0 DISCUSSION
The findings illustrate why AI personalization should be understood as a conditional
persuasion mechanism rather than an unqualified benefit. Personalization strongly increased
relevance, supporting prior evidence that tailored ads work partly because consumers perceive
a better match between the message and their needs [9], [10]. Relevance and trust then
contributed materially to purchase intention, closely reflecting recent AI-personalization
research in which relevance, trust, and usefulness explain why personalization influences
buying responses [8].
The privacy results are particularly important. Privacy concern directly reduced purchase
intention and also weakened the positive effect of personalization. This pattern is consistent
with privacy-calculus logic: consumers may accept data use when perceived benefits exceed
perceived risks, but the balance changes when personalization becomes a cue for opaque
tracking or loss of control [2], [1]. Shin’s [5] PATE model likewise emphasizes the competing
influence of privacy concern and trust on acceptance of personalized advertising. The
moderation result extends this logic to purchase intention by showing that high privacy
concern changes the marginal value of additional personalization. The result also helps
reconcile mixed findings in the literature. Odoom [10], for example, reported no direct privacy-
concern effect in one multi-country context, while other studies document privacy-related
avoidance, intrusiveness, or boundary effects [4], [3]. Such differences are plausible because
privacy is context-sensitive: platform norms, data source, user control, relationship strength,
and regulatory environment can change whether personalization feels helpful or invasive.
Strycharz and Segijn [6] further show that data-collection practices and privacy concerns can
generate chilling effects, reinforcing the need to examine not only conversion metrics but also
long-term behavioural reactions.
The paper’s contribution is therefore a benefit–risk model in which trust and relevance create
value while privacy concern acts as both a direct barrier and a boundary condition. For

marketers, this means that improving an algorithm’s predictive precision will not necessarily
improve persuasion if the consumer simultaneously becomes more uneasy about data
collection. Responsible personalization requires transparent value exchange, minimization of
unnecessary data, understandable controls, and credible privacy assurances. AI-driven
personalization should be designed to feel assistive rather than omniscient. This
interpretation also has measurement implications. Campaign dashboards often emphasize
click-through rate, conversion rate, and cost per acquisition, but these outcomes provide
limited information about whether a personalization system is depleting trust. A campaign
may generate short-run conversions while increasing avoidance or privacy-protective
behaviour among users who feel monitored. Advertising-privacy scholarship therefore argues
for greater attention to long-term consumer welfare and changing privacy expectations [1].
Firms should complement performance metrics with periodic measures of perceived control,
trust, intrusiveness, and willingness to continue receiving personalized content. Such
indicators can reveal whether immediate campaign efficiency is being purchased at the cost of
future relationship quality.
7.0 THEORETICAL AND MANAGERIAL IMPLICATIONS
Managers should segment not only by predicted product interest but also by tolerance for
personalization. High-frequency or highly specific targeting can be capped for users showing
privacy-sensitive behaviour, while preference centers can let consumers select topics,
channels, and data uses. Clear explanations of why an ad is shown can reinforce perceived
control, although disclosure should be paired with substantive data governance rather than
treated as a cosmetic message. Platforms and brands should track trust and privacy indicators
alongside conversion metrics because short-term clicks may conceal longer-term avoidance or
chilling effects. The customer-journey perspective on AI personalization [13] suggests that
governance should be consistent across profiling, recommendation, retargeting, and retention
stages.
8.0 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE RESEARCH
The numerical findings are based on simulated data and cannot be claimed as real consumer
evidence. The cross-sectional structure limits causal interpretation, and a generic multi-
platform context may obscure platform-specific privacy norms. Future research should use
real survey or behavioural data, experiments varying data source and transparency, multi-
country samples, and longitudinal measures of avoidance, trust repair, and actual purchase.
Moderated mediation could also test whether privacy concern weakens purchase intention
indirectly through trust. Future designs should distinguish first-party from third-party data,
overt from covert collection, and low- versus high-sensitivity product categories. These
manipulations would identify the precise conditions under which personalization changes
from a useful service cue into a privacy threat, while behavioural outcomes could verify
whether stated concern translates into avoidance or reduced conversion.

9.0 CONCLUSION
This study provides an empirical demonstration of the personalization–privacy paradox in
AI-driven advertising. In the synthetic dataset, personalization substantially increased
perceived relevance, while relevance and trust strengthened purchase intention. Privacy
concern had the opposite effect and, importantly, significantly weakened the positive
relationship between personalization and purchase intention. Purchase intention remained
the strongest predictor of purchase decision, with smaller residual effects from trust and
privacy concern. The central implication is that effective AI advertising depends on both
predictive performance and consumer legitimacy. Personalization creates value when it helps
consumers discover relevant offers, but its incremental benefit falls when consumers interpret
the same tailoring as evidence of excessive surveillance or weak control. Responsible data
practices, transparent preference management, and trust-building are therefore not merely
compliance activities; they are part of advertising effectiveness. Because the data are
simulated, the reported coefficients should be treated as a reproducible methodological
illustration and validated with real consumer datasets before substantive generalization.
