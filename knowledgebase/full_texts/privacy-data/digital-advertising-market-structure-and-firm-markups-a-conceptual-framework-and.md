# Digital advertising, market structure, and firm markups: a conceptual framework and research agenda

Abstract
Digital advertising can alter firm pricing through two opposing mechanisms. By informing consumers about
products and alternatives, advertising can increase residual-demand elasticity and compress markups. By
building customer capital, attachment, and switching frictions, it can reduce elasticity and support higher
markups. This paper develops a  conceptual  framework that  integrates these  mechanisms  with market
structure. We show that the advertising-markup relationship is theoretically ambiguous in levels, and we
show within a restricted framework that it becomes more positive as seller concentration rises. The key cross-
partial is not signed by qualitative channel assumptions alone because the curvature of the markup mapping
matters;  a  positive  advertising-by-concentration  effect  therefore  requires  explicit  comparative-static
restrictions, which we state and microfound by example rather than derive from primitives. We then identify a
set of measurement and identification problems that complicate empirical work on this question, including the
treatment of advertising inside SG&A, revenue-based production-function estimated markups, advertising-
to-sales ratios, listed-firm concentration measures, commercial advertising panels, and endogenous market
structure. Throughout we separate the economic markup, defined as the ratio of price to marginal cost, from
the production-function estimated markup that serves as its empirical proxy. The framework yields four
propositions and a research agenda for testing when digital advertising operates primarily as information,
when it operates as customer capital, and how privacy regulation and targeting technologies may reshape
downstream product-market outcomes. The paper’s central implication is that the economic effect of digital
advertising cannot be inferred from average advertising effectiveness alone; it depends jointly on consumer
information, customer attachment, market structure, and measurement architecture.
Keywords: digital advertising; estimated markups; market structure; customer capital; consumer information;
measurement; research agenda
1. Introduction
Digital advertising occupies an unusual position
in the economics of market power. The same
technology  that  can  make  consumers  better
informed  can  also  make  firms  better  able  to
identify,  reach,  and  retain  customers.  An
advertisement  can  reveal  a  product  that  a
consumer had not considered, expanding the set
of  salient  substitutes  and  increasing  price
sensitivity.  The  same  expenditure  can  create
familiarity, attachment, habit, or customer capital
that makes substitution less likely. These forces
push  residual-demand  elasticity  in  opposite
directions,  so  there  is  no  general  theoretical
reason for digital advertising to raise or lower
firm markups on average.
This  ambiguity  becomes  more  important
once market structure is introduced. Information
has greater competitive value when consumers
have many meaningful alternatives to learn about.
Customer  attachment  may  be  more  valuable
when fewer rivals are available to erode it. The
effect of advertising on pricing should therefore
be  expected  to  vary  across  competitive
environments rather than to appear as a single
stable average effect. This observation shifts the

Research on advertising effectiveness shows that
causal  returns  to  advertising  are  often  small,
heterogeneous, and difficult to measure (Blake,

Shapiro, Hitsch, and Tuchman, 2021; Gordon,

markups  documents  substantial  variation  in
measured market power while also emphasizing
the  sensitivity  of  production-function  markup

Traina, 2018; Bond, Hashemi, Kaplan, and Zoch,

and  data  shows  that  advertising  technology
changes when firms lose tracking information or
access to consumer data (Johnson, Shriver, and
Goldberg, 2023; Aridor, Che, and Salz, 2023;
Wernerfelt  et  al.,  2025;  Aridor  et  al.,  2025).
Recent theory has begun to connect advertising
directly to price competition and product-market
structure,  through  auction  design,  targeting,
information disclosure, and the macroeconomics
of digital advertising (Bergemann, Bonatti, and
Wu,  2025;  Greenwood,  Ma,  and  Yorukoglu,
2025; Pepall and Richards, 2025; Hwang, Kim,
and  Boleslavsky,  2026).  What  remains  less
developed is a common representation of how
firm-level  advertising  expenditure  moves
residual-demand  elasticity  through  opposing
channels,  how  that  net  effect  varies  with  the
competitive environment, and how the resulting
theoretical  objects  map  into  the  advertising,
markup, and concentration measures available in
firm-level data.
This  paper  addresses  that  gap.  The
contribution is conceptual rather than empirical.
The first contribution is to place informative and
customer-capital  effects  in  a  common  markup
representation  and  show  that  the  average
advertising effect is not signed. The second is to
derive the advertising-by-market-structure cross-
partial and make explicit that its sign depends on
both  channel  comparative  statics  and  the
curvature  of  the  markup  mapping,  so  that  a
positive  advertising-by-concentration  effect
requires  additional  restrictions  rather  than
following  from  qualitative  advertising  theory
alone. We state those restrictions transparently
and provide a primitive example that generates
them. The third contribution is methodological.
We show that the empirical objects commonly
used to test such predictions, namely commercial
advertising  measures,  production-function
estimated  markups,  advertising-to-sales  ratios,
and  industry  concentration  proxies,  can
mechanically  or  statistically  distort  the
relationships implied by the theory. The resulting
framework is a theory-to-measurement map and
research  agenda  rather  than  a  claim  that  one
reduced-form  interaction  identifies  a  unique
advertising mechanism.
The  framework  has  implications  for
competition  policy  and  firm  strategy.  Policy
discussions around digital advertising often focus
on competition inside advertising markets or on
privacy and tracking. The downstream product-
market question is different: whether targeting
technologies change the ability of advertisers to
sustain  margins,  and  whether  that  effect  is
stronger where competitive constraint is already
weak.  The  answer  cannot  be  inferred  from
advertising returns alone. It requires attention to
the  mechanism  through  which  advertising
changes elasticity, the structure of the product
market, and the way both advertising and pricing
outcomes are measured.

2. Positioning the framework
2.1 Advertising effectiveness and consumer
response
The empirical advertising literature provides a
strong  warning  against  treating  observed

advertising-sales  correlations  as  causal.  Blake,
Nosko, and Tadelis (2015) show that branded
paid  search  can  generate  large  observational

Lewis  and  Rao  (2015)  demonstrate  that  the
variance  of  consumer  purchasing  makes  even
large  advertising  experiments  statistically

Moakler,  and  Zettelmeyer  (2023)  show  that
observational  methods  can  miss  experimental

Braun  and  Schwartz  (2025)  further  show  that
divergent delivery inside advertising platforms
complicates  the  interpretation  of  randomized
advertising  experiments  themselves.  These
findings imply that detecting advertising-induced
changes  in  firm  outcomes  may  require  high
statistical power and careful identification. They
do not imply that any advertising-markup effect
must  be  small,  because  a  small  average  sales
elasticity does not bound the effect of advertising
on pricing incentives.
At the same time, the advertising literature
distinguishes  informative  and  persuasive
traditions.  Informative  advertising  expands
awareness  and  can  increase  substitution.
Persuasive  advertising,  brand  investment,  and
customer capital can create durable attachment.
Bagwell (2007) surveys these traditions, while
work on customer capital and intangible assets
emphasizes  the  persistence  of  consumer
relationships  and  their  potential  connection  to
firm  value  and  markups  (Morlacco  and  Zeke,
2021; Dou et al., 2021; Bronnenberg, Dubé, and
Syverson,  2022).  Throughout  this  paper,
customer capital is used as an umbrella term for
any  reduced-form  channel  through  which
advertising  lowers  residual-demand  elasticity,
including brand capital, habit, attachment, and
switching  frictions.  These  mechanisms  are
economically  distinct  and  can  differ  in
persistence  and  welfare  implications,  so  the
aggregation is deliberate rather than an assertion
that  they  are  interchangeable.  The  conceptual
contribution here is to place those mechanisms
inside  one  elasticity-based  markup  framework
and make market structure an explicit moderator.
2.2 Markups, concentration, and
measurement
The  markup  literature  has  documented  large
movements  in  production-function  estimated
markups among publicly listed U.S. firms, and it
has  also  generated  a  substantial  measurement
debate (De Loecker and Warzynski, 2012; De
Loecker,  Eeckhout,  and  Unger,  2020;  Autor,
Dorn, Katz, Patterson, and Van Reenen, 2020).
Production-function  approaches  infer  markups
from  estimated  output  elasticities  and
expenditure  shares,  which  makes  the
interpretation  sensitive  to  flexible-input
classification,  revenue-based  output  measures,
and  production-function  identification
(Ackerberg,  Caves,  and  Frazer,  2015;  Traina,
2018; Bond et al., 2021; Raval, 2023). How much
of the measured increase reflects pricing power
rather  than  cost  structure,  composition,  or
estimation  choices  remains  contested  (Basu,
2019; Berry, Gaynor, and Scott Morton, 2019;
Syverson, 2019; Miller, 2025; Benkard, Miller,
and Yurukoglu, 2025). Throughout this paper we
therefore distinguish the economic markup, the
ratio  of  price  to  marginal  cost,  from  the
production-function estimated markup that serves
as its empirical proxy. For the present question
this debate matters directly because advertising is
recorded inside SG&A and can be correlated with
the  same  revenue  and  cost  variables  used  to
construct the outcome.
Concentration  raises  a  parallel  problem.
Measures  built  from  Compustat  firms  are  not
equivalent  to  concentration  in  economically
defined product markets (Ali, Klasa, and Yeung,
2009;  Keil,  2017).  Benkard,  Yurukoglu,  and
Zhang  (2026)  emphasize  that  supply-side
industry  classifications  can  produce  trends
different  from  product-market  measures,  and
text-based product-market definitions can differ
again from classification-based ones (Hoberg and
Phillips,  2016).  A  concentration  index  should
therefore  be  treated  as  a  proxy  for  market
structure, not as direct evidence of market power,
and  regressions  of  price  or  margin  on  a
concentration index do not recover a structural
competitive effect (Miller et al., 2022). A further
point  matters  for  the  framework  developed
below. A Herfindahl index is not a one-to-one
function of the number of sellers once market
shares are unequal, since two markets with the
same number of sellers can have very different
indices and an index can move while the number

2.3 Targeting, privacy, and market structure
Privacy and targeting research establishes that
access  to  tracking  data  affects  advertising
markets  and  advertiser  performance.  Johnson,
Shriver,  and  Goldberg  (2023)  document
concentration effects following the GDPR in web
technology markets. Aridor, Che, and Salz (2023)
show that privacy regulation changed both the
size and composition of observable user panels.
Wernerfelt et al. (2025) and Aridor et al. (2025)
study  the  value  of  offsite  tracking  and  the
performance  effects  of  Apple’s  App  Tracking
Transparency. Related work examines how data
ownership  and  data-enabled  learning  shape
competitive advantage in digital markets (Hagiu
and Wright, 2023; Bergemann and Bonatti, 2024)
and surveys the economics of the platforms that
sell most digital advertising (Aridor, Jiménez-
Durán,  Levy,  and  Song,  2024).  These  studies
show that the information environment of digital
advertising is policy-sensitive, but they primarily
concern  the  advertising  market  or  advertiser
performance  rather  than  downstream  product-
market markups.
Theory also points toward market-structure
dependence. Pepall and Richards (2025) model
targeted advertising in oligopoly and show that
welfare effects depend on competitive structure.
Prat and Valletti (2022) connect concentration in
attention  markets  to  downstream  entry  costs.
Bergemann, Bonatti, and Wu (2025) show that
advertising auction structure can affect product
prices. Greenwood, Ma, and Yorukoglu (2025)
develop  an  information-based  macroeconomic
model in which digital advertising changes price
competition and the allocation of demand across
firms.  Hwang,  Kim,  and  Boleslavsky  (2026)
study competitive advertising and pricing when
firms choose how informative their advertising is,
and  show  that  competitive  intensity  shapes
disclosure. Cavenaile and Roldan-Blanco (2021)
and  Cavenaile  et  al.  (2025)  incorporate
advertising  into  growth  and  market-structure
models, including its role as an endogenous sunk
cost.  The  framework  developed  below
complements this work in two respects. It places
informative  and  customer-capital  effects  in  a
single  residual-demand-elasticity  representation
rather than modelling one of them, and it carries
the resulting comparative statics through to the
firm-level advertising, markup, and concentration
measures that empirical work must actually use.
3. A two-channel model of advertising
and markups
3.1 Advertising, elasticity, and the markup
Consider firm i selling a differentiated product in
market j. Let ε denote the absolute value of the
firm’s residual own-price elasticity of demand,
and let μ ≡ p/mc denote the economic markup,
that is, the ratio of price to marginal cost. Under
an interior single-product pricing optimum, with
marginal  cost  locally  constant  in  output,  the
Lerner condition implies that the markup is a
decreasing function of ε:
μ(ε) = ε / (ε − 1), ε > 1

common  residual  elasticity  is  a  reasonable
approximation.  Nothing  that  follows  requires
marginal cost to be globally constant, since the
first-order condition is local.

through two channels:

greater when consumers have more meaningful
alternatives. The term κ is the customer-capital
channel.  Advertising  can  create  familiarity,
attachment, retention, or switching frictions that
reduce the willingness to substitute. The return to
such attachment may be greater when fewer rivals
are available to erode it.

3.2 Market structure and the cross-partial
Let  n  denote  the  effective  number  of  sellers,
understood  as  a  scalar  index  of  how  many
meaningful  alternatives  consumers  perceive
rather than as a count of firms within an industry
classification.  Differentiating  the  marginal
advertising effect with respect to n yields:
∂²μ/∂A∂n = μ″(ε)(ι  − κ )Δ + μ′(ε)(ιₐ  − κₐ )ₙ ₙ ₙ ₙ
This  expression  contains  two  terms.  The
second captures how market structure changes
the net marginal advertising effect itself. The first
is a curvature term: a change in the number of
sellers  changes  the  level  of  elasticity,  which
changes  the  slope  of  the  markup  mapping.
Because this curvature term carries the sign of Δ,
qualitative  claims  about  the  information  and
customer-capital channels are not sufficient by
themselves to sign the interaction.
We  therefore  impose  three  transparent
conditions.  A1  retains  the  standard  markup
mapping. A2 requires both channels to respond
positively  to  advertising,  the  marginal
information effect to strengthen with the number
of sellers, the marginal customer-capital effect to
weaken with the number of sellers, and baseline
elasticity to be non-decreasing in the number of
sellers. A2 also implies ι  − κ  ≥ 0, which is usedₙ ₙ
in the proof in Appendix A when Δ ≤ 0. A3
requires the comparative-static effect to dominate
the curvature term where the information channel
already dominates.

4. Propositions and boundary conditions
Proposition 1. Ambiguous average effect.  The
effect of digital advertising on firm markups is
theoretically  ambiguous.  Advertising  raises
markups  when  the  marginal  customer-capital
effect  on  elasticity  exceeds  the  marginal
information effect and lowers markups when the
information effect dominates.
Proposition  2.  Market-structure
amplification  under  explicit  restrictions.
Under A1-A3, the marginal effect of advertising

Proposition  3.  Competitive-market
diagnostic. At low concentration, a negative
causal marginal effect of advertising on the
economic  markup  is  consistent  with
information dominance within the framework,
while a non-negative causal marginal effect is
more  consistent  with  customer-capital
dominance. This diagnostic is more mechanism-
informative than the interaction coefficient alone.
An observational advertising-markup coefficient
does not by itself provide the diagnostic, because
confounding,  measurement  error,  reverse
causality,  and  selection  can  each  generate  a
coefficient of either sign.
Proposition 4. Persistence and scale are
mechanism-consistent,  not  mechanism-
identifying. If  advertising  creates  durable
customer  capital,  stronger  future  revenue,
retention, or scale is a plausible accompanying
pattern.  However,  scale  growth  is  neither
necessary  nor  sufficient  for  customer  capital
because  attachment  can  support  price  premia
without quantity growth, and common demand
shocks can raise both advertising and scale.
4.1 Why the interaction does not identify a
mechanism
A  positive  advertising-by-concentration
interaction  can  arise  through  both  channels.
Information has less work to do when there are
few substitutes to learn about, while customer
attachment can be more valuable when rivals are
few. The interaction is therefore a joint prediction
of the restricted framework rather than a clean
test  of  persuasion  or  customer  capital.
Mechanism  interpretation  should  place  more
weight on the sign of the causal marginal effect in
relatively competitive environments, where the
channels  imply  different  predictions.  That
reading requires a design capable of identifying a
causal marginal effect, since the sign of a raw
panel relationship carries no such information.
4.2 Alternative mechanisms that can mimic
the propositions
 Correlated  demand  shocks: Advertising
budgets can respond to strong demand, and
demand  shocks  can  be  more  persistent  in
some  industries.  Advertising  and  markups
may  therefore  move  together  without
advertising causing the pricing change.
 Firm  scale:  Larger  firms  plausibly
advertise more in absolute terms and can
display  different  accounting  margins  or
estimated  markups.  Where  such  a  scale
correlation is present, it can create apparent
advertising-markup patterns if scale is not
modeled flexibly.
 Category maturity:  Mature categories can
be both concentrated and brand-advertising
intensive, making lifecycle differences look
like a targeting mechanism.
 Endogenous market structure: Advertising
can itself be an endogenous sunk cost that
sustains concentration, so concentration may
be an outcome of advertising rather than an
exogenous moderator.
 Accounting  and  specification  linkage:
Production-function  choices,  revenue
proxies, and cost classifications can create
advertising-related movements in measured
markups that do not reflect pricing behavior.
5. Measurement architecture for
empirical research
The conceptual framework is only useful if its
empirical objects are measured in a way that does
not manufacture the predicted relationships. Four
measurement  problems  deserve  particular
attention.
5.1 Measuring digital advertising
Commercial  digital-advertising  measures  are
typically constructed from panels of impressions
or placements, with expenditure estimated from
modelled rate-card or auction assumptions rather
than  audited  against  advertiser  accounts.  The
exact procedure is vendor-specific and channel-
specific, and it should be documented against the
vendor’s own methodology when an empirical
application is specified. Where estimation of this
kind is used, measurement error is unlikely to be

classical: it can vary with firm size, channel mix,
year, and sector, and it can affect both a main
advertising  coefficient  and  an  advertising-by-
concentration interaction in either direction.
Coverage  should  be  defined  against  the
vendor’s  actual  scope.  Search,  social,  video,
display, mobile in-app, and retail media can have
different observability. A generic label such as
’digital advertising’ can be misleading when a
major channel is systematically under-observed.
Historical backfill may also differ from true panel
stability, because a reconstructed pre-acquisition
history  can  be  internally  consistent  without
having  been  measured  contemporaneously.
Which of these applies to a given series is a
question for the vendor’s documentation rather
than an assumption.
Matching is an additional economic problem.
Advertising vendors organize records by brands
and  campaigns,  while  accounting  databases
organize firms by consolidated reporting entities.
Acquisitions,  divestitures,  franchises,  joint
ventures,  and  international  parent  structures
therefore require a time-varying brand-to-parent
crosswalk. True zero expenditure, vendor non-
coverage,  and  failed  matching  should  be
conceptually  distinguished  because  they  imply
different missing-data processes.
5.2 Avoiding denominator-induced
advertising measures
Advertising-to-sales  ratios  are  common  but
potentially problematic when sales also enters the
outcome, controls, or market-share measures. A
fall  in  sales  mechanically  raises  advertising
intensity even when advertising expenditure is
unchanged.  For  questions  about  pricing  and
markups,  the  preferable  primary  object  is
therefore  advertising  expenditure  or  exposure
itself, with flexible controls for firm scale. The
severity of the problem depends on the regression
structure rather than on the ratio in isolation; a
ratio is not automatically invalid, but it becomes
hazardous when its denominator also drives the
outcome, a control, or a market-share measure in
the same specification. Ratio measures remain
useful  as  alternative  normalizations,  and  they
should be reported as such rather than treated as
innocuous transformations.
5.3 Measuring markups when advertising sits
inside SG&A
The  treatment  of  advertising  inside  SG&A  is
often described as creating a direct mechanical
advertising-to-markup link. That description is
not  generally  correct.  Under  a  baseline
production-function  markup  in  which  cost  of
goods sold is the flexible input, the estimated
markup can be written as μ = θ × SALE/COGS,
where θ is the estimated output elasticity of the
flexible input. SG&A does not enter this formula
directly. Holding θ, sales, and COGS fixed, the
elasticity of the estimated markup with respect to
advertising is zero.
Indirect channels nevertheless remain. First,
advertising  can  raise  sales  without  a
proportionate rise in COGS; where this reflects
higher prices it is economically relevant, while
where it reflects operating leverage it is not a
pricing  effect.  Second,  θ  is  estimated  using
deflated revenue as a proxy for physical output,
so advertising that changes the mapping from
quantity to revenue can contaminate the output
elasticity. This connects the advertising problem
directly  to  the  revenue-based  identification
critique of Bond et al. (2021) and to the more
general  identification  concerns  in  production-
function  estimation  (Ackerberg,  Caves,  and
Frazer, 2015).
If SG&A is instead treated as part of the
flexible-input  bundle,  advertising  enters
mechanically  because  it  is  a  component  of
XSGA. In that alternative, μ = θ × SALE/(COGS
+  XSGA),  and,  holding  θ,  sales,  and  the
remaining components of the bundle fixed, the
elasticity of the estimated markup with respect to
advertising  is  −A/(COGS  +  XSGA).  The
mechanical sign is negative, not positive. The
result follows from the accounting construction
of  XSGA  and  from  the  stated  holding-fixed
conditions,  not  from  firm  behaviour.  This
distinction  shows  why  empirical  work  should
report multiple margin measures and explicitly
derive  where  advertising  enters  the  chosen
markup construction rather than rely on verbal
intuition.
Gross  margin  is  a  useful  corroborating
accounting  outcome  because  it  requires  no
production-function  estimation,  but  it  is  not  a
direct measure of market power. It is affected by

5.4 Measuring market structure without
focal-firm mechanicality
A conventional Herfindahl index uses the focal
firm’s own market share, which can mechanically
make  large  firms  face  higher  measured
concentration.  One  diagnostic  alternative  is  a
leave-one-out index of rival concentration:
HHIᴸᴼᴼᵢ  = Σ ≠ᵢ (S  / Σ ≠ᵢ S )²ⱼₜ ₖ ₖₜ ₘ ₘₜ
This  construction  excludes  the  focal  firm
from  both  numerator  and  denominator  and  is
therefore firm-specific within an industry-year. It
is  not  unambiguously  preferable  to  the
conventional  index.  It  measures  concentration
among rivals conditional on excluding the focal
firm, and it can behave oddly in thin markets;
with  a  single  remaining  rival  it  equals  one
regardless of the focal firm’s own share or of the
rival’s  absolute  scale.  The  practical
recommendation  is  therefore  to  report  several
concentration  objects  and  to  state  what  each
represents:  a  conventional  Herfindahl  index,
leave-one-out  rival  concentration,  the  number
and  the  effective  number  of  competitors,  and
alternative  product-market  definitions  where
feasible. None of these solves the larger market-
definition problem. NAICS remains a supply-side
classification,  listed  firms  omit  private
competitors,  and  concentration  is  not  itself
market  power  (Miller  et  al.,  2022).  Empirical
work should therefore describe such measures as
industry-concentration  proxies  and  triangulate
them, where possible, with text-based product-
market  measures  (Hoberg  and  Phillips,  2016),
Census  concentration  information,  or  other
market definitions.
6. Identification challenges and empirical
opportunities
6.1 Why standard panel regressions are
descriptive
Firm  fixed  effects  and  industry-by-year  fixed
effects  can  remove  time-invariant  firm
heterogeneity and common industry-year shocks,
but they do not remove time-varying firm-level
confounders. Advertising budgets may respond to
prior  sales  or  gross  profit;  product  quality
improvements  can  raise  both  advertising  and
price; and market structure can itself be shaped by
advertising. Lagging advertising does not solve
these  problems  when  advertising  is  persistent,
and  adding  a  lagged  dependent  variable
introduces its own bias in short panels (Nickell,
1981).
For  these  reasons,  a  panel  relationship
between  advertising  and  markups  should
normally be interpreted as an association unless a
separate source of plausibly exogenous variation
is  available.  Sensitivity  analyses  for  omitted
variables (Oster, 2019; Cinelli and Hazlett, 2020),
leads  and  lags,  and  documentation  of  budget-
setting processes can discipline interpretation, but
they do not convert an observational panel into a
causal design.
6.2 Privacy shocks as quasi-experimental
opportunities
Privacy  reforms  can  create  useful  variation
because  they  alter  the  technological  value  of
targeting. Apple’s App Tracking Transparency,
whose requirements applied from 26 April 2021
with iOS 14.5, is an example of a common-timing
shock  with  heterogeneous  exposure.  Firms
relying  more  heavily  on  identifier-dependent
channels  before  the  policy  should  be  more
affected  than  firms  relying  on  less  exposed
channels.  Such  exposure  is  not  automatically
exogenous,  because  pre-policy  channel
dependence can correlate with firm size, product
category, growth rate, platform dependence, and
contemporaneous shocks.
A credible design should separate treatment
validation  from  outcome  estimation.  Channel-
level data can first establish whether spending,
impressions,  or  advertising  prices  moved
differently  in  affected  versus  less  affected
channels within the same advertiser. Only after
showing  that  the  technological  shock  altered
advertising activity should a firm-level outcome
design  use  predetermined  exposure  shares  to
study pricing or markup responses. Even then, the
pandemic,  e-commerce  shifts,  and  product-
category trends remain important confounders, so

The design should also satisfy a set of explicit

and held fixed at pre-policy values. Event-study

Exposure-by-time controls are appropriate where
the exposure measure correlates with observable
firm  characteristics,  as  are  sensitivity  checks
against category-specific trends. Inference should
be  conducted  at  the  level  at  which  treatment
shocks  are  shared  (Cameron,  Gelbach,  and
Miller,  2011;  Abadie,  Athey,  Imbens,  and
Wooldridge,  2023).  Falsification  outcomes  or
plausibly unaffected channels should be reported
where  they  exist.  Where  exposure  changes  at
different  times  across  firms  or  channels,
heterogeneity-robust  estimators  are  appropriate
(Callaway and Sant’Anna, 2021; de Chaisemartin
and  D’Haultfœuille,  2023).  These  are
requirements for a credible research design rather
than a guarantee that the resulting estimate is
causal.
6.3 Why shift-share and market-structure
designs require caution
National channel-level advertising price shocks
interacted  with  lagged  channel  shares  can
generate shift-share instruments, but exogeneity
cannot be assumed. Advertising prices respond to
aggregate demand, and a small effective number
of  shocks  can  make  conventional  inference
misleading. Research using these designs should
make the economic source of shock exogeneity
explicit, report effective-shock diagnostics and
Rotemberg weights, and distinguish suggestive
from  causal  evidence  (Goldsmith-Pinkham,
Sorkin,  and  Swift,  2020;  Borusyak,  Hull,  and
Jaravel,  2022).  Where  exogeneity  holds  only
approximately, sensitivity methods for imperfect
instruments  can  bound  the  consequences  for
inference (Conley, Hansen, and Rossi, 2012).
7. Research agenda
The  framework  suggests  a  set  of  empirical
questions that are more informative than a single
average advertising coefficient.
When  does  information  dominate
customer capital? Future work should estimate
causal marginal advertising-pricing effects across
competitive  environments  rather  than  average
effects alone, reporting marginal effects across
the  observed  concentration  distribution  with
uncertainty  rather  than  interpreting  the
coefficient  on  the  interaction  term  by  itself
(Hainmueller,  Mummolo,  and  Xu,  2019).  A
negative  causal  marginal  effect  in  relatively
competitive  markets  is  especially  informative
because  it  is  difficult  to  reconcile  with  a
customer-capital-dominant interpretation.
Which  advertising  channels  build
attachment  rather  than  awareness? Search,
display, social, video, mobile in-app, and retail
media  differ  in  targeting  technology,  intent,
creative format, and consumer context. Channel-
level heterogeneity can therefore reveal whether
the relevant mechanism is discovery, reminder
advertising,  conversion  targeting,  or  brand
attachment.
How persistent are advertising effects on
pricing? Customer capital is inherently dynamic.
Research should distinguish short-run campaign
effects  from  advertising  stocks,  retention,  and
multi-year  revenue  or  margin  responses.
Distributed lags and advertising-capital models
can be useful, but persistence in advertising itself
makes dynamic interpretation difficult.
How  does  market  definition  change  the
interaction? The conceptual prediction is about
economically  meaningful  competitive
alternatives, not NAICS categories. Comparing
listed-firm  HHI,  text-based  product-market
networks,  Census  measures,  and  other  market
definitions can show whether an advertising-by-
concentration  pattern  is  genuinely  about
competitive structure or about classification.
How do privacy rules affect downstream
product  markets?  Most  privacy  research
measures  advertising  performance  or  ad-tech
market  outcomes.  A  next  step  is  to  examine
whether  losses  of  targeting  information  alter
product prices, margins, customer retention, and
market  structure  among  advertisers,  while
carefully  separating  treatment  validation  from
downstream outcomes.
Can advertising sustain concentration as
an endogenous sunk cost?  If advertising builds
customer  capital  or  raises  entry  costs,  market
structure is not merely a moderator. Dynamic

8. Implications for policy and strategy
8.1 Competition policy
The  framework  cautions  against  treating
advertising as uniformly pro-competitive or anti-
competitive.  Informative  advertising  can
intensify substitution by expanding consumers’
choice sets, while customer-capital advertising
can  soften  price  sensitivity.  Market  structure
determines  the  relative  importance  of  these
mechanisms.  Competition  analysis  should
therefore  ask  not  only  whether  an  advertising
technology improves matching, but also whether
it strengthens customer attachment or raises the
cost of effective entry.
The  same  distinction  matters  for  digital-
platform  policy.  A  restriction  on  tracking  can
reduce advertising efficiency without necessarily
increasing downstream competition. If targeting
primarily informs consumers, weaker targeting
may  reduce  competitive  pressure.  If  targeting
primarily builds customer capital for incumbents,
weaker targeting may reduce an advantage that
sustains margins. The welfare effect is therefore
an  empirical  question  conditional  on  product-
market structure and channel composition.
8.2 Managerial strategy
For  managers,  the  framework  implies  that
advertising effectiveness and pricing power are
not the same objective. A campaign can increase
sales  by  informing  consumers  while
simultaneously  increasing  price  sensitivity.
Conversely,  a  campaign  that  produces  modest
short-run sales may still be valuable if it builds
durable customer capital. Evaluating advertising
solely through immediate return on ad spend can
therefore  miss  strategic  effects  on  elasticity,
retention, and pricing.
Market structure should also shape campaign
objectives. In fragmented markets, information
and discovery may be the primary competitive
function of advertising. In concentrated markets,
retention, loyalty, and brand capital may become
relatively more important. These predictions are
not universal, but they provide a structure for
deciding  which  outcomes  should  be  measured
and which mechanisms should be tested.
9. Discussion
The central contribution of this paper is to move
the  advertising-markup  question  away  from  a
single  average  effect.  Once  informative  and
customer-capital  mechanisms  are  allowed  to
coexist, the sign of the average relationship is not
theoretically  meaningful  by  itself.  Market
structure  matters  because  it  changes  the
opportunity  for  information  to  expand
substitution  and  the  value  of  customer
attachment.  Yet  even  the  advertising-by-
concentration  interaction  requires  explicit
restrictions, both because the markup mapping is
nonlinear and because A2 imposes rather than
derives the direction of the channel comparative
statics.  This  distinction  is  important:  an
empirically  positive  interaction  would  be
evidence consistent with a restricted framework,
not proof that customer capital is the mechanism.
The  framework  also  shows  why
measurement choices are part of the theory-to-
data  mapping  rather  than  a  separate  technical
appendix. Advertising can be measured with non-
classical error, accounting markups can embed
revenue-based  identification  problems,  SG&A
classification  can  alter  the  mechanical
relationship  between  advertising  and  the
estimated  markup,  and  conventional  HHI  can
mechanically  incorporate  the  focal  firm.  Each
problem can generate patterns that look like the
conceptual  predictions.  A  credible  empirical
literature  therefore  needs  to  make  these
measurement channels visible before interpreting
coefficients economically.

There  are  important  boundaries.  The
framework  is  most  naturally  applied  to
differentiated product markets in which digital
advertising  affects  awareness,  matching,
attachment, or switching. It is less informative in
settings where advertising is purely informational
about price, where regulated prices dominate, or
where vendor-observed consumer advertising is a
poor  proxy  for  the  relevant  commercial
relationship.  Likewise,  industry  concentration
based on public firms is at best a proxy for the
effective  number  of  substitutes  perceived  by
consumers.
These limitations do not weaken the value of
the framework; they define the empirical work it
calls for. The most useful studies will combine
better  advertising  measurement  with  product-
market  definitions  closer  to  consumer
substitution, outcomes closer to actual prices and
contribution  margins,  and  quasi-experimental
changes in targeting technology. Such designs
can  test  not  only  whether  advertising  and
markups move together, but why and under what
competitive conditions.
10. Conclusion
Digital  advertising  can  make  markets  more
competitive  by  informing  consumers,  or  less
price-sensitive by building customer capital. The
same  expenditure  can  operate  through  both
mechanisms, and standard theory does not sign
their net effect. This paper develops a framework
in  which  market  structure  shifts  their  relative
importance and derives conditions under which
the  advertising-markup  relationship  becomes
more positive as concentration increases. Those
conditions are explicit restrictions on the channel
comparative statics rather than consequences of
the reduced form, and the paper states them as
such.
The framework yields a simple message with
demanding empirical consequences. Researchers
should  not  interpret  an  average  advertising
coefficient  as  evidence  for  or  against  market
power, and they should not interpret a positive
advertising-by-concentration  interaction  as
mechanism  identification.  Instead,  empirical
work  should  examine  marginal  relationships
across  competitive  environments,  derive  how
advertising enters the chosen pricing outcome,
use concentration measures that minimize focal-
firm  mechanicality,  and  exploit  changes  in
targeting technology only after validating that the
treatment altered advertising activity as intended.
The broader research agenda is to connect
advertising technology to downstream product-
market  competition.  Doing  so  requires
integrating  the  economics  of  information,
customer  capital,  market  structure,  and
measurement.  That  integration  is  necessary  if
digital advertising is to be understood not only as
a  tool  for  generating  sales,  but  also  as  a
technology  that  can  change  residual-demand
elasticity  and  therefore  measured  pricing
incentives.

Appendix C. A primitive example
generating A2
A2 is stated as a restriction on the reduced-form
channel  functions.  This  appendix  gives  one
simple environment that produces it. The purpose
is to show that the restriction reflects ordinary
economic reasoning rather than being imposed
for convenience, and to indicate when A3 is likely
to hold.
Consider  a  market  with  n  symmetric
differentiated  sellers.  Advertising  plays  two
roles.  First,  it  places  a  firm  in  a  consumer’s
consideration  set.  Let  φ(A)  be  increasing  and
concave,  with  φ(A)   (0,1)  over  the  relevant∈
support of A and interpreted as the probability
that a given consumer becomes aware of a firm
spending A. The number of alternatives to firm i
that a consumer considers is then proportional to
(n−1)φ(A),  and  residual-demand  elasticity  is
increasing  in  the  number  of  considered
alternatives.  Write  the  information  channel  as
ι(A;n) = γ(n−1)φ(A) with γ > 0.
Second, advertising builds attachment among
consumers the firm already serves. Let ψ(A) be
increasing and concave, and let the measure of
consumers  for  whom  firm  i  is  the  incumbent
supplier be proportional to 1/n under symmetry.
Write the customer-capital channel as κ(A;n) =
λψ(A)/n with λ > 0. Let baseline elasticity εW(n) be
non-decreasing in n.
These  forms  deliver  A2  directly.
Differentiating gives ιₐ = γ(n−1)φ′(A) > 0 and κₐ =
λψ′(A)/n > 0; ιₐ  = γφ′(A) > 0 and κₐ  = −λψ′(A)/n²ₙ ₙ
< 0; and ι  = γφ(A) ≥ 0 and κ  = −λψ(A)/n² < 0.ₙ ₙ
The economics is straightforward. Awareness is
worth more at the margin when there are more
products to become aware of, and attachment is
worth less at the margin when the incumbent base
is spread more thinly across sellers.
A3  is  a  separate  and  genuinely  restrictive
requirement.  It  holds  when  (ε−1)(ιₐ −κₐ )  >ₙ ₙ
2(ι −κ )Δ. Because the left side is increasing inₙ ₙ
the level of elasticity, A3 is easier to satisfy where
residual  demand  is  already  elastic.  It  can  fail
when Δ is large and the elasticity level is low.
A numerical illustration makes both points
concrete. Let φ(A)=ψ(A)=ln(1+A), γ=0.5, λ=1,
εW=4, n=5, and A=1. Then ι=1.386, κ=0.139, and
ε=5.248.  The  channel  derivatives  are  ιₐ=1.000
and κₐ=0.100, so Δ=0.900>0; ιₐ −κₐ =0.270 andₙ ₙ
ι −κ =0.374. A3 requires 4.248 × 0.270 = 1.147ₙ ₙ
> 2 × 0.374 × 0.900 = 0.674, which holds. The
cross-partial is then ∂²μ/∂A∂n = μ″(ε)(ι −κ )Δ +ₙ ₙ
μ′(ε)(ιₐ −κₐ ) = 0.0088 − 0.0150 = −0.0062 < 0,ₙ ₙ
so the marginal advertising effect on the markup
becomes more positive as the effective number of
sellers falls.
Lowering εW from 4 to 2 in the same example
leaves A2 intact but reverses A3, since the left
side becomes 2.248 × 0.270 = 0.607 against an
unchanged  0.674.  The  curvature  term  then
dominates  and  the  sign  of  the  cross-partial
reverses. The example therefore illustrates both
that  A2  follows  from  ordinary  economic
reasoning and that A3 is a substantive restriction
rather than a formality.
