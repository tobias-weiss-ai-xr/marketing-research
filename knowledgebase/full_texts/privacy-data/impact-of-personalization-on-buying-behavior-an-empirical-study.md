ABSTRACT:
Personalizedmarketing is characterized by targeting advertising and product recommendations based on customer data tomaximize e-commerce purchases. This
study examines the link between perceived personalization and buying behavior and compares the effect with brand trust and privacy concern, while controlling
for age and gender. Online consumers (n = 171) participated in a convenience sample survey that used five-point Likert scales (Cronbach’s α = .82–.86) to
measurethevariablesofinterest.Datawereanalyzedusingchi-squaretestsofindependence,one-wayANOVAwithTukeyposthoccomparisonsandsimpleand
multiple linear regression. Purchase frequency through personalized advertising was associated with age group (χ²(4) = 19.24, p < .001, Cramér’s V = .24), but
purchasing after a personalized advertisement was not associated with gender (χ²(1) = 1.04, p = .307). Buying behavior differed across age groups (F(2, 168) =
6.86, p =.001, η² =.076) andpurchase-frequency groups (F(2, 168) = 6.76, p =.002, η² = .075); post hoc follow-ups showed that 18–25-year-olds scored higher
than older respondents and that infrequent buyers scored lower than the other groups. Perceived personalization alone explained 45.9% of the variance in buying
behavior; adding brand trust andprivacy concern explained 55.6% (adjusted R²= .548), with personalization (β= .54) andtrust (β= .32)as positive and privacy
concern(β=−.13)asasmallnegative predictor.Thefindingssuggestthatbusinessesshouldemployrelevantpersonalizationandtransparentdatapractices.

analysis
1. Introduction
E-commerce, social media, and mobile applications have enabled firms to engage with consumers in new ways. Instead of disseminating a uniform
message toa large audience,marketers cannowusebrowsing andpurchasehistories todeliver targetedadvertisements andproductrecommendations.
Common examples include recommendations on online stores and retargeting advertisements on social media platforms. Personalization of this kind
aimstomakeadvertisingeffective,byreducingsearchefforts,whichisexpectedtoraiseconversionandretention.
Thesamedatacollectionraisesconcernsaboutprivacyandmanipulation.Consumersmaywelcomerelevantoffers,yetfeelthattheyarebeingwatched.
This tension is known as the personalization–privacy paradox: the commercial benefits of personalization are not uniformly positive and depend on
consumers’ trust and characteristics. The issue is particularly relevant in India, where mobile-based retail and online advertising are growing quickly
amongyoungerconsumers.

2. Literature review and hypotheses
2.1 Personalization and consumer response
Early work on website and email personalization shows that tailoring content to individuals can boost response. Ansari and Mela (2003) found that
customizingemailcontentincreasedresponserates,andTamandHo(2006)showedthatwebpersonalizationinfluenceshowusersprocessinformation
and make decisions. Bleier and Eisenbeiss (2015) found that the effectiveness of personalized ads depends on the interaction of what is personalized,
when and where, suggesting that relevance, not personalization, is key to response. Under the theory of planned behavior (Ajzen, 1991), advertising
thatencouragespositiveattitudesshouldenhancepurchaseintentionsand,ultimately,behavior.
2.2 Trust
Trust reduces perceived risk and uncertainty during online exchanges. Morgan and Hunt (1994) identified trust as a critical driver of relationship
commitment, and later work treats it as a prerequisite for consumers to agree to the use of their information. Consumers who trust a brand are more
likelytofavorcustomizedpromotionsbecausetheybelievethebrandhandlesdataethically.
2.3 Privacy concern and the personalization–privacy paradox
Awad and Krishnan (2006) described a personalization–privacy paradox in which consumers value tailored services but are reluctant to share the
information needed to provide them. White et al. (2008) found that highly personalized solicitations can provoke reactance, and Aguirre et al. (2015)
showedthatpersonalizationcanreducetheresponsewhenconsumersbecomeawarethattheirdataarecollectedwithouttransparency.Privacyconcern
isexpectedtomoderatetheeffectofpersonalizationonpurchasing.
2.4 Demographic differences
Youngerconsumers spendmore timeondigitalplatforms andaremoreaware of algorithmicrecommendations,sotheirexposuretoandacceptanceof
personalized advertising may differ from that of older consumers. Evidence on gender differences in online buying motivations is mixed. These
observationsmotivatethefollowinghypotheses.
2.5 Research gap
Most studies examine personalization, trust, and privacy separately, often using experimental studies in Western samples. Few integrate them in a
survey-basedmodelthatexplorestheself-reportedbuyingbehaviorofyoungIndianconsumers.Thisstudyfillsthisgap.

3. Research methodology:
3.1 Research design
Thestudyisadescriptive,explanatory,quantitativeinvestigationthatusesacross-sectionalsurvey.
3.2 Sample and sampling
Thepopulation comprises consumers who shoponlineandhave encounteredtargeted ads or recommendations. Anon-probabilityconvenience sample
of 171 respondents was collected using an online Google Form shared on social media and messaging platforms. Respondents were required to have
beenexposedtotargetedadvertisementsorrecommendations.
3.3 Instrument
Datawere collected with a structured questionnaire (Appendix A). Section A recorded gender, age group (18–25, 26–35, 36 and above), frequency of
buying through personalized advertising (rarely, sometimes, often) and whether a purchase had been made after a personalized advertisement in the
previous three months (yes/no). Section B contained 14 statements rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree) to
measurefourconstructs:PerceivedPersonalization(PER,4items),BrandTrust(TRU,3items),PrivacyConcern(PRV,3items)andBuyingBehavior
(BB,4items).Eachconstructscoreisthemeanofitsitems.
3.4 Analytical procedures
All analyses were performed in Microsoft Excel. Descriptive statistics and Cronbach’s alpha were used to profile respondents and assess reliability,
withα ≥ .70 taken as acceptable (Nunnally, 1978). Chi-square tests of independence were used to examine H1 and H2, with Cramér’s V as the effect
size and expected cell counts compared against the minimum of 5. One-way ANOVA was used to examine H3 and H4, with eta squared (η²) as the
effect size and Tukey HSD post hoc comparisons, computed from the group means and pooled within-group mean square, to identify pairwise
differences.Simpleandmultiplelinearregression(ExcelLINEST)wereusedtoexamineH5andH6.Themultipleregressionmodelis:
BB=β0+β1(PER)+β2(TRU)+β3(PRV)+ε
Multicollinearitywasassessedwithvarianceinflationfactors(VIF)derivedfromthepredictorcorrelationmatrix.EffectsizesfollowCohen(1988).

5. Discussion:
Perceived personalization is the main correlate of buying behavior in this sample, consistent with earlier evidence that relevance enhances advertising
performance(Bleier&Eisenbeiss,2015;Tam&Ho,2006).Trustaddsexplanatorypowerandprivacyconcernhasasmallnegativeassociation,inline
withthe personalization–privacyparadox (Awad &Krishnan, 2006) and reactance to intrusive targeting(White et al., 2008). Personalizationseems to
workbestwhenconsumersbelievetheirdataarecollectedandusedtransparently–thenextstepistotestthisinfuturework.
Thefindingssuggestthatbusinesses shouldembracerelevantpersonalizationandbetransparentabouttheirdatapracticestoenhanceconsumerbuying
behavior.Thiscouldenablefirmstoachievetheirmarketinggoalswhilealsorespectingconsumers’privacyconcerns.
5.1 Managerial implications
• Ensurethatinvestmentsprioritizerecommendationsthatappealtorelevantconsumers.
• Enhancetrustbyencouragingdisclosureofinformation,simplifyingconsentandpromotingopenness.
• Improve privacy practices byexplainingthe reasons behind eachspecific advertisement, and givingusers more control over how their data
iscollected.
• Personalizationshouldbeprioritized foryoungbuyers, butasofter approachisneeded forolder consumers, whomakefewerpurchases via
suchadvertisements.
5.2 Limitations and future research
Becausetheresearchused aconveniencesample of171participants andreliedon self-reporting,its abilitytogeneralize is limited,anditisvulnerable
to individual differences and recall and social-desirability biases. Since both the predictors and outcome were measured in the same survey, common-
method bias cannot be ruled out. The cross-sectional design means that the findings only reflect associations, not causal relationships. The buying-
behaviorscalemeasuresreportedbuyingtendencies,notactualbuyingbehaviors,andoverlapsincontentwithpurchasefrequencymayhaveartificially
inflatedtheANOVA results ofH4.Likert composites were treatedas intervaldata,andage-groupanalysis usedthree bandswiththeoldest containing
onlyn= 38respondents.Anyextensionoftheanalysiswouldneed toincludediagnostictestsoftheANOVA, regression,andassumptions(normality,
homogeneityofvariance,residualplots).Futurestudiesshoulduseaprobabilisticsample,includeadditionalvariables,suchasthetypeofplatformand
income,testthemediating/moderatingeffectsoftrust,andusestructuralequationmodeling.
6. Conclusion:
This study investigated the role of personalization, trust, and privacy concern as predictors of buying behavior using chi-square tests, ANOVA, and
regression analysis. Together, the three predictors explained 55.6% of the variance: personalization (POS) and trust predicted buying tendency
positively, while privacy concern (PC) predicted it negatively. Purchase frequency and buying behavior varied with age, peaking among 18–25-year-
olds but not differing by gender. Personalization, therefore, should be implemented carefully to maximize sales conversions by targeting the right
consumers.
