Abstract. Customized online advertisements can be comfy and embarrassing
simultaneously. A search may save a consumer time through a recommendation that comes
after a search. However, it can also indicate how much a platform has been tracking the
activity of that individual. This study employs a dual-path model, as the same advertisement
may have a potential benefit, relevance, and two potential costs: privacy issues and
intrusiveness. The evidence sources are 13 publications that were created in 2012-2026 and
were structured into a theory-driven evidence map; a questionnaire was not included. H1-
H7 were used practically as labels to sort findings. They were not tested on a new sample. A
number of papers associated personalization or targeting with increased relevance, attention,
positive attitudes, clicks, or purchase intention. This trend was not as favorable when data
collection was hidden, a personal sign was sensitive, or users had no control. Whereas there
are published results regarding the individual relationships postulated under H5 and H6,
none of the reviewed studies illustrate the entire mediation process to support the direct
effect. There is even less evidence to support H7. Age groups were also focused on Gen Z,
with none of the 13 papers designed for Gen Z, Millennials, and Gen X. The testimony was
such as to give a tentative response. Beneficial personalization might be reasonable, as long
as individuals can comprehend the data practices and do not find it overwhelming.
Keywords: Personalized Digital Advertising, Consumer Profiling, Perceived Relevance,
Privacy Concern, Generational Cohorts
1. Introduction
Think of seeing a pair of shoes on the internet on Monday, and the week proceeds with seeing some
version of the same advertisement. This is an experience with which I am well acquainted, and this
is the problem of this paper. The recurrent advertisement could save time since it can match an
already existing interest. It can also be disconcerting as it reveals that the site has a previous search,
click, buy, or even location. The traces are aggregated together into profiles that determine who gets
an advertisement. The advertisement is thus a sales message and an indication that profiling has

reduced prices [1]. The more appropriate the advertising fit, it does not necessarily imply the
outcome is better for the consumer.
This ambivalent result can be found in acceptance studies. According to a survey conducted by
McKee et al., 414 Gen Z consumers found out that personalization is beneficial, and privacy regards
both the brand response [2]. Hasrama et al. also address the topic of trust, transparency, and privacy,
but their inductive analysis is defined as hypothetical and not provided with p-values [3]. There are
some conditions that are more evident through experiments. Personalized advertisements increased
attitudes and purchasement intentions in the research carried out by Lamprinakos et al., whereas
clandestine information gathering of the identical kind of advertisement turned out to be more
obtrusive [4]. Tucker discovered that reactance would be mitigated and the response to clicks
enhanced through privacy controls [5]. A photograph of the participant was used in an eye-tracking
study, and 80 percent of the respondents reported that they were uncomfortable with such a
personalization [6]. These findings cannot be used to make a general assertion that personalization is
ether good or bad.
Profiling has another occurrence in a broader technical system. AI-driven advertising has the
capability to integrate information across websites, handheld devices, social media, customer
databases, travel histories, and feedback and then tailor an audience or a message of creation [7].
The Gen Z Indian consumers interviewed express curiosity and interest, fear, and suspicion [8]. The
practice is associated with privacy threats, and a review of targeted advertising reports that there is
no pooled meta-analytic effect [9]. Consent, choice, and continuing control is another interview
study [10]. The recommendation might be valued and yet disliked by a consumer in terms of the
way it was created.
The gaps in the field are indicated by other sources. Transparency and control are highlighted in a
review of 56 papers by Labib, but some privacy technologies have little evidence in the real world
[11]. Theoretically, Rhodes and Zhou demonstrate that the privacy decision of one individual can
have an impact on the offers presented to other consumers [12]. The review of 155 articles indexed
in Scopus by Ahuja et al. shows less digitally engaged consumers as under-researched [13]. This is
important to a generational study. Gen Z has useful evidence, whereas there is less similar evidence
regarding Millennials and Gen X.
The initial sketch posited whether these good and bad roads vary among three generations, and
the question remains in this paper. Published evidence is, however, the answer as opposed to a new
survey. This study used 13 sources and read them using the original framework and categorized each
suggested relationship based on the type of evidence that is factual. Some of the links are direct; the
rest of the links are conditional or indirect. The comparison of the three cohorts is not done. That is
why the answer and the point at which the 13 papers cease to be informative are reported in this
research.
Primary research question: How does personalized digital advertising affect consumer
acceptance, and how do the effects differ among Generation Z, Millennials, and Generation X?
Sub-question 1: How do consumer profiling and personalized recommendation mechanisms
improve perceived ad relevance?
Sub-question 2: How do privacy concerns and perceived intrusiveness reduce acceptance of
personalized advertising?

2. Literature review and theoretical framework
2.1. Consumer profiling and personalized recommendation mechanisms
Consumer profiling can be best understood as a series. A platform initially captures declared,
behavioral, contextual, or transactional information. It then aggregates those cues into a profile or
prediction, categorizes the user into a segment, chooses an advert, and it can vary its product, text,
image, timing, or channel. Mogaji et al. conceptual model explains how AI can facilitate these stages
[7]. It comes in handy to explain the mechanism, but alone it does not demonstrate that an AI-
generated ad alters acceptance.
The notions of targeting and personalization are similar but not the same. Targeting is who will
see the advertisement; personalization will alter the message or offer based on information about the
person receiving it. Tucker distinguishes between personalized copy and advertising, which is
simply targeted [5]. Malheiros et al. change the cue displayed in the creative itself, between a
holiday destination and age, name, and photograph [6]. Lamprinakos et al. differ in terms of
personalization and the delivery of the information either formally or monitored covertly [4]. These
mechanisms are thus coded independently in this review. They are also introduced to the same
upstream paradigm due to the fact that both of them utilize profile data to enhance the consumer-ad
fit; targeting evidence is not perceived as evidence that consumers found the creative itself to be
personalized. The designs indicate that consumers consider the implied data practice and the match
together.
Ahuja and colleagues put the algorithm in a broader framework of AI attributes, consumer
attributes, consumer reactions and attitudes, firm-specific decisions, and situational factors [13].
This is relevant to the current model. Acceptance is not directly determined in the profile. It alters
the message that the consumer is viewing, and the response is influenced by the relevance, privacy
interpretation, prior experience, and situational context.
2.2. Benefits of personalized digital advertising
The benefit pathway proposed is initiated with the perception of relevance. A message that aligns
itself with an existing task or preference can decrease the amount of search and also make the
advertized alternative more accessible to consider. Products that were targeted in the study by Mustri
et al. proved to be more applicable and offered more purchase intentions in comparison to random
products [1]. Lamprinakos et al. discovered that the attitude and purchase intentions were better with
personalized ads as opposed to non-personalized ads [4]. The cue used in the study by Malheiros et
al. was not only applicable in the task of booking, but it was also more comfortable than the highly
identifying cues [6]. The relevance is thus a positive aspect that the studies back, but not the
boundless use of data.
The result is also important. Attention, attitude, intent to buy a particular product, brand loyalty,
and actual consumer welfare are not synonymous. One can win the eye with a cue but not accept it,
and a purchase is not an intention. The first difference is presented by the condition of photograph in
the study by Malheiros et al.; the second by the price and vendor results in the study by Mustri et al.
[6, 1]. H1 and H2 are thus compared with the results of each of the studies, but not all positive
measures are considered as a common effect.

2.3. Risks of personalized digital advertising
The risk pathway starts with the revelation of more knowledge than anticipated by the consumer in
the ad. A covert collection, an identity signal that is sensitive, an inference that is surprising or the
lack of control may lead to a feeling of privacy intrusion and perceived intrusion. Covert collection
was used to increase the intrusiveness in the study by Lamprinakos et al. [4]. In the study conducted
by Tucker [5], weird personal information was more apt to lead to reactance when there was no
control of privacy. Names and photographs decreased comfort in the study by Malheiros et al. [6]. It
is not merely that data have been used, but what data have been used, how they have been acquired,
and whether their use is sensible in the task at hand.
The privacy calculus theory can be used to understand the reasons why there are benefits and
risks. Customers can choose to trade off the expected convenience with the expected privacy loss
instead of taking all personalization or none. This coexistence is directly supported by McKee et al.:
perceived personalization benefit was correlated with positive brand responses, whereas the privacy
concern was correlated with them in a negative way [2]. Interest, along with surveillance issues and
ineffective consent, were also reported by the participants of the interview [8, 10].
Agency alters the determination. The overt collection is more effective than the covert in the
study by Lamprinakos et al., and more privacy control reduced reactance in the study by Tucker [4,
5]. The subjects of the study by Roshith et al. gave consent, choice and control against the complete
depersonalization [10]. At the review level, Labib comes up with the same conclusion [11]. The
transparency and control do not eliminate all the concerns, but they provide the consumer with an
opportunity to comprehend the exchange and change it.
2.4. Differences among consumer groups
The original framework defines three cohorts: Generation Z aged 18-29, Millennials aged 30-44,
and Generation X aged 45-60. These are operational definitions for the proposed study rather than
universally fixed generational boundaries. A comparative analysis should hold the stimulus and
measures constant; otherwise, culture, education, platform use, income, or product category may be
mistaken for age.
The available evidence is concentrated in Gen Z. McKee et al. sample 414 U.S. Gen Z
consumers, and Peter et al. and Roshith et al. sample 40 and 20 Indian Gen Z consumers [2, 8, 10].
Hasrama et al. include adults aged 18-64 but do not report inferential comparisons among the three
cohorts [3]. The studies provide useful within-group evidence, including gender and privacy-
orientation differences, but they do not establish a generational ranking.
The corpus contains stronger evidence for psychological and situational differences. In the article
by Lamprinakos et al., emotion had an impact on the magnitude of personalization effects [4].
Responses to odd personal information were altered in the presence of privacy controls in the study
by Tucker, and comfort in the presence of cue sensitivity in the study by Malheiros et al. [5, 6].
These findings support consumer heterogeneity, while Ahuja et al.'s review confirms that older and
less digitally engaged groups remain a gap [13]. H7 is therefore posed as a non-directional
hypothesis, exactly as in the original outline.

3. Methodology
3.1. Theory-driven structured review and source corpus
This paper is a structured review and evidence map that is driven by a theory. It was predetermined
in its evidence base: the same 13 articles chosen in the assignment, all of which are from 2012-2026.
This study has read all the documents to the end and retained all 13, even in instances where a
certain source had poor methods or unreported information. No fresh survey, interview, experiment,
or recruiting of participants occurred. H1-H7 are no longer relevant as hypotheses that are tested on
a new sample. They are applied as questions, which assist in organizing the literature.
This cannot be referred to as a comprehensive systematic review. The 13 items were not based on
a new, reproducible database search and screening process. These are laboratory and field
experiments, cross-sectional surveys, qualitative interviews, reviews, a conceptual advertising
model, and a formal economic model. This diversity is helpful in making comparisons of
mechanisms, but it eliminates a traditional meta-analysis. The outcomes of fixation time, attitude,
clicks, purchase intention, loyalty, interview themes, prices, and consumer surplus differ and were
not pooled.
3.2. Construct boundaries and evidence coding
For each source, this research recorded its design, sample or document base, country, age group,
advertising mechanism, data collection method, outcomes, reported figures, and main limitation.
This research kept targeting and message personalization separate. Targeting concerns who or what
is selected for an ad; message personalization concerns how the creative is changed for a particular
recipient. Both fit the wider process of profile-based tailoring, but a targeted-versus-random
comparison only shows delivered relevance. It does not automatically prove that the consumer
experienced the creative as personalized. This research also treated overt or covert collection and
weak or strong identity cues as conditions of data practice and cue intensity.
These outcomes could not be treated as substitutes. A person may notice an ad without finding it
relevant, and may find it relevant without clicking or buying. This research therefore recorded
relevance, attention, clicks, and purchase-related responses separately. This research used causal
wording only for randomized or field-experimental comparisons. Survey coefficients are described
as associations. Interview findings are treated as participant accounts, while review papers and
models provide secondary or theoretical evidence.
3.3. Evidence-mapping rules and ethics
Figures that were included in the entire text were copied into the evidence map. In this study,
missing p-values or repair inconsistencies in a source were not estimated. This study predetermined
four labels of evidence before making a comparison of the studies. By direct convergent evidence, it
is meant that two or more independent empirical sources have looked at the said connection and
have indicated the same overall direction. The direction is said to be conditional when it is

contingent on a certain cue, practice of data, or environment. H5 and H6 require an indirect-effect

The research gathered no identifiable information, as only published or public material, which

4.2. Benefit pathway: evidence for H1 and H2
The literature has direct convergent support in line with H1, but does not blur the line between
targeting and message personalization. Mustri et al. carried out their research on 487 participants
who gave 1,461 links to ads of which 1,169 were found to fit the criteria and compared to matched
search and random products [1]. This is first-hand proof that the relevance sent by profile-enabled
targeting was delivered, rather than that participants felt that the creative was personalized. In Study
1 of Lamprinakos et al., personalized advertising boosted mean attitude and purchase intention by
1.18 points (to 5.22) and 1.44 points (to 4.81) on seven-point scales, respectively [4]. The cue of the
holiday-destination used in the study provided by Malheiros et al. is also a sign that the information
about a task may cause interest without the unpleasant experience like that caused by a photograph
[6]. The overall movement of these varying mechanisms of operation is a more fitting consumer-ad
fit, despite the fact that the targeted products, even in the work by Mustri et al., were still small in
terms of relevance and compared to the search condition [1].
Direct convergent evidence also exists in the literature in line with H2, the difference being
between intention and behavior. Relevance revealed the relative advantage of targeted compared to
random products in the Mustri et al. study and the personalized condition in the Lamprinakos et al.
study enhanced attitudes and purchase intentions [1, 4]. Tucker supplies the strongest behavioral
outcome [5]: 79 campaigns reached 1.2 million unique users and received 1,995 clicks; after
stronger privacy controls were introduced, personalized ads received a markedly stronger click
response. The field setting involved nonprofit advertising during a specific policy change, so the
magnitude should not be generalized to every commercial campaign.

4.3. Risk pathway: evidence for H3 and H4
The literature gives conditional evidence that is in line with H3. It does not demonstrate that all
personalization increases necessarily make privacy an issue. It demonstrates a more particular trend:
the risks are increased when the personalization is based on hidden gathering, atypical personal data,
or highly identifying signals. In Lamprinakos et al., the perceived intrusiveness went up to 4.00 with
covert collection as opposed to 3.32 [4]. In Malheiros et al. research, the age, name and photograph
comfort with holiday destination decreased to 4.0 to 2.9 [6]. These contrasts validate the direction
suggested under boundary conditions that can be identified.
H4 convergent evidence is given in the literature on multiple designs. Personalized brand loyalty
had a negative relationship with privacy concern [2]. In a study by Tucker, the abnormal personal
information produced a greater reactance when there was no privacy control [5]. The pattern of
discomfort in the research by Malheiros et al. is intrusiveness as compared to lower acceptability,
and the interview research by Peter et al. and Roshith et al. relate perceived surveillance, lack of
clarity in consent, and the absence of control to suspicion or resistance [6, 8, 10]. The qualitative
results do not have an effect size, but their direction is consistent with the quantitative results.

H3 and H4 do not account for an added cost due to consumer welfare. In the study by Mustri et
al., the average search prices were less by 10 percent lower than advertised prices [1]. The
advertised price was 7.75% lower than the average search price but 7.9% higher than the lowest
price on the first page of search results out of 609 identical products offered by various vendors. An
individualized advertisement can then be viewed and may not provide the optimal, readily
noticeable bargain.
4.4. Mediating pathways: evidence for H5 and H6
H5 is only indirectly supported in accordance with the mediation rule of the methodology.
Relevance is the mechanism proposed by Mustri et al. as the advantage of targeted over random
products [1], and Lamprinakos et al. demonstrate a chain from a personalized condition to more
positive cognitive responses, attitude, and purchase intention [4]. McKee et al. also find a positive
association between perceived personalization benefit and personalized brand loyalty to be strong
and positive [2]. These component connections fit into the suggested pathway, although none of
these reported findings support the indirect impact of personalization on acceptance via perceived
relevance in a single model. The review does not thus purport to have proven that mediation has
been proven.
H6 is also only indirectly supported. The data practice, intrusiveness, and lesser advertising
reactions are related to the covert-collection experiment in the study by Lamprinakos et al. [4]. A
randomized survey of 240 participants by Tucker demonstrates that the control of privacy decreased
reactance and enhanced positive response and click intention when atypical personal information
was involved [5]. More personal cues correlate with discomfort, as Malheiros et al. associate [6].
The links between the components have been consistent with the negative mechanism, and the
reviewed findings have no indirect effect of personalization on acceptance via privacy concern or
intrusiveness. H6 is thus a theoretically but not empirically validated mediation effect.
4.5. Generational comparison: evidence for H7
The corpus does not give enough comparative evidence on H7 since the common-design comparison
that would be needed is missing. The 414-subject design of the structure in the research by McKee
et al. just examines Gen Z [2]. Gen Z is also studied in the two Indian interview studies, and
purposive samples of 40 and 20 [8, 10] are used. They strengthen the data regarding a single cohort
but are unable to approximate the disparities in the strength of the paths across Generation Z,
Millennials, and Generation X.
Hasrama et al. cover adults 18-64 [3], which is not valid cohort-comparison evidence due to
broad age coverage. The regression analysis is clearly stated as hypothetical, p-values are not
provided, and no common-model estimates of the three cohorts are presented. These reporting
limitations would have precluded the study from bearing the inferential burden of H7 even if age-
stratified descriptive results had been reported. The evidence gap is thus demonstrated largely due to
the Gen Z-only focus of McKee et al., Peter et al., and Roshith et al. complemented by the overall
review of Ahuja et al. [2, 8, 10, 13].
In-group and situational differences are, however, discernible. A higher level of privacy concern
was reported among women as compared to men in the study by McKee et al. (4.10 vs 3.83, p <.01),
and benefit and concern went hand in hand in the same Gen Z sample [2]. A study conducted by
Lamprinakos et al.modified the effect of personalization and the collection method by emotion [4].
Privacy control was different in the study of Tucker and sensitivity of a cue was different in the

5. Discussion
5.1. The dual-path model and consumer acceptance
The primary pattern of the evidence map can be used as a conditional response to the primary
research question. When personalised digital advertising produces a useful match, acceptance can be
increased, but when it produces a sense of concealment, excessiveness, or uncontrollability, the
same process can lead to a decrease in acceptance. The pathways of benefits and risks do not offset
before consumer response. These are two different assessments of the same experience: Was it
useful, and was the platform entitled to know this?
This takeaway explains a seeming inconsistency of results. Respondents in Gen Z will be able to
enjoy the benefits of personalization, but privacy concerns remain a predictor of brand response [2].
Curiosity and fear can be reported simultaneously by the participants of the interview [8]. Without
requesting the total elimination of personalized advertising, consumers are allowed to object to data
practices [10]. Relevance is not, hence, evidence of trust, and privacy concern is neither evidence
that the recommendation is of no use.
5.2. What the evidence says about generational cohorts
The initial scheme rightly considers cohort difference to be an empirical query and not an empirical
assumption. The literature reviewed advocates the significance of consumer heterogeneity, but not a

The same advertisements and measures were not provided to the older consumers. A cross-study
comparison might thus cause confusion between age and culture, numerical literacy, career,
experience with the platform, or style of products.
The lack of a direct comparison does not make the cohort focus irrelevant. It determines the exact
point where the literature ends. Direct and conditional evidence discusses some sections of the
benefit and risk pathways, but the literature is silent on how these factors are moderated by
Generation Z, Millennials, and Generation X. This is an evidence-map gap, rather than a result that
the cohorts are the same. H7 is not removed, but it is retained and goes as having inadequate
comparative evidence.
5.3. Theoretical and managerial implications
Theoretically, the results can be used to support a privacy-calculus model together with the addition
of agency as a boundary condition. An advantage-risk calculation modifies when the user is able to
comprehend the data source, modify permissions, or decline a type of personalization. It was not
only a procedural requirement in the study by Tucker and overt collection in the study by
Lamprinakos et al. to control the response to the advertisement [5, 4].
When it comes to advertisers, precision ought to imply the minimum of personal information
required to make a useful matching. The holiday-destination result reported by Malheiros et al.
indicates that the information that is relevant to the task can be useful without the identity reveal [6].
The explanation of data collection should be short and simple, and permission should be revocable.
A user who desires less tracking should be given a true low-personalization choice, whereas a user
who does not mind being tailored should be given a choice of what types of data are used.
Evaluation of campaigns should also not be focused on click-through rate. Relevance, attention,
attitude, click intention, and purchase intention are important; however, opt-outs, complaints,
intrusiveness, vendor quality, price competitiveness, and longer-term trust are also important. The
reason why is in the welfare information in the study by Mustri et al.: the well-crafted ad can
promote a visible offer that is not superior even though it is below the superior [1].
5.4. Limitations
There are four limitations of this study. First, the evidence base of this review was pre-determined
by the 13 documents instead of being identified by using a new reproducible database search and
screening protocol; the review is thus organized and constrained, not comprehensive. Second, the
sources incorporate various designs and results, and a pooled effect is invalid. Third, the cohort
evidence is also focused on Gen Z, U.S. university samples and informed urban Indian participants.
Lastly, the sources are not of the same quality. A conference draft is defined as replicated as in the
study by Mustri et al., part of the inferential analysis is described as hypothetical as in the study by
Hasrama et al., and there are conflicting counts of studies reported but are not pooled as defined in
the study by Ali et al. [1, 3, 7, 12, 9].
The new proposition-level classifications can be interpreted as an evidence map, but not as a
replacement for a new three-group statistical test. They indicate the points of convergence between
independent findings, and that only component associations or conditioning patterns have been
reported, with missing evidence. The operationalizations of profile-enabled tailoring in the source
studies are also different: targeting an action of selecting a recipient or a product, and message
personalization is an action of modifying the creative. The simultaneous mapping of both

6. Conclusion
The evidence map widely concurs with the main reasoning of the initial sketch. Perceived relevance
can be enhanced by consumer profiling and recommendations, relevance can be used to enhance
advertising acceptance and purchase intention. Intrusiveness and privacy concerns may also be
caused by personalization when data collection is covert, the cue is strongly identifying, or when the
user has no control. Direct convergent evidence is present in H1, H2 and H4, whereas H3 is covered
by conditional evidence. H5 and H6 are indirectly evidenced to have component links, yet the
reviewed results are not a formal mediation.
The cohort question is not determined. The articles, however, indicate that the personalization-
privacy conflict is present among Gen Zers. They also complain about gender, emotion, and
sensitivity of cues as well as privacy control differences. No one puts Gen Z, Millennials, and Gen X
in an equal situation. For that reason, H7 is registered on the list of gaps in comparative evidence
instead of a conclusion on the absence of cohort difference. Practically speaking, the most sound
lesson is quite meek. The amount of personal data shown in the ad should be as little as the match
requires, the benefit must be visible, the reason why the ad was displayed should be mentioned, and
the user should be able to alter the permission.
