ABSTRACT: The rapid expansion of social media has transformed the production, circulation and consumption of information
and has fundamentally altered the nature of public discourse in India. Social media platforms are no longer merely passive
intermediaries that host user -generated content. Through recommender systems, ranking mechanisms, automated content
moderation, profiling and targeted advertising, they increasingly influence what users encounter, what information gains visibility
and what forms of speech are amplified or marginalised. This transformation raises a regulatory question that extends beyond
conventional content moderation: to what extent should platforms be accountable for the design and consequences of algorithmic
systems that structure digital communication and public discourse?
India’s regulatory framework, principally comprising the Information Technology Act, 2000 and the Information Technology
(Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics C ode) Rules, 2021, as subsequently amended, has progressively expanded
intermediary due-diligence, content moderation and grievance-redressal obligations. However, the regulatory framework remains
substantially oriented towards intermediary liability and co ntent governance rather than the systemic risks associated with
algorithmic curation, recommendation and amplification. In particular, comprehensive and enforceable requirements relating to
algorithmic transparency, systemic -risk assessment, independent au diting, recommender -system governance, researcher access
and algorithmic impact assessment remain underdeveloped.
This paper examines the transition from content -centred regulation towards algorithmic accountability in India. It adopts a
qualitative, doctr inal and comparative methodology, analysing legislation, subordinate legislation, constitutional principles,
judicial decisions, policy documents and scholarly literature. India’s emerging framework is comparatively examined against t he
European Union’s Digital Services Act and the comparatively fragmented and platform-led regulatory model of the United States.
The paper argues that India’s principal regulatory challenge is not simply a deficiency of content moderation but a regulator y
mismatch between conv entional intermediary regulation and the increasingly systemic power exercised by algorithmic
infrastructures. It proposes a rights -based, risk -sensitive and proportionate framework centred on meaningful transparency,
algorithmic impact assessment, independent auditing, systemic-risk mitigation, procedural safeguards, researcher access, effective
user redress and independent institutional oversight. Such a framework should regulate demonstrable risks rather than
technological innovation itself, thereby maintaining an appropriate balance between platform accountability, democratic freedom,
privacy, innovation and freedom of expression.

INTRODUCTION
Social media platforms have become central infrastructures of contemporary communication, information exchange and public
discourse. In India, their significance extends well beyond interpersonal communication. Social media increasingly influences
political communication, electoral mobilisation, news consumption, public opinion, commercial advertising, social movements
and the formation of digital communities. The transformation is therefore not merely quantitative, reflected in the growing number
of users and volume of content, but also qualitative. The architecture through which information reaches users has itself bec ome
algorithmically mediated.
Contemporary social media platforms do not simply provide a technological space in which users communicate. They determine,
to varying degrees, which content becomes visible, the order in which it appears, the users to whom it is recommended and the
extent to which it is amplified or deprioritised. Recommender systems, ranking algorithms, personalised feeds, automated

moderation tools and advertising systems have therefore become important mechanisms through which platforms exercise social
and communicative power.
This development creates a significant challenge for conventional approaches to intermediary regulation. Traditional intermediary
regulation is primarily concerned with questions such as whether a platform should be legally responsible for unlawful third-party
content, whether it has complied with prescribed due -diligence obligations and whether it has appropriately responded to
complaints or takedown requests. Algorithmic accountability raises a broader question: how should platforms be held
responsible for the technological systems through which they determine the visibility, ranking, recommendation,
amplification or suppression of information?
The distinction is important because the influence of platforms is not confined to content that they remove. A platform may comply
with content-removal requirements while its recommender system simultaneously gives disproportionate visibility to particular
categories of information. Similarly, content may remain technically available but receive substantially reduced visibility because
of ranking or recommendation decisions. Consequently, regulation that focuses exclusively on the legality of individual items  of
content may overlook the systemic effects produced by the architecture through which information is  distributed.
India has developed a substantial legal and regulatory framework governing intermediaries. Section 79 of the Information
Technology Act, 2000 provides conditional protection to intermediaries from liability for third -party information, subjec t to
specified statutory conditions. The Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021
subsequently expanded the due -diligence and compliance framework applicable to intermediaries and significant social media
intermediaries. The framework has continued to evolve through subsequent amendments and policy initiatives.
These developments demonstrate that India does not lack regulation. The more significant concern is that the regulatory framework
has historically be en organised primarily around intermediary liability, unlawful content, due diligence and procedural
compliance, while comparatively less attention has been devoted to the systemic risks generated by algorithmic recommendation,
ranking and amplification.
The regulatory challenge is particularly important in the Indian context. India’s digital public sphere operates within a high ly
diverse social environment characterised by linguistic plurality, regional differences, socio-economic inequality, caste and gender
hierarchies, political contestation and varying levels of digital literacy. Algorithmic systems designed around engagement,
personalisation and behavioural prediction may interact with these structural inequalities in ways that are difficult to detect through
conventional content-moderation mechanisms.
Algorithmic accountability therefore has both a technological and a constitutional dimension. It concerns transparency and
explainability, but also equality, freedom of expression, privacy, procedural fai rness and democratic participation. The question
is not whether algorithms are inherently harmful. Algorithms can improve information discovery, accessibility and user
experience. The regulatory concern arises when their design, operation or consequences create significant and foreseeable risks to
individuals or society without adequate mechanisms of scrutiny and redress.
The European Union’s Digital Services Act (DSA) represents an important development in this regard. Rather than focusing
exclusively on individual items of unlawful content, the DSA adopts a risk -based approach for the largest platforms and search
engines, including obligations concerning systemic -risk assessment, mitigation, independent auditing, transparency and
recommender systems. The European framework therefore provides a useful comparative reference for examining how platform
responsibility can move beyond conventional intermediary liability.
The United States presents a substantially different model. Its regulatory environment combin es strong constitutional protection
for speech, intermediary liability protections, sectoral regulation, litigation and extensive platform self -governance. This model
provides an important counterpoint because it highlights the risks of excessive state intervention and the importance of preserving
private editorial autonomy, while also illustrating the limitations of fragmented regulation.
Against this background, this paper examines the following central question:
To what extent does India’s existing socia l media regulatory framework provide meaningful algorithmic accountability,
and what legal and institutional reforms are required to ensure responsible platform governance while protecting
innovation and fundamental rights?
The paper argues that India shou ld move from a predominantly content-moderation paradigm towards a risk -based
algorithmic-accountability paradigm . This does not require direct governmental control over algorithms or compulsory
disclosure of proprietary source code. Rather, it requires legally enforceable mechanisms through which platforms with substantial
systemic impact can be required to identify, assess, mitigate and demonstrate accountability for algorithmic risks.
Understanding Algorithmic Accountability
Meaning and Scope
Algorithmic accountability refers broadly to the mechanisms through which organisations deploying consequential algorithmic
systems can be required to explain, justify, monitor and take responsibility for the operation and consequences of those syst ems.

The concept should not, however, be reduced to transparency. Transparency is an important component of accountability, but
disclosure by itself does not guarantee fairness, intelligibility or effective oversight. An algorithm may be disclosed without being
understandable to ordinary users or independently assessable by regulators and researchers.
Algorithmic accountability therefore incorporates at least five interconnected dimensions:
1. Transparency: disclosure of meaningful information concerning the purpose, operation  and governance of algorithmic
systems.
2. Explainability: the capacity to provide intelligible reasons for significant automated decisions or outcomes.
3. Auditability: the possibility of independent assessment of relevant systems, processes and outcomes.
4. Fairness and non-discrimination: identification and mitigation of discriminatory or systematically unequal outcomes.
5. Remedial accountability:  effective mechanisms through which affected persons can challenge decisions and obtain
appropriate remedies.
In the context of social media, these dimensions become particularly important because platforms exercise power not only through
removal but also through distribution.
A conventional content -moderation framework asks whether content should be permitted or removed. An algorithmic -
accountability framework asks an additional set of questions:
• Why was particular content recommended?
• Why was it shown to one group of users and not another?
• What factors determine its ranking?
• What incentives are embedded in the optimisation process?
• What risks arise from repeated amplification?
• Can users understand and challenge consequential decisions?
• Can independent actors verify the platform’s claims?
These questions shift the regulatory focus from individual pieces of content towards the technological architecture of information
distribution.
Algorithmic Governance and Governance of Algorithms
A distinction should also be made between algorithmic governance and governance of algorithms.
Algorithmic governance refers to the use of computational systems to organise information, make predictions, classify users and
assist or determine decisions.
Governance of algorithms refers to the legal, institutional and ethical mechanisms through which such systems are regulated.
This paper is primarily concerned with the latter. Its objective is not to determine how platforms should technically construct their
algorithms but to examine what accountability obligations should apply when algorithmic systems have significant implications
for rights and public interests.
Platform Governance and the Changing Nature of Intermediary Power
The traditional concept of an intermediary assumes a relatively passive technological actor connecting users to information.
Contemporary platforms complicate this assumption.
Platforms establish community standards, determine content visibility, design interfaces, curate feeds, recommend content,
personalise advertisements, suspend accounts and employ automated systems to enforce their rules. They therefore perform
functions that resemble a combination of technological infrastructure, private regulation and media curation.
This development has generated the concept of platform governance. Platforms govern not only through explicit rules but also
through technical des ign. Code, ranking systems, default settings, recommendation mechanisms and interface architecture can
influence user behaviour as effectively as formal rules.
Algorithmic systems are consequently part of the institutional structure through which platforms  exercise power.
The significance of this development becomes clearer when the distinction between hosting and curation is considered.
A platform that merely stores content occupies a different regulatory position from a platform that actively selects, ran ks and
recommends content to particular users. Recommendation involves an additional layer of decision -making. The platform is not
merely transmitting information; it is constructing an informational environment.
The more extensive this role becomes, the stronger the case for corresponding accountability obligations.
This does not mean that platforms should automatically lose intermediary protections whenever they use algorithms. Such an
approach would risk creating uncertainty and could undermine legitima te technological innovation. Instead, the regulatory
framework should distinguish between different categories of platform activity and impose obligations proportionate to system ic
risk.

REVIEW OF LITERATURE
Platform Governance

The literature on platform governance demonstrates that digital platforms should not be understood merely as neutral
intermediaries. Gorwa’s work conceptualises platform governance as an interaction between private platform rules, state
regulation and broader social norms.
This literature is important because it establishes that platforms exercise normative authority. Their community standards an d
enforcement systems determine the conditions under which speech can circulate.
The implication for India is that regulatory analysis cannot stop at the question of intermediary liability. It must also consider how
private platform rules and technical architectures interact with public values.
Algorithmic Accountability
The literature on algorithmic accountability c hallenges the assumption that algorithmic decision -making is inherently neutral or
objective. Algorithms embody choices concerning datasets, optimisation objectives, classifications and institutional prioriti es.
Binns has emphasised the relationship between algorithmic accountability and public reason, while Ananny and Crawford caution
against treating transparency as an adequate solution to algorithmic opacity. Their work suggests that meaningful accountabil ity
requires more than technical disclosure. It r equires institutions capable of interpreting information, evaluating consequences and
providing remedies.
This insight is particularly relevant to social media governance. A platform’s publication of a general description of its
recommender system may satisfy a limited conception of transparency without enabling meaningful scrutiny of systemic harms.
Automated Content Moderation
Research on algorithmic content moderation highlights the tension between the scale of online communication and the limitations
of automated decision -making. Platforms process enormous quantities of content, making complete human moderation
impractical. Automation consequently becomes operationally attractive.
However, automated moderation can struggle with context -dependent expression, satire, political speech, cultural references and
multilingual communication. Errors can disproportionately affect marginalised communities whose languages, experiences and
modes of expression may be inadequately represented in training datasets.
The problem therefore has an important equality dimension. Algorithmic accountability must address not only whether platforms
make mistakes but whether such errors are distributed unevenly across social groups.
Digital Rights and Freedom of Expression
The literature on online speech demonstrates that platform regulation presents a difficult balance between addressing harmful
content and protecting freedom of expression.
David Kaye’s work emphasises the importance of human-rights principles in regulating online platforms. Napoli similarly argues
for reconsidering social media governance in the context of disinformation and public -interest communication.
This scholarship is particularly relevant to India because regulation of social media operates within a constitu tional framework
protecting freedom of speech and expression while permitting reasonable restrictions.
The challenge is therefore not to maximise regulation but to design proportionate and procedurally fair regulation.
Surveillance, Data and Platform Power
Zuboff’s analysis of surveillance capitalism provides another dimension to the debate. Platforms increasingly rely on large -scale
data collection, behavioural prediction and personalised advertising. Algorithmic governance is therefore closely connected to
data governance.
The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 adds an important layer to India’s regulatory architecture by establishing statutory
principles concerning the processing of digital personal data. However, data protection and algorithmic accountability should not
be treated as identical. A platform may comply with data-processing requirements while its recommendation system creates other
forms of systemic harm.
Research Gap
The existing scholarship provides substantial conceptual foundatio ns for understanding platform governance, algorithmic
accountability and digital rights. However, an important gap remains in connecting these debates to India’s evolving intermediary
regulatory framework.
Indian regulatory discussions have predominantly concentrated on intermediary liability, content removal, due diligence and
grievance redressal. Comparatively less attention has been given to the systems through which platforms determine the
distribution, ranking and amplification of content.
The present study addresses this gap by conceptualising algorithmic accountability as a distinct regulatory layer and examining
how India can move from content-centred regulation towards systems-level platform governance.

India’s Existing Legal Framework
Information Technology Act, 2000
The Information Technology Act, 2000 remains the principal statutory foundation of India’s digital regulatory framework.
Section 79 provides conditional protection to intermediaries from liability for third-party information, data or communication links
made available through their systems, subject to specified conditions and due -diligence requirements.
The provision is significant because intermediary protection has historically been central to India’s approach to platform
regulation. It establishes an important balance between protecting intermediaries from automatic liability and requiring them to
meet specified statutory conditions.
However, Section 79 is not an algorithmic-accountability provision. It does not establish a comprehensive framework for assessing
recommender systems, algorithmic amplification, systemic risks or algorithmic discrimination.
This distinction is fundamental to the argument of the present paper.
IT Rules, 2021
The IT Rules, 2021 substantially expanded the regulatory obligations imposed upon intermediaries, particularly significant social
media intermediaries.

The regulatory framework has continued to evolve through amendments and policy initiatives. Consequently, the 2021 Rules

The continuing evolution of India’s regulatory framework demonstrates both the significance and difficulty of governing rapidl y
changing digital technologies. It also reinforces the need for a framework that is technologically neutral and capable of addressing
future algorithmic systems without requiring constant legislative reconstruction.
Constitutional Framework
Algorithmic accountability must also be situated within India’s constitutional framework.
Three fundamental rights are particularly relevant.
Article 14: Equality and Non-Arbitrariness
Algorithmic systems may produce discriminatory outcomes even where discrimination is not intentional. The principle of equality
therefore raises questions concerning the fairness and non-arbitrariness of high-impact algorithmic systems.
Article 19(1)(a): Freedom of Speech and Expression
Algorithmic ranking and content moderation directly affect the visibility and circulation of speech. Both state regulation an d
private platform decisions can therefore influence the effective exercise of expressive freedom.
Article 21: Privacy and Personal Liberty
Personalised recommendation systems often depend upon extensive data collection and behavioural profiling. Algorithmic
accountability must therefore be considered alongside privacy and data-protection principles.
The constitutional framework consequently requires a careful balance. Regulation should address genuine systemic harms while
avoiding unnecessary interference with lawful expression, privacy and technological innovat ion.

• what content users encounter;

• which content is recommended;

Comparative Analysis
European Union: Risk-Based Platform Governance
The European Union’s Digital Services Act represents one of the most significant contemporary attempts to regulate large digi tal
platforms through a systemic-risk framework.
The DSA recognises that very large online platforms and search engines can generate risks extending beyond individual pieces of
unlawful content. It therefore requires these entities to assess and mitigate syste mic risks, including risks relating to fundamental
rights, civic discourse and certain forms of manipulation.
The framework also incorporates independent auditing, enhanced transparency obligations, researcher access and user choice
concerning recommender systems.
The central lesson for India is not that the DSA should be copied wholesale. The more relevant lesson is the principle that platform
obligations can be calibrated according to systemic importance and risk.
This approach moves beyond the assumption that all intermediaries should be regulated in essentially the same way.
United States: Fragmented and Platform-Led Governance
The US model differs substantially from the EU approach.
Its regulatory architecture is shaped by:
• intermediary liability protections;
• First Amendment principles;
• sector-specific regulation;
• federal and state enforcement;
• litigation; and
• private platform governance.
The principal strength of this approach is the strong protection it affords private editorial and expressive autonomy . Its limitation
is fragmentation and the absence of a comprehensive federal systemic-risk framework equivalent to the DSA.
The US experience therefore demonstrates that algorithmic accountability must be designed carefully to avoid converting
legitimate regulation into government control over lawful speech.
India in Comparative Perspective
The comparative analysis suggests that India currently occupies an intermediate position.
India has moved beyond a purely laissez-faire approach by imposing substantial intermediary obligations. However, its regulatory
framework remains less developed than the EU’s systemic-risk architecture with respect to algorithmic accountability.
At the same time, India should not simply reproduce the European approach. India’s const itutional structure, administrative
capacity, social diversity, market conditions and institutional environment require a context -sensitive model.
The appropriate Indian framework should therefore combine:
the EU’s emphasis on systemic risk and independent  accountability + the US emphasis on expressive freedom and limits
on state control + India’s own constitutional commitment to equality, liberty, privacy and due process.

Algorithmic Bias and the Indian Context
Algorithmic bias is often discussed as a purely technical problem. Such an approach is inadequate.
Bias may originate in:
• training data;
• classification categories;
• optimisation objectives;
• proxy variables;
• linguistic limitations;
• platform policies;
• user behaviour; and
• historical inequalities reproduced through data.
In India, the problem has additional complexity because digital communication operates across multiple languages, regions and
social groups.
A moderation or recommendation system that performs adequately in English may  operate differently in Indian languages.
Similarly, an algorithm trained on globally dominant datasets may fail to understand local political, cultural and social con texts.
Algorithmic bias should therefore be treated as a potential equality and representation problem, not merely as an engineering
error.

Algorithmic Amplification and the Democratic Public Sphere
One of the most important consequences of recommender systems is the transformation of visibility into a technologically
mediated resource.
In a conventional media environment, editorial institutions determine what informati on receives prominence. In social media
environments, this function is increasingly distributed through algorithmic systems.
This creates a new form of power: algorithmic gatekeeping.
The central concern is not that algorithms necessarily promote false or polarising information. Rather, optimisation for engagement
may create incentives under which content capable of generating strong emotional reactions receives disproportionate visibili ty.
The democratic significance of this process is considerable.
If algorithmic systems influence which political arguments reach large audiences, then recommender systems become part of the
infrastructure of democratic communication.
This does not justify governmental determination of what users should see. Such an approach would create serious risks to freedom
of expression.
The appropriate regulatory response is therefore to make the process of amplification accountable rather than the content
predetermined.
In other words, the state should regulate the conditions under which high-impact algorithmic systems operate rather than becoming
the arbiter of permissible political opinion.
Procedural Justice and User Rights
Algorithmic accountability also requires stronger procedural safeguards.
Where platforms make significant de cisions concerning content, accounts or visibility, users should, subject to legitimate
exceptions, have access to:
1. meaningful notice;
2. intelligible reasons;
3. an opportunity to seek review;
4. timely appeal;
5. human intervention in appropriate cases; and
6. an effective escalation mechanism.
The purpose of these safeguards is not merely administrative fairness. They recognise that platform decisions may have significant
consequences for political participation, reputation, livelihood and access to information.
Procedural justice is therefore an essential bridge between algorithmic accountability and fundamental rights.
Towards an Indian Framework of Algorithmic Accountability
The analysis suggests the need for a layered and risk-sensitive framework rather than a single universal regulatory model.
Risk-Based Classification
Regulatory obligations should correspond to the scale, reach and potential impact of platforms.
A small platform serving a limited user base should not face the same compliance burden as a platfo rm capable of influencing
national political discourse.
The regulatory framework should therefore identify systemically significant platforms  and impose enhanced obligations upon
them.

Platforms with substantial systemic reac h should be required to conduct periodic algorithmic impact assessments for high -risk
systems.
Assessments should consider:
• freedom of expression;
• equality and non-discrimination;
• privacy;
• misinformation and manipulation;
• risks to children;
• political and electoral communication;
• linguistic and cultural bias; and

The objective should be preventive rather than punitive.
Independent Auditing
Independent auditing should be introduced for high-impact algorithmic systems.
Audits should assess:
• governance procedures;
• documented objectives;
• foreseeable risks;
• bias;
• moderation accuracy;
• recommender-system effects;
• mitigation measures; and
• compliance with applicable legal requirements.
Auditors must possess technical competence and sufficient independence from the platforms being assessed.
Meaningful Transparency
India should move beyond generic transparency reporting towards structured and risk-relevant transparency.
Platforms should disclose, subject to legitimate confidentiality protections:
• the principal categories of recommender systems;
• the objectives and significant parameters governing ranking;
• material changes to recommendation systems;
• risk-assessment findings;
• mitigation measures;
• moderation error rates;
• appeal outcomes; and
• information necessary for qualified independent research.
Transparency should be meaningful rather than merely formal.
Researcher and Civil Society Access
Independent research is essential for identifying systemic harms that regulators and platforms may  overlook.
Qualified researchers should therefore be provided controlled access to relevant platform data, subject to appropriate safegu ards
concerning privacy, security and commercial confidentiality.
Civil society organisations should also have opportunities to contribute evidence concerning the effects of algorithmic systems on
vulnerable and marginalised communities.
Stronger User Redress
Users should be able to challenge significant platform decisions through accessible and timely procedures.
The redress mechanism should distinguish between routine moderation decisions and high -impact decisions involving political
expression, public-interest communication, account suspension or systematic deprioritisation.
Independent Oversight
India should consider est ablishing or designating a specialised institutional mechanism for algorithmic and platform
accountability.
Such an institution should be independent in its oversight functions and should possess expertise in law, technology, human rights
and data governance.
Its function should not be to determine which political views or opinions are acceptable.
Its role should instead be to supervise whether systemically important platforms comply with legally prescribed requirements
concerning risk assessment, transparency, auditing, procedural fairness and user rights.
Protection of Innovation
Algorithmic regulation should be guided by proportionality and technological neutrality.
The objective should not be mandatory disclosure of proprietary source code or pr ior approval of every algorithmic innovation.
Rather, regulation should focus on demonstrable and foreseeable risks.
This approach would enable India to protect technological innovation while establishing meaningful accountability for systems
capable of producing significant social consequences.

DISCUSSION
The central finding of this doctrinal and comparative analysis is that India’s principal challenge is not an absence of socia l media
regulation but a mismatch between the object of regulation and the technological organisation of contemporary platforms.
The existing framework performs important functions. Intermediary due diligence, grievance redressal, content moderation and
mechanisms addressing unlawful content remain essential components of digital governance.
However, these mechanisms largely operate at the level of individual content and individual complaints.
Algorithmic governance operates at a different level.
It concerns the systems through which millions of individual decisions are made concerning visibility, ranking, recommendation,
profiling and amplification.
This distinction can be expressed as:
Content creation → Algorithmic selection → Amplification → User exposure → Social consequences
Traditional intermediary regulation focuses heavily on content and platform responses to content.
Algorithmic accountability requires regulatory attention to the entire chain.
The significance of this approach is particularly apparent in relation to democratic communication. A platform can comply wit h
individual takedown requirements while its algorithmic architecture continues to influence political polarisation, misinformation ,
social exclusion or unequal visibility.
The paper therefore proposes a conceptual shift:
from regulating content alone to regulating the conditions under which algorithmic systems distribute content.
This shift also changes the meaning of platform responsibility.
Platform responsibility should not mean that platforms become guarantors of every consequence produced by their users. Su ch a
standard would be unrealistic and could undermine freedom of expression.
Rather, responsibility should be proportionate to:
• the platform’s systemic reach;
• the foreseeability of the risk;
• the degree of control exercised by the platform;
• the potential severity of harm; and
• the feasibility of mitigation.
This produces a more defensible principle of proportionate algorithmic responsibility.

CONCLUSION
Social media regulation in India is entering a qualitatively different phase. The central regulatory question can no longer be
confined to whether platforms remove unlawful content or comply with intermediary due -diligence requirements. The deeper
question concerns the algorithmic systems through which platforms organise visibility, recommendation, rankin g, amplification
and user interaction.
India has developed an increasingly elaborate framework of intermediary governance through the Information Technology Act,
2000 and the IT Rules, 2021, together with subsequent amendments and broader developments in d igital and data governance.
However, the technological transformation of platforms requires a corresponding transformation in the regulatory framework.
The central argument of this paper is that India should move from a predominantly content-moderation paradigm towards a
risk-based algorithmic-accountability paradigm.
Such a transition does not require unrestricted governmental access to proprietary algorithms, direct state control over
recommendation systems or excessive regulation of lawful speech. Instead, it requires a framework through which platforms with
substantial systemic impact are required to identify, assess, mitigate and disclose relevant algorithmic risks.
The comparative experience of the European Union demonstrates the value of systemic -risk assessment, independent auditing,
enhanced transparency, researcher access and user choice in recommender systems. The United States, in contrast, demonstrates
the importance of protecting expressive autonomy and limiting governmental interference while a lso revealing the limitations of
fragmented and predominantly platform-led governance.
India therefore needs a distinct regulatory model that draws selectively from both approaches while remaining grounded in its own
constitutional and institutional context.
Such a framework should rest on six principles:
1. Meaningful transparency
2. Risk-based algorithmic impact assessment
3. Independent auditing
4. Procedural fairness and user redress
5. Independent institutional oversight

6. Protection of fundamental rights and innovation
The objective should not be to regulate algorithms simply because they are algorithms. Regulation should instead focus on systems
whose design or operation creates significant and foreseeable risks to fundamental rights, democratic discourse or public welfare.
The transition from content moderation to algorithmic accountability is therefore more than a technical modification to India ’s
social media laws. It represents a fundamental reconceptualisation of platform power.
When platforms function as  important infrastructures of public communication, the technological systems through which they
determine visibility and participation cannot remain outside meaningful structures of accountability.
The future of social media regulation in India should con sequently be based not on choosing between innovation and regulation,
but on establishing the institutional conditions under which innovation can coexist with transparency, accountability,
constitutional rights and democratic responsibility.
