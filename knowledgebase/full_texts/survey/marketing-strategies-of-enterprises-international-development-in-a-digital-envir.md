Abstract. Digital transformation has reshaped firms' international marketing practices, with
social media emerging as a vital communication channel for global brand outreach, yet
cross‑cultural frictions often weaken marketing effectiveness. This paper adopts the
literature review method. By sorting out studies collected from mainstream academic
databases, it explores digital international marketing, cross‑cultural social media
communication and corresponding corporate strategies. It first analyzes how digital
platforms and big data analytics remodel traditional ways to enter foreign markets, pushing
firms to focus more on long‑term brand relationship building. Drawing on cross‑cultural
dimension theories, this work discusses how cultural differences exert moderating impacts
on marketing performance, and explores the balance between global standardization and
localized operation. It further investigates creator collaboration and interactive two‑way
social media tactics, with special focus on resource‑constrained small and medium‑sized
enterprises. This literature review also highlights the value of combining digital capabilities
with culture‑responsive marketing practices during overseas expansion. Such integration
enables firms to lift audience engagement, strengthen brand trust and adapt marketing plans
to diverse consumer contexts. Finally, this review identifies research limitations including
scarce cross‑national comparative evidence and insufficient studies on long‑term brand
reputation. It also offers suggestions for future empirical exploration.
Keywords: Digital marketing, Cross‑cultural social media communication, Enterprise
internationalization, Glocalisation
1. Introduction
1.1. Research background and significance
Digital‑driven shifts have brought substantial adjustments to operational patterns for international
enterprises operating across global markets. Social‑media channels have grown into indispensable
communication vehicles, allowing brands to establish connections and conduct interactions with
overseas consumer groups. Nevertheless, cultural disparities across national contexts frequently
prevent global digital marketing initiatives from delivering anticipated outcomes, creating tangible

circumstances, this study aims to identify practical approaches that help enterprises enhance the
performance of their overseas digital marketing activities. Adopting the literature‑review approach,

1.2. Three core research questions
• In what ways does digital transformation reshape enterprises' overseas market-entry approaches
and their organisation of international marketing communication activities?
• To what extent do cross-cultural differences shape real-world implementation and overall
operational outputs for digital marketing practices within cross-border business environments?
• What practical roles can social-media platforms play in supporting global brand development
and sustaining firms' international expansion processes?
1.3. Literature selection standard
The theoretical analysis of this paper draws upon high‑quality, peer‑reviewed academic publications.
All source materials are English journal articles retrieved from three widely‑recognised academic
databases: Web of Science, ScienceDirect and Google Scholar. Six core papers provide foundational
support for the theoretical framework of this research. Beyond these key works, this literature
review incorporates a broad range of relevant scholarly studies to enrich and expand the scope of the
analysis.
1.4. Paper structure arrangement
This paper follows a logically coherent structural framework to organise its content. Chapter Two
explores how continuous advances in digital technology have altered international marketing
strategies. Built upon Hofstede's cultural theory as its primary analytical foundation, Chapter Three
examines how cross‑cultural factors shape real‑world performance outcomes of cross‑border digital
marketing. Chapter Four assesses the practical value and functions of social media in facilitating
enterprises' global brand‑building efforts. The concluding chapter summarises core research
findings, points out the inherent limitations of this investigation, and proposes potential directions
for future scholarly inquiry.
2. The evolution of international marketing strategies under a digital background
2.1. The subversion of traditional international marketing models by digital technology
Traditional analytical frameworks governing cross-border marketing activities have long been
restricted by geographic limitations and the need for significant physical investment in foreign
markets [1]. In this traditional business context, companies mostly expand their global reach by
setting up overseas branch institutions or building cooperative partnerships with local regional
agents. Such traditional expansion modes require massive financial resource inputs and expose
businesses to diverse operational risks brought by cross-border physical layout and resource
deployment. As a result, the internationalization progress of most enterprises used to advance at a
moderate and gradual pace, which aligns with the resource-dependent expansion logic proposed in
classic international business theories.

Recent advances in digital technology have rewritten the traditional rules of cross-border
business operation and corporate global expansion [1]. Greatly reduced transaction costs and
streamlined cross-border coordination have freed firms from reliance on large-scale physical
investment and local facility deployment in target markets. Unlike traditional multinational
companies that expand overseas step by step, many digitally born enterprises adopt a global market
orientation from the start of their founding, breaking the conventional incremental
internationalization development model [1]. Digital-enabled disintermediation reorganises internal
and cross-border workflows and strengthens the practical value of non-equity entry modes such as
direct exporting. Although geographical influences on cross-border trade are weakened, national
formal and informal institutional systems still generate tangible constraints for international
operators in the digital era, which cannot be fully neglected [1].
2.2. Digital platforms as new channels to enter foreign markets
Digital platforms constitute alternative virtual entry pathways for organisations pursuing
international market development, providing practical substitutes for conventional physical-oriented
market-entry mechanisms [1]. Prior research summarises three typical digital-oriented strategic
orientations for international operating firms: owning and running independent digital platforms,
acting as participant users on existing global platforms, and conducting comprehensive digital
transformation for legacy traditional businesses [1]
For the majority of traditional export-oriented enterprises and small-and-medium-sized
organisations, participating as platform users represents the most accessible strategic choice. Instead
of constructing local operational infrastructure abroad, these firms can reach large-scale overseas
consumer groups through third-party global digital ecosystems [1]. This channel mode effectively
lowers the capital threshold for foreign-market access and facilitates lightweight international
expansion. Nevertheless, practical risks accompany these new opportunities. Firms utilising
platform channels are required to cope with fierce intra-platform market competition, institutional
gaps between home and host markets, as well as potential channel conflicts with pre-established
offline overseas distributors [1]. Accordingly, access to digital platform channels alone cannot
guarantee satisfactory international marketing performance.
2.3. Data analysis and precise digital marketing improve operating efficiency
Digital platforms create feasible access routes for overseas market participation, while big-data
analytics further promote operational optimisation within international marketing practice [2].
Enterprises are able to collect multidimensional behavioural information from cross-border digital
touchpoints, including user browsing trails, clickstream records, search intentions, social-media
feedback and historical transaction data [2]. These varied data sources allow businesses to build
complete consumer portraits and identify distinct preference patterns across dispersed overseas
customer segments
Marketers can leverage these consumer‑oriented insights to carry out data‑driven audience
segmentation and tailor‑made marketing plans. Instead of delivering identical promotional materials
to every overseas audience group [2], firms can adopt targeted communication strategies. This
approach cuts down unnecessary spending on scarce marketing resources, raises how relevant
marketing messages feel for target audiences, and drives stronger conversion results. Besides,
modern big‑data analytics enables enterprises to monitor and analyse the full consumer journey in
international markets in real time. Marketing teams can spot operational bottlenecks at each phase of

2.4. Strategic shift: from single product promotion to global user relationship and brand value
building
Advancements in digital infrastructure and data‑processing capabilities have reshaped the strategic
logic of corporate international marketing activities [2]. Traditional cross‑border marketing practices
tend to centre on physical goods. Under such models, enterprises focus on selling tangible
merchandise to foreign purchasers to secure immediate sales‑driven revenue growth.
Within today's digital business environment, product‑centred promotional tactics can no longer
underpin firms' long‑term competitive edge in worldwide markets. Big‑data solutions empower
brands to deliver customised services and sustain ongoing two‑way communication with overseas
consumers across varied geographic areas. These efforts can boost user engagement and cultivate
stable customer loyalty [2]. Viewed within the broader international business ecosystem, modern
firms need to maintain durable interactive bonds with end‑user groups and diverse industry
stakeholders, rather than limiting operations to product sales and order fulfilment [1]. Sustained
customer‑relationship management acts as a fundamental condition for accumulating enduring
global brand equity. Driven by such industry‑wide shifts, international marketing strategies have
gradually moved away from transaction‑focused product selling towards relationship‑oriented global
brand development. Social media serves as a critical enabler for this strategic reorientation. It offers
enterprises a primary channel to preserve connections with overseas audiences and carry out
cross‑cultural marketing communication. For this reason, the subsequent chapter explores how
cultural disparities shape real‑world social‑media marketing outcomes.
3. Cultural influences on cross-border social-media marketing
3.1. National cultural dimensions as theoretical framework for cross-border social media
marketing
National culture constitutes the deep-seated set of shared values and beliefs that shape consumer
cognition, preference, and behaviour within a given market, which serves as the fundamental
analytical lens to interpret audience feedback toward cross-border brand communication on social-
media platforms. National-level cultural traits can explain why identical marketing content may
generate highly divergent responses among consumers located in different overseas markets [3].
This framework extends Hofstede's classical cultural-dimension theory and highlights that power
distance, individualism-collectivism as well as uncertainty avoidance exert observable influence
over how social-media users process brand-related information, evaluate symbolic content and
engage in online interactions with corporate accounts.
When Chinese brands roll out international social-media campaigns, they are not merely
delivering product-centred promotional messages, but transmitting value‑laden symbolic narratives
embedded within domestic cultural backgrounds. Lin and Lou emphasise that marketers should
avoid the assumption that consumer psychology operates uniformly across geographically separated
markets; even visually similar short-video posts may trigger varied emotional reactions owing to
gaps in cultural value orientations. For instance, content highlighting collective honour and group-
oriented storytelling tends to resonate better within collectivism-oriented societies, whereas

From the perspective of practical brand management, the cultural-dimension framework provides

3.2. Empirical evidence for cultural discrepancies constraining cross-border social-media
conversion
The above-mentioned theoretical inference regarding cultural influences on consumer behaviour has
obtained empirical support from studies focusing on the digital-marketing performance of
internationalised enterprises. Studies have shown that promotional content developed according to
domestic cultural logics, without targeted adjustments, will lower user trust, reduce public
willingness to participate in online discussions, and eventually damage conversion outcomes of
overseas social-media campaigns [4]. Cultural conflicts do not always lead to obvious public
controversies. More commonly, their negative impacts remain implicit, reflected by low interaction
volumes, passive browsing behaviour, and users ' reluctance to complete subsequent purchasing
actions.
Cultural differences act as moderating factors rather than direct causes of marketing failure. That
is to say, high‑quality product performance cannot fully offset communication losses brought by
socially inappropriate social‑media materials. Quite a number of Chinese brands deliver competitive
product quality in global markets, yet fail to convert platform exposure into stable brand loyalty.
One major reason lies in the mismatch between their social‑ media outputs and local audiences'
habitual ways of interpreting symbolic information. Such cultural incompatibility can appear in
numerous details, covering visual symbols, narrative structures, festival‑themed marketing schemes,
as well as interactive tones between brands and their followers.
For ordinary social-media users, cultural influences mostly function on a subconscious level.
Audiences rarely put forward explicit objections against brand posts from the cultural perspective,
but their actual consumption choices reflect such implicit psychological resistance. As a result,
simple post-event data statistics are insufficient to locate cultural-adaptation defects. Enterprises
need to combine platform operational data with cultural background analysis, so as to detect
invisible restrictions brought by cross-market cultural gaps.
3.3. Standardisation localisation dilemma and practical adaptation for international social-
media marketing
Enterprises expanding into culturally diverse overseas markets will inevitably face the classic
strategic trade-off between marketing standardisation and localisation. Two mainstream strategic
logics for cross-border digital branding have been summarised in existing literature [5].
Standardisation strategies aim to maintain unified global brand positioning, pursue cost advantages
and realise consistent brand images across multiple regions. Localisation strategies, on the other
hand, advocate modifying communication content and operational modes to fit specific market
contexts. Neither of these two extreme strategies can adapt to all market conditions. Blind pursuit of

standardisation ignores cultural diversity among overseas consumers, while complete localisation
may dilute core brand connotations and bring repeated operational costs for multinational firms.
Under such circumstances, the glocalisation strategy, which combines global unified positioning
with local targeted adjustments, becomes a feasible solution for social-media operations. It preserves
core brand identities while partially adapting communication elements to local-market demands.
Research data collected from European export‑oriented enterprises suggests that social‑media
localisation work goes far beyond basic text translation [6]. It requires comprehensive adjustments
in multiple layers, including textual expressions, symbolic design and value‑oriented narrative
frameworks according to cultural features of target countries. Based on questionnaire surveys among
medium‑sized and large Polish export firms, the study confirms that culturally adapted social‑media
content helps improve brand performance in foreign markets. Nevertheless, positive marketing
outcomes cannot be guaranteed merely by producing culture‑friendly materials.
How firms arrange internal power and control mechanisms also greatly shapes cross‑border
social‑media marketing effectiveness [6]. Highly centralised management, where the headquarters
strictly controls all overseas social‑media outputs, limits local branches' flexibility in responding to
cultural contexts and thus undermines communication performance. Still, granting full discretionary
power to external local partners also creates obvious risks. Excessive reliance on local influencers
and third‑party agencies may weaken brand‑equity accumulation, because external collaborators
may prioritise self‑promotion rather than accurately conveying brand values. A balanced
management model is therefore recommended: the headquarters keeps control over core brand
positioning, while granting local subsidiaries appropriate decision‑making space for content
adjustment and real‑time user interaction.
Although the empirical conclusions above originate from European enterprise samples, they still
deliver practical insights for Chinese brands carrying out social‑media international expansion.
When implementing glocalised social‑media strategies, firms need to distinguish unchangeable core
brand assets from adjustable communication components. Core brand values and brand personalities
should remain consistent worldwide. In contrast, creative presentation forms, narrative perspectives
and interactive mechanisms need corresponding cultural revisions for different target markets.
Meanwhile, enterprises must adopt a cautious attitude towards cooperation with local content
collaborators, set up complete content‑review procedures, and avoid over‑dependence on external
parties to complete brand‑building tasks. Through well‑designed content localisation and reasonable
allocation of internal decision‑making power, brands can mitigate adverse effects caused by
cross‑cultural gaps and achieve better overall performance on overseas social‑media platforms.
4. Social media communication strategies for enterprise internationalization
4.1. Core value of social media in global brand dissemination
Digital‑driven shifts in global business settings have reshaped how brands communicate across
international markets. Traditional cross‑border branding relied heavily on standardized mass‑media
advertising. These tactics delivered one‑way brand messages to foreign audiences, offered little
room for two‑way exchanges, and failed to cater to varied local market demands. The rise of social
media platforms has removed many geographical and communication barriers for international
brand promotion. It enables multinational enterprises to engage in dynamic two‑way interactions
with worldwide consumer groups. This major shift not only improves how cross‑border information
circulates, but also reshapes the core logic behind building and sustaining stable consumer‑brand
bonds across diverse global markets.

Global branding built upon social media carries a unique competitive edge: it helps develop
relational social capital within transnational online brand communities. Past academic studies show
that key social‑capital components, including shared community objectives, interactive reciprocity
norms and unified group perceptions, can spark consumers' emotional attachment toward brands and
encourage active participation in community activities. Positive user experiences of this kind can
trigger organic word‑of‑mouth and brand advocacy. Multinational brands can thus expand market
influence through organic growth, instead of relying on large‑scale paid promotional spending. Even
so, the marketing performance of such social capital varies greatly across cultural contexts. In
collectivist Eastern markets, interpersonal and community trust plays a vital role in strengthening
consumers' brand recognition and loyalty. By contrast, this trust‑driven mechanism brings limited
marketing returns within individualistic Western market environments [7]. Such sharp cultural
differences indicate that one‑size‑fits‑all social‑media marketing tactics cannot support global brand
operations. Businesses must carry out context‑specific local adjustments to maintain steady and
effective cross‑border brand communication.
While social media delivers substantial advantages for cross‑border brand outreach, firms may
misjudge real marketing outcomes if they over‑focus on superficial platform metrics. In real‑world
business practice, numerous multinational corporations assess overseas marketing performance only
through visible platform interactions such as likes, comments and shares. Yet consumer engagement
inside online brand communities is a multi‑faceted concept. It covers emotional attachment,
cognitive recognition and behavioural involvement. Plenty of overseas silent users maintain deep
emotional and cognitive connections with target brands, even without leaving explicit interactive
traces. Existing scholarship divides community‑based consumers into three categories: emotional
participants, cognitive thinkers and behavioural activists. Each group follows distinct developmental
paths toward brand loyalty [8].
Global brand operators should move past simplistic evaluations built on isolated metrics. Instead,
they ought to adopt multi‑faceted assessment frameworks that take consumers' emotional
perceptions, cognitive stances and real‑world behavioural feedback into account. Such a holistic
assessment approach delivers a more authentic picture of how international brands interact with their
overseas consumer bases. When firms refine their evaluation standards, they gain the capacity to
revise and refine their cross‑border social‑media marketing tactics in response to shifting market
realities. These continuous revisions allow global brands to maintain lasting competitive advantages
within the intricate, ever‑changing international business ecosystem.
4.2. Typical cross-cultural social media operation strategies
For global brands trying to reach consumers abroad, working with social media creators has proven
to be a highly effective way to enter overseas markets. Traditional mass-media advertising, by
contrast, pushes the same brand message to a large and mixed audience. Influencer marketing works
differently. It relies on the close relationships creators already have with their followers online, and
through those connections brands can reach specific groups of overseas consumers without spending
heavily on offline media. But simply partnering with famous creators does not mean a brand will get
stable, long-term results, especially when markets differ so much in culture and values.
To understand why influencer marketing works the way it does, human-brand theory offers a
useful lens. Social media creators themselves function as human brands, and they build emotional
bonds with followers by meeting three psychological needs: the desire to be inspired by an ideal
self, the sense of belonging to a group, and the feeling of personal competence [9]. Once followers
feel emotionally close to a creator, that feeling tends to rub off on whatever products or brands the

creator recommends. This transfer effect is the core reason why influencer endorsements often feel
more persuasive than ads that come directly from a company. Importantly, these emotional bonds
grow out of who the creator is as a person and the quality of their content—not out of how many
followers they have. This means brands picking creators for cross-border campaigns should care
more about how genuinely the creator connects with their audience than about raw follower
numbers.
When brands run these campaigns across countries, they cannot ignore local cultural norms in the
content they produce. Even a globally famous creator can get a negative reaction from overseas
audiences if the content clashes with what local people consider normal or right. Whether a brand
uses one unified global approach or adapts its content to each local market depends on what the
target market looks like and what the campaign is trying to achieve [10]. Content that feels
culturally specific may be well received in individualistic societies, but the same content can cause
problems in collectivist ones. This is why no single content strategy works the same way in every
country.
Besides the emotional bond between creators and their followers and the need to adapt content
across cultures, how authentic an influencer appears to be also plays a major role in whether cross-
border influencer marketing succeeds.
Authenticity cannot be regarded as an inherent personal characteristic of content creators. Rather,
influencers deliberately shape this public perception by means of four practical communication
tactics: transparency, immediacy, ordinariness and emotional passion [11]. Cross‑cultural empirical
evidence indicates that content creators across different cultural backgrounds deploy these
authenticity‑building strategies in distinct manners. Influencers coming from individualistic cultural
markets are more inclined to deliver personal self‑disclosure and adopt spontaneous presentation
styles. In contrast, those active in collectivist cultural environments place comparatively less focus
on individual‑oriented self‑exposure. For this reason, global brands ought to realign their
expectations for authentic‑oriented content while cooperating with local overseas creators, rather
than rigidly copying authenticity criteria developed within their home markets.
4.3. Social-media-driven internationalisation for small-and medium-sized enterprises
This section investigates how social media facilitates international growth for SMEs with limited
resources. Drawing on the cross‑border influencer communication analysed earlier, social media
creates workable routes for small‑and‑medium‑sized enterprises (SMEs) to expand into global
markets. Unlike big multinational corporations, the majority of SMEs work with tight resource
boundaries. These limitations show up in restricted overseas marketing spending, little hands‑on
know‑how in foreign markets, and poorly built offline distribution channels overseas. Traditional
cross‑border marketing tactics, for example joining overseas trade shows or opening local
subsidiaries, call for large‑scale financial and human investment, which many small‑sized
businesses cannot afford. Even though social‑media platforms lower the barrier to connect with
overseas audiences, merely launching official accounts and sharing posts fails to generate satisfying
overseas business performance. SMEs have to deploy tactics that fit their real‑world resource levels
to run successful cross‑border brand communication.
For export-focused SMEs from emerging economies, social-media strategies should integrate two
distinct sets of activities to achieve better overseas customer-related performance [12]. The "talking"
dimension refers to outward‑oriented information dissemination: enterprises publish brand and
product-related content on social platforms, build cross-border business connections and raise brand
visibility among overseas audiences. By comparison, the "listening" dimension centres on gathering

market intelligence. Firms track online public discussions, collect genuine consumer feedback from

market monitoring cannot bring meaningful improvements to export‑ oriented customer metrics.
Superior performance emerges only when firms execute both tactics concurrently. Listening
activities facilitate promotion adaptation tailored for target foreign markets, whereas talking-

Despite lowered material barriers brought by social‑media platforms, resource‑constrained SMEs
still face multiple practical hurdles when rolling out social-media-led overseas marketing initiatives
[13]. Social-media marketing generates business value by extending firms' customer reach,
optimising customer relationships and helping organisations identify authentic consumer demands.
Based on empirical evidence collected from emerging-market SME samples, perceived practical
value, operational simplicity and compatibility between social-media tools and existing business
workflows positively encourage firms to adopt social-media marketing. On the contrary, cost-related
concerns produce significant negative effects on firms' willingness to implement such marketing
schemes [13]. Apart from direct platform operating costs, invisible obstacles deserve attention,
including insufficient staff training on digital marketing and inadequate internal organisational
support. Even if social-media tools themselves remain readily accessible, shortages of skilled
personnel and imperfect supporting arrangements will prevent firms from translating platform
resources into tangible cross-border business gains. Reduced-cost access to digital platforms,
accordingly, does not automatically translate into international marketing success for SMEs. Firms
need to allocate matching manpower and improve internal arrangements while capitalising on
social-media-enabled opportunities.
SMEs' social-media-driven overseas expansion cannot detach from cross-cultural communication
principles outlined in earlier chapters. Whether implementing dual-purpose social- media tactics or
addressing cost and capability bottlenecks, firms must take target market cultural contexts into
consideration. When creating content for foreign audiences, SMEs should avoid directly replicating
domestic content frameworks. They can make use of market insights obtained through listening-
mode social media practices to fine-tune content styles, thematic choices and value orientations, so
as to reduce cultural misreading and communication friction in cross-border brand outreach.
5. Conclusion
This chapter summarises the core consensus drawn from the reviewed literature, identifies research
limitations within present-day scholarship concerning social-media-driven cross-border brand
communication, and points out potential avenues for further investigation. Without building an
overly complex analytical framework, this section connects the three central themes developed
throughout earlier chapters.
Scholarly research generally arrives at three connected conclusions. To begin with, digital
transformation has reshaped long‑standing operational routines within international marketing.
Brands can no longer rely solely on offline media or big‑size overseas trade shows to carry out
global brand communication. Digital platforms offer cost‑effective, decentralized channels and alter
the ways businesses interact with foreign consumers. Secondly, cross‑cultural differences act as a
key moderating variable for marketing performance. Cultural gaps between individualistic and

collectivist societies shape how target audiences understand social‑media posts, influencer‑promoted
content and overall brand image. Standardized worldwide communication plans rarely generate
stable effects across different regional markets. Thirdly, social‑media tools offer realistic options for
global expansion for both giant multinational firms and resource‑constrained SMEs. Big brands
partner with influencers to develop emotional bonds with international consumers. At the same time,
SMEs make use of two‑way social‑media activities such as content publishing and market
monitoring to offset the resource disadvantages that block their entry into foreign markets. Taken
together, these three findings illustrate how digital progress, cross‑cultural environments and
platform‑driven marketing work together to influence modern cross‑border brand development.
Even though existing literature has reached these consistent conclusions, several obvious research
gaps can be identified from the reviewed studies. A large number of empirical investigations gather
survey‑based data solely from emerging markets within a single country. Studies focusing on SMEs'
social‑media‑driven international expansion often rely on data collected from one national
environment, and cross‑country comparative analysis remains scarce. Research on influencer
marketing mechanisms and the digital international expansion of SMEs usually develops as two
independent research streams. Only a small number of scholars build a unified analytical framework
to examine how cross‑cultural norms affect both influencer campaigns and social media operations
run by small businesses. Apart from that, most empirical papers prioritize measurable export
performance metrics. Comparatively, intangible brand‑building achievements, such as stable brand
reputation built in overseas markets, have not received adequate academic attention.
Such gaps offer space for further academic exploration. Researchers may carry out cross‑national
comparative studies to check whether existing empirical findings can be generalized to different
emerging economies. By comparing data across countries, academics can identify how institutional
differences and cultural diversity change the real effects of social media marketing practices.
Meanwhile, later research can combine influencer marketing theories with studies about the
internationalization of small and medium enterprises. Follow‑up projects may investigate how small
overseas enterprises cooperate with local social media creators to improve cross‑border brand
communication and offset their natural shortages in internal resources and overseas operational
capacity. Researchers are also encouraged to look beyond short‑term export revenue data. They
should attach greater importance to long‑term brand reputation growth generated by cross‑border
social media activities. Studies in this field can enrich our understanding of non‑financial reputation
benefits brought by digital marketing efforts.
