Abstract
Green marketing communication (GMC) enables brands to signal environmental responsibility, yet its effectiveness remains
inconsistent across contexts. We conduct a meta-analysis of 156 studies comprising 146,961 participants to examine how
GMC influences consumers’ evaluative and behavioral responses. Results show that while GMC has a positive overall
effect, it is less effective for green brands than for conventional brands, with effectiveness varying across brand types and
communication characteristics. For green brands, GMC is more effective for new brands, communication that is emotional,
abstract, delivered via packaging, and supported by lower levels of evidence. For conventional brands, effectiveness is
enhanced by rational messaging, advertising, stronger supporting evidence, and brand familiarity. Meta-analytic structural
equation modelling shows that GMC operates through both message and product evaluation. Conceptually, we demon -
strate that GMC effectiveness depends on the diagnostic value of environmental claims and whether they are interpreted
as informative or persuasive, providing a generalizable explanation for mixed findings.

Sustainability has become a central concern for firms fac -
ing pressure from consumers, regulators, and civil society
to address environmental issues (Paul et al., 2025; White et
al., 2019). In response, brands increasingly promote their
environmental credentials through green marketing com -
munication (GMC), which involves the use of owned (e.g.,
eco-labeling) and paid (e.g., green advertising) media to
highlight a product or brand’s environmental benefits. GMC
is intended to enhance brand evaluations and influence
consumer behavior by raising awareness of environmental
initiatives and signaling alignment with broader societal
values (Cronin Jr et al., 2011).

contingent on contextual factors that remain insufficiently

dimensions, including brand, product, messaging and exe -

that concrete green messages are more persuasive (Muralid-

superior effectiveness of abstract green messages (Pittman
and Abell 2021; Yang et al. 2015a, b). These conflicting
findings indicate that existing research lacks an integrative
framework that explains when different green communica -
tion strategies are effective.
Beyond the mixed findings reported for those variables,
several potential boundary conditions remain underex -
plored. One such factor is brand greenness. Brands differ
in the extent to which they are defined by environmental
responsibility. Green brands (e.g., Tesla, Beyond Meat)
position environmental responsibility as core to their iden -
tity, while conventional brands may adopt GMC for stra -
tegic or reputational purposes (da Luz et al., 2020). This
distinction raises fundamental theoretical tension. For green
brands, environmental responsibility is already embedded
in highly accessible brand associations within the brand
schema, such that additional environmental claims com -
municated through GMC may provide limited diagnostic
information beyond what consumers already know about
the brand (Erdem & Swait, 2004; Feldman & Lynch, 1988).
In contrast, for conventional brands, environmental claims
communicated through GMC may provide more diagnos -
tic value, helping consumers update their evaluations of the
brand (Feldman & Lynch, 1988; Menon et al., 1995). How-
ever, increased emphasis on environmental claims within
GMC may also heighten the salience of persuasive intent,
leading consumers to question the motives underlying
the communication (Friestad & Wright, 1994; Obermiller
& Spangenberg, 1998; Parguel et al., 2015). As a result,
it remains unclear whether GMC is more effective when
environmental claims communicated through GMC rein -
force existing associations (Mandler, 1982; Meyers-Levy &
Tybout, 1989) or when they provide new diagnostic infor -
mation (Feldman & Lynch, 1988; Menon et al., 1995).
Despite the growing prevalence of GMC, theoretical
explanations for consumer responses remain fragmented.
Beyond experimental research, a substantial body of con -
ceptual and qualitative work has examined how envi -
ronmental claims are constructed and interpreted. Early
studies focused on the types of environmental claims used
in advertising (Banerjee et al., 1995; Carlson et al., 1993),
while subsequent research highlighted issues of credibil -
ity, greenwashing, and identity-based motives (Delmas &
Burbano, 2011; White et al., 2019). Although this work has
generated important insights into how consumers interpret
environmental communication, it provides limited cumula -
tive evidence regarding when and why GMC is effective.
Prior research has drawn on diverse theoretical per -
spectives, but these are typically examined in isolation,
limiting the ability to explain how GMC shapes consumer
evaluations and behavior across contexts. To address these
research gaps, we conduct a meta-analysis of 156 experi -
mental studies (112 papers), systematically evaluating the
impact of GMC on both evaluative (e.g., brand attitudes,
product evaluation, message evaluation) and behavioral
consumer responses (e.g., behavioral intention, engage -
ment). Conceptually, we draw on the accessibility-diag -
nosticity framework (Feldman & Lynch, 1988), arguing
that the effectiveness of GMC depends on the extent to
which environmental claims communicated through GMC
provide diagnostic information for evaluating a brand or
product (Erdem & Swait, 2004). We further propose that
the perceived diagnosticity of environmental claims com -
municated through GMC is shaped by consumers’ existing
brand schemas and their interpretation of persuasive intent.
This perspective enables a more unified explanation of why
GMC strengthens consumer responses in some contexts yet
weakens them in others.
Our study makes three contributions. First, we provide
the most comprehensive meta-analysis of experimental
GMC research to date, offering systematic evidence on the
causal effects of GMC on consumer responses and helping
reconcile conflicting findings in the literature. Second, we
identify important boundary conditions of GMC effective -
ness by examining moderators across brand, product, mes -
sage, and executional dimensions. In doing so, we show that
GMC is less effective for green than for conventional brands
and develop an informational value perspective to explain
when environmental claims are more or less effective. In
doing so, we extend accessibility-diagnosticity theory by
demonstrating how brand greenness shapes the extent to
which environmental claims are perceived as diagnostic
for judgment formation. Last, using meta-analytic struc -
tural equation modelling (MASEM), we demonstrate that
GMC operates through both message and product evalu -
ation, with the relative importance of these pathways dif -
fering across brand contexts. Together, these contributions
provide a more coherent explanation of GMC effectiveness
and extend understanding of how consumers evaluate envi-
ronmental claims.

Theoretical background
Green Marketing Communication (GMC)
Green marketing communication refers to the use of com -
munication tools such as paid media (e.g., advertising) or
owned media (e.g., labeling) to highlight the environmental
attributes of products, services, or broader corporate initia -
tives (Podvorica & Ukaj, 2019).
GMC is related to, but distinct from, broader concepts
such as green marketing, green advertising, and green
packaging communication (see Table 1). Green marketing
represents a strategic, firm-level approach that integrates
environmental considerations across the marketing mix
(Leonidou et al. 2013a, b), whereas green advertising refers
specifically to paid communications containing environ -
mental claims (Banerjee et al., 1995; Fowler & Close, 2012;
Leonidou & Leonidou, 2011). Green packaging communi -
cation captures sustainability-related information conveyed
through consumer-facing cues such as labels, certifications,
and visual signals embedded in packaging (Ollitervo et al.,
2025; Polonsky et al., 1998; Sigurdsson et al., 2025; Spack
et al., 2012). In this research, we focus on GMC as a com -
munication-level construct, encompassing both paid and
owned media, rather than broader strategic or operational
practices.
GMC has become an important tool for firms seeking
to enhance their environmental positioning and appeal to
environmentally conscious consumers (Szabo & Webster,
2021). However, its effectiveness is not solely determined
by message content but also depends on how such claims
are interpreted. Consumers often approach environmental
communication with skepticism, particularly when it origi -
nates from firms perceived to be opportunistically leverag -
ing sustainability trends (Nyilasy et al., 2014; Pomering &
Johnson, 2009). This highlights the importance of under -
standing not only what firms communicate, but how con -
sumers evaluate and respond to environmental claims
communicated through GMC. Sustainability represents a
broad organizational and societal objective encompassing
environmental, social, and economic considerations. Within
this broader domain, green marketing focuses specifically
on environmental aspects of sustainability (Polonsky et al.
1998; Leonidou et al. 2013a, b), while green marketing com-
munication (GMC) represents the communication-related
component of green marketing. Accordingly, GMC should
be viewed as a more specific construct situated within the
broader sustainability and green marketing literature.
Shen et al. (2024) provide an initial meta-analytic assess-
ment of GMC by focusing on green messages in advertis -
ing using experimental studies (16 studies; 45 experimental
pairs). Their findings show positive effects on attitudes and
purchase intentions, with variation across a limited set of
message- and context-level moderators. However, this
work does not consider broader forms of GMC, the role
of brand type, or the mechanisms underlying these effects.
Building on this foundation, the present study extends
prior research by examining GMC across both owned and
paid media, incorporating brand context, and providing a
theory-driven explanation of effectiveness grounded in the
accessibility-diagnosticity framework (Feldman & Lynch,
1988). Specifically, we examine how the diagnostic value
of environmental claims communicated through GMC var -
ies across brand contexts and shapes consumer responses
to GMC.

To address this gap, we examine the conditions under
which GMC influences consumer responses differently for
green and conventional brands and the mechanisms through
which these effects occur.
Accessibility, diagnosticity, and informational value
in GMC
Consumers rarely evaluate marketing communications in
isolation. Rather, they rely on both information contained
within the message and pre-existing knowledge stored
in memory when forming judgments (Feldman & Lynch,
1988). The accessibility-diagnosticity framework proposes
that information influences evaluations when it is both
accessible and perceived as diagnostic for the judgment
task (Feldman & Lynch, 1988; Lynch et al., 1988). Con -
sequently, the effectiveness of marketing communication
depends on the extent to which information provides use -
ful insight, beyond other information already accessible in
memory, for evaluating a brand or product (Erdem & Swait,
2004; Feldman & Lynch, 1988). We refer to this as the
informational value of environmental claims communicated
through GMC.
Whether information is perceived as diagnostic depends,
in part, on how it relates to consumers’ existing beliefs and
expectations. Schema theory suggests that these beliefs
and expectations are organized within schemas, which are
knowledge structures that contain beliefs, expectations,
and associations about an object and guide the interpreta -
tion of new information (Mandler, 1982; Meyers-Levy &
Tybout, 1989; Halkias & Kokkinaki, 2014). When infor -
mation aligns with existing schemas, it is processed flu -
ently and reinforces established brand meaning (Lee &
Labroo, 2004; Meyers-Levy & Tybout, 1989). The match-
up hypothesis and integrated marketing communications
research similarly show that congruent messages are per -
ceived as more credible and coherent, strengthening brand
evaluations (Delgado-Ballester et al., 2012; Kamins, 1990).
However, congruence does not inherently guarantee effec -
tiveness, as highly expected information may offer limited
diagnostic value. When messages simply confirm existing
associations, fluent processing reflects confirmation rather
than learning, reducing their ability to influence evaluations
(Keller, 1993). In accessibility-diagnosticity terms, highly
expected information may be accessible but contribute little
additional diagnostic value for consumer judgment (Feld -
man & Lynch, 1988; Menon et al., 1995).
In contrast, information that departs from expectations
can increase informational value by prompting greater
cognitive engagement and providing new insights, particu -
larly when the discrepancy is interpretable (Mandler, 1982;
Meyers-Levy & Tybout, 1989). Thus, the effectiveness of
marketing communication depends not on congruence per
se, but on whether the message meaningfully extends or
updates existing schemas and provides diagnostic informa -
tion for evaluation. Such information is more likely to be
perceived as diagnostic because it contributes knowledge
beyond what consumers already know about the brand
(Feldman & Lynch, 1988; Lynch, 2006).
Applied to GMC, this suggests that the informational value
of environmental claims may depend on the brand’s existing
environmental positioning. For green brands, environmental
responsibility is already embedded within the brand schema,
meaning consumers possess readily accessible environmen-
tal associations prior to GMC exposure. As a result, such
communication may be processed fluently but provide lim -
ited diagnostic information, reducing its ability to influence
consumer evaluations. In contrast, for conventional brands,
environmental claims communicated through GMC are less
expected and therefore more likely to provide diagnostic
information that helps consumers update their understanding
of the brand (Feldman & Lynch, 1988; Menon et al., 1995).
Thus, the impact of GMC depends not only on the diagnostic
value of environmental claims, but also on the accessibility of
environmental associations already stored in memory.
While diagnostic information can influence consumer
judgments, its impact also depends on how consumers
interpret the intent of the communication. The Persuasion
Knowledge Model (PKM) suggests that consumers use their
knowledge of persuasion tactics to interpret the motives

underlying marketing communications, particularly when
cues signal an attempt to influence (Friestad & Wright,
1994). When persuasion knowledge is activated, consumers
are more likely to shift from evaluating message content to
evaluating the intent of the communicator, which can lead
to the discounting of claims. This process is especially rel -
evant in green advertising contexts, where consumers are
increasingly aware of greenwashing and the strategic use of
environmental claims (Delmas & Burbano, 2011). Mislead-
ing impressions can arise not only from explicit claims but
also from executional elements such as nature imagery and
environmental symbolism (Parguel et al., 2015). As a result,
consumers may approach green messages with heightened
sensitivity to cues that signal exaggeration or strategic intent.
Importantly, persuasion knowledge is more likely to be
activated when messages appear overly salient, redundant,
or unnecessary. When environmental claims are strongly
emphasized despite already being expected, consumers
may infer persuasive intent, shifting attention from message
content to the motives of the communicator. As a result,
claims that provide limited diagnostic information may be
discounted and become less effective (Feldman & Lynch,
1988; Menon et al., 1995).
This mechanism is particularly relevant for green brands,
where environmental responsibility is already central to brand
meaning. Because environmental attributes are expected,
additional environmental claims may provide limited diag -
nostic value and be perceived as overly salient, increasing the
likelihood that persuasion knowledge is activated (Friestad &
Wright, 1994; Obermiller & Spangenberg, 1998). In contrast,
for conventional brands, similar claims are more likely to be
perceived as necessary and informative, reducing the likeli-
hood of skeptical interpretation and supporting their accep-
tance at face value (Newell & Goldsmith, 1998).
Building on the accessibility-diagnosticity framework,
we propose that the effectiveness of GMC depends on the
informational value of environmental claims communicated
through GMC. Schema-based processing determines the
extent to which environmental claims are perceived as diag-
nostic, while persuasion knowledge influences the extent to
which that information is relied upon or discounted in con -
sumer judgment.
Conceptual framework
We develop a conceptual framework (Fig. 1) integrating the
direct effect of GMC on consumer responses, the role of
moderators and their interaction with brand type, and the
mediating role of message and brand evaluation. We pro -
pose that the effectiveness of GMC depends on the informa-
tional value of environmental claims and on how different
communication features increase or decrease the diagnos -
tic value consumers assign to those claims across brand
contexts.
Consumer responses to GMC
The effectiveness of GMC (vs. control) is reflected in dif -
ferent consumer responses including behavioral responses
(behavioral intentions, engagement) and evaluative
responses (brand evaluation, message evaluation, product
evaluation, see Table 2 for definition and aliases for these
variables).
Research examining the effectiveness of GMC on con -
sumer responses reveals mixed findings. For behavioral
responses some studies suggest GMC is less effective than
control conditions (Hartmann et al., 2014; Mannem et al.,
2023), while others find no significant difference (Carfora &
Catellani, 2022) or positive outcomes (Gahlot Sarkar et al.,
2019). A similar pattern emerges for attitudinal responses,
where GMC sometimes leads to less favorable brand or mes-
sage evaluations (Rienzo, 2007), has no significant impact
(Xue & Muralidharan, 2015), or significantly enhances
attitudes (Hartmann and Apaolaza-Ibáñez 2009a, b). These
mixed findings highlight the need to consider moderating
variables that may shape the effectiveness of GMC across
different contexts.
Moderating variables
We identified and categorized moderators of GMC effec -
tiveness into four dimensions including brand (i.e., type
and familiarity), product (i.e., product type, benefits), mes -
sage (i.e., appeals, concreteness, sidedness), and execution
features (i.e., GMC medium, color theme, green evidence)
based on previous meta-analytic approaches in market -
ing communication (Eisend & Tarrahi, 2016). As shown
in Table 3, many of these moderators have been examined
in previous studies, although findings are often mixed. For
example, message concreteness has been found to enhance
GMC effectiveness (Muralidharan et al., 2023; Usrey et
al., 2020), show no effect (Janssen et al., 2022), or favor
abstract messages (Yang et al. 2015a, b). Some modera -
tors remain unexamined, including brand type and medium,
which highlight important gaps our study addresses. We also
account for publication status (published vs. unpublished)
and journal quality (high vs. lower quality) to control for
potential biases on the evidence base.

For green brands, environmental responsibility is already

vice benefits (e.g., luxury fashion, fast food, alcohol), which
provide immediate gratification but entail long-term costs
(Hsee et al., 2005; Okada, 2005).
For green brands, environmental responsibility is central to
the brand schema. When promoting virtue products, GMC
aligns with existing expectations but may provide limited
diagnostic information, constraining informational value
(Keller, 1993). Because environmental claims are already
expected, such claims may contribute little additional
insight for evaluating the brand or product.

environmental claims may be less expected and therefore
more diagnostic (Mandler, 1982; Meyers-Levy & Tybout,

senting only positive environmental aspects, or two-sided,
acknowledging both strengths and limitations (e.g., high -

costs; Eisend, 2006). Two-sided messages are often seen
as persuasive because they enhance perceptions of honesty,

tral to the brand schema. In this context, consumers may
expect environmental claims to be confidently communi -

expectations but provide limited new information (Keller,

strong environmental associations, as such information may
contribute relatively little informational value for evaluating
the brand’s environmental performance.

likely to be met with initial skepticism. In this context, two-

as a signal of transparency, thereby enhancing credibility
and reducing resistance (Atkinson & Rosenthal, 2014).

one-sided approach may provide weaker reassurance when
environmental positioning is not yet established (Foreh &

signals such as eco-labels at the point of purchase (Atkinson

salience of persuasive intent when such claims are already
expected, encouraging consumers to focus on why the claim
is being communicated rather than on the claim itself (Fries-

For conventional brands, advertising provides greater
scope to articulate and emphasize a focal environmental

ronmental initiatives more explicitly (Kim et al., 2022).
Such communication may increase the informational value
of environmental claims by providing consumers with a
clearer basis for understanding and evaluating the brand’s
environment positioning.

mational evaluation (Atkinson & Rosenthal, 2014).

Supporting evidence  The strength of evidence provided
in GMC shapes how consumers judge its credibility. High-
evidence messages include third-party certifications, eco-
labels, or verifiable metrics, while low-evidence messages
rely on general or vague claims without external validation
(Stokes & Turri, 2015). Evidence level is especially criti -
cal in sustainability contexts, where consumer skepticism
about greenwashing is widespread (Nyilasy et al., 2014).
For green brands, environmental responsibility is central
to brand identity. Additional evidence may provide limited
diagnostic value, as consumers already possess relevant
environmental associations about the brand (Keller, 1993).
Consequently, highly substantiated claims may contribute
relatively little additional information for evaluating the
brand’s environmental performance.
For conventional brands, stronger evidence plays a
critical role in establishing credibility by increasing the
diagnostic value of environmental claims and reducing
uncertainty (Atkinson & Rosenthal, 2014; Erdem & Swait,
2004). In contrast, low-evidence claims may provide
insufficient support and be more readily questioned (Wong
& Lai, 2024).
H9 Green marketing communication will be more effective
for conventional than for green brands when supporting
evidence is high (vs. low).
Mediation mechanisms
To explain how GMC shapes consumer responses, we pro -
pose a dual-pathway structure comprising message evalu -
ation and product evaluation. Message evaluation reflects
how consumers assess the clarity, credibility, and persua -
siveness of the environmental message (Olsen et al., 2014),
whereas product evaluation captures how these claims influ-
ence perceptions of product quality, value, and environmen-
tal benefits (Atkinson & Rosenthal, 2014). Consistent with
the accessibility-diagnosticity framework, these pathways
reflect how environmental claims contribute diagnostic
value for evaluating the brand and its offerings.
For green brands, GMC is more likely to operate through
product evaluation. Because environmental responsibil -
ity is central to the brand schema, product-level cues such
as eco-labels or claims about recyclability and energy effi -
ciency directly shape perceptions of quality and desirability
(White et al., 2019). In this context, environmental claims are
expected and provide limited diagnostic information, reduc-
ing the role of message-level evaluation. Instead, consumers
rely more on product-level cues, which provide more tangible
and diagnostic information for evaluating the product (Erdem
& Swait, 2004), and these inferences drive overall responses.
For conventional brands, GMC is more likely to operate
through message evaluation. When environmental associa -
tions are not embedded in the brand schema, message-level
information plays a central role by providing diagnostic
information for evaluating the brand. Clear, credible, and
well-framed messages reduce skepticism (Foreh & Grier,
2003), enhance authenticity (Eisend & Tarrahi, 2022), and
improve brand evaluations (Kim et al., 2022). In this case,
the message is the primary source of diagnostic informa -
tion, whereas for green brands it contributes relatively less
diagnostic value.

through product evaluation will be stronger for green
brands, whereas the indirect effect through message
evaluation will be stronger for conventional brands.

All effect sizes were converted into correlations to standard-
ize the data. Correlations were adjusted for measurement
errors in the dependent variables. When reliability data
were not reported in a study or when a single-item measure
was used, the mean reliability for the construct across all
included studies was applied. To integrate effect sizes, as
several studies reported multiple effect sizes for the same
consumer response variable, we used HLM (Hierarchical
Linear Model) and metafor package in R to aggregate effect
sizes. We specified the following model:

Meta-analytic structural equation model
The pooled correlation matrices for green and conventional
brands (Web Appendix L) were used as input for meta-
analytic structural equation modelling (MASEM) in R to
test our conceptual model separately for each brand con -
dition. Correlations among constructs were derived from
primary studies included in the meta-analysis by aggregat -
ing effect sizes representing the same variable pairs. These
extracted correlations were then pooled to generate overall
meta-analytic correlation matrices for green and conven -
tional brands, which served as the input for the MASEM
analysis. We applied the harmonic mean sample sizes of
901 (green brands) and 965 (conventional brands). Model fit
was evaluated using several goodness-of-fit indices, includ-
ing the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR),
Comparative Fit Index (CFI), and Goodness-of-Fit Index
(GFI). To further examine the mediation mechanism under
each condition, we conducted a multi-group SEM analysis
using Wald tests. All structural paths were initially con -
strained to be equal across groups, and each path was then
freed sequentially to assess significant differences using chi-
square difference tests between the fully constrained and
partially constrained models.

Discussion
Our findings provide a more precise account of when and
why green marketing communication is effective. Although
the meta-analysis confirms that GMC has a positive over -
all impact on consumer responses, this effect is not uniform
and varies systematically across brand contexts. GMC is
consistently less effective for green brands than for conven-
tional brands, and its effectiveness depends on how brand-,
product-, message-, and executional-level features shape the
informational value of environmental claims.
The moderator and mediation analyses indicate that
GMC does not operate simply by reinforcing alignment
with brand positioning, but by shaping the diagnostic value
of environmental claims and how those claims are inter -
preted by consumers. As shown in Table 9, GMC is most
effective when environmental claims provide diagnostic
information beyond consumers’ existing brand knowl -
edge and accessible environmental associations. For green
brands, where environmental claims communicated through
GMC are already expected, many forms of GMC contribute
relatively little additional diagnostic information and may,
when emphasized, reduce effectiveness. In contrast, for con-
ventional brands, environmental claims are more likely to
shape consumer evaluations because they provide diagnos -
tic information that reduces uncertainty. The findings sum -
marized in Table 9 provide a unified explanation for GMC
effectiveness across brand contexts and help reconcile pre -
viously mixed and sometimes contradictory results.

These findings extend accessibility-diagnosticity theory
in several important ways. First, they demonstrate that
the diagnostic value of environmental claims is inher -
ently relative and depends on the accessibility of prior
environmental associations. Second, they suggest that
highly accessible information may reduce the influence
of subsequent communication by limiting its incremen -
tal informational value. Third, the pattern of supported
and unsupported hypotheses indicates that communica -
tion features only produce differential effects when they
meaningfully alter the diagnostic value of environmental
claims relative to information already accessible in mem -

with persuasion knowledge further suggests that environ -
mental claims may also lose influence when limited infor -
mational value increases the salience of persuasive intent
(Friestad & Wright, 1994).
This research further contributes by identifying a con -
sistent pattern across a wide range of moderators. Prior
studies have examined individual factors such as message
appeals, concreteness, supporting evidence, and executional
cues, often producing mixed findings. For example, some
studies find that concrete or information-rich messages
enhance effectiveness (Muralidharan et al., 2023; Usrey et
al., 2020), whereas others find no effect or even advantages
for more abstract or value-based appeals (Janssen et al.,
2022; Pittman & Abell, 2021). Similarly, research on sup -
porting evidence and eco-labels shows both positive effects
through credibility enhancement (Bickart & Ruth, 2012;
Atkinson & Rosenthal, 2014) and null or negative effects
when claims are perceived as excessive or uninformative
(Stokes & Turri, 2015). Our meta-analysis reconciles these
inconsistencies by showing that the effectiveness of these
features depends on how they influence both the diagnostic
value of environmental claims relative to consumers’ exist-
ing brand knowledge and the extent to which they increase
the salience of persuasive intent across brand contexts. For
green brands, conditions that increase the salience, specific-
ity, or prominence of GMC, such as high levels of supporting
evidence, concrete claims, and advertising-based delivery,
are associated with reduced effectiveness. This pattern is
consistent with prior work showing that highly salient or
emphasized environmental claims can reduce effectiveness
when perceived as excessive or uninformative (Stokes &
Turri, 2015; Parguel et al., 2015). For conventional brands,
these same conditions enhance effectiveness. This reversal
pattern demonstrates that more detailed or prominent GMC
does not universally improve persuasion. Instead, its effec -
tiveness depends on whether it contributes diagnostic infor-
mation or increases the likelihood that claims are perceived
as redundant or overly emphasized.
Importantly, the pattern of supported and unsupported
interaction effects further reinforces this informational
perspective. Not all moderators interact significantly with
brand type, and this is theoretically meaningful rather than
incidental. Moderators that directly influence the infor -
mational value of environmental claims, such as message
appeal, communication medium, and supporting evidence,
exhibit systematic interaction effects, while others, such
as product type, product benefits, message sidedness, and
color theme, do not consistently produce meaningful dif -
ferences across brand types. This suggests that variation
in GMC effectiveness is not driven by contextual features
per se, but by whether those features meaningfully alter the
diagnostic value of environmental claims relative to alterna-
tive information sources available to consumers (Feldman
& Lynch, 1988). In other words, moderators only produce
differential effects across brand types when they change
how much diagnostic information environmental claims
provide or how those claims are interpreted with respect to
existing accessible information.

hypotheses. Product type (H2), product benefits (H3), mes -

significant differences between green and conventional
brands. The absence of significant interaction effects is
theoretically meaningful because it demonstrates that brand
type does not universally moderate GMC effectiveness.
Instead, differential effects emerge only when communica -
tion features change the diagnostic value of environmental
claims relative to information already accessible in mem -
ory. This pattern suggests that these features are less likely

The research also extends theory by clarifying the role
of evaluative mechanisms through which GMC influences
consumer responses. While prior work has emphasized the
importance of message credibility and product-related per -
ceptions (e.g., Olsen et al., 2014; White et al., 2019), the rel-
ative role of these mechanisms has remained unclear. Using
meta-analytic structural equation modelling, we show that
GMC operates through both message evaluation and product
evaluation, but that their relative importance differs across
brand contexts. Message evaluation emerges as the domi -
nant pathway overall, particularly for conventional brands,
where environmental claims provide a more diagnostic basis
for evaluating the brand because fewer environmental asso-
ciations are accessible prior to message exposure. For green
brands, product evaluation plays a more balanced role, as
consumers rely relatively more on product-level cues when
message-level information provides limited diagnostic
value. These findings extend existing models of advertis -
ing effectiveness by demonstrating how the processing of
GMC reflects both informational evaluation and sensitivity
to how claims are presented. Importantly, this pattern also
helps explain the broader moderator results observed for
green brands. The relative importance of product evaluation
is consistent with the finding that effective GMC for green
brands relies less on persuading consumers through explicit
environmental communication and more on reinforcing
already-accessible environmental associations. This helps
explain why packaging communication, emotional appeals,
and lower levels of supporting evidence are relatively more
effective for green brands, as these approaches reinforce
existing associations without requiring highly salient envi -
ronmental claims.
These contributions advance the literature on GMC,
advertising effectiveness, and consumer information pro -
cessing in several important ways. First, they provide a
cumulative and generalizable assessment of GMC effec -
tiveness, extending prior narrative and small-scale meta-
analytic work (e.g., Shen et al., 2024) by incorporating a
broader set of contexts and moderators. Second, they move
beyond fragmented moderator findings by identifying a
coherent pattern that explains when different communica -
tion strategies enhance or reduce effectiveness. Third, they
integrate insights from information processing and persua -
sion knowledge perspectives to show that the effectiveness
of GMC depends not only on whether environmental claims
align with brand expectations, but on whether provide diag-
nostic information beyond consumers’ existing accessible
environmental associations without increasing the salience
of persuasive intent.
Managerial implications
This meta-analysis is based on a diverse empirical base
spanning multiple regions, consumer segments, and prod -
uct categories. The studies include samples primarily from
North America, followed by Asia and Europe, with respon-
dents largely comprising young adults and a balanced gen -
der distribution. Product categories range from food and
beverages to household products, personal care, fashion,
electronics, energy, automobiles, and services (see Web
Appendix D).
The findings suggest that managers should begin by
assessing the extent to which consumers already associate
their brand with environmental responsibility. This diag -
nostic question fundamentally changes how GMC should
be designed and communicated. More environmental com -
munication is not necessarily better communication. Rather,
the effectiveness of GMC depends on whether environ -
mental claims provide information that consumers perceive
as genuinely informative rather than redundant or overly
persuasive.
For managers of green brands, the implication is not
to communicate more environmental information, but to
communicate environmental responsibility differently.
Because consumers already hold accessible environmen -
tal associations, increasingly explicit, evidence-heavy,
or highly promotional environmental claims are unlikely
to provide substantial new information and may instead
heighten perceptions of persuasive intent (Friestad &
Wright, 1994; Obermiller & Spangenberg, 1998). Manag-
ers should therefore prioritise communication that subtly
reinforces environmental identity. Emotional and abstract
appeals are particularly effective because they reinforce
brand meaning without duplicating environmental asso -
ciations that consumers already hold (White et al., 2019;
Pittman & Abell, 2021). Similarly, packaging communica -
tion is relatively more effective than advertising because
it embeds environmental responsibility within the product
experience and reduces the salience of persuasive intent.
Lower levels of supporting evidence are also more effective
than highly detailed substantiation, as excessive justifica -
tion may offer limited additional informational value and
instead heighten scepticism (Friestad & Wright, 1994). The

rather than implied. Managers should provide clear explana-

allow sufficient space to communicate and justify environ -

benefit from GMC because existing brand associations pro-

supporting evidence further enhance effectiveness because
they increase the informational value of environmental
claims and reduce uncertainty surrounding the brand’s envi-
ronmental commitment (Erdem & Swait, 2004). Packaging
communication alone may provide insufficient opportunity
to establish new environmental associations.
Overall, managers should avoid adopting a one-size-fits-
all approach to GMC. Before designing environmental com-
munication strategies, firms should first evaluate whether
environmental responsibility is already embedded within
consumers’ perceptions of the brand. For green brands, the
objective of GMC should be reinforcement rather than per -
suasion. For conventional brands, the objective should be
information provision and association building. Figure 2

Limitations and future research direction
This study offers robust theoretical and practical insights,
but several limitations warrant consideration. As a meta-
analysis, the findings are necessarily constrained by the
constructs, contexts, and operationalizations available in
existing experimental research. While this allows for a
systematic and cumulative assessment of GMC effective -
ness, it also limits the ability to examine emerging forms of
GMC and more nuanced mechanisms underlying consumer
responses (Hunter & Schmidt, 2004).
The findings also highlight several opportunities for
future research. In particular, the informational value
framework developed in this research raises important
questions regarding how the diagnostic value of environ -
mental claims evolves over time, how consumers infer
persuasive intent, how brand greenness shapes the acces -
sibility of environmental associations, and why some
communication features alter GMC effectiveness whereas
others do not. The results further suggest opportunities to
examine the relative roles of message and product evalu -
ation, as well as the implications of emerging commu -
nication technologies such as generative AI and digital
verification systems.
