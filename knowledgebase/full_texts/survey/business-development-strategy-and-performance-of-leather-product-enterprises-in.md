ABSTRACT
Purpose: This study examines the direct effects of business development strategy dimensions on
the performance of leather product SMEs in Kaduna and Kano States, Nigeria.
Design/Methodology/Approach: A quantitative, cross -sectional survey design was adopted.
Grounded in the Resource-Based View and the Dynamic Capabilities perspective, five hypotheses
were formulated and tested on data collected from 361 leather product enterprises using simple
random sampling and analysed using partial least squares structural equation modelling (PLS -
SEM) on a psychometrically refined measurement model, following an iterative item-purification
procedure.
Research Limitation: The research is confined to a single industry and two states, limiting
transferability beyond the leather product segment in Northwest Nigeria.
Findings: The refined model achieved satisfactory reliability and convergent validity (Cronbach's
alpha 0.82 –0.92; composite reliability 0.82 –0.92; average variance extracted 0.59 –0.74) and
acceptable, though not uniformly strong, discriminant validity and overall m odel fit. Human
capital strategy exerted the strongest, most highly significant positive effect on performance (β =
0.505, p < .001), followe d by operational strategy (β = 0.137, p = .059, significant at the 90%
confidence level) and market strategy (β = 0.127, p = .045, significant at the 95% level). Product
strategy (β = 0.069, p = .371) and digital marketing strategy (β = -0.052, p = .154) d id not exert
statistically significant effects.
Practical Implication: Enterprise managers and the support agencies serving this segment should
prioritise human capital development as the most dependable investment and approach digital
marketing investment cautiously, ensuring complementary digital proficiency and infrastructure
capability are addressed.
Social Implication: Because leather product manufacture in Kaduna and Kano sustains artisan
livelihoods and skill -based employment within a largely informal  sector, strengthening skills in
this way could improve enterprise performance and also protect the livelihoods and craft heritage
that depend on the sector.
Originality/Value: This study provides novel empirical evidence using core integrative internal
competencies and external foresights in the emerging economy , that is, five distinct business
development strategy dimensions.
Keywords: Digital marketing . emerging economy . enterprise performance . human capital
strategy.  leather industry

INTRODUCTION
Small and medium enterprises (SMEs) are a central pillar of manufacturing employment and value
addition across emerging economies, and Nigeria's leather product sub -sector occupies a
strategically important po sition within the country's non -oil manufacturing base, drawing on its
large livestock population and long -standing artisanal tanning tradition ( Mrindoko, 2022; Food
and Agricultural Organisation [FAO], 2018).

Enterprises in this sub -sector, concentrated in Kaduna and Kano States in Northwest Nigeria,
compete and grow through a combination of strategic capabilities that this study treats collectively
as business development strategy, and that are conceptualised in the business development
literature along five specific dim ensions: product strategy, market strategy, operational strategy,
human capital strategy, and digital marketing strategy. Understanding the direct contribution of
each of these five dimensions to enterprise performance is a foundational empirical question, both
for enterprise managers deciding where to invest scarce resources and for the government agencies
and development partners that design support programmes for this sub -sector, and, by extension,
for comparable artisanal manufacturing sub-sectors in other emerging economies.
Despite the volume of research linking business development strategy to SME performance
globally, comparatively little of this evidence has been generated specifically within Nigeria's
leather product sub-sector, where enterprises operate under distinctive conditions: a predominantly
artisanal production base, limited formal access to finance and digital infrastructure, and a policy
environment in which government support for the sector has been the subject of considerable
public attention without a correspondingly rigorous empirical account of which dimensions of
business development strategy actually drive performance ( Churk, 2022; National Leather and
Leather Products Policy, 2022). This research directly addresses this evidence gap by  analysing
the relationship between the five dimensions of business development strategy highlighted above
and enterprise performance in a sample of 361 registered leather product enterprises.
The specific objectives of this research are to determine the direct effect of product strategy, market
strategy, operational strategy, human capital strategy, and digital marketing strategy on the
performance of leather product enterprises in Kaduna and Kano States, and to establish, through
the pattern of results, which o f these strategic aspects warrants priority attention from enterprise
managers and policymakers seeking to strengthen performance in this segment.

Theoretical Framework
This research is rooted in the Reso urce-Based View (RBV), which states that a firm's persistent
competitive advantage derives from resources and capabilities that are relevant, rare, imperfectly
imitable, and irreplaceable (Barney, 1991). Business development strategy, as analysed in this
research, is understood as the umbrella construct encompassing five distinct bundles of firm -
specific resources and routines through which a leather product enterprise pursues competitive

performance. Furthermore, the selective use of RBV limits SMEs' performance outcomes in
emerging economies because it focuses on internal competencies and neglects the rapidly changing
business environment.

Product Strategy and Enterprise Performance
Product strategy refers to a firm's conscious decisions about  product quality, design,
differentiation, and innovation, intended to create and maintain a  distinctive market position. A
systematic review of Porter's Five Forces framework in the SME context emphasises that
differentiation-based strategies, of which product strategy is a central component, are a recognised
route for small firms to mitigate competitive pressure and improve performance outcomes
(Ndzabukelwako, Mereko, Sambo, & Thango, 2024). Within Nigeria's leather product segment ,
product-related capability, including tanning quality and finished-product craftsmanship, has long
been identified as an underlying factor in  export competitiveness and market access (Junaidu,
2012). On this ground, it is hypothesised that:

H1: Product strategy has a significant positive effect on the performance of leather product
enterprises.

Market Strategy and Enterprise Performance
Market strategy refers to a firm's approach to market segmentation, positioning, and differentiation
relative to competitors. Research on differentiation -based market strategy in other service and
knowledge-intensive industries has shown that a clearly articulated market positioning approach
is associated with improved competitive and financial outcomes, even in resource -constrained
organisational settings (Hu, 2024).

Extending this logic to a manufacturing SME context, a leather product enterprise that identifies
and serves a defined customer segment, rather than competing on an undifferentiated basis, is
projected to achieve superior performance outcomes. Accordingly, it is hypothesised that:

Operational Strategy and Enterprise Performance
Operational strategy refers to a firm's production efficiency, quality management, inventory
control, and process optimisation. Findings from European SMEs show that operational efficiency
is a key channel through which regional business research and innovation investments translate
into innovative sales output, emphasising the practical, performance-relevant nature of operational
capability even when investment inputs are held constant (Teirlinck & Khoshnevis, 2022). For an
artisanal manufacturing segment such as leather products, where production processes have
traditionally depended on manual, small -batch methods, advancement in process efficiency and
quality control represent a feasible and direct pathway to improved enterprise performance. It is
therefore hypothesised that:
H3: Operational strategy has a significant positive effect on the perf ormance of leather product
enterprises.

Human Capital Strategy and Enterprise Performance
Human capital strategy concerns a firm's investment in recruitment, training, employee
development, and retention. Human capital investment has been shown to produce a measurable
return when evaluated using formal return-on-investment methodologies, reinforcing the view that
workforce development is not simply a cost centre but a performance-relevant strategic investment
(Dadd & Hinton, 2023). In the specific cont ext of small and medium -sized enterprises, business
development services integrated with human capital investment have been found to significantly
influence SMME performance (Shezi, 2019). Given that leather product manufacturing is a skill -
and craft -intensive activity in which the quality of finished output relies heavily on worker
competence, it is hypothesised that:

H4: Human capital strategy has a significant positive effect on the performance of leather product
enterprises.

Digital Marketing Strategy and Enterprise Performance
Digital marketing strategy involves a firm's use of social media, online promotion, and digital
customer engagement channels. Research on SMEs in other developing market contexts has found
that digital marketing strategies play a crucial role in brand creation and market visibility
(Jumbulingam, Fadel, Al-Daqqaq, & Mimaki, 2024), while studies of professional and business -
to-business networking platforms have shown that social media use can strengthen business
relationship development and performance outcomes (Quinton & Wilson, 2016). Extending these
findings to the Nigerian leather product segment, it is hypothesised that digital marketing
engagement will similarly support enterprise performance; nevertheless, the lower baseline levels
of digital infrastructure and digital proficiency that may characterise artisanal manufacturing

METHODOLOGY
Research Design, Population, and Sample
This research adopted a quantitative, cross -sectional survey design. The research population
comprised 26,527 registered tanneries and artisan leather enterprises operating in Kaduna and
Kano States, Northwest Nigeria (17,243 in Kano and 9,284 in Kaduna), drawn from National
Leather and Leather Products Association of Nigeria (NALPAN) membership records. The
Yamane (1967) formula determined a sample of 393 enterprises at a five percentage margin of
error, and the researchers selected enterprises using simple random sampling. The researchers
administered structured questionnaires to owner -managers and retained 361 valid responses for
analysis after data screening, representing the final analytic sample for this research.

Measures
All constructs were measured using multi-item, five-point Likert-type scales. Product, market, and
operational strategy were each originally measured with seven items, human capital strategy with
five items, and digital marketing strategy with seven items; enterprise performance was measured
with ten items. In line with the iterative item -purification procedure recommended for reflective
PLS-SEM measurement models (Hair, Risher, Sarstedt, & Ringle, 2019), indicators exhibiting
standardised outer loadings below the conventional 0.70 threshold, or exhibiting problematic
cross-loading and residual correlation patterns with indicators of other constructs, were removed
from each construct's item pool prior to structural estimation. Table 1 reports the number of items
retained for each construct after this refinement, along with  the resulting reliability and validity
statistics.

Analytical Technique and Assessment Criteria
Partial least squares structural equation modelling (PLS -SEM) was used to estimate the
measurement and structural models, implemented in SmartPLS. The measurement model was
assessed for internal consistency reliability using Cronbach's alpha and composite reliability
(rho_c), against minimum thresholds of 0.70 and 0.80 respec tively; for convergent validity using
the Average Variance Extracted (AVE), against a minimum threshold of 0.50; and for discriminant
validity using the Heterotrait -Monotrait (HTMT) ratio, against a conservative threshold of 0.85
and a more liberal threshold of 0.90 (Hair et al., 2019; Henseler, Ringle, & Sarstedt, 2015). Overall
model fit was assessed using the Chi -square statistic and ChiSqr/df ratio, the Root Mean Square
Error of Approximation (RMSEA), the Standardised Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), t he
Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI) and Adjusted GFI, the Normed Fit Index (NFI), Tucker-Lewis Index
(TLI), and Comparative Fit Index (CFI), interpreted against the benchmarks summarised by Hu
and Bentler (1999). The structural model was assessed for multicolli nearity using the Variance
Inflation Factor (VIF) against a threshold of 5.0, and the five hypothesised paths were tested for

Discussion
Taken together, the results indicate that the five dimensions of business development strategy
analysed in this research do not contribute evenly to the performance of leather product enterprises:
one dimension emerges as a strong and dependable driver, two contribute more modestly, and two
show no measurable effect. The most substantial finding in these results, the highly significant
effect of human capital strategy on enterprise performance (H4 supported), is consistent with the
theoretical expectation, rooted in the Resource -Based View, that workforce -related resources
constitute a valuable and difficult-to-imitate source of competitive advantage (Barney, 1991), and
conforms to prior findings that human capital investment and business development services
collectively and significantly influence SMME performance (Shezi, 2019) and produce a
measurable return when formally evaluated (Dadd & Hinton, 2023).

In a craft- and skill-intensive manufacturing activity such as leather product manufacture, where
the quality of the finished product depends directly on the artisan's tacit skill, this result is
unsurprising and reinforces human capital development as the most dependable lever of business
development strategy available to enterprise managers in this population.

Turning next to the two dimensions of business development strategy that registered more modest,
though still significant, effects, the support found for market strategy (H2) and, at a more marginal
90% confidence level, operational strategy (H3), indicates that market positioning and production-
process efficiency both make  a genuine, if comparatively modest, contribution to performance.
The marginal statistical strength of the operational strategy effect, together with the borderline
discriminant validity observed between operational and human capital strategy (HTMT = 0.845 ,
Section 4.1), suggests that respondents in this population may not sharply separate production -
process improvements from workforce -capability improvements in practice, likely because both
are frequently pursued jointly, for example through on -the-job tra ining that simultaneously
upgrades worker skill and production technique. This conceptual proximity, rather than a
substantive absence of an operational strategy effect, plausibly explains its comparatively weaker
statistical showing relative to human capital strategy.

By contrast, the two remaining dimensions of business development strategy, product strategy and
digital marketing strategy, showed no measurable performance effect in this population, and each
merits separate consideration. The non-significant effect of product strategy (H1 not supported) is
the more substantively striking of the two findings, given the theoretical expectation, supported in
the wider SME literature, that differentiation -based product strategies improve competitive
outcomes (Ndzabukelwako et al., 2024). A likely explanation, coherent with the artisanal,
intermediary-dependent structure of the leather product segment, is that product-related decisions
in this population are limited by standardised raw-material inputs, established buyer specifications,
and limited scope for feasible product differentiation, such that variation in reported product
strategy activity does not translate into variation in performance outcomes as directly as it m ight
in a sector with greater product-design latitude.

The second of these two non -significant results, the non -significant and directionally negative
effect of digital marketing strategy (H5 not supported), departs from evidence in other SME
contexts that digital marketing activity supports brand creation and market visibility (Jumbulingam
et al., 2024) and that social media engagement strengthens business relationship development and
performance (Quinton & Wilson, 2016). This difference is likely attributable to the lower baseline
level of digital proficiency and digital infrastructure documented more broadly in Nigeria's
artisanal manufacturing segment: digital marketing activity undertaken without the
complementary digital proficiency, secure payment infrastructure, and logistics capability required
to transform online engagement into sales may fail to gain a positive performance return, and may
in some cases shift scarce managerial attention and resources away from higher -return aspects of
business development strategy such as human capital development. This finding should not be
read as evidence that digital marketing is inherently unproductive for leather product enterprises,
but rather that its current mode of practice in this population has not yet rea ched the threshold of
complementary capability required for it to register as a significant, let alone positive, performance
driver.

CONCLUSION
This research analy sed the direct effects of five dimensions of business dev elopment strategy,
product, market, operational, human capital, and digital marketing strategy, on the performance of
361 leather product enterprises in Kaduna and Kano States, Northwest Nigeria. Human capital
strategy emerged as the strongest and most reliable driver of enterprise performance; market and
operational strategy had significant, though comparatively modest, positive effects; and product
and digital marketing strategy had no statistically significant effects in this population.

For theory, the findings offer qualified support for the Resource -Based View and Dynamic
Capabilities perspectives as applied to an artisanal manufacturing SME context, while also
indicating that not every theoretically motivated resource bundle translates  into a measurable
performance effect under the structural, market, and infrastructural constraints characteristic of
this sub-sector; product and digital marketing strategy in particular warrant further theoretical and
empirical unpacking rather than a straightforward extension of findings from better -resourced or
more digitally-mature SME contexts. For applied purposes, the findings suggest that enterprise
managers and the support agencies that serve this segment should give precedence to human
capital development as the most dependable investment, treat market positioning and operational
efficiency as valuable secondary priorities, and approach digital marketing investment cautiously,
ensuring that complementary digital proficiency and infrastructure capability are addressed before,
or alongside, digital marketing spend.

This research faces several constraints. The cross-sectional design does not permit causal inference
regarding any of the five tested relationships. The measurement model was refined through a data-
driven item-purification procedure that was not cross-validated on an independent holdout sample,
and the resulting reliability, validity, and structural estimates should be interpreted as specific to
this sample pending replication. Overall model fit, while acceptable on some indices (SRMR, CFI),
fell short of the strictest conventional benchmarks on others (ChiSqr/df, RMSEA, NFI, TLI), a
limitation that should temper the confidence with which the reported effects, particularly the
marginally significant operational strategy effect, are generalised.

The marginal discriminant validity between human capital and operational strategy adds
uncertainty to the precise separation of these two constructs' unique effects. Lastly, the research is
confined to a single industry and two states, limiting transferability beyond the leather product
segment in Northwest Nigeria. Further investigation could extend this work through longitudinal
designs capable of supporting cau sal inference, through confirmatory factor analysis on an
independent sample to resolve the human capital -operational strategy discriminant validity
concern, and through qualitative or mixed-methods investigation of why digital marketing strategy
has not yet translated into a measurable performance effect in this population.
