# Supply chain performance, price margins and marketing efficiency in banana distribution

International Journal of Biology Sciences 2026; 8(9): 142-145
Supply chain performance, price margins and marketing
efficiency in banana distribution
Akanksha K Kadam1, Umrao S Bondar2, Vishwanath A Shinde3, Sachin V Satpute4,

A BSTRACT
This empirical investigation examines the structural dynamics of post-harvest supply chains, market in-
termediation, and commercial impediments in commercial banana (Musa spp.) production across west-
ern Maharashtra, India. Utilizing cross-sectional survey data collected during the 2024–25 agricultural
year from 90 stratified growers in the Phaltan and Man tahsils of Satara district, the study evaluates dis-
posal behavior, transactional costs, price spreads, and marketing efficiency across three distinct supply
pathways. The aggregate marketable surplus reached 97.67 per cent (636.66 q/farm) of gross produc-
tion, reflecting high commercial orientation. The distribution pathways comprised: Channel I (Producer
- Consumer), Channel II (Producer - Wholesaler - Retailer - Consumer), and Channel III (Producer -
Pre-harvest Contractor - Wholesaler - Retailer - Consumer). Findings demonstrate an inverse relation-
ship between farm scale and direct marketing: while smallholders directed 46.04 per cent of their sur-
plus through Channel I, medium and large enterprises routed 57.66 per cent and 62.72 per cent through
Channel III, respectively, trading economic margin for logistical risk mitigation. Channel I exhibited the
highest Modified Marketing Efficiency index (30.74), securing 96.75 per cent ( ₹ 1,683.03/q) of the ter-
minal consumer rupee for growers. Conversely, Channel III registered the lowest efficiency (2.10)
alongside an extensive price spread ( ₹ 1,104.19/q), driven by intermediary margins totaling ₹ 618.24 per
quintal.

Introduction
Horticultural commercialization serves as a primary engine for enhancing farm household incomes, with ba-
nana (Musa spp.) representing one of the most vital fruit crops due to its year-round harvestability, high yield
potential, and substantial economic returns. However, because banana is a highly perishable commodity with
a short post-harvest shelf life and vulnerability to physical damage, producer profitability depends directly
on the speed and efficiency of the downstream marketing system. Any delay, mishandling, or inefficiency
during post-harvest movement triggers rapid quality degradation and forces distress sales at heavily dis-
counted rates. In major production pockets like the Satara district of Maharashtra, banana growers face criti-
cal marketing challenges arising from multi-tiered intermediary supply chains, price volatility, and a com-
plete absence of localized cold-storage infrastructure. To mitigate assembly risks, large and medium produc-
ers predominantly offload their crops to pre-harvest contractors, trading off substantial economic margins to
avoid transit and packaging burdens. Conversely, while direct marketing enables smallholders to retain a
higher share of the consumer rupee, it remains structurally inadequate for liquidating large commercial vol-
umes. A precise evaluation of disposal patterns, transactional marketing costs, and intermediary price
spreads is imperative to identify where economic surplus is absorbed along the supply chain. This study ana-
lyzes the structural dynamics of banana marketing across Phaltan and Man tahsils in Satara district, compar-
ing the efficiency of prevailing market channels and prioritizing the core commercial constraints faced by
growers to inform policy and supply-chain improvements.
Materials and methods
The research framework reviews post-harvest management, output disposal patterns, and market transactions
utilizing structured data aggregation protocols. Commercial performance is measured using aggregate mar-
keting outlays borne by producers and intermediaries, price gap computations defined by the variance be-
tween consumer costs and farm-gate receipts, and Acharya’s Modified Marketing Efficiency (MME) index.
Comparative assessments draw upon established agricultural trade literature addressing price transmission
and network performance.

Results and discussion
The commercial success of banana cultivation in the study area is fun-
damentally tied to the operational and economic efficiency of its sup-
ply chains, given the highly perishable nature and limited post-harvest
shelf life of the fruit. Across the surveyed farms, aggregate banana
production averaged 651.84 quintals per farm. Out of this total vol-
ume, domestic utilization for home consumption accounted for an av-
erage of 0.16 per cent (1.10 quintals), quantities distributed as gratis
made up 0.17 per cent (1.16 quintals), and crop losses due to pests
and diseases stood at 1.98 per cent (12.91 quintals). After accounting
for these retentions and field losses, the net marketable surplus aver-
aged 636.66 quintals per farm, representing 97.67 per cent of total
production.
Producers distributed this substantial marketable surplus through
three primary marketing channels: Channel I (Producer - Consumer),
Channel II (Producer - Wholesaler - Retailer - Consumer), and
Channel III (Producer - Pre-harvest Contractor - Wholesaler - Retailer
- Consumer). Channel III served as the dominant trade pathway, han-
dling 49.41 per cent (314.58 quintals) of the total volume. Medium-
scale and large-scale producers heavily favoured this route channeling
57.66 per cent and 62.72 per cent of their respective harvests through
it primarily to transfer market risks and logistical burdens onto pre-
harvest contractors. Channel II emerged as the second most utilized
route, capturing 31.86 per cent (202.89 quintals) of the total volume
relatively uniformly across all farm categories. Conversely, Channel I
accounted for 18.71 per cent (119.17 quintals) of total shipments,
with smallholders relying heavily on direct consumer sales (46.04 per
cent of their produce) to capture higher net returns, whereas medium
and large growers made minimal use of direct retail.
Marketing expenses and price spreads varied extensively across these
distribution pathways, directly impacting net farmer realizations. For
growers utilizing Channel I, total marketing costs were minimal at
₹ 56.59 per quintal, allowing producers to realize a net price of
₹ 1,683.03 per quintal (96.75 per cent of the consumer price) and
achieve the highest Modified Marketing Efficiency index of 30.74. In
Channel II, total farmer marketing costs rose to ₹ 102.25 per quintal,
resulting in a net realization of ₹ 1,107.87 per quintal and an effi-
ciency index of 2.64. In contrast, farmers opting for Channel III in-
curred zero direct marketing costs ( ₹ 0.00 per quintal) because pre-
harvest contractors absorbed all post-harvest handling, packaging, la-
bor, and transport expenses at the farm gate, providing a uniform net
price of ₹ 1,218.61 per quintal. However, the multi-tiered involvement
of contractors, wholesalers, and retailers in Channel III generated the
widest price spread of ₹ 1,104.19 per quintal, pulling the marketing ef-
ficiency down to a low of 2.10.
These structural dynamics highlight that while pre-harvest contracting
provides immediate convenience and risk mitigation, it creates wide
price spreads driven by cumulative intermediary margins.
Consequently, cultivators encounter significant commercial hurdles,
including exploitative middleman malpractices, acute seasonal price
fluctuations, the complete absence of local cold-storage and preserva-
tion infrastructure, high transportation costs to terminal markets, and
a general lack of localized market intelligence. Addressing these sys-
temic bottlenecks requires institutional interventions such as the es-
tablishment of decentralized cold-chain facilities and farmer-producer
marketing platforms to protect growers from excessive intermediary
rent-seeking.

Conclusion
Channel I (Producer - Consumer) is the most economically efficient
route, enabling producers to realize 96.75% of the consumer's price.
However, Channel III remains the dominant marketing pathway due
to convenience and risk transfer to pre-harvest contractors, despite
wider price spreads ( ₹ 1,104.19/q) and lower marketing efficiency
(2.10).
