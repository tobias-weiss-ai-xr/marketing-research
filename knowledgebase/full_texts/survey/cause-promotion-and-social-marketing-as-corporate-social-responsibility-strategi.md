ABSTRACT

Corporate social responsibility (CSR) increasingly requires firms to align social contributions
with credible stakeholder engagement and measurable outcomes. This integrative literature
review examines cause promotion and social marketing as marketing-related CSR strategies while
clarifying their distinction from cause -related marketing (CRM). Cause promotion primarily
builds awareness, participation, fundraising, and volunteer support; CRM links corporate
contributions to consumer transactions; and corpora te social marketing applies marketing
principles to encourage voluntary behavior change. Synthesizing foundational theory, empirical
studies, reviews, meta-analytic evidence, and recent sustainability communication research, the
study identifies distinct m echanisms, outcomes, and boundary conditions. Cause promotion is
most effective for generating attention and engagement, whereas social marketing is better suited
to converting awareness into behavioral outcomes. Effectiveness depends on authenticity, cause–
company fit, transparency, credibility, stakeholder involvement, contextual fit, and behavioral
accessibility. The paper proposes an integrative CSR pathway to guide the selection, sequencing,
and evaluation of marketing-related CSR initiatives for sustainable societal impact.

Keywords: corporate social responsibility, cause promotion, social marketing, sustainability

INTRODUCTION
Corporate social responsibility (CSR) has moved from a peripheral philanthropic
activity toward a strategic domain in which firms are expected to account for economic,
social, and environmental consequences while maintaining organizational viability.
Management and marketing research consequently treats CSR not only as a question of
corporate conduct but also as a stakeholder relationship process in which firms identify

Toñacao et al. / IJASSD 8 (2) 2026, 129-142

 130
salient expectations, design responses, and communicate those responses credibly
(Maignan & Ferrell, 2004; Aguinis & Glavas, 2012). Marketing is particularly relevant
because many CSR initiatives depend on awareness, stakeholder participation, value
alignment, communication, and behavior change. Recent reviews likewise show that
CSR-related marketing research has expanded substantially and increasingly intersects
with sustainability communication and responsible consumption (Quezado et al., 2022;
Braga et al., 2024).
Within this broad domain, cause promotion and social marketing are frequently
discussed as practical routes through which firms can support social causes. Cause
promotion uses corporate resources to increase awareness and concern for a cause or to
support fundraising, participation, or volunteer recruitment (Ásványi, 2021; Kotler &
Lee, 2005a). Social marketing, in contrast, uses marketing concepts and tools to influence
voluntary behaviors that improve personal or societal welfare (Andreasen, 1994; Basil,
2019). When a corporation sponsors or implements such a behavior-change program, the
activity is commonly described as corporate social marketing (Kotler & Lee, 2005a; Inoue
& Kent, 2014). These strategies can therefore contribute to CSR through different
mechanisms: one primarily builds attention and engagement, while the other focuses on
behavioral outcomes.
A major conceptual difficulty in the existing manuscript and in parts of the broader
applied literature is the tendency to use cause promotion, cause marketing, and cause -
related marketing (CRM) interchangeably. This conflation weakens construct validity.
Kotler and Lee (2005a) explicitly distinguish cause promotion from CRM and corporate
social marketing. CRM is transaction -linked: the firm commits a contribution to a
designated cause when consumers engage in revenue -generating exchanges. This core
feature is also central to the classic definition of CRM (Varadarajan & Menon, 1988) and
to contemporary systematic and meta-analytic research (Bhatti et al., 2023; Schamp et al.,
2023). By contrast, cause promotion does not require a purchase -linked donation, an d
social marketing does not require a donation at all. It requires a behavioral objective.
Clarifying these boundaries matters strategically. Firms can create awareness
without producing behavior change, and they can generate a positive brand response
without demonstrating social impact. Conversely, a well -designed behavior -change
intervention may produce social benefits but fail to earn stakeholder trust when corporate
motives appear opportunistic or communication lacks transparency. Research on CSR
communication, CRM, and perceived authenticity shows that stakeholder attributions,
credibility, cause fit, and communication design can determine whether social initiatives
produce support or skepticism (Baghi et al., 2009; Du et al., 2010; Jeon & An, 2019;
Schamp et al., 2023). Thus, an effective CSR strategy requires both construct clarity and
an e xplicit theory of how communication, participation, and behavioral intervention
connect to outcomes.
The issue is also increasingly relevant in emerging-market and Philippine business
contexts, where CSR communication is occurring alongside stronger sustainability
disclosure expectations. In the Philippines, the Securities and Exchange Commission has

moved toward internationally aligned sustainability disclosure requirements through SEC
Memorandum Circular No. 16, Series of 2025, adopting PFRS S1 and PFRS S2 on a

Against this background, this review addresses three questions: (1) How should
cause promotion, cause -related marketing, and corporate social marketing be
conceptually distinguished within CSR? (2) Through what mechanisms and boundary
conditions do cause p romotion and social marketing generate corporate and societal
outcomes? and (3) How can the two strategies be integrated without reducing CSR to
promotional activity? The article contributes by correcting construct ambiguity,
synthesizing evidence on mecha nisms and moderators, developing a staged integration
framework, and identifying a research agenda for business organizations in global,
national, and local contexts.

LITERATURE REVIEW
CSR, Stakeholders, and Marketing-Related Social Initiatives
CSR is inherently multi-stakeholder. Firms respond not only to consumers but also
to employees, communities, governments, investors, civil society, and other groups that
can affect or are affected by corporate conduct. Maignan and Ferrell (2004) position
marketing as a mechanism for identifying stakeholder norms and developing
organizational responses, while Peloza and Shang (2011) show that CSR can create
different forms of value for different stakeholder groups. This perspective cautions
against evaluatin g CSR only through purchase intention or brand attitude. A socially
oriented initiative may be strategically useful when it strengthens trust, legitimacy,
employee engagement, community cooperation, or long -term stakeholder relationships
even when immediate sales effects are limited.
Communication nevertheless remains necessary because stakeholders cannot
respond to CSR activities they do not recognize or understand. Du et al. (2010) identify
low awareness and unfavorable attributions as major barriers to realizing business returns
from CSR communication. Ihlen et al. (2011) similarly emphasize that CSR
communication is not a neutral transmission process: message credibility, stakeholder
interpretation, corporate history, and the perceived relationship between claims and
conduct affect legitimacy. Braga et al. (2024) extend this logic to sustainability
communication, showing that the field increasingly links reporting, advertising, consumer
response, and pro -sustainable behavior. Cause promotion and social marketing are
therefore best viewed as distinct tools located within a wider architecture of responsible
business practice and stakeholder communication.

Cause promotion is therefore not a synonym for CRM. Ásványi (2021) describes
cause promotion as the use of corporate resources to raise awareness and participation
around a cause. CRM, in contrast, emerged as a strategic alignment of marketing and
philanthropy in which contributions are linked to consumer transactions (Varadarajan &
Menon, 1988). Contemporary review evidence confirms that CRM has developed into a
strategic CSR initiative but remains conceptually anchored in the transaction -linked
contribution mechanism (Bhatti et al., 2023). Hanzaee et al. (2019), Sheikh and Beise -
Zee (2011), and Thomas and Kureshi (2017) further demonstrate that cause type, cause
specificity, perceived fit, and consumer affinity can influence responses to cause-oriented
marketing.
Social marketing differs more fundamentally because its focal outcome is behavior.
Andreasen (1994) argues that social marketing should be defined by its behavioral

objective rather than by the simple use of promotional techniques for a social issue. Basil
(2019) similarly treats audience research, exchange, segmentation, competition, and the
marketing mix as elements of social marketing practice. Corporate social mar keting
applies this logic to company -supported initiatives. Inoue and Kent (2014) propose that
such initiatives can generate both social and business benefits, but their effectiveness
depends substantially on corporate credibility. Pharr and Lough (2012) l ikewise show
empirically that social marketing and CRM can be distinguished based on their target,
exchange, benefits, and marketing orientation.

Mechanisms Linking CSR Initiatives to Corporate and Societal Outcomes
Cause-oriented CSR initiatives can influence corporate outcomes through several
pathways. First, visibility and awareness can make stakeholders more likely to recognize
a firm’s social engagement. Second, perceived value congruence can strengthen
identification between stakeholders and the organization. Third, repeated credible
conduct can contribute to trust, legitimacy, and relationship quality. These outcomes,
however, are contingent rather than automatic. CSR communication can backfire when
stakeholders infer self -serving motives, perceive inconsistency between a firm’s core
practices and its social claims, or cannot determine how resources are used (Baghi et al.,
2009; Du et al., 2010).
CRM evidence illustrates this contingency clearly. Schamp et al. (2023),
synthesizing 237 studies, report stronger average effects for attitudinal responses than for
behavioral responses, with substantial heterogeneity across studies. Their findings
emphasize transparency, sincerity cues, purchase context, and emotional attachment as
important moderators. This distinction is central to the present article: favorable attitudes
toward a campaign are not equivalent to durable social behavior or social impact. An
organization that seeks measurable change should therefore avoid treating campaign
exposure, likes, impressions, or donations as substitutes for behavioral indicators.
The behavior-change literature provides a complementary perspective. White et al.
(2019) show that sustainable consumer behavior is shaped by social influence, habit
formation, individual self -concept, feelings and cognition, and tangibility. Corporate
social marketing can use these insights to diagnose why a desired behavior is not
occurring and to redesign the behavioral environment, value proposition, access,
incentives, social norms, or communication. Accordingly, the strategic advantage of
integrating cause promotion with social marketing lies not in conducting more
communication, but in using communication to create readiness for a subsequent
behavior-focused intervention.

RESEARCH METHOD
This study adopts an integrative literature review design rather than claiming a fully
systematic review. Integrative reviews are appropriate when a research problem requires
conceptual clarification and synthesis across theoretical, empirical, and methodo logical
streams (Torraco, 2005; Snyder, 2019). This design is particularly suitable here because

 134
the relevant literature spans CSR, marketing, social marketing, consumer behavior,
stakeholder theory, sustainability communication, and cause -related marketing. The
objective is analytical integration and framework development, not statistical aggregation
or exhaustive bibliometric coverage.
The review proceeded in four stages. First, the problem was framed around
construct boundaries and strategic integration. Core search concepts included
combinations of “cause promotion,” “cause -related marketing,” “cause marketing,”
“corporate social marketing,” “social marketing,” “corporate social responsibility,” “CSR
communication,” “authenticity,” “stakeholder behavior,” and “sustainability
communication.” Second, literature was retained when it contributed directly to at least
one of four analytical f unctions: defining the constructs, explaining their mechanisms,
providing empirical evidence on outcomes or moderators, or locating the strategies within
broader CSR and sustainability practice. Foundational works were retained because
construct definition  is historically dependent, while recent review and meta -analytic
studies were emphasized to update the evidence base.
Third, the sources were coded thematically according to (a) initiative type, (b)
primary objective, (c) mechanism, (d) focal stakeholder outcome, (e) societal outcome,
(f) moderators and risks, and (g) contextual level. Peer -reviewed journal articles,
scholarly books, and authoritative regulatory material formed the primary evidentiary
base. The practitioner-oriented source retained from the original reference list was used
only as contextual material and not as decisive evidence for theoretical claims. Fou rth,
the coded literature was compared for convergence, conceptual disagreement, and
missing links. The synthesis then generated a staged framework connecting awareness
and mobilization to behavior change and impact measurement.
This review has two deliberate boundaries. First, it does not treat CRM evidence as
direct evidence for cause promotion, even though the literatures overlap. CRM studies
are used to illuminate adjacent mechanisms such as cause fit, authenticity, skepticism ,
and transaction-linked consumer response. Second, the discussion of global, Philippine,
and local contexts is interpretive rather than a separate empirical comparison. The
Philippines and Negros Island provide a managerial context for applying the framework,
not a claim that the review identified a representative sample of local CSR campaigns.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Cause Promotion: Strongest at Awareness, Salience, and Mobilization
The literature supports a relatively consistent interpretation of cause promotion as
an awareness - and participation -oriented CSR initiative. A company may provide
funding, media space, distribution channels, employee involvement, sponsorship, or other
resources to increase public concern or participation around a social issue (Ásványi, 2021;
Kotler & Lee, 2005a). This function is strategically important because stakeholder
awareness is a precondition for many downstream responses. Du et al. (2010) note tha t
low CSR awareness is itself a barrier to stakeholder support. Cause promotion can

Toñacao et al. / IJASSD 8 (2) 2026, 129-142

 135
therefore create visibility for an issue, lower informational barriers, and signal that the
organization is willing to allocate resources to a public concern.
However, the social value of awareness depends on what follows it. Reach,
impressions, event attendance, and resource mobilization are legitimate proximal
indicators, but they do not establish sustained behavior change. The practical risk is a
“communication completion” bias in which the firm treats campaign visibility as the end
point of CSR performance. This problem becomes more serious in sustainability contexts,
where stakeholders increasingly expect credible evidence that communication
corresponds with  substantive organizational practices (Ihlen et al., 2011; Braga et al.,
2024). Cause promotion is therefore most defensible when its objective is explicitly
awareness or mobilization, or when it is designed as the first stage of a broader
intervention.

Corporate social marketing: behavior change as the defining outcome
Corporate social marketing shifts the unit of success from message exposure to
behavioral movement. The firm identifies a socially desirable target behavior, analyzes
barriers and competing behaviors, develops an exchange proposition, and uses marketing
tools to make the desired behavior more attractive and feasible (Basil, 2019; Kotler &
Lee, 2005b). Examples may include reducing waste, increasing recycling, improving
safety, adopting preventive health practices, conserving water or energy, or choosing
more sustainable forms of consumption. The specific social issue is less important
conceptually than the presence of a defined behavior and a strategy designed to influence
it.
This distinction has management implications. A behavior -focused CSR
intervention requires more than persuasive communication. If consumers are asked to
segregate waste but appropriate bins are unavailable, if employees are encouraged to
conserve energy but operating systems make conservation difficult, or if communities are
asked to adopt a health practice without access to the required service, promotion alone
cannot solve the behavioral constraint. Social marketing therefore directs managerial
attention toward the full value exchange, including costs, convenience, social norms,
perceived efficacy, access, and competing alternatives. White et al. (2019) reinforce this
point by showing that sustainable behavior is affected by multiple psychological and
contextual mechanisms.
Corporate credibility is also a boundary condition. Inoue and Kent (2014) argue that
companies can generate social and business benefits through corporate social marketing
when stakeholders consider the corporate source credible. Truong and Hall (2017),
examining corporate social marketing in tourism, similarly raise ethical questions about
the relationship between social objectives and corporate interests. These studies suggest
that the legitimacy of corporate involvement depends on whether the organizatio n’s
broader conduct is compatible with the behavior it promotes.

CRM is Adjacent Evidence, Not a Substitute Construct

The reviewed CRM literature is extensive and useful, but it must be interpreted at
the correct construct level. CRM links a corporate contribution to a consumer transaction
(Varadarajan & Menon, 1988; Bhatti et al., 2023). This mechanism gives CRM a direct
commercial trigger that cause promotion does not require. Moharam and Elsamadicy
(2020) emphasize this conceptual connection between marketing objectives and social
causes, while Schamp et al. (2023) demonstrate that CRM produces moderate attitudinal
effects on average but weaker behavioral effects. Thus, CRM can support CSR and cause
financing, but positive consumer response should not be equated with social behavior
change.
At the same time, CRM research provides relevant lessons for cause promotion and
social marketing because all three initiatives are exposed to attributional risk. Baghi et al.
(2009) describe a “dark side” of CRM communication when marketing design raises
doubts about corporate motives or use of funds. Sheikh and Beise -Zee (2011) show that
consumer cause affinity shapes response to cause -specific initiatives, and Thomas and
Kureshi (2017) demonstrate that cause type and scope can influence preferences. Hanzaee
et al. (2019) further compare cause marketing and CRM effects. These findings support
a broader principle: CSR -related marketing should be designed around stakeholder
relevance and credible fit rather than assuming that any socially desirable cause wil l
produce favorable responses.

Authenticity, Transparency, and Fit Are Cross-Cutting Boundary Conditions
Authenticity is a recurring condition across the literature. Jeon and An (2019) show
that perceived CSR motives influence authenticity and company attitudes, with
differences between cause promotion and CRM. Value -driven and stakeholder -driven
motives tend to support authenticity, whereas strongly egoistic attributions can undermine
it. This finding helps explain why apparently similar CSR campaigns can generate
different stakeholder reactions. The issue is not only what the company does but also
whether st akeholders perceive continuity between the initiative, the firm’s values, its
history, and its core business practices.
Transparency operates in parallel. Stakeholders are more likely to distrust a
campaign when contribution rules are unclear, impact claims are vague, or
communication emphasizes corporate virtue without verifiable outcomes. Meta -analytic
CRM evidence identifies transparency and sincerity signals among important moderators
(Schamp et al., 2023), while CSR communication research emphasizes the role of
message content, channels, and stakeholder characteristics (Du et al., 2010). For
managers, this means that ca mpaign design should specify the problem addressed, the
firm’s contribution, partner responsibilities, behavioral objective where applicable, and
the metrics used to assess progress.
It should also be interpreted carefully. A strong cause –company connection can
improve comprehensibility and credibility, but an overly self -serving fit may increase
suspicions that the cause is merely instrumental. The relevant managerial standard is
therefore not maximum similarity but defensible strategic coherence: the company should

be able to explain why the issue matters to its stakeholders, why it has legitimate
capabilities or responsibilities in the area, and how the initiative connects to actual
business practices.

promotion and social marketing. Cause promotion can create attention, issue salience,
stakeholder recruitment, and resource mobilization. Social marketing can then translat e
this readiness into a defined behavioral intervention. The sequence is not mandatory for
every CSR program, but it offers a useful logic when an organization seeks both visibility
and measurable change.

The framework also separates corporate outcomes from social outcomes. Brand

Global, National, and Local Application
At the global level, firms often select social issues that are broadly recognizable
across markets, including climate action, public health, inclusion, or responsible
consumption. The advantage is strategic coherence and scalability; the limitation is that
the same message or behavioral intervention may not translate across cultural and
institutional settings. Cross -market adaptation therefore requires more than translating
communication. It requires reassessing stakeholder priorities, social norms,
infrastructure, incentives, and the practical feasibility of the target behavior. Practitioner-
oriented discussions of CSR globalization make a similar localization argument, although
such sources should supplement rather than replace scholarly evidence (How, 2025).
At the national level, CSR operates within regulatory, policy, and cultural
expectations. In the Philippines, the shift toward PFRS S1 and PFRS S2 sustainability
disclosures increases pressure on covered companies to connect sustainability narratives
with governance, risks, metrics, and decision -useful information (Securities and
Exchange Commission Philippines, 2025). For cause promotion and social marketing, the
implication is indirect but important: firms face a stronger institutional environment for
substantiating sustainability-related claims and explaining how social initiatives connect
to material business and stakeholder issues.
At the local level, community specificity becomes more important. For businesses
in Negros Island, an analytically sound application would begin with stakeholder
diagnosis rather than assuming that a global CSR theme is locally salient. A company
might find that community priorities concern livelihood, disaster resilience, health, waste,
education, or environmental stewardship, but the appropriate issue and target behavior
should be established through local evidence and consultation. Local CSR is therefore not
simply a smaller version of a global campaign. It requires adaptation of both the social
problem definition and the intervention mechanism.

Managerial Implications
First, managers should select the CSR initiative by objective. If the objective is
awareness, fundraising, volunteering, or public participation, cause promotion is
appropriate. If the objective is transaction -linked giving, CRM is appropriate. If the

Toñacao et al. / IJASSD 8 (2) 2026, 129-142

 139
objective is a specific behavior change, corporate social marketing is the appropriate
design logic. Combining labels without clarifying the mechanism makes evaluation
difficult and can create misleading claims.
Second, firms should measure outputs and outcomes separately. Media reach, event
participation, donated resources, and campaign engagement are outputs or proximal
outcomes. They should not be reported as evidence of changed behavior or improved
social cond itions unless corresponding measures exist. This distinction is especially
important when sustainability or public-health language is used.
Third, authenticity should be treated as a design requirement rather than a
communication tactic. CSR campaigns should align with internal practices, disclose the
role of partners and resources, avoid exaggerated impact claims, and provide stakeholders
with a clear explanation of why the firm is involved. Credible partnerships with
community organizations, public agencies, or expert institutions can strengthen capability
and legitimacy but do not substitute for corporate accountability.
Fourth, managers should design for context. A campaign that is effective globally
may be ineffective locally if the desired behavior is culturally inappropriate, too costly,
inconvenient, or unsupported by infrastructure. Local stakeholder research is ther efore
necessary before scaling a social marketing intervention.

Research Agenda
The literature remains uneven across constructs and levels of analysis. CRM has a
comparatively mature evidence base, including systematic reviews and meta -analysis,
whereas direct comparative evidence on cause promotion and corporate social marketing
remains more limited. Future research should therefore move beyond campaign attitude
measures and examine how initiative design affects behavioral and societal outcomes
over time.

CONCLUSION
This study concludes that cause promotion, corporate social marketing, and cause-
related marketing are distinct but complementary CSR strategies. Cause promotion
primarily builds awareness and mobilizes stakeholder support, corporate social marketing
facilitates voluntary behavior change, while cause -related marketing links corporate
contributions to consumer transactions. Their effectiveness depends on selecting the
strategy that best matches the social problem and intended outcome. When awareness and
behavioral change are both required, a staged approach from awareness and engagement
to behavioral intervention and impact evaluation provides a more coherent CSR pathway.
Across these strategies, authenticity, transparency, corporate credibility, cause -company
fit, stakeholder relevance, contextual adaptation, and behavioral feasibility remain critical
determinants of effectiveness. Therefore, CSR performance should be assessed not only
through campaign visibility or brand response, but also through measurable  stakeholder
actions, social outcomes, and responsible value creation.

This study is limited by its integrative review design, the absence of PRISMA-based
screening and formal quality assessment, and the uneven volume of evidence across cause
promotion, social marketing, and cause-related marketing. In addition, the Philippine and
Negros contexts are used as contextual applications rather than representative empirical
evidence. Future studies should employ systematic review procedures with explicit search
and screening criteria, conduct field -based research in underexplored co ntexts, and
empirically test how integrated CSR strategies influence stakeholder behavior, social
impact, and long-term organizational value.
