ABSTRACT
This study assessed the major traditional techniques for aerial yam production,
processing, and consumption using an empirical case from Southwest Ethiopia. A
mixed method was employed to generate the primary data: a household survey and
in-depth interviews. A household survey was conducted with a total sample of 384
farm households in the Yayo District. To complement the household survey, 12 focus
group discussions were held. The survey results showed that the majorit y of
respondents were male (73.2%), 38% had no formal education. The mean age of
the sample farmers was 48.5 with a minimum of 24 years and a maximum of 82.
This explorative study indicates that two types of aerial yam were identified in this
study, the edible and wild aerial yams, respectively. The study also showed that
aerial yam is often grown in home gardens as well as in farm plots. The mean size
of the aerial yam plot was 0.022 (ha). The mean yield of aerial yam was 70.7 quintals
per hectare (ha). The results revealed that 74.5% and 15.4% of the farmers used
aerial yam as a food source and additional income source, respectively. In terms of
consumption, farmers (43.2% of %) consumed aerial yam as fried, boiled, or roasted.
More than 68% of the respondents reported that the wild aerial yam was not
consumed due to its toxicity problem. The main challenges in the aerial yam value
chain included a lack of extension services (4 6.9%), lack of awareness about
production and consumption (16.1%), traditional farming practices (15.6%), low
market demand (14.1%), and lack of sufficient planting land (7.3%).  The study
concluded that although aerial yam is an important food and income resource for
smallholders, its production, processing, and utilization are constrained by
knowledge gaps, inadequate support and market limitations.  The study mainly
recommends improving extension services, raising awareness and promoting better
production, utilization and marketing practices for aerial yam in the study area.

INTRODUCTION
Food and nutrition security are major concerns in many developing countries
including Ethiopia. Some wild root and tuber crops occupy a remarkable position in
food security in these countries because of their high calorific value and superior
carbohydrate content. It has become extremely important to diversify the present-
day agricultural system and search for alternative food and feed ingredients to
address food and nutrition insecurity. Alternative underutilized root and tuber crops
may contribute to sustainable food systems under climate change resilience [1].
Root and tuber crops contribute significantly to global dietary needs after cereal
crops [2]. Yam is one of the principal root and tuber crops, including cassava and
potato, which are widely grown and consumed as subsistence staples [3]. Yams
(Dioscorea spp.) are vine plants grown as staple food in tropical and sub-tropical
regions that produce underground tubers or aerial bulbils [4]. Yams are edible
energy-rich tuber crops developed from modified and thickened underground stem
storage organs that are bulky, perishable and vegetatively propagated by tuber [5,6].
Among the different types of root and tuber crops, yams (Dioscorea spp.) are the
most used staple food, livestock feed, and raw materials to produce various industrial
products [7].
Yams (Dioscorea spp.) constitute the predominant starchy staple in sub-Saharan
Africa, where food security is a critical issue for the growing population. Despite the
importance of yams for people and as a source of food security, attention to yam
production remains questionable. The ever-increasing population and the
associated food insecurity problem necessitates research into under-utilized and
crops, such as D. bulbifera which are less preferred than other yam types.
In Ethiopia, Yam (Dioscorea spp.) is widely grown in the southern and southwestern
parts of the country and plays a vital role in local food security. It serves as a ‘life-
saving’ plant group for marginal farming and forest-dwelling communities during
periods of food scarcity [8]. The aerial yam (Dioscorea bulbifera) is a basic food crop
for smallholder farmers in these regions [9]. However, studies on the factors affecting
production and postharvest handling of aerial yam (AY) are limited. Therefore, this
study aimed to assess indigenous techniques in aerial yam production, processing,
and postharvest handling in Southwest Ethiopia. The study also focused on the food
preparation and consumption practices of aerial yam in the study area.

Data Collection
This study employed a mixed-methods approach, integrating both quantitative and
qualitative data collection techniques to generate comprehensive and reliable
primary data. Data were collected from individual respondents and focus group
discussion (FGD) participants after clearly explaining the purpose of the study and
participants’ rights, including voluntary participation and confidentiality.
Multiple data collection tools were used to address the study objectives and enable
triangulation of findings. These included a cross-sectional household survey, focus
group discussions (FGDs), key informant interviews (KIIs), and field observations
conducted in crop farms and major market areas of aerial yam growers in Yayo
woreda.
Household Survey
A semi-structured questionnaire was administered to 384 randomly selected
households, comprising both aerial yam growers and non-growers, across twelve
kebeles in Yayo woreda. The questionnaire captured information on aerial yam
cultivation and productivity, purposes of production, farmers’ perceptions of the crop,
production constraints, postharvest handling practices, storage and losses, food
preparation methods, modes of consumption, household food security status and
the social characteristics of consumers.
Focus Group Discussions (FGDs)
Focus group discussions were conducted to capture collective perspectives and
enhance understanding of issues emerging from household surveys. Each FGD
consisted of eight participants, including elderly individuals and female household
heads. Preliminary field visits were conducted during the proposal development
stage, during which discussions were held with Woreda Agriculture Office experts
and Development Agents to refine the research tools.
An open-ended discussion checklist was developed, and four FGDs (one per
selected kebele) were conducted. Discussions focused on the general agricultural
context of the study area, growers’ preferences for local aerial yam varieties and
selection criteria (using pairwise ranking), identification and prioritization of
production constraints; income sources; indigenous production practices, utilization
of aerial yam; perceptions of production trends, reasons for low production relative
to other crops and the perceived contribution of aerial yam to household food

security. Findings from the FGDs were used to substantiate and triangulate
household survey data.
Key Informant Interviews and Field Observations
Key informant interviews were conducted with agricultural experts, development
agents, and other knowledgeable stakeholders to obtain contextual and technical
insights into aerial yam production and management. Field observations were
carried out in crop fields and market areas to directly assess production practices,
postharvest handling, and market dynamics.
Data Analysis
Both qualitative and quantitative data were analyzed using a mixed -methods
framework. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics,
percentages, means, standard deviations, frequencies, and ranges, employing
Statistical Packages for Social Sciences (SPSS) software (version 16.0). These
analyses were used to examine variations in aerial yam production and the influence
of sociodemographic factors such as age, farming experience, farm size, family size
and education level on the number of morphotypes maintained at household and
kebele levels. Qualitative data from FGDs, KIIs and observations were thematically
analyzed and used to complement and explain quantitative findings.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Socio-demographic characteristics of aerial yam farmers
The socio-demographic characteristics of the surveyed cultivated aerial yam (AY)
farmers are summarized in Table 1. Most respondents were male (73.2%) and
26.8% were female, reflecting differences in farmland access. Educational levels
varied, with 38% having no formal education, 29.7% primary education, 27.3%
secondary education and 5.0% college-level education. The mean age of farmers
was 48.5 years (range: 24–82), with a mean farming experience of 26.48 years
(range: 2–60), and a mean annual income of 58,056 ETB (range: 10,000–200,000).
Cross-tabulation of socio-demographic factors with indigenous aerial yam practices
showed that older farmers (>50 years) maintained more morphotypes (mean: 6.2)
than younger farmers (mean: 4.8), and male farmers were more engaged in
cultivation (78%) than female farmers (55%). Farmers with primary or secondary
education were more likely to adopt improved processing techniques (45% and 52%,
respectively) compared to those with no formal education (28%). Income influenced
both production scale and morphotype diversity, while older and higher-income
farmers tended to maintain a broader variety of aerial yam for household
consumption and market sale. These results highlight how socio -demographic

Productions of Aerial yam
Aerial yam Yield and Production Practices
In this study, farmers relied on both crop farming and livestock husbandry as their
primary sources of food and income. The main crops grown in the area were maize
(Zea mays) and coffee (Coffea arabica), while aerial yam was cultivated on a much
smaller scale. Survey results showed that the mean plot size allocated to aerial yam
production was only 0.022 ha, with an average yield of 70.7 quintals per hectare (qt
ha⁻¹), reflecting the low priority given to this crop compared with maize and coffee
(Table 2).
Focus group discussion (FGD) participants from the 12 kebeles explained that the
local community prioritized coffee and maize because of their higher economic value
and familiarity, and they produced limited amounts of aerial yam due to a lack of
knowledge about its food uses. Key informants, including Development Agents,
noted that aerial yam is often intercropped with coffee, and that its cultivation
remains limited because extension services, training programs and input distribution
are predominantly focused on cereals and cash crops. As a result, farmers have
limited access to improved production practices, yield-enhancing technologies, and
information on the crop’s full potential. These qualitative insights were strongly
supported by the quantitative findings, showing that most land is allocated to maize
and coffee, while aerial yam occupies only a minor portion of farmland.
Survey results showed that the majority of farmers obtained planting materials for
aerial yam from their own seed tubers (42.7%), followed by neighbors (28.2%),
relatives (14.8%), and the market (14.3%) (Table 3). The experience of sharing seed
tuber/true seeds imbued society with a sense of the value of the crop for food,
cultural and medicinal values. Focus group Discussions added depth to this finding
by explaining that seed exchange is based on trust and long -standing social
relationships. Participants emphasized that seed sharing plays an important role in
conserving local aerial yam types and maintaining cultural ties within the community.
Key informant interview confirmed that there is no formal seed system for aerial yam
in the study area. Development Agents explained that aerial yam is excluded from
certified seed programs, forcing farmers to rely on informal systems. While this
system supports genetic diversity, informants warned that it also exposes farmers to
yield inconsistency and disease transmission.

Characterization of aerial yam
The local name of aerial yam is “Kotteharre” or “Kottache” in the study area as it
resembles the foot of a donkey. Both cultivated and wild types of aerial yam were
grown in the study area. The edible plant is cultivated by farmers, whereas the wild
grows by itself. The wild type is usually grown on coffee farms. The survey results
clearly differentiated cultivated and wild aerial yam based on plant height, leaf shape,
flowering behavior, and tuber characteristics. About 98.2% of the respondents
revealed that cultivated aerial yam is taller. However, 1.8% of the respondents
indicated that the height of the cultivated aerial yam was shorter than that wild type
aerial yam. About 98.7% of the respondents suggested that the wild type of aerial
yam had a short height. Ninety-nine percent of the respondents reported that the leaf
arrangement of the cultivated aerial yam was heart-shaped, with broad leaves.
However, 98.7% of the respondents indicated that the wild type of the aerial yam is
also heart-shaped with tapered leaves (Figure 2).

The qualitative findings from FGDs strongly support the survey results. Participants

Key informant interviews with officials from the Yayo Woreda agriculture office

historically received little attention from research and extension services because it
is classified as an underutilized crop and not considered a priority root and tuber

Abebe [16] highlighted that inadequate government support in research and
extension services is a key constraint affecting the production of other root and tuber
crops such as Plectranthus edulis.
Harvesting, Postharvest Handling and Storage of Aerial Yam
Harvesting time in a year
The harvesting period of aerial yam in Yayo Woreda extends from August to
December, as indicated by household survey results, focus group discussions
(FGDs), and key informant interviews (KIIs). According to the survey findings (Table
5), 43.8% of aerial yam growers reported that the crop is ready for harvest in August,

 https://doi.org/10.18697/ajfand.152.26025
 29688
followed by 31.3% in September. A smaller proportion of respondents indicated
harvesting in October (7.6%), while 17.4% reported harvesting during November and
December. Farmers noted that aerial yam generally reaches maturity five to six
months after planting, although this varies depending on management practices and
local agro-ecological conditions.
Insights from FGDs support these findings, as farmers explained that harvesting is
often done in stages rather than at once, depending on household food needs and
the maturity status of the crop. Focus group discussion participants also emphasized
that early harvesting is sometimes practiced to meet immediate consumption needs,
even though it may reduce yield and storability. Key informants from the Yayo
Woreda agriculture office confirmed that the extended harvesting period reflects the
traditional production system , which lacks standardized agronomic
recommendations for planting and harvesting times.
Farmers in the study area rely on indigenous knowledge and visual indicators to
determine the maturity of aerial yam (Table 5). Survey results show that 41.7% of
respondents identified leaf wilting as the main indicator of maturity, while 34.4%
reported that maturity is confirmed when bulbils fall off the vine with slight touch.
Additionally, 24.0% of respondents indicated that a change in leaf color from green
to yellow signals readiness for harvest. Focus group discussions further revealed
that farmers often use a combination of these indicators, rather than a single sign,
to avoid harvesting immature tubers.
Key informants highlighted that harvesting practices, maturity indicators, and
postharvest handling methods vary across locations and among different root and
tuber crops grown in the area. They also noted that the absence of extension support
has resulted in non-uniform harvesting practices and limited knowledge of improved
postharvest handling and storage techniques. As a result, farmers must wait more
than five months to obtain the first harvest, relying heavily on experience rather than
technical guidance.
Indigenous Postharvest Handling and Storage of Aerial Yam
Postharvest losses of aerial yam in Yayo Woreda are influenced by a combination
of biological and handling-related factors, as evidenced by the survey, focus group
discussions (FGDs) and key informant interviews (KIIs). According to the household
survey (Table 6), 69.8% of respondents identified rodent and insect infestation as
the major postharvest problem, while 30.2% attributed losses to physical damage
during transportation and storage. These findings were strongly supported by FGDs,
where participants consistently emphasized beetles as the most destructive
postharvest pests affecting aerial yam during storage. Similarly, key informants from
the district agriculture office confirmed that insect pests particularly beetles are the

 29689
most prevalent cause of postharvest deterioration of aerial yam in the area. These
results align with findings from a related study by Garedew et al. [17] conducted in
Sheko District, Southwest Ethiopia, which reported that beetles were the dominant
cause of storage damage, with additional spoilage resulting from moisture
accumulation. This consistency across studies and data sources highlights insect
infestation as a critical challenge in the postharvest management of aerial yam.
Regarding postharvest handling practices, survey results indicated that only a
minority of farmers applied any form of preservation treatment before storage. About
29.4% of respondents reported drying aerial yams prior to storage, 16.5% washed
the produce, while the majority (54.2%) indicated that no special postharvest
treatment was practiced. Focus group discussions revealed that drying is mainly
practiced to reduce moisture content and delay spoilage, though the method is often
informal and lacks standardization. Key informants further noted that limited
awareness and lack of improved storage technologies contribute to the low adoption
of effective postharvest treatments.
Smallholder farmers in Yayo Woreda employ multiple indigenous storage
technologies. As shown in Table 6, 55.7% of respondents stored aerial yams in jute
(fiber) bags, 26.8% stored them on the floor in dry places, and 17.4% used traditional
baskets. Focus group discussions provided deeper insight into these practices,
explaining that the local community commonly uses Gundo and Guuboo traditional
woven storage baskets made from locally available plant materials. These
containers, named in Afan Oromo, are valued for their affordability, accessibility, and
ability to allow air circulation, which helps reduce moisture buildup during storage.
Key informant interview also acknowledged that these indigenous storage methods
are well adapted to local conditions and cultural practices.
However, despite their advantages, FGDs and survey data revealed significant
limitations associated with these traditional storage systems. When Gundo and
Guuboo baskets or jute bags are placed directly on open floors, aerial yams become
highly vulnerable to pests and environmental stress. Respondents reported common
storage-related losses, including sprouting (38.3%), dehydration (33.9%), and rotting
(27.9%). Key informants emphasized that poor ventilation, direct contact with the
ground, and inadequate protection from insects and rodents exacerbate these
losses.
Under these prevailing indigenous storage conditions, the maximum storage
duration of aerial yam after harvesting was reported to be approximately five to six
months without severe spoilage (Table 6). Overall, the findings of the present study
indicate that while indigenous postharvest handling and storage practices are widely
used and culturally appropriate, they are insufficient to effectively minimize

postharvest losses. Strengthening these traditional systems through improved pest
management, better floor insulation, and enhanced drying and storage techniques
could substantially reduce losses and extend the shelf life of aerial yam.
Utilization
Contribution of Aerial Yam for Household Food Security
The contribution of aerial yam to household food security in the study area was
perceived differently among respondents. According to the household survey, 54.4%
of respondents reported that aerial yam makes a significant contribution to
household food security, particularly during periods of food shortage, while 45.6%
perceived its contribution as insignificant, describing it as an underutilized crop. The
latter group attributed this to limited consumption practices and low awareness of its
food use and nutritional benefits, as reported by 99% of respondents.
Findings from FGDs reinforced these perceptions. Participants explained that
although aerial yam is locally available, it is not regularly included in household diets
because younger generations, in particular, lack knowledge of its preparation
methods and nutritional importance. Some FGD participants noted that aerial yam
is mainly consumed occasionally or during times of food scarcity rather than as a
staple food. Key informant interviews with development agents and district
agriculture officials further confirmed that inadequate extension support and the
absence of promotion activities for underutilized crops have contributed to the low
utilization of aerial yam for food security, despite its production potential.
Purpose of Producing Aerial Yam
The survey results revealed that aerial yam is produced for multiple purposes in the
study area (Table 7). The majority of growers (74.5%) reported producing aerial yam
primarily for household food consumption. About 13.8% of respondents indicated
that they used the crop as a source of additional income, mainly to purchase
essential food items such as edible oil, salt and spices. Smaller proportions of
respondents reported using aerial yam for medicinal purposes (5.2%), cultural value
(3.1%) and animal feed (1.8%).
Focus group discussions participants expanded on these findings by explaining that
aerial yam is often consumed during communal farm activities or group labor
arrangements, where it is prepared and shared among participants. This observation
was also supported by a development agent, who noted that aerial yam is sometimes
eaten during group work on farms as a readily available local food. Additionally,
some FGD participants reported the use of aerial yam tubers in traditional medicine,
particularly for the treatment of gastritis and diabetes. However, both FGDs and KIIs

 https://doi.org/10.18697/ajfand.152.26025
 29691
emphasized that these medicinal claims are based on indigenous knowledge and
require further scientific validation.
Regarding market value, survey results indicated variability in the selling price of
aerial yam in local markets. About 57.8% of respondents reported that 1 kg of aerial
yam was sold for approximately 10 Ethiopian birr (ETB), while 25.5% reported a
price of 5 ETB per kg. A smaller proportion (11.7%) indicated that the price could
reach up to 20 ETB per kg, depending on market demand and season (Table 7).
Focus group discussions consistently highlighted that the low and unstable market
price discourages farmers from expanding aerial yam production, as it is considered
less profitable compared to other crops.
Key informant interviews with agricultural extension workers provided further insight
into local marketing practices. They explained that aerial yam bulbils are commonly
sold in small quantities by placing 7–10 bulbils on the ground (madab) for 10–20
ETB at local markets or farm gates (Figure 6). Government officials also noted that
marketing is largely informal and poorly organized, with limited market linkage and
value addition. These findings are consistent with a study by Mbaya et al. [18] in
Borno State, Nigeria, which reported that aerial yam marketing is predominantly
carried out by women and children and often sold alongside non -timber forest
products in open spaces such as roadsides, motor parks and small markets.

The consumption of wild type aerial yam was generally avoided in the study area.
According to the survey (Table 8), 68% of respondents reported that wild type aerial
yam is not consumed due to its perceived toxicity. Others cited practical limitations,

including long cooking time (16.4%) and a burning or irritating sensation in the mouth
when consumed (15.6%). Focus group discussions confirmed that community
members have strong indigenous knowledge distinguishing cultivated aerial yam
from wild types and intentionally avoid consuming the latter. Development agents
and government officials supported these findings, explaining that extension
services discourage the consumption of wild aerial yam due to safety concerns.
Most respondents also indicated that wild type aerial yam is not commonly used as
animal feed. However, 22.9% of respondents reported that wild animals, particularly
monkeys, consume wild aerial yam (Table 8). Focus group discussions further
explained that the presence of wild aerial yam in forested areas mainly serves as a
food source for wildlife rather than for domestic use.
Preparation Techniques
Processing and preparation of aerial yam in the southwest part of Ethiopia,
particularly in Yayo Woreda, are largely informal and household-based. Evidence
from the study consistently indicated that aerial yam processing is limited to simple
traditional methods, with no involvement of formal or small -scale processing
enterprises in the study area.
According to the survey results, the dominant preparation methods of aerial yam are
boiling and frying. For boiled aerial yam, respondents described a common
preparation process that involves sorting and washing the raw tubers, followed by
boiling in hot water for 30–60 minutes. After boiling, the tubers are cooled, peeled,
sliced and consumed. The boiled aerial yam is commonly served with kochkocha, a
paste made from green or red pepper with a pungent taste. Focus group discussions
confirmed that this preparation method is widely practiced at the household level and
is considered the simplest and safest way to consume aerial yam. Participants
emphasized that boiling reduces bitterness and improves palatability. One key
informant, a kebele leader, also reported that boiled aerial yam is the most common
form of consumption in the local community.
Fried aerial yam is prepared in a manner similar to potato-based dishes (Figure 7).
Survey data showed that fried aerial yam is commonly prepared using previously
boiled tubers, which are sliced into pieces resembling potato chips or cubes.
According to FGDs, onions are first cooked in edible oil, followed by the addition of
red pepper and other locally available spices. The sliced aerial yam is then added
and fried until fully cooked. This dish is usually served with injera or bread. Key
informants from the agricultural extension office noted that pre-boiling before frying
is a local practice intended to reduce cooking time and improve texture.

Despite these preparation methods, the survey revealed that aerial yam is not used
to prepare major staple foods such as injera, nor is it processed into flatbread, bread
or porridge. Both FGDs and KIIs explained that this limited culinary use is mainly
due to a lack of knowledge on alternative processing techniques, unfamiliarity with
recipe diversification and the absence of training or demonstrations on value-added
uses of aerial yam. Extension workers further noted that aerial yam has not been
incorporated into local food processing initiatives or nutrition education programs.
In terms of consumption patterns, aerial yam is mainly eaten as lunch in boiled or
fried form. Roasted aerial yam was also reported as a common practice, particularly
during farm work. According to the survey, 43.2% of respondents indicated that they
roast aerial yam tubers in the field and consume them as lunch during agricultural
activities. Focus group discussions supported this finding, explaining that roasting is
convenient during farm work because it requires minimal preparation and no cooking
utensils. Aerial yam was also consumed as a snack, especially when boiled, as
noted by FGD participants.
Survey results further indicated that 44.5% of respondents consumed aerial yam as
a supplementary food, particularly in the evening. Respondents explained that aerial
yam is an accessible and low-cost food resource during the dry season, mainly from
September to January, when other food options are limited. However, KIIs
highlighted that aerial yam is not consumed throughout the year due to its seasonal
availability and limited storage life. Government officials and development agents
emphasized that the seasonal nature of the crop, combined with inadequate
preservation and processing methods, restricts its regular inclusion in household
diets.

CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS FOR DEVELOPMENT
This study assessed indigenous techniques for aerial yam (Dioscorea bulbifera)
production, processing and consumption in Yayo Woreda, Southwest Ethiopia. The
findings indicate that aerial yam is cultivated both in home gardens and farm plots,
serving as an important food source and an additional income source. The wild type
is largely avoided due to toxicity and long cooking time, suggesting the need for
further research on its safe utilization.
The production of aerial yam is declining due to low awareness of its nutritional and
economic value, limited market demand, and inadequate agronomic practices. Major
constraints include insufficient extension support, reliance on traditional farming
methods, and limited access to markets. Despite these challenges, farmers possess
substantial indigenous knowledge regarding crop identification, harvesting,
postharvest handling and household processing.
To enhance aerial yam production and utilization, the study recommends promoting
indigenous knowledge alongside improved agronomic practices, postharvest
handling and processing techniques. Raising community awareness of aerial yam’s
role in food security and income generation is essential. Extension services should
actively support farmers, integrating traditional practices with research -based
interventions to ensure sustainable production, improved utilization, and increased
household benefits in the study area.
