# Brand language on social media: a systematic literature review

ORIGINAL ARTICLE
Italian Journal of Marketing          (2026) 2026:8

Abstract
The growing availability of large-scale digital data and advanced analytical tools
enables consumer researchers to investigate brand language with unprecedented
depth and precision. While the study of brand language has long been a central
focus in marketing research, there is growing interest in how brands communicate
in the specific context of social media and how this language shapes consumer
responses. This article presents the first systematic review of academic research on
brand language on social media, identifying 51 peer-reviewed articles published
between 2016 and 2024. The review has two primary theoretical objectives. First,
to synthesize existing findings and offer a comprehensive overview of the linguis -
tic strategies brands use to engage and persuade consumers on social media plat -
forms Second, to identify gaps in the literature and outline promising avenues for
future research. Specifically, the review uncovers seven key content dimensions
(textual cue, linguistic component, focal language, brand type, modality, empirical
approach, and outcome) which serve as an organizing framework for the exist -
ing studies. In addition to contributing to the marketing and consumer behavior
literature, this research offers actionable insights for practitioners seeking to craft
effective marketing messages on social media.

1 Introduction
5.2 billion people worldwide use social media, a figure projected to rise to nearly
six billion by 2027. Not surprisingly, brands invested $230 billion in social media
advertising in 2024 alone (Statista, 2025). The rapid growth of digital marketing and
social media has fundamentally transformed branding (Swaminathan et al., 2020).
Platforms such as Instagram, X (formerly Twitter), and TikTok now enable market -
ers to disseminate real-time brand communications that reach millions of consumers
simultaneously. Prior research demonstrates that a strong brand presence on social
media not only enhances consumer engagement but also fosters more favorable
brand attitudes and drives sales (Akpinar & Berger, 2017; Hollebeek & Macky, 2019;
Liadeli et al., 2023). This raises a critical question: how can brands cultivate an effec-
tive social media presence?
One key factor identified in existing research is the language brands use in their
communications (Kronrod, 2022; Packard & Berger, 2024; Pezzuti, 2023; Pezzuti &
Leonhardt, 2023; Villarroel Ordenes et al., 2019). This insight aligns with the broader
field of brand linguistics, which examines how the language employed by brands
across marketing contexts shapes consumer perceptions, attitudes, and behaviors
(Carnevale et al., 2017). Scholars have examined how brand language influences
consumers in traditional advertising (e.g., McQuarrie & Mick, 1996), brand naming
(Luna et al., 2013), and customer service interactions (Packard et al., 2018). This
extensive literature highlights that even subtle changes in wording can significantly
impact consumer responses.
Although language has long been central to marketing and consumer research,
recent years have witnessed a marked surge in interest and scholarly activity (Pack -
ard & Berger, 2024). This growth has been facilitated by the availability of large-
scale digital data sources (e.g., social media conversations) and advanced analytical
tools (e.g., Wordify; Hovy et al., 2021), which allow researchers to examine consumer
and brand language with unprecedented depth and precision (Boegershausen et al.,
2022). Consistent with this, scholars have begun to investigate how brand language
influences the success of marketing communications in the specific context of social
media platforms.
As in more traditional channels, linguistic cues play a pivotal role in shaping con-
sumer responses to social media brand communications (Kronrod, 2022). Research
in this area draws on core linguistic components such as semantics (the study of
meaning), syntax (the study of sentence structure), and discourse (the study of text)
to understand how consumers react to social media messages. For example, studies
have found that sensory words like “juicy” drive higher engagement with influencer
content (Cascio Rizzo et al., 2023), Instagram and Facebook brand posts with moder-
ate syntactic surprise are more successful (Atalay, El Kihal, & Ellsaesser, 2023), and
responses to social media comments using an assertive rather than a sarcastic tone
improve brand attitudes (Batista et al., 2022). While brand language on social media
remains one of the most underexplored areas within consumer language research
(Packard & Berger, 2024), the quick accumulation of empirical studies over the past
decade is notable (as illustrated in Fig. 2).

However, while the rapid growth of research on brand language in social media
is exciting for the field and consistently demonstrates the importance of linguistic
choices for social media communication success, the existing literature has devel -
oped in a fragmented manner. Studies vary substantially in the linguistic features
examined (e.g., pronouns, emotionality, syntax), the platforms analyzed (e.g., Ins -
tagram, X, TikTok), the empirical approaches adopted (e.g., experiments, field data,
text mining), and the outcome variables used to capture consumer responses (e.g.,
engagement, brand attitudes, purchase intentions). As a result, insights on brand lan-
guage in social media are dispersed across isolated streams of research. This makes it
difficult to assess what is known and identify systematic patterns, thus limiting cumu-
lative knowledge development and delineating clear directions for future research.
To address these challenges, I conducted a systematic literature review of aca -
demic studies on brand language on social media and its impact on consumers. I iden-
tified 51 articles published between 2016 and 2024 in high-quality, peer-reviewed
journals. Consistent with best practices for review research (Palmatier et al., 2018),
my objective is not to adjudicate conflicting effects, but to organize, synthesize,
and integrate a fragmented body of knowledge. Specifically, I mapped how brand
language has been studied across various dimensions, including linguistic features,
empirical approaches, outcome variables, thereby providing conceptual clarity and
a structured agenda for future research. Based on this body of work, I synthesized
existing findings and delineated key themes designed to inform current understand -
ing and guide future research.
This research makes three primary contributions. First, it provides a descriptive
overview of current knowledge on how brands use language on social media and how
this affects consumers, organizing existing findings across seven key content dimen-
sions. Second, by identifying critical themes and research gaps, it generates promis -
ing questions to guide future work in the area of consumer language research. Third,
by synthesizing academic insights on brands’ linguistic choices, it offers practical
guidance to practitioners aiming to craft effective social media messages.
2 Method and data collection
To examine the existing literature, I conducted a systematic literature review follow-
ing established methodologies (Campbell et al., 2025; Palmatier et al., 2018; Tran-
field et al., 2003). In line with recent marketing research (e.g., Branca et al., 2024),
I specifically adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and
Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Page et al., 2021). PRISMA provides a struc-
tured framework that ensures systematic reviews and meta-analyses are performed
and reported in a transparent and replicable manner. Figure 1 illustrates the process,
which I detail below.
For data collection, I searched Web of Science and Scopus with the aim of identi-
fying the most comprehensive set of articles examining brand language use on social
media from a consumer perspective. To this end, I developed the following search
algorithm (where the asterisk captures multiple word endings):

to ensure journal quality, I retained only articles published in Q1 and Q2 business/

Finally, I restricted the sample to articles written in English. After applying these cri-

from Scopus comprising 82 articles.

I merged these datasets and removed duplicates, yielding a unique set of 150 arti-
cles. I then screened these articles based on their titles, abstracts, and journals, apply-
ing four exclusion criteria. To follow, I describe each criterion in detail to ensure
replicability of my procedure (Palmatier et al., 2018).
First, I excluded articles where the independent variable is not a textual cue. By
“textual cue,” I refer to any semantic (e.g., sensory words), grammatical/syntactic
(e.g., pronouns), pragmatic (e.g., sarcastic tone), discourse-level (e.g., informality),
or crosscultural linguistic (e.g., code-switched language) feature that can be identi -
fied, manipulated, or measured within a text. Accordingly, all papers that focus on
brand social media messages but do not focus on specific textual cues were excluded
from the current review. For example, I excluded one paper where the predictor is
“post content” referring to whether a brand post is more socializing (e.g., it includes
polls, giveaways, games) or informative (e.g., it includes information about prices,
quality, performance). While this variable is related to how a post is written, it is not
specifically related to the language used in the post.
Second, I excluded articles where the dependent variable is not a consumer
response. By “consumer response,” I refer to any behavioral (e.g., sharing a brand
post), attitudinal (e.g., purchase intentions), or psychological (trust perceptions) reac-
tion that consumers exhibit toward a brand, message, or marketing stimulus. I am
interested in consumer responses that arise as a consequence of the linguistic choices
made by brands. Some marketing language papers focus on other types of outcomes
that fall outside this scope, such as firm-level financial outcomes or linguistic choices
themselves. For example, I excluded one study in which the dependent variable was
venture funding. Similarly, I excluded a study that analyzed differences in linguistic
choices in social media (dependent variable) between brand ambassadors and pros -
pects (independent variable).
Third, I excluded articles where the message source was not a brand. Following
existing work (e.g., Swaminathan et al., 2020), I define a brand broadly as any type
of “branded entity.” In addition to traditional commercial brands, branded entities
may include platforms (e.g., Uber), locations (e.g., Bologna, Italy), or social move -
ments (e.g., Black Lives Matter). Under this conceptualization, public figures such as
influencers, politicians, and celebrities are also considered (human) brands. Accord -
ingly, I included studies in which any form of brand served as the message source
and produced the textual cue of interest. In contrast, I excluded studies in which the
message source was an entity other than a brand. For example, I excluded papers in
which consumers themselves were the message source.
Fourth, I excluded articles that were conceptual rather than empirical. The ratio -
nale behind this choice is that this review aims to assess how specific textual cues
used by brands on social media shape consumer responses, a task that necessitates
empirical evidence. Conceptual papers offer valuable theoretical insights, but they
do not provide the data needed to evaluate effects, identify patterns, or compare out-
comes across studies.
This process resulted in a sample of 40 articles. To ensure comprehensive cover -
age, I complemented this search with a manual snowballing approach (Campbell
et al., 2025), which identified 11 additional relevant articles. This brought the final
sample to 51 articles.

While I initially screened articles based on their titles, abstracts, and journals, I
analyzed the remaining 51 articles by reading them in full. After reviewing an initial
subset of five articles (approximately 10% of the total sample), I developed an initial
list of article features to be coded across the full sample. I used these features as a
starting point to derive seven “key content insights” that I describe in detail in the
Results section.
Specifically, to identify these dimensions, I followed an iterative inductive-deduc-
tive coding process. First, I conducted open coding on the initial subset of five articles
to identify recurring analytical foci used to study brand language on social media (i.e.,
inductive process). Second, I organized some of the selected recurring analytical foci
into broader conceptual categories (i.e., deductive process), drawing on established
linguistic theory (Kronrod, 2022; Packard & Berger, 2024). In other words, while
some key content dimensions emerged inductively, I derived others deductively.
For example, the “textual cue” dimension (see more details in the Results section)
emerged inductively from close reading of the initial subset of articles, as I observed
that prior studies consistently centered their analyses on identifiable linguistic fea -
tures (e.g., fluency, pronouns, emojis). I then adopted a deductive approach to orga -
nize individual textual cues within the broader “linguistic component” dimension
(e.g., semantics, discourse, grammar/syntax). This back-and-forth process between
data-driven category identification and theory-driven refinement was repeated across
dimensions until the coding scheme stabilized.

3.2.1 Textual cue
By textual cue, I refer to any measurable or identifiable characteristic of language use
within a text, encompassing elements such as words, phrases, structures, or stylistic
markers that collectively shape the form, tone, or function of communication. For
example, concreteness is a textual cue because it captures how specific, tangible, and
perceptible a word or text is (Packard & Berger, 2021). I also consider paralinguistic
elements such as emojis as textual cues (Luangrath et al., 2023). I observed substan-
tial variability, with more than 50 distinct textual cues examined across studies. The
most frequently analyzed cues include emotionality (19.6%), fluency (11.8%) 1, and
pronouns (11.8%).
Emotionality refers to the emotional content conveyed through words and expres-
sions. This linguistic feature has received considerable scholarly attention in con -
sumer language research, possibly explained (also) by the availability of tools capable
of quantifying it, such as the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC; Boyd et al.,
2022), the Valence-Arousal-Dominance dictionary (V AD; Mohammad, 2018), and
the Dictionary of Affect in Language (DAL; Whissell, 2008). Emotionality is typi -
cally conceptualized along two key dimensions: valence and arousal (Berger & Milk-
man, 2012). Valence refers to the emotional direction of the language—whether it
conveys positive emotions (e.g., happiness) or negative ones (e.g., sadness). Arousal,
by contrast, reflects the level of emotional intensity or activation conveyed by the
text, ranging from low to high. Each emotion can be characterized by a unique
combination of these two dimensions. Sadness, for example, is negative and low in
arousal, whereas anger is negative but high in arousal.
Research on brand language on social media has investigated emotionality in
terms of both valence (e.g., Lee, 2021) and arousal (e.g., Cascio Rizzo et al., 2024),
with valence receiving more attention overall. Notably, emotionality is the most fre-
quently studied linguistic feature in the context of human brands. For example, when
influencer content is perceived as high in arousal, it tends to drive greater (lower)
engagement for micro- (macro-) influencers (Cascio Rizzo et al., 2024). When celeb-

Fluency, also referred to as “processing ease” or “text readability,” describes the

or sentence length), and syntactic features (e.g., the number of hierarchical layers

Overall, existing findings highlight the importance of fluency (“the pleasure of
ease;” Topolinski & Reber, 2010) for the success of brand messages. For example,
when social media brand communications are easier to process, consumers are more
likely to like, share, or comment on them (Pancer et al., 2019). Similarly, messages
that are easier to read tend to drive higher consumers’ sustained attention (Berger et
al., 2023). It is worth noting, however, that prior research has examined the effects of
text fluency primarily in relation to engagement outcomes, leaving open the question
of how fluency may influence other consumer responses (e.g., trust, brand attitudes,
purchase intentions).

3.2.2 Linguistic component
Linguistic components constitute the foundational domains of linguistic analysis that
collectively explain how language encodes and communicates meaning (Kronrod,
2022). For example, grammar/syntax is a linguistic component as it encompasses the
rules and principles that govern how words are arranged into phrases, clauses, and
sentences (Packard & Berger, 2024). Through this review, I identified five key lin -
guistic components (see Table 4 for definitions). Among these, semantics emerges as
the most frequently studied component, appearing in 54.9% of the reviewed articles.
This is followed by discourse (25.5%) and grammar/syntax (21.6%).
Semantics focuses on the meaning of individual words (Palmer, 1981). How do
consumers construct meaning when exposed to brands’ word choices? How are they
influenced by this meaning? This is what studying semantics in marketing means.
For example, using a negation such as “Don’t wait to subscribe to our newsletter” as
opposed to a positive construction like “Proceed to subscribe to our newsletter” can
communicate to consumers the meaning that the brand is more powerful (Pezzuti &
Leonhardt, 2023). Power meaning is also heightened when brands use words denot -
ing certainty like “always” or “forever” in social media communications (Pezzuti
et al., 2021). In the influencer marketing context, using sensory words like “tasty”
rather than non-sensory words like “good” signals to consumers that the influencer

ment (Cascio Rizzo et al., 2023). Notably, fluency and emotionality, the two most

is not always the case: analyzing individual emotion-laden words reflects a seman -

While semantics examines the meaning of individual words, discourse shifts focus
to the meaning of the broader text: how sequences of sounds, words, and sentences
combine into messages (Östman & Virtanen, 2022). Marketing research on dis -
course investigates how the flow of language across sentences influences consumer
responses. For instance, Gretry et al. ( 2017) demonstrate that text informality can
increase brand trust, but only when consumers are already familiar with the brand.
Similarly, Barcelos, Dantas, and Sénécal ( 2018) show that the success of using a
human (vs. corporate) tone on social media depends on whether consumers pursue
hedonic or utilitarian goals. Beyond formality and tone, other discourse styles can
also shape consumer reactions. For example, Liu et al. ( 2020) find that posts with
a high empathetic tone tend to receive more likes, comments, and shares, while an
overly analytical tone can reduce engagement.
Finally, grammar/syntax concerns the structural rules governing word forms
and their arrangement within sentences (Givón, 1979). In the context of consumer
research, examining grammar and syntax means analyzing how the composition and
positioning of words shape message effectiveness (Kronrod, 2022). For example,
Atalay et al. (2023) show that syntactic variations—such as saying “Apply today to
join a great team!” instead of “Join a great team, apply today!”—can meaningfully
influence consumer reactions. In particular, messages that contain moderate syntactic

3.2.3 Focal language
Language use also varies by country and nationality. While the vast majority of stud-
ies in this review (84.3%) analyze English-language content, a small subset focuses
on other languages. Specifically, 9.8% of studies examine Chinese, and four addi -
tional studies each investigate a different language: Spanish, Arabic, German, and
Ladin.
Building on the present review, I argue that it would be important to move beyond
English in research on brand language. First, although the relevance of English-lan -
guage studies is self-evident, it is important to recognize that approximately 75%
of the global population speaks a language other than English—either as their first
language or exclusively (International Center for Language Studies, 2025). Second,
linguistic structures, cultural norms, and symbolic systems differ substantially across
languages, influencing both the production and perception of language (Kollmeier
et al., 2015). Take Italian, for instance. Italian tends to favor longer and more syn -
tactically complex sentences than English, which may shape how Italian consumers
interpret brand messages. Additionally, phonetic differences are notable: some Eng -
lish sounds do not exist in Italian. For example, the digraph “ch” is consistently pro-
nounced as /k/ in Italian, unlike the English /tʃ/ pronunciation (as in “cheese”). These
phonological differences may affect how brand messages are received, particularly in
audio and video content on social media platforms.
In line with the view proposed here, the reviewed existing research underscores the
importance of examining language-specific and culture-specific effects. For example,
in the context of Chinese language, Chang et al. (2019) find that first-person narration
in social media brand messages increases engagement by enhancing social belong -
ing motives, whereas third-person narration boosts engagement by appealing to self-
enhancement motives. These motivations are deeply embedded in cultural values and
may thus vary across focal languages. Furthermore, recent work by Hassan and Wang
(2024) reveals that not only do consumer responses differ depending on the language
used by brands but brands themselves adopt distinct linguistic strategies based on the
focal language of communication. For example, brands have been found to use inti -
mate address cues when targeting Chinese consumers, code-mixing and dialect when
addressing Egyptian audiences, and colloquial expressions in messages intended for
a global audience. Such findings highlight the need to study linguistic cues within
their cultural and linguistic context.

3.2.4 Brand type
I distinguish between corporate brands (e.g., traditional product brands) and human
brands (e.g., influencers), as the impact of language may vary depending on the
source of the message. Human brands, for example, may be perceived as psychologi-
cally closer and more relatable to consumers than corporations, which can enhance
the perceived authenticity and persuasive power of their language. Among the 51
studies reviewed, 10 (19.6%) focus on human brands. While influencers represent the
majority of cases (7 out of 10), the literature also includes athletes, celebrities, and
streamers. For example, Fox and Stafford (2021) find that athletes who use language
reflecting authenticity and clout receive more engagement, with these effects moder-
ated by the athlete’s performance success. Aleti et al. ( 2019) demonstrate that emo-
tional expression can increase word-of-mouth for celebrities, while Ma et al. ( 2024)
show that such emotionality can enhance both sales and livestream performance for
streamers.
Although corporate and human brands constitute the two dominant categories in
the existing literature, social media environments feature a broader range of brand
subtypes that warrant greater scholarly attention. These include, among others, plat -
form brands (e.g., Vinted), cause or nonprofit brands (e.g., Black Lives Matter), and
place brands (e.g., I Amsterdam; Swaminathan et al., 2020). Among the analyzed
papers focusing on corporate brands, only two (4.8%) explicitly distinguish between
different corporate brand subtypes, both focusing on place brands (Lonardi et al.,
2024; Pachucki et al., 2022). First, Lonardi and colleagues ( 2024) find that the use
of minority languages in place brand social media posts increases perceptions of
place authenticity and emotional experience, leading to more favorable consumer
responses. Second, Pachucki et al. (2022) examine how the language adopted by place
brands changed following the 2020 pandemic and how these changes (e.g., increased
expressions of uncertainty and emotionality) affected consumer engagement.
While existing research provides valuable insights into language use by corporate
brands, a more nuanced differentiation among corporate brand types could meaning-
fully advance consumer language research. Platform brands, for instance, are distinc-
tive in terms of the frequency and regularity of consumer interaction, as consumers
often engage with these brands on a daily basis. This reliance on repeated interac -
tion suggests that platform brands’ social media communications may be particularly
consequential in shaping consumer responses over time, and that specific linguistic
features may differentially influence their effectiveness. Similarly, cause or nonprofit
brands might be particularly associated with heightened expectations of authenticity,
ethicality, and moral credibility (Morhart et al., 2015), which may alter how consum-
ers interpret and respond to their linguistic choices.
More broadly, different corporate brand types are likely to evoke distinct con -
sumer expectations and mental associations (Aaker, 1997). These associative differ-
ences and the distinct brand personalities tied to various corporate brand subtypes
suggest that consumers may interpret (and respond to) brand language in systemati -
cally different ways depending on brand type.

3.2.5 Modality
Language can be conveyed in either written or spoken form. On social media, brands
routinely employ both modalities—written language in captions and post descrip -
tions, and spoken language in video content. This review reveals that 11.8% of stud-
ies examined spoken language (with three studies focusing exclusively on spoken
language and three analyzing both spoken and written language), while the remaining
88.2% focused solely on written content.
Research on spoken language has primarily concentrated on human brands (e.g.,
influencers), with studies analyzing audio content from platforms like Instagram,
TikTok, and YouTube (Cascio Rizzo et al., 2023, 2024; Holiday et al., 2023; Lee &
Theokary, 2021; Ma et al., 2024). One notable exception is Wahid et al. (2023), who
examine spoken language in the context of corporate brands on TikTok. Their find -
ings indicate that, for global (smartphone) brands, informational content generates
higher engagement than emotional content, that English or code-switching increases
engagement overall, and that nonverbal elements have little effect on engagement.
Although this work begins to shed light on how non-human brands use spoken lan -
guage on social media, its findings also underscore the need for further research
examining how distinct communicative modalities operate in corporate social media
communications.
3.2.6 Empirical approach
As previously discussed, the increased availability of large-scale digital datasets,
combined with advances in analytical techniques, has significantly expanded the pos-
sibilities for studying language in social media contexts. This trend is evident in the
empirical strategies adopted in the literature: while 21.6% have used purely experi -
mental designs, 74.5% of studies have relied on observational data.
Although controlled experiments, where language stimuli are manipulated in iso-
lation, remain a valuable tool in brand language research, there is a clear and growing
shift toward multi-method investigations (12 articles in this review, e.g., Atalay et al.,
2023; Berger et al., 2023; Cascio Rizzo et al., 2023; Lee, 2021; Pancer et al., 2019).
These studies integrate experimental approaches with analyses of unstructured field
data, such as brand-generated content or user interactions on platforms like Face -
book, X, Instagram, and TikTok. Depending on the research question, field data may
include text-based posts, images, or videos.
The integration of multiple methodologies allows researchers to leverage the
complementary strengths of different approaches. Experimental studies offer strong
internal validity and causal inference, while field data provide external validity and
generalizability to real-world settings. When combined, these approaches help over-
come the limitations inherent in each method, such as the artificiality of laboratory
settings or the correlational nature of observational data, thereby producing more
robust and meaningful insights into how brand language functions in digital environ-
ments (Packard & Berger, 2024).

3.2.7 Outcome
Finally, in line with the predominant empirical approaches, most studies (64.7%)
have examined engagement as the primary outcome variable, typically operational -
ized as a composite of social media metrics such as likes, comments, and shares
(Pezzuti, 2023). This focus is well-justified, as engagement is both a well-established
predictor of sales (Liadeli et al., 2023) and a readily available metric in real-world
social media data.
That said, a growing body of research is beginning to explore outcomes beyond
engagement. Specifically, several studies highlight that linguistic choices can shape
a wider range of brand perceptions and consumer responses, such as purchase
intentions (5.9%) and various brand evaluations (15.7%). For example, Gretry et
al. (2017) show that informal language enhances brand trust for familiar brands but
diminishes it for unfamiliar ones, depending on expectations about communication
norms. Similarly, Lee (2021) finds that emotional language in brand messages lowers
perceptions of brand status, again driven by normative expectations around brand
communication. Focusing on minority language, Lonardi et al. (2024) further reveal
that linguistic cues can impact destination brand image through increased perceptions
of place authenticity.
3.2.8 Integrating key content insights
Taken together, the seven key content insights reveal a research domain characterized
less by contradictory empirical evidence than by patterned concentration and frag -
mentation across analytical dimensions. More specifically, the reviewed literature
exhibits systematic asymmetries. Across studies, linguistic cues such as emotional -
ity, fluency, and pronouns consistently emerge as influential predictors of consumer
responses such as engagement, suggesting broad convergence in the direction of
effects. At the same time, this apparent consistency reflects a strong focus on a lim -
ited subset of linguistic features rather than comprehensive theoretical agreement
about the full range of effects of brand language on social media.
Similarly, semantic features are studied far more frequently than pragmatic or
crosscultural components. Written English-language content dominates over spoken
or multilingual communication. Moreover, engagement outcomes prevail over atti -
tudinal and psychological responses. Finally, brand type is often treated in coarse
categories that overlook meaningful heterogeneity. As a result, findings tend to be
context-sensitive and contingent on factors such as brand type, modality, platform
norms, and consumer expectations, rather than universally generalizable.
Thus, the findings presented here do not suggest that the primary challenge facing
this literature is not resolving conflicting findings but rather integrating dispersed
insights that have accumulated across parallel research streams. By synthesizing
prior work along seven key content insights, this review provides a structured lens
for understanding how brand language on social media has been studied to date,
highlights the limitations of existing knowledge, and suggests where theoretical and
empirical development is most needed. These integrative insights directly inform the
research questions outlined in the following section.

4 Avenues for future research
In light of the reviewed literature and the key content dimensions identified, I propose
a set of research questions aimed at advancing consumer language research within
the domain of brand linguistics (Carnevale et al., 2017) in the social media context.
These questions are informed by the identified gap in the literature, as well as cur -
rent research/societal trends (e.g., the use of Artificial Intelligence in marketing),
and classic theoretical frameworks (e.g., persuasion knowledge; Friestad & Wright,
1994). Importantly, I aimed to identify research questions that are particularly well
suited to the social media context rather than to linguistics more broadly. Social
media platforms constitute a unique communicative environment, and it is therefore
critical to focus on research questions that explicitly account for this uniqueness.
Moreover, although the existing literature offers substantial opportunities for
future research, as highlighted by review, I focus on a subset of research questions
that could be particularly interesting for the social media context and empirically
feasible. Put differently, while numerous research questions could emerge from the
review, I examine a limited subset in depth for reasons of parsimony and strategic
focus. Specifically, I focus on questions that (I think) are both compelling and readily
investigable.
In several cases, these questions are interrelated, reflecting the interconnected
nature of the content insights from which they emerge. For example, the suggestion
to adopt a crosscultural linguistic perspective naturally aligns with the call to study
focal languages beyond English. A summary of these research questions is presented

attention devoted to other linguistic components such as pragmatics and crosscultural
linguistics. This imbalance may be partly explained by the fact that semantic features
are often easier to manipulate or detect, as they can involve relatively minor linguis-
tic changes. However, other linguistic components are also amenable to systematic
investigation, especially given the growing availability of digital tools for text analy-
sis. As a result, important questions remain unanswered regarding how consumers
infer brand intent beyond literal word meaning. I argue that social media environ -
ments provide a particularly fertile ground for examining such questions.
First of all, given the unique characteristics of social media platforms compared to
more traditional marketing channels, brands increasingly communicate in conversa -
tional ways that resemble everyday consumer interactions, often adopting informal
or playful tones (Gretry et al., 2017). In this context, brands may rely on indirect or
suggestive language to prompt consumer action (e.g., “You know what to do ”)
instead of being openly promotional (e.g., “You should try this new product”). Future
research could investigate whether such pragmatic cues are perceived as playful,
persuasive, or manipulative, and how these perceptions shape consumer responses.
Moreover, scholars could examine how the abstractness of brand language influ -
ences consumer interpretation and whether this effect varies across product catego -
ries, consumption goals, or levels of consumer expertise. On social media, brands
can convey messages using varying degrees of abstractness. For example, they may
announce a product launch through song lyrics, memes, or emoji-based messages.
Although prior research suggests that concrete language often enhances perceived
helpfulness and satisfaction (Packard & Berger, 2021), more abstract forms of com-
munication may be more effective in social media contexts where playfulness, cre -
ativity, and cultural resonance are valued.
Finally, as brands increasingly rely on artificial intelligence to generate social
media content (often under time pressure and to reach culturally diverse audiences;
Kalum, 2025), an important open question concerns language and cultural alignment.

written in their native language compared to content produced in English, and how

4.3 Focal language
Despite the global reach of social media platforms, existing research on brand lan -
guage remains heavily centered on English-language content. The current literature
review indicates that 84.3% of prior studies on brand language on social media focus
exclusively on English (English-language messages and English-speaking consum -
ers), highlighting a significant gap in understanding how brand communication oper-
ates in multilingual contexts. In light of this imbalance, I identify two promising
directions for future research. The first concerns how English-language brand mes -
sages are perceived by non-English-speaking consumers. The second concerns how
English-language brand messages that incorporate code-switching are evaluated by
non-English-speaking consumers.
Both research avenues are motivated by two fundamental considerations. First,
compared to traditional advertising, brand messages on social media have a global
reach: Italian, Dutch, or Japanese consumers can easily follow U.S.-based brands,
read their content, and engage with it. This globalization of brand communication
raises important questions about how linguistic choices shape engagement and
broader consumer responses across linguistic boundaries. Second, languages differ
substantially in their grammatical distinctions, morphological inflection, and syn -
tactic structures. For example, Finnish is often characterized as particularly complex
for non-native speakers due to its rich inflectional system (Latomaa, 1998). Such
structural differences may meaningfully shape how consumers perceive and process
brand messages written in a non-native language.
Accordingly, a question for future research is how non-English-speaking consum-
ers (especially those used to linguistically complex languages) respond to English
brand messages. How do features such as passive voice, syntactic complexity, or
linguistic formality influence brand perceptions from the perspective of non-Eng -
lish-speaking audiences? While linguistic simplicity might be the “go-to” on social
media, consumers whose native languages encode greater grammatical or morpho -
logical complexity may instead prefer more sophisticated forms of brand commu -
nication, which could influence perceptions of brand credibility, brand competence,
brand status.
An additional question for future inquiry is how non-English-speaking consumers
respond to global brands’ use of code-switching (Gardner-Chloros, 2009). Although
switching between languages may signal inclusivity or global orientation, it may also
be perceived as inauthentic or culturally insensitive (Ahn et al., 2017). Understanding
when and for whom code-switching enhances versus undermines brand evaluations,
and how these effects vary across linguistic and cultural contexts, thus represents a
rich opportunity for future research.

4.4 Brand type
An additional promising direction for future research concerns the role of brand type
in shaping how consumers interpret and respond to brand language on social media.
Prior work in consumer behavior and marketing recognizes that brands differ mean -
ingfully in their social roles (Fournier & Eckhardt, 2019; Swaminathan et al., 2020).
However, much of the existing research on brand language implicitly treats brands
as homogeneous communicators. Given that virtually all brand types are active on
social media platforms, from human brands to nonprofit brands, this raises impor -
tant unanswered questions about whether (and how) linguistic strategies should vary
across different types of brands.
One question that could be relevant to investigate concerns platforms such as
Vinted, Airbnb, or Instagram. That is, platform brands (Wichmann et al., 2022).
These brands primarily function as facilitators of interaction and exchange between
users rather than as direct providers of products or services (Perren & Kozinets,
2018). Because platform brands are embedded in consumers’ everyday routines and
enable repeated, ongoing interactions among users (e.g., buying, selling, coordinat -
ing), social media communication can be particularly relevant for them. Indeed, on
social media, platform brands do not merely promote offerings but also engage in
ongoing conversations that can help and assist consumers who use their platforms
on a daily basis. For example, consider a buyer who engages in daily conversations
with a seller on Vinted to negotiate the price of a sweater. This buyer interacts with
the platform on a regular basis and may require the platform’s support or assistance in
day-to-day use. In this example, social media platforms therefore would allow Vinted
to communicate directly and efficiently with this buyer (and with other users facing
similar issues).
Given their distinctive role and function, certain linguistic components or textual
cues may be especially relevant for platform brands compared to other brand types.
In particular, because platform brands rely heavily on trust and open communica -
tion (Casidy et al., 2022; Sedighi et al., 2022), language complexity or sophistica -
tion may play a consequential role in shaping consumer responses. Future research
could therefore examine whether more complex or sophisticated language enhances
perceptions of competence and legitimacy for platform brands, or whether linguistic
simplicity instead facilitates ease of use and engagement by reducing cognitive effort.
A second question focuses on how consumers interpret emotional language when
it is used by different types of brands in the context of social media. Social media
platforms constitute a distinctive communication environment in which brand mes -
sages are often highly personal and emotionally expressive (Boyd, 2010), and prior
research shows that such emotional expression plays a central role in shaping mes -
sage diffusion (Berger & Milkman, 2012). Within this environment, emotional
appeals by nonprofit brands may be perceived as particularly appropriate and authen-
tic. At the same time, social media blurs the boundaries between interpersonal com -
munication and strategic persuasion, thereby heightening consumers’ sensitivity to
brands’ underlying motives (Campbell & Kirmani, 2000; Campbell et al., 2013). As
a result, the same emotional language that should enhance perceived authenticity for
nonprofit brands may be interpreted as manipulative or self-serving when employed

by for-profit brands. Understanding how emotional language is differentially evalu -
ated across brand types on social media may therefore shed light on when emotional
cues enhance persuasion versus when they trigger skepticism or activate persuasion
knowledge.
4.5 Modality
Although social media platforms increasingly integrate written, spoken, and audio -
visual content, most of the research reviewed on brand language continues to focus
on written text when examining corporate brands. With only one exception (Wahid et
al., 2023), studies that investigate spoken language have primarily focused on human
brands. Yet corporate brands also communicate via spoken language on social media
platforms such as Instagram and TikTok, using human representatives (e.g., employ-
ees, creators, or social media managers), voiceovers (e.g., human or synthetic voices
narrating videos), or mascots (e.g., well-known brand characters that take over social
media accounts). Accordingly, I suggest that studying spoken language in the context
of corporate brands represents a fruitful avenue for future research. I identify three
potential subareas within this domain.
First, the modality through which linguistic cues are delivered may influence the
extent to which corporate brand language activates persuasion knowledge. Future
research could therefore investigate whether the effectiveness of persuasive linguistic
strategies (e.g., informal language; Gretry et al., 2017) varies systematically across
written and spoken modalities. For example, studies could examine whether spoken
language fosters greater perceived similarity between the brand and the consumer,
thereby attenuating persuasion knowledge and reducing attributions of ulterior
motives (Friestad & Wright, 1994), compared to equivalent written messages.
Second, from an information-processing perspective, future research could exam-
ine whether written language indeed prompts deeper cognitive elaboration than
spoken language due to its more deliberate and self-paced nature, and how such
differences affect memory, learning, and message comprehension in social media
contexts (MacInnis & Price, 1987). Importantly, scholars could also explore bound-
ary conditions under which spoken language may compensate for lower elaboration
through vividness, emotional expressiveness, or narrative delivery.
Third, future research could investigate the relationship between paratextual cues
in writing and paralinguistic cues in speech. For instance, studies could examine
whether written intensifiers, emojis, or punctuation marks have functional equiva -
lents in spoken language (e.g., pitch, pauses, or intonation), and whether these cues
are processed similarly by consumers across modalities. Relatedly, future research
could explore whether intensifiers such as “really,” “super,” or “absolutely” are per-
ceived as more authentic or exaggerated when expressed orally rather than in writing.

5 General discussion
The increasing availability of large-scale textual datasets and automated linguistic
analysis tools has opened new avenues for examining subtle linguistic cues in brand
communication (Berger et al., 2020; Boegershausen et al., 2022; Packard & Berger,
2024). While marketing scholars have long recognized the role of language in tradi -
tional domains such as advertising, brand naming, and customer service, the rise of
social media has heightened interest in the language brands use in digital environ -
ments. What happens when corporate brands announce a new product using words
that convey certainty like “always?” How do consumers respond to influencers who
use sensory terms like “tasty?” Do consumers engage more with brands that place
sales-related words at the beginning or at the end of a tweet? These are just a few of
the many questions that researchers have begun to examine in the emerging domain
of brand linguistics on social media (Pezzuti et al., 2021; Cascio Rizzo et al., 2023;
Jalali & Papatla, 2019).
The present research offers a systematic review of 51 academic articles on brand
language on social media published between 2016 and 2024 across 24 top-tier jour -
nals. First, this review documents a surge in scholarly attention to brand language
in digital contexts. The sheer volume of publications within this short period under -
scores the growing importance of this topic. Second, the reviewed literature consis -
tently shows that even subtle variations in the language brands use on social media
can meaningfully shape consumer responses—from increased intentions to like a
post to greater willingness to try the brand’s products. Third, despite this progress, the
linguistic richness of brand communication on social media remains only partially
explored as advances in data accessibility and computational methods now make
it possible to investigate a broader and more nuanced set of linguistic features than
previously studied. This gap is also mirrored in practice: many social media manag -
ers still struggle to craft compelling brand messages, often producing content that is
ignored by consumers (Farace et al., 2025; Villarroel Ordenes et al., 2019). Together,
these insights highlight the continued need to examine which linguistic choices
enhance the effectiveness of brand communication in digital spaces.
5.1 Theoretical contribution
This systematic literature review makes two primary theoretical contributions. First,
by examining 51 academic articles published in high-quality journals, this research
systematizes existing knowledge on brand language on social media. It represents
the first comprehensive effort to organize and critically review this emerging sub -
field, complementing existing reviews on the broader domain of consumer language
research (e.g., Packard & Berger, 2024). Existing studies on brand language on social
media exhibit great heterogeneity in terms of linguistic features examined, social
media platforms analyzed, empirical approaches employed, and so forth. As a result,
insights are fragmented, making it difficult to assess cumulative knowledge and iden-
tify systematic patterns. This systematic literature review organizes, synthesizes,
and integrates this body of work to provide conceptual clarity. Specifically, I catego-
rize prior research on brand linguistic choices in social media communications into

seven key content dimensions, providing scholars with a structured overview of the
current state of knowledge. Identifying these dimensions can allow researchers to
clearly grasp what has been studied and discern broader trends in consumer language
research. Moreover, the synthesis of findings underscores the theoretical and practi -
cal relevance of studying linguistic cues in marketing communication, demonstrat -
ing how even subtle variations in language can meaningfully influence consumer
responses such as engagement with social media posts, brand evaluations, and pur -
chase intentions.
Second, this review identifies critical gaps in the literature and, building on the
seven dimensions, proposes a set of research questions to guide future inquiry. While
the growing scholarly interest in brand language on social media is evident (par -
ticularly with 2023 and 2024 emerging as the most productive years) much remains
unknown about how consumers perceive and react to specific linguistic strategies
in brand communication, especially given the increasing availability of large-scale
datasets and advanced analytical tools for studying language. For example, among
the various textual cues analyzed, most studies have concentrated on three topics
(i.e., emotionality, fluency, and pronouns) despite the vast range of additional lin -
guistic features available to brands. Similarly, while existing work has primarily
focused on the semantic, grammatical/syntactic, and discourse levels of language,
considerably less attention has been devoted to pragmatics, crosscultural linguis -
tics, and phonetics, which are equally foundational components of linguistic mean -
ing (Kronrod, 2022). In terms of consumer responses, current research has heavily
emphasized behavioral engagement metrics (e.g., likes, shares, and comments),
which are indeed critical for marketers (Liadeli et al., 2023). However, emerging
findings also point to the importance of linguistic cues in shaping other outcomes,
such as brand trust (Gretry et al., 2017) and intentions to support the brand (Park et
al., 2023). This review therefore not only consolidates existing knowledge but also
illuminates underexplored areas and outlines promising avenues for future research
in the field of brand linguistics on social media, bridging together brand linguis -
tics and psychological/sociopsychological theoretical frameworks (e.g., persuasion
knowledge, information processing, brand personality; Aaker, 1997; Friestad &
Wright, 1994; MacInnis & Price, 1987).
5.2 Managerial contribution
This research also contributes to marketing practice. By summarizing and organizing
existing findings in brand linguistics in the social media context, it provides social
media managers with a practical framework and set of guidelines to craft more effec-
tive brand messages. Specifically, I structure the reviewed literature across seven
key content dimensions that, while theoretically grounded, also hold clear practi -
cal relevance. For example, by mapping which textual cues are associated with spe -
cific consumer outcomes (e.g., engagement, trust, purchase intention), this review
helps practitioners identify which linguistic strategies are most likely to succeed, and
which may backfire, on social media platforms.
Despite the growing importance of social media in brand communication, many
managers continue to struggle with crafting messages that resonate. Brand posts

often fail to generate engagement or get lost in the noise of increasingly saturated
platforms, where companies of all types and sizes compete for attention (Villarroel et
al., 2019). The findings from this work underscore that language is not merely a vehi-
cle for content, but a strategic tool that managers can deliberately shape to enhance
effectiveness. In this sense, the present review offers not only a theoretical synthesis
but also a practical guide for leveraging language more intentionally in digital brand
communication. In what follows, I outline a set of practical insights that may be use-
ful in guiding marketers’ linguistic choices on social media. Although these insights
are not exhaustive, I selected them because they (i) recur across multiple studies
in the reviewed literature and (ii) lend themselves to relatively easy-to-implement
managerial guidance.
First, the effectiveness of linguistic strategies is contingent to brands’ characteris-
tics such as brand familiarity, brand involvement, or brand status. For example, Gre-
try et al. (2017) demonstrate that informal language increases brand trust only when
the brand is already familiar, but backfires for unfamiliar brands due to violations of
social norms. Similarly, Lee (2021) finds that emotional brand language lowers per -
ceived brand status, but this occurs particularly for high-status brands. Accordingly,
marketers and social media managers should carefully align linguistic strategies with
the specific type of brand they represent.
Second, the review highlights that optimizing for engagement is not equivalent to
optimizing for trust, status, or persuasion. While engagement metrics dominate the
literature, several studies show that linguistic cues that increase likes, comments, or
shares can have neutral or negative effects on deeper brand evaluations. For instance,
Lonardi et al. ( 2024) find that minority language use improves destination brand
image only through increased perceptions of authenticity, indicating that effects
are mediated rather than unconditional. These findings imply that managers should
align linguistic choices with specific campaign objectives rather than assuming that
engagement-maximizing language is always desirable.
Third, when engagement is the primary goal, the literature offers relatively con -
sistent guidance regarding fluency and readability. Multiple studies show that easier-
to-process brand messages generate stronger engagement outcomes. For example,
Pancer et al. ( 2019) demonstrate that more readable Facebook posts receive more
likes, comments, and shares, and Berger et al. ( 2023) show that fluency-related lin -
guistic features enhance sustained attention. However, the review also reveals that
fluency has been examined primarily in relation to engagement, leaving its effects on
other consumer responses. Accordingly, managers should treat fluency as a reliable
engagement lever, while remaining cautious about generalizing its benefits to other
outcomes.
Fourth, research on emojis and textual paralanguage suggests that these cues can
enhance engagement, but only under specific conditions. McShane et al. ( 2021), for
example, find that emojis increase engagement by enhancing perceived playfulness,
with effects depending on how emojis interact with accompanying text. Along a simi-
lar line, Maiberger, Schindler, and Koschate-Fischer (2024) further show that facial
emojis increase persuasion by amplifying emotional arousal, but only when they reit-
erate rather than replace verbal expressions. Collectively, these findings indicate that
emojis and other forms of textual paralanguage can have persuasive effects, but also

caution marketers against using them indiscriminately, suggesting that their effec -
tiveness is highly context dependent rather than universally beneficial.
Fifth, the review underscores that linguistic effects do not generalize seamlessly
across languages and cultures. Although most studies focus on English-language
content, evidence from non-English contexts shows systematic variation. Chang et
al. (2019) demonstrate that narrative person effects differ in Chinese-language brand
communications depending on brand image and motivational orientation. Overall,
the existing literature cautions managers against directly transferring linguistic “best
practices” across linguistic or cultural contexts without adaptation.
Finally, as previously discussed, the literature reveals a strong modality asymme-
try. The vast majority of reviewed studies examine written language, with relatively
few focusing on spoken language in video-based social media (e.g., Cascio Rizzo et
al., 2023, 2024). As a result, managers should recognize that current evidence-based
guidance is stronger for text-based communication (e.g., captions, tweets) than for
spoken or audiovisual content and should validate text-derived linguistic strategies
when applying them to video-centric platforms.
Overall, this review suggests that linguistic strategies can meaningfully enhance
social media performance, but only when deployed in alignment with brand type,
communication goals, cultural context, and modality. Treating brand language as a
contingent strategic resource, rather than a set of universally effective tactics, repre -
sents the most actionable managerial insight emerging from the literature.
5.3 Limitations and future research
This research is not without limitations, which I hope could offer promising directions
for future work. First, my review focuses on academic articles published between
2016 and 2024. While I found no relevant peer-reviewed articles published before
2016, important studies may have already emerged in 2025. I chose to conclude data
collection in 2024 to align with the approach taken by prior systematic reviews in
marketing (e.g., Branca et al., 2024) and to maintain consistency with the PRISMA
framework. However, future reviews could extend the time frame to include more
recent contributions and assess how the field continues to evolve. Given the growing
interest in brand language on social media, I anticipate that additional review efforts
will be necessary to capture ongoing developments.
Second, in line with established review protocols (Page et al., 2021), I limited the
scope to peer-reviewed journal articles published in English. While this focus ensures
consistency and rigor, it excludes potentially valuable insights from non-English
publications. This is especially relevant in a field like brand linguistics, where cross-
linguistic variation and non-English-speaking contexts are critical, as suggested here.
Future research could thus expand the scope of systematic reviews to include aca -
demic work published in other languages, thereby enriching our understanding of
global brand communication strategies.
Finally, I identified seven key content dimensions to organize the reviewed lit -
erature. While these dimensions offer both theoretical and managerial value, other
classification schemes may yield additional insights. For example, future research
could explore how findings vary by product category, industry sector, or social media
