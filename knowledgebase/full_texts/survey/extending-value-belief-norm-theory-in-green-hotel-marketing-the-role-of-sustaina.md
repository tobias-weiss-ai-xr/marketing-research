This literature review synthesizes recent scholarship (2018–2026) on green hotel
marketing and consumer behavior to establish the theoretical foundation for
extending Value-Belief-Norm (VBN) theory. The review examines five intercon-
nected domains: (1) green hotel visit intentions and willingness to pay price pre-
miums, (2) applications and limitations of VBN theory in tourism contexts, (3) the
role of eco-guilt in pro-environmental behaviors, (4) green pride and its authentic
versus hubristic dimensions in sustainable consumption, and (5) hotel sustain -
ability claims and their effects on consumer trust and responses. The synthesis
reveals that while VBN theory has been successfully integrated with other frame-
works to predict green hotel patronage, significant gaps remain in understanding
the emotional mechanisms, particularly anticipated eco-guilt and differentiated
forms of pride, that mediate between sustainability communications and behav-
ioral outcomes. Current research demonstrates that sustainability claims, certifi-
cations, and message characteristics significantly influence consumer trust and
willingness to pay, yet the psychological pathways through which these com -
munications activate moral emotions remain underexplored. Conceptually, the
review extends VBN by integrating anticipated eco-guilt and differentiated forms
of green pride into the VBN pathway and by explicitly modelling how hotel sus-
tainability claims trigger these moral emotions to shape visit intentions and price
premium willingness.

1 Introduction
The global hospitality industry faces mounting pressure to adopt sustainable practices as
environmental concerns intensify and consumer awareness of climate change grows. Green
hotels accommodations that implement environmentally responsible practices such as energy
conservation, water management, waste reduction, and sustainable sourcing—have emerged
as a significant market segment. Understanding what motivates tourists to choose green hotels
and pay premium prices for sustainable accommodations has become a critical research prior-
ity for both academics and practitioners. In this context, I distinguish between two key

behavioral outcomes examined in this review. Visit intention refers to
a traveler’s stated likelihood of choosing a green hotel over non-green
alternatives, whereas willingness to pay a price premium refers to the
additional monetary amount a traveler is prepared to pay for a green
hotel compared to a comparable conventional hotel. These outcomes
are related but conceptually distinct and may be driven by partially
different cognitive, emotional, and trust pathways.
Value-Belief-Norm (VBN) theory, which extends the Norm
Activation Model by incorporating personal values and ecological
worldviews, has emerged as a prominent framework for explaining
pro-environmental behaviors in tourism contexts. However, recent
scholarship suggests that VBN theory’s focus on cognitive pathways
may overlook important emotional mechanisms that influence green
consumption decisions. Specifically, anticipated moral emotions such
as eco-guilt and pride may serve as critical mediators between sustain-
ability-related stimuli and behavioral intentions, yet these emotional
pathways remain underexplored in the green hotel literature. This
review therefore proposes a focused emotional extension of VBN in
green hotel contexts. In particular, I extend the traditional value →
belief → norm → behavior chain by (a) integrating anticipated eco-
guilt and green pride (with a distinction between authentic and
hubristic pride) as explicit mediators between personal norms and the
two behavioral outcomes: visit intention and willingness to pay a pre-
mium, and (b) positioning hotel sustainability claims and their per-
ceived credibility and authenticity as external stimuli that activate
these emotional pathways and interact with green trust.
This literature review addresses these gaps by synthesizing
research across five interconnected domains: (1) the determinants of
green hotel visit intentions and willingness to pay price premiums, (2)
the applications and limitations of VBN theory in tourism and hospi-
tality research, (3) the role of eco-guilt in pro-environmental and tour-
ism behaviors, (4) green pride and the distinction between authentic
and hubristic pride in sustainable consumption, and (5) hotel sustain-
ability claims, eco-labels, and their effects on consumer trust and
responses. By clearly separating visit intentions from willingness to
pay a price premium and by specifying how anticipated eco-guilt and
differentiated forms of pride are integrated into the VBN pathway, this
review establishes the theoretical foundation for an emotionally
enriched VBN framework that explains how sustainability communi-
cations shape green hotel patronage and premium payment
willingness.
1.1 Review approach
This paper adopts an SLR-light review approach to synthesize
recent scholarship on green hotel marketing and VBN extensions. I
focused on peer-reviewed journal articles published between 2018 and
2026, identified through searches in Scopus, Web of Science, and
Google Scholar using combinations of keywords such as “green hotel, ”
“eco-hotel, ” “Value-Belief-Norm theory, ” “eco-guilt, ” “pride, ” “moral
emotions, ” “sustainability claims, ” and “greenwashing. ” Studies were
included if they examined (a) green hotel visit intentions or willing-
ness to pay a price premium, (b) applications or extensions of VBN in
tourism or hospitality, (c) eco-guilt or pride in pro-environmental or
tourism decisions, or (d) hotel sustainability claims, eco-labels, green
trust, or related communication effects. Conceptual papers, empirical
surveys, experiments, and mixed-method studies were all considered,
but conference proceedings and non-scholarly reports were excluded.
This search and screening process yielded a core set of recent,
high-impact studies that are organized into five domains reviewed in
Sections 2–6.
2 Green hotels: visit intentions and
willingness to pay price premiums
2.1 Determinants of green hotel visit
intentions
Research consistently demonstrates that multiple psychological,
social, and contextual factors influence tourists’ intentions to visit
green hotels. Ferreira et al. (2023) emphasize the importance of com-
bining psychological factors, such as environmental values, attitudes,
and personal norms—with contextual factors including hotel attri-
butes, location, and price to comprehensively understand green hotel
patronage intentions. This multi-dimensional perspective aligns with
calls for more holistic models that transcend single-theory approaches.
Personal values play a foundational role in shaping green hotel
visit intentions. Zhang et al. (2025) found that altruistic, biospheric,
and collectivistic values significantly influence both ecocentric and
implicit attitudes toward green hotels among young Chinese consum-
ers, which subsequently impact awareness of consequences, ascription
of responsibility, and personal norms. Similarly, Wang et al. (2024)
demonstrated that these value orientations create cascading effects
through attitudes and norms, ultimately predicting visit intentions
among Chinese university students. These findings underscore the
importance of values as antecedents in the motivational chain leading
to green hotel patronage.
Attitudes toward green hotels emerge as particularly strong pre-
dictors of visit intentions. Tan et al. (2021)  applied Behavioural
Reasoning Theory and identified attitude as the strongest predictor of
green hotel patronage intention, mediating the effects of both “reasons
for” (perceived green benefits) and “reasons against” (perceived green
costs) patronage. This finding highlights the central role of evaluative
judgments in translating motivational factors into behavioral
intentions.
Social norms also significantly influence green hotel choices. Fauzi
et al. (2022) found that subjective norms, perceptions of social pres-
sure from important others, significantly impact visit intentions, sug-
gesting that green hotel patronage is partly driven by social conformity
and the desire for social approval. This social dimension complements
the personal moral obligations emphasized in VBN theory.
2.2 Willingness to pay price premiums for
sustainability
While visit intentions are important, willingness to pay (WTP)
price premiums represents a more demanding behavioral commit-
ment that reflects the economic value consumers place on sustain-
ability. González-Rodríguez et al. (2020)  investigated factors
influencing customers’ willingness to pay premiums for environmen-
tally friendly hotels, finding that environmental concerns and percep-
tions of hotels’ environmental practices and images significantly
predict WTP . This research integrates Social Identity Theory with
VBN theory, suggesting that consumers’ self-concepts as environmen-
tally responsible individuals motivate premium payments.

Cross-national research reveals substantial variation in WTP
across cultural contexts. Shehawy et al. (2024) examined consumers’
willingness to pay more for green hotels across seven countries, iden-
tifying subjective norms, perceived consumer effectiveness, personal
moral norms, environmental concerns, and personality traits (extra-
version and agreeableness) as key predictors. Importantly, their mul-
tigroup analysis revealed that a “one-size-fits-all” approach is
inadequate, as the relative importance of these factors varies signifi-
cantly across national contexts. This finding underscores the need for
culturally sensitive models of green hotel patronage.
Recent evidence suggests that eco-conscious travelers, particularly
younger generations, demonstrate higher WTP for certified sustain-
able hotels (Velaoras et al., 2025). Thapa et al. (2025) found that 71%
of travelers in Kathmandu support mandatory sustainability certifica-
tions for all hotels, indicating strong consumer demand for verified
sustainability credentials. However, the same study revealed that 52%
of respondents experience eco-guilt when staying at non-eco-friendly
hotels, suggesting that negative anticipated emotions may also drive
WTP for green alternatives.
The economic implications of green certifications are substantial.
Arbelo et al. (2025) assessed the revenue impact of eco-certification in
the hospitality sector, providing empirical evidence that certified hotels
can command price premiums. Lee et al. (2019) demonstrated that
green certificates and awards positively affect consumers’ perceived
value, which in turn influences satisfaction, intention to revisit, and
intention to pay green premiums. These findings suggest that sustain-
ability credentials translate into tangible economic benefits for hotels.
2.3 Cross-cultural and demographic
variations
Demographic and cultural factors moderate the relationships
between psychological antecedents and green hotel patronage. Fei et
al. (2024) examined Generation Z’s purchase decisions regarding envi-
ronmental sustainability practices in economy hotels using choice-
based conjoint analysis, revealing that younger consumers weigh
sustainability attributes differently than older generations. This gen-
erational variation suggests that marketing strategies must be tailored
to specific demographic segments.
Cultural context also shapes the effectiveness of different motiva-
tional factors. The research by Zhang et al. (2025) and Wang et al.
(2024) on Chinese consumers highlights the importance of collectiv-
istic values in Asian contexts, whereas Western research tends to
emphasize individualistic values such as personal moral obligations.
These cultural differences suggest that theoretical models developed
in Western contexts may require adaptation when applied to Asian or
other non-Western markets.
3 Value-belief-norm theory in tourism
and green hotel research
3.1 Theoretical foundations and Core
constructs
Value-Belief-Norm (VBN) theory, developed by Stern and col-
leagues, extends the Norm Activation Model (NAM) by
incorporating personal values and ecological worldviews as anteced-
ents to pro-environmental behavior (Fauzi et al., 2022). The theory
posits a causal chain: personal values (altruistic, biospheric, egois -
tic) → ecological worldview → awareness of consequences → ascrip-
tion of responsibility → personal norms → pro-environmental
behavior. This sequential model suggests that pro-environmental
actions are ultimately driven by personal moral obligations that arise
when individuals perceive environmental threats and accept respon-
sibility for addressing them.
The classic VBN model specifies a predominantly cognitive and
normative chain from values to beliefs to personal moral norms and,
ultimately, behavior. In the context of this review, I propose that antici-
pated moral emotions enter this chain at two specific points. First,
personal norms and awareness of consequences/ascription of respon-
sibility give rise to anticipated eco-guilt and anticipated green pride,
such that individuals who feel morally obliged to protect the environ-
ment also expect to feel guilty if they do not act accordingly and proud
if they do. Second, hotel sustainability claims and their perceived
authenticity directly activate these anticipated emotions by making the
environmental consequences of hotel choice salient. In the extended
VBN framework developed here, anticipated eco-guilt and green pride
thus operate as mediators between personal norms and the two behav-
ioral outcomes—visit intention and willingness to pay a price pre-
mium, while also serving as emotional responses to external
sustainability communications.
In the context of green hotel patronage, VBN theory provides a
framework for understanding how deeply held values translate into
specific behavioral intentions. Waris et al. (2023) applied extended
VBN theory to examine how egoistic values, pro-environmental
beliefs, and personal norms shape tourists’ intentions to visit green
hotels, finding significant support for the value-belief-norm pathway.
The theory’s emphasis on moral obligations distinguishes it from
purely rational choice models, positioning green hotel patronage as a
morally motivated behavior rather than merely a utilitarian decision.
3.2 Integration with theory of planned
behavior
Recognizing that VBN theory’s focus on values and moral norms
may neglect important cognitive and social factors, several researchers
have integrated VBN with the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB).
Fauzi et al. (2022) merged TPB and VBN theory to predict tourists’
intention to visit green hotels, arguing that this integration provides a
more holistic framework by incorporating attitudes, subjective norms,
and perceived behavioral control alongside values, beliefs, and per-
sonal norms. Their findings demonstrated that both TPB and VBN
constructs significantly predict visit intentions, with perceived moral
obligation (from VBN) influencing both subjective norms and per-
sonal norms.
Zhang et al. (2025) similarly combined TPB and VBN theory to
predict young Chinese consumers’ intention to choose green hotels,
finding that the integrated model effectively closes the attitude-inten-
tion gap. Their research revealed that altruistic, biospheric, and col-
lectivistic values influence both ecocentric and implicit attitudes,
which then impact awareness of consequences, ascription of respon-
sibility, and personal norms. Wang et al. (2024) extended this integra-
tion by demonstrating causal effects from attitudes to awareness of
consequences, ascription of responsibility, personal norms, and ulti-
mately visit intentions among Chinese university students.

These integrative approaches suggest that green hotel patronage is
influenced by both rational evaluations (attitudes, perceived behav-
ioral control) and moral considerations (personal norms, moral obli-
gations). The combined framework acknowledges that consumers
weigh both the practical benefits and costs of green hotels and the
moral imperative to act in environmentally responsible ways.
3.3 Extensions and applications in green
hotel contexts
Beyond integration with TPB, researchers have extended VBN
theory by incorporating additional constructs relevant to green hotel
contexts. Waris et al. (2023) incorporated information publicity into
the VBN framework, demonstrating that the availability and visibility
of information about green hotels significantly influences pro-envi-
ronmental beliefs and personal norms. This extension recognizes that
consumers’ moral obligations depend partly on their awareness of
environmental issues and available sustainable alternatives.
Fauzi et al. (2022) extended VBN by examining green trust as a
moderating factor, finding that trust in hotels’ environmental claims
influences the relationship between personal norms and visit inten-
tions. However, their research revealed that green trust showed an
insignificant direct relationship with visit intentions in the Malaysian
context, suggesting that skepticism about greenwashing may attenuate
the effects of moral motivations. This finding highlights the impor-
tance of credible sustainability communications in activating VBN
pathways.
3.4 Limitations and research gaps
Despite its widespread application, VBN theory faces several limi-
tations in explaining green hotel patronage. Fauzi et al. (2022) explic-
itly note that VBN’s focus on values and moral norms neglects
cognition and reasoning about particular issues, potentially overlook-
ing important decision-making processes. The theory’s emphasis on
stable personal values may also fail to account for situational factors
and contextual variations that influence green hotel choices.
Most critically for the current research, VBN theory does not explic-
itly incorporate emotional mechanisms beyond the general concept of
moral obligation. While personal norms reflect a sense of moral obliga-
tion, the theory does not differentiate between specific moral emotions
such as guilt and pride, nor does it address anticipated emotions that
may arise from contemplating different behavioral choices. Su et al.
(2022) demonstrated that guilt and moral obligation mediate the effects
of message framing on willingness to pay premiums for pro-environ-
mental hotels, suggesting that specific emotional responses to sustain-
ability communications play important roles not captured by standard
VBN models. While personal norms and moral obligation capture
whether individuals feel they should act pro-environmentally, eco-guilt
captures how they expect to feel if they fail to act accordingly; distin-
guishing these constructs allows the extended VBN framework to sepa-
rate normative judgments from emotional enforcement mechanisms.
Within the VBN framework, personal norms and moral obliga-
tion capture a cognitive–evaluative “ought” judgment (e.g., “I feel I
should choose environmentally responsible hotels”), whereas eco-guilt
captures the negative emotional response that arises when individuals
anticipate or recognize a failure to live up to these norms. Personal
norms therefore represent a sense of moral duty rooted in values,
awareness of consequences, and ascription of responsibility, while
anticipated eco-guilt represents the affective discomfort associated
with violating this duty. In the extended VBN model, personal norms
remain the core normative construct, and anticipated eco-guilt is con-
ceptualized as a downstream emotional consequence of strong per-
sonal norms (and heightened AC/AR) that motivates corrective
action, such as choosing green hotels or paying a price premium.
Furthermore, VBN theory does not address how external com-
munications such as sustainability claims, eco-labels, and green mar-
keting messages activate or inhibit the value-belief-norm pathway. The
theory treats values and beliefs as relatively stable individual charac-
teristics but does not explain how situational stimuli trigger moral
considerations. This gap is particularly important in marketing con-
texts where hotels actively communicate sustainability credentials to
influence consumer choices.
4 Eco-guilt in pro-environmental and
tourism behaviors
4.1 Conceptualizing eco-guilt in consumer
contexts
Eco-guilt, also termed environmental guilt or green guilt, refers to
the negative emotional state arising from awareness that one’s actions
harm the environment or from failing to act in environmentally respon-
sible ways. As a moral emotion, eco-guilt serves a regulatory function,
motivating individuals to align their behaviors with environmental
values and norms. In consumer contexts, eco-guilt can be experienced
retrospectively (after engaging in environmentally harmful consump-
tion) or anticipated (when contemplating such consumption).
The role of guilt in pro-environmental behavior has received
increasing attention in environmental psychology, yet its application
to hospitality and tourism contexts remains limited. Guilt operates
through self-evaluation processes: individuals compare their actual or
anticipated behaviors against internalized standards of environmental
responsibility, and discrepancies generate negative affect that moti-
vates corrective action. This mechanism suggests that eco-guilt could
serve as a powerful motivator for choosing green hotels and paying
premiums to avoid environmentally harmful accommodations.
Within the VBN framework, moral obligation is typically repre-
sented by personal norms—a felt sense that one “should” act in an envi-
ronmentally responsible way—whereas eco-guilt is the negative
emotional response that arises when one anticipates or recognizes a fail-
ure to live up to those norms. Personal norms therefore capture a cogni-
tive–evaluative “ought” judgment, while eco-guilt captures the affective
discomfort associated with violating that judgment. In the extended
VBN model proposed here, personal norms remain the core normative
construct, but anticipated eco-guilt is conceptualized as a downstream
emotional consequence of strong personal norms and awareness of con-
sequences/ascription of responsibility, which in turn motivates corrective
behavior (choosing green hotels, paying a premium).
4.2 Anticipated eco-guilt as a motivational
mechanism
Anticipated emotions, emotional states that individuals expect
to experience as consequences of their decisions, play crucial roles

in decision-making. Anticipated eco-guilt specifically refers to the
expectation of feeling guilty if one chooses an environmentally
harmful option. This forward-looking emotional mechanism differs
from retrospective guilt and may be particularly relevant in pre-
purchase contexts where consumers evaluate alternative hotel
options.
Su et al. (2022) provided important evidence for guilt as a mediat-
ing mechanism in green hotel contexts. Their research demonstrated
that guilt and moral obligation mediate the congruence effect between
message framing (gain vs. loss) and mindset (fixed vs. growth) on
consumers’ willingness to pay premiums for pro-environmental
hotels. Specifically, loss-framed messages activated guilt among con-
sumers with fixed mindsets, while gain-framed messages activated
moral obligation among those with growth mindsets. This finding
suggests that sustainability communications can strategically trigger
anticipated guilt to motivate premium payments.
Thapa et al. (2025)  found that 52% of travelers in Kathmandu
experience eco-guilt when staying at non-eco-friendly hotels, provid-
ing direct evidence that guilt is a salient emotion in hotel choice con-
texts. This substantial proportion suggests that eco-guilt is not merely
a theoretical construct but a commonly experienced emotion that
likely influences actual booking decisions. However, the study did not
examine whether this guilt translates into behavioral intentions or
willingness to pay premiums, leaving important questions
unanswered.
More specifically, I conceptualize anticipated eco-guilt as arising
at the intersection of awareness of consequences, ascription of respon-
sibility, and personal norms: individuals who (a) recognize the envi-
ronmental harm of non-green hotels (AC), (b) feel responsible for
those consequences (AR), and (c) hold strong personal norms around
environmental protection are most likely to anticipate feeling guilty if
they choose a non-green hotel. This anticipated guilt is theorized to
mediate the effect of these VBN constructs—and of sustainability
communications that heighten AC and AR—on visit intention and
willingness to pay a premium.
I therefore posit that individuals high in AC, AR, and personal
norms will anticipate stronger eco-guilt if they select a non-green
hotel option, and that this anticipated eco-guilt mediates the effects of
AC, AR, and norms as well as of sustainability claims that make these
elements salient on green hotel visit intention and willingness to pay
a price premium.
4.3 Eco-guilt in hospitality and tourism
decisions
The hospitality and tourism sectors present particularly relevant
contexts for eco-guilt given the significant environmental impacts of
travel and accommodation. Hotels consume substantial energy and
water, generate waste, and contribute to carbon emissions, making
them visible targets for environmental concern. Consumers who hold
pro-environmental values may experience guilt when their accom-
modation choices conflict with these values.
However, research explicitly examining eco-guilt in green hotel
contexts remains scarce. While studies have examined general envi-
ronmental concerns, attitudes, and moral obligations ( González-
Rodríguez et al., 2020 ; Fauzi et al., 2022 ), few have specifically
measured eco-guilt or distinguished it from related constructs such as
personal norms or moral obligations. This gap is problematic because
guilt represents a distinct emotional experience with unique
motivational properties that may not be fully captured by cognitive
constructs like norms.
4.4 Research gaps in eco-guilt literature
Several critical gaps exist in the eco-guilt literature as it relates to
green hotels. First, research has not systematically examined how hotel
sustainability claims trigger anticipated eco-guilt. If consumers expect
to feel guilty staying at non-green hotels, sustainability communica-
tions that highlight environmental impacts may amplify this antici-
pated guilt, thereby increasing green hotel patronage and premium
payment willingness. Understanding this mechanism could inform
more effective green marketing strategies.
Second, the relationship between eco-guilt and other VBN con-
structs remains unclear. Does eco-guilt operate as a mediator between
personal norms and behavioral intentions, or does it represent a paral-
lel pathway? How do anticipated and retrospective guilt interact with
awareness of consequences and ascription of responsibility? These
theoretical questions require empirical investigation.
Third, individual differences in susceptibility to eco-guilt have not
been adequately explored in hospitality contexts. Some consumers
may be more prone to experiencing eco-guilt based on their values,
personality traits, or prior experiences. Understanding these indi -
vidual differences could enable more targeted marketing approaches.
5 Green pride and the dual facets of
pride in sustainable consumption
5.1 Pride as a moral emotion in
pro-environmental behavior
While guilt represents a negative moral emotion that motivates
avoidance of norm-violating behaviors, pride represents a positive
moral emotion that reinforces norm-consistent behaviors. In pro-
environmental contexts, green pride refers to the positive emotional
state arising from engaging in environmentally responsible actions.
Pride serves a reinforcement function, increasing the likelihood that
individuals will repeat pro-environmental behaviors and maintain
their environmental identities.
Pride is fundamentally a self-conscious emotion that arises from
self-evaluation. When individuals perceive that their actions align
with valued standards such as environmental responsibility they expe-
rience pride. This positive affect reinforces the behavior and strength-
ens commitment to environmental values. In green hotel contexts,
consumers who choose sustainable accommodations may experience
pride in making environmentally responsible choices, which could
increase satisfaction, loyalty, and willingness to pay premiums.
5.2 Authentic versus hubristic pride:
theoretical distinctions
Recent psychological research distinguishes between two facets of
pride: authentic pride and hubristic pride. Authentic pride arises from
genuine accomplishments and is associated with prosocial behaviors,
achievement motivation, and stable self-esteem. It reflects pride in
one’s actions and efforts rather than in one’s inherent superiority. In

contrast, hubristic pride arises from inflated self-perceptions and is
associated with narcissism, aggression, and unstable self-esteem. It
reflects pride in one’s perceived superiority over others.
This distinction has important implications for sustainable con-
sumption. Authentic green pride would arise from genuine commit-
ment to environmental values and satisfaction in making responsible
choices. Consumers experiencing authentic pride would feel good
about their contributions to environmental protection without need-
ing to demonstrate superiority over others. In contrast, hubristic green
pride would arise from using green consumption to signal status or
superiority. Consumers experiencing hubristic pride would be moti-
vated by self-enhancement and social comparison rather than genuine
environmental concern.
These two forms of pride may have different antecedents and con-
sequences in green hotel contexts. Authentic pride may be more
strongly linked to intrinsic environmental values and may predict
sustained commitment to green hotels regardless of social visibility.
Hubristic pride may be more strongly linked to social image concerns
and may predict green hotel choices primarily when such choices are
visible to others or confer status benefits.
5.3 Pride in sustainable consumption
contexts
Despite the theoretical importance of pride in sustainable con-
sumption, empirical research on green pride in hospitality contexts is
virtually absent from the literature reviewed. None of the top-ranked
papers explicitly examined pride as a motivational mechanism in
green hotel choices. This represents a significant gap given that pride
likely plays an important role in reinforcing green consumption
behaviors.
The absence of pride research in green hotel contexts is particu-
larly striking given evidence from other domains that positive emo-
tions enhance pro-environmental behaviors. If consumers anticipate
feeling proud when choosing green hotels, this anticipated pride could
motivate patronage and premium payments. Moreover, if actual expe-
riences of pride reinforce green hotel choices, this could increase cus-
tomer loyalty and positive word-of-mouth.
5.4 Research gaps in pride and green hotel
choices
Several critical research gaps exist regarding pride in green hotel
contexts. First, no research has examined whether consumers antici-
pate experiencing pride when choosing green hotels or whether such
anticipated pride influences booking decisions and willingness to pay
premiums. Understanding this mechanism could reveal an important
positive emotional pathway complementing the negative pathway of
anticipated guilt.
Second, the distinction between authentic and hubristic pride has
not been applied to green hotel consumption. Do sustainability claims
that emphasize environmental benefits (potentially triggering authen-
tic pride) have different effects than claims emphasizing status or
exclusivity (potentially triggering hubristic pride)? How do these dif-
ferent forms of pride relate to intrinsic versus extrinsic motivations for
green consumption?
Third, the interplay between pride and guilt in green hotel deci -
sions remains unexplored. These emotions may operate as comple -
mentary motivators, with anticipated guilt pushing consumers away
from conventional hotels and anticipated pride pulling them toward
green alternatives. Understanding how these emotions interact
could inform more nuanced theoretical models and marketing
strategies.
5.4.1 Propositions and measurement
H1 (authentic pride pathway). Exposure to sustainability claims
that emphasize genuine environmental benefits (e.g., concrete
reductions in energy use and waste) will increase anticipated
authentic green pride, which in turn will positively predict (a)
green hotel visit intention and (b) willingness to pay a price
premium.
H2 (hubristic pride pathway). Exposure to sustainability claims
that emphasize status or exclusivity (e.g., “elite eco-traveler expe-
riences”) will increase anticipated hubristic green pride, which
will more strongly predict socially visible outcomes such as will-
ingness to share stays on social media and willingness to recom-
mend the hotel to others (WOM), but may have weaker or less
stable effects on repeat booking intentions.
H3 (authentic vs. hubristic pride and loyalty). Authentic green
pride will be more strongly associated with long-term loyalty
behaviors (e.g., intention to rebook the same green hotel, prefer-
ence for certified green brands) than hubristic green pride,
which will be more strongly associated with one-off, status-sig-
naling choices and self-presentational behaviors.
6 Hotel sustainability claims,
eco-labels, and consumer responses
6.1 Eco-labels and green certifications:
effects on consumer perceptions
Eco-labels and green certifications serve as informational cues
that signal hotels’ environmental commitments and performance.
These credentials can reduce information asymmetry, helping con-
sumers identify genuinely sustainable options in a crowded market-
place. Research consistently demonstrates that eco-labels and
certifications positively influence consumer perceptions and behav-
ioral intentions.
Lee et al. (2019) found that green certificates and awards positively
affect consumers’ perceived value, which in turn predicts satisfaction,
intention to revisit, and intention to pay green premiums in Sri
Lankan hotels. This research demonstrates that certifications create
value perceptions that translate into both attitudinal and behavioral
outcomes. Similarly, Godovykh et al. (2024) provided empirical evi-
dence that the presence of sustainable badges positively impacts
guests’ attitudes toward hotels and their intentions to book, contribut-
ing to understanding of how certification effectiveness influences
guest choices.
Velaoras et al. (2025) examined the role of environmental certifica-
tions such as LEED, Green Key, and EarthCheck in the hospitality indus-
try, finding that eco-conscious travelers are willing to pay premiums for
certified hotels. Their research also identified consumer awareness of

certification schemes as a limiting factor, suggesting that the effectiveness
of certifications depends partly on consumer knowledge and recogni-
tion. This finding highlights the importance of both obtaining certifica-
tions and effectively communicating them to target audiences.
Yildiz et al. (2023)  applied signal theory to examine how per -
ceived green labels influence eco-conscious tourists’ online booking
intentions, finding that green labels decrease perceived risk of unsus-
tainability and increase trust in hotels’ sustainability. Green trust and
green perceived risk mediated the relationship between label percep-
tion and booking intentions, suggesting that certifications work by
reducing uncertainty and building confidence in sustainability claims.
6.2 Greenwashing and consumer skepticism
Despite the positive effects of genuine sustainability credentials, the
prevalence of greenwashing—misleading or unsubstantiated environ-
mental claims has generated consumer skepticism that can undermine
green marketing efforts. Kalyanpur (2025) notes that almost 60% of sus-
tainability claims across sectors are misleading, leading to widespread
consumer skepticism. This finding underscores the challenge hotels face
in establishing credibility for their environmental initiatives.
Alyahia et al. (2024) investigated greenwashing behavior in the
hotel industry, finding that greenwashing negatively impacts green
trust, which in turn negatively affects patronage intentions including
willingness to stay and pay premiums. This research demonstrates that
deceptive sustainability claims have tangible negative consequences
for hotels. Importantly, the study found that perceptions of green
authenticity and green transparency significantly attenuate green -
washing’s negative influence on green trust, suggesting that clear com-
munication and genuine practices can counteract skepticism.
Zhang et al. (2022) examined whether greenwashing influences
green product experience in emerging hospitality markets post-
COVID-19, finding that greenwashing negatively impacts consumer
green product experience, leading to lower repurchase intention and
negative word-of-mouth. The study identified consumer confusion
and perceived risk as psychological mechanisms through which gre-
enwashing operates, with perceived risk moderating the relationship
between greenwashing and product experience.
Fauzi et al. (2022) noted that green skepticism deters tourists from
forming green trust in the Malaysian context, suggesting that consum-
ers are cautious about how hotels engage in green activities. They
called for substantial proof of firm action to overcome skepticism and
recommended establishing green hotel rating systems and appropriate
legal frameworks to regulate “green” claims, as the current market
lacks specific rating tools.
6.3 Green trust, authenticity, and
transparency
Green trust, consumers’ willingness to rely on hotels’ environmen-
tal claims based on confidence in their environmental performance
emerges as a critical mediating variable between sustainability com-
munications and behavioral outcomes. Multiple studies identify green
trust as essential for converting sustainability credentials into patron-
age intentions and premium payments (Fauzi et al., 2022; Yildiz et al.,
2023; Alyahia et al., 2024).
Alyahia et al. (2024) and Chung (2020) demonstrated that green
authenticity and green transparency serve as antidotes to greenwash-
ing’s negative effects. Green authenticity refers to perceptions that
hotels’ environmental commitments are genuine rather than merely
symbolic, while green transparency refers to openness and clarity in
communicating environmental practices. Both constructs signifi -
cantly attenuate the negative relationship between greenwashing per-
ceptions and green trust, suggesting that hotels can build trust through
authentic practices and transparent communications.
Kalyanpur (2025)  emphasized the importance of clear, honest
communication and credible third-party green certifications (such
as Green Seal and LEED) to build guest trust and avoid greenwash -
ing. The research noted that 61% of consumers believe companies
use green marketing primarily to boost sales rather than from genu -
ine environmental commitment, highlighting the depth of skepti -
cism that hotels must overcome. Transparency and authenticity in
eco-initiatives emerge as crucial for establishing credibility.
6.4 Message characteristics: concreteness,
credibility, and framing
Beyond the presence or absence of certifications, the characteristics
of sustainability messages significantly influence consumer responses.
Topcuoglu et al. (2021) investigated green message strategies and green
brand image in hotel contexts, finding that green message abstractness
negatively impacts brand image perceptions and behavioral intentions.
This suggests that concrete, specific sustainability claims are more
effective than vague or abstract environmental appeals.
Su et al. (2022)  examined the joint effects of message framing
(gain vs. loss) and consumer mindset (fixed vs. growth) on willingness
to pay premiums for pro-environmental hotels. Their research
revealed a congruence effect: loss-framed messages (emphasizing
environmental harms avoided) were more effective for consumers
with fixed mindsets, while gain-framed messages (emphasizing envi-
ronmental benefits achieved) were more effective for those with
growth mindsets. Guilt and moral obligation mediated these effects,
suggesting that message framing influences behavioral intentions by
triggering specific emotional and moral responses.
Hu et al. (2025) examined the role of psychological ambivalence in
consumers’ hesitation to choose green hotels, finding that argument
quality and source credibility moderate the relationship between ambiva-
lent attitudes and behavioral intentions. High-quality arguments and
credible sources can overcome ambivalence and promote patronage, sug-
gesting that the persuasiveness of sustainability communications
depends on both content quality and source characteristics.
6.5 Research gaps in sustainability
communication
Despite substantial research on sustainability communications,
several gaps remain. First, research has not systematically examined
how different message characteristics (concreteness, credibility, fram-
ing) interact with emotional responses (guilt, pride) to influence green
hotel choices. While Su et al. (2022) demonstrated that framing affects
guilt, comprehensive models integrating message characteristics, emo-
tional responses, and behavioral outcomes are lacking.
Second, the mechanisms through which sustainability claims acti-
vate VBN pathways remain unclear. Do sustainability messages pri-
marily influence awareness of consequences, ascription of
responsibility, or personal norms? How do different types of claims
(environmental benefits, social responsibility, economic savings) dif-
ferentially activate VBN constructs?
Third, research has not adequately examined how the distinction
between authentic and hubristic pride relates to different types of

sustainability claims. Claims emphasizing environmental benefits may
trigger authentic pride, while claims emphasizing exclusivity or status
may trigger hubristic pride. Understanding these relationships could
inform more effective message strategies.

The literature reviewed reveals several important patterns and gaps
that justify extending VBN theory to incorporate emotional mechanisms
and sustainability communications. First, while VBN theory successfully
predicts green hotel visit intentions and willingness to pay premiums, its
focus on cognitive pathways (values → beliefs → norms) overlooks
important emotional mechanisms. Research demonstrates that guilt
mediates the effects of message framing on premium payment willing-
ness (Su et al., 2022) and that substantial proportions of travelers experi-
ence eco-guilt when staying at non-green hotels (Thapa et al., 2025), yet
VBN models do not explicitly incorporate guilt as a distinct construct.
Second, the literature reveals a striking absence of research on
pride in green hotel contexts despite pride’s theoretical importance as
a positive moral emotion that reinforces pro-environmental behaviors.
While guilt may motivate avoidance of conventional hotels, pride may
motivate approach toward green alternatives. The distinction between
authentic and hubristic pride further suggests that different types of
sustainability claims may trigger different forms of pride with distinct
motivational consequences.
Third, research demonstrates that sustainability claims, eco-labels,
and message characteristics significantly influence green trust, per-
ceived value, and behavioral intentions (Lee et al., 2019; Yildiz et al.,
2023; Alyahia et al., 2024; Godovykh et al., 2024), yet the psychologi-
cal mechanisms through which these communications operate remain
underspecified. Sustainability claims likely influence behavioral inten-
tions partly by triggering anticipated emotional responses—consum-
ers may anticipate feeling guilty if they choose non-green hotels or
proud if they choose green alternatives. These anticipated emotions
may mediate the effects of sustainability communications on visit
intentions and premium payment willingness.
Fourth, the integration of VBN theory with TPB (Fauzi et al., 2022;
Zhang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024) demonstrates the value of com-
bining cognitive and normative pathways, yet these integrated models
still do not incorporate emotional mechanisms. A more comprehen-
sive framework would integrate values, beliefs, norms, attitudes, and
emotions to provide a holistic understanding of green hotel patronage.
Fifth, research on greenwashing and green trust (Alyahia et al.,
2024; Zhang et al., 2022; Kalyanpur, 2025) reveals that the credibility
and authenticity of sustainability claims moderate their effects on
consumer responses. This suggests that the relationship between sus-
tainability communications and emotional responses may be contin-
gent on perceived authenticity. Authentic claims may trigger genuine
moral emotions, while perceived greenwashing may trigger skepticism
and negative emotions.
The synthesis of these findings suggests a theoretical model in
which hotel sustainability claims influence visit intentions and willing-
ness to pay premiums through multiple pathways: (1) a cognitive
pathway through awareness of consequences, ascription of responsi-
bility, and personal norms (consistent with VBN theory); (2) an emo-
tional pathway through anticipated eco-guilt and green pride; and (3)
a trust pathway through green trust and perceived authenticity. These
pathways likely interact, with emotional responses reinforcing norma-
tive motivations and trust perceptions moderating the activation of
both cognitive and emotional mechanisms. Table 1 summarizes the
five domains covered in this review, highlighting typical methods,
central constructs, core findings, and the unresolved gaps that moti-
vate an emotional extension of VBN in green hotel contexts.
8 Conclusion and future research
directions
This literature review has synthesized recent research on green
hotel marketing and consumer behavior across five key domains: visit
intentions and willingness to pay, applications and extensions of VBN
theory, eco-guilt, green pride, and sustainability communications. It
shows that, although existing work has substantially advanced under-
standing of cognitive and normative determinants of green hotel
patronage, critical gaps remain around emotional mechanisms and
their integration with established theoretical frameworks.
The most significant gap concerns the lack of research examining
how hotel sustainability claims trigger anticipated moral emotions—
specifically eco-guilt and green pride—and how these emotions medi-
ate the effects of sustainability communications on visit intentions and
willingness to pay price premiums. While VBN theory offers a robust
account of how values, beliefs, and personal norms shape pro-environ-
mental behavior, its neglect of emotional mechanisms limits its explan-
atory power in marketing contexts where sustainability communications
actively seek to influence consumer choice. Conceptually distinguish-
ing eco-guilt from moral obligation and personal norms clarifies how
negative moral emotions can be modeled as enforcement mechanisms
within the VBN chain rather than as redundant cognitive constructs.
Future research should therefore extend VBN theory by incorpo-
rating anticipated eco-guilt and green pride as explicit mediators
between sustainability claims and behavioral outcomes. Such work
should distinguish between authentic and hubristic pride, as these
forms of pride have different antecedents (intrinsic vs. extrinsic moti-
vations) and consequences (sustained commitment vs. status-driven,
visibility-seeking consumption). Authentic and hubristic pride can be
operationalized using adapted items from established pride scales
(e.g., “I feel proud because I am doing something good for the envi-
ronment” for authentic pride; “I feel proud because staying here shows
I am better than other travelers” for hubristic pride), framed around
green hotel choice scenarios. Behavioral indicators of pride-related
outcomes could include intention to rebook, willingness to pay a
specified price premium, likelihood of posting about the stay on social
media, and likelihood of recommending the hotel to friends and
family (word-of-mouth). Experimental designs can manipulate

Methodologically, future studies should employ experimental and

From a practical standpoint, the review points to three concrete
contributions for managers and policymakers in the green hotel
sector. First, it suggests that sustainability messages should be designed
not only to inform but also to ethically activate anticipated eco-guilt
and authentic pride—for example, by highlighting concrete environ-
mental harms avoided and tangible environmental benefits achieved—
while avoiding manipulative or exaggerated claims that could induce
backlash or greenwashing accusations. Second, it underscores the
importance of emphasizing authentic environmental benefits rather
than status or exclusivity, particularly for segments with strong intrin-
sic environmental values, as authentic pride is more likely to support
long-term loyalty and stable willingness to pay. Third, it indicates that
hotels should integrate credible certifications, transparent reporting,
and emotionally resonant storytelling into a coherent communication
strategy that simultaneously builds green trust, reinforces personal
norms, and elicits constructive moral emotions.
Taken together, specifying how eco-guilt can be modeled as an
emotional enforcement mechanism downstream of personal norms
and how authentic and hubristic green pride can be operationalized
and tested demonstrates that positive and negative moral emotions
can be empirically integrated into green hotel research. These exten-
sions offer a roadmap for moving beyond purely cognitive applications
of VBN toward emotionally enriched models of green hotel visit
intention and willingness to pay price premiums. In conclusion,
extending VBN theory to incorporate anticipated moral emotions
represents a promising direction for advancing both theoretical
understanding and managerial practice in green hotel marketing, by
providing a more comprehensive framework for how sustainability
communications shape tourists’ choices and their willingness to pay
for environmentally responsible accommodations.
