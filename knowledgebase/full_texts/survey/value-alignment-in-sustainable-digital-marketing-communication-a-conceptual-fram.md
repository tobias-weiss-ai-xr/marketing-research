This conceptual article examines sustainable digital marketing communication
as a process of value alignment across organizational and societal contexts.
Building on strategic communication, prosocial values, CSR communication,
sustainability communication, and greenwashing research, it argues that
sustainability-oriented communication in digital environments should not be
reduced to promotional messaging or technological adoption. Instead, it
requires coordination among material practices, cultural values, stakeholder
expectations, institutional supports, and ethical responsibilities. The article
develops a 2 × 2 framework that distinguishes organizational and societal
levels as well as material and cultural dimensions of sustainability. It then
extends this structure through an interaction matrix, a value-alignment model,
and selected interaction mechanisms to explain how sustainability claims are
coordinated, interpreted, and evaluated. The framework clarifies how credible
sustainable digital marketing communication depends on alignment among
organizational practices, organizational values, societal infrastructures, and
public expectations. It also conceptualizes greenwashing as a form of value
misalignment that may emerge when sustainability claims are selective,
symbolic, weakly supported, or disconnected from stakeholder expectations.
By proposing research propositions for future empirical testing, the article
contributes to sustainable marketing and sustainability communication
research by offering a conceptual basis for examining credibility, trust,
legitimacy, stakeholder engagement, and greenwashing skepticism in
digital environments.

1 Introduction
Sustainable marketing has become increasingly intertwined
with digital communication. In contemporary marketing
environments, sustainability is not expressed only through
products, services, or corporate commitments. Sustainability is
also shaped by the ways in which organizations frame
environmental and social responsibilities, communicate
sustainability initiatives, and make those initiatives visible to
consumers and stakeholders. Digital marketing is therefore no
longer limited to promotional activity; digital marketing
increasingly contributes to broader organizational goals,
including the advancement of the United Nations Sustainable
Development Goals (SDGs). For organizations seeking to
address environmental, social, and economic challenges, the
integration of sustainability into marketing and communication
practices has become a strategic necessity rather than a
peripheral concern (Narayan et al., 2025; Popescu et al., 2023).
Organizational strategy and leadership also shape the
communication of sustainability commitments. Transformational
and ethical leadership approaches can facilitate the integration
of corporate social responsibility (CSR) into organizational
strategies, enabling sustainability to become embedded within
core operations rather than remaining outside the organization’s
central activities (Velmurugan et al., 2025). Within digital
marketing communication (DMC), clearly articulated
sustainability messages can enhance consumer perceptions,
strengthen brand equity, and foster long-term stakeholder
relationships (Narayan et al., 2025). Sustainable marketing
communication therefore operates between organizational
intention and public interpretation: organizations design
sustainability messages strategically, while consumers,
stakeholders, and wider publics evaluate the credibility of
sustainability messages in relation to organizational actions and
social values.
Digital platforms have intensified the public circulation and
evaluation of sustainability narratives. Social media platforms
provide direct channels for stakeholder engagement and allow
organizations to promote sustainability narratives with greater
visibility. Greater platform visibility also increases the need to
maintain message authenticity and avoid greenwashing,
especially when sustainability claims are compared with
observable organizational practices in interactive digital
environments (Lee, 2017; Murtarelli et al., 2023). Digital
technologies also create new possibilities for sustainability
communication. Practices such as virtual influencers and data-
driven personalization demonstrate how digital technologies can
support sustainability communication by influencing public
awareness and shaping consumer behavior (Durmuş Şenyapar
et al., 2024; Hoai Lan et al., 2025). The same digital conditions
that expand the reach of sustainable marketing messages also
raise practical and ethical challenges. Sustainable digital
marketing remains constrained by increased operational costs,
difficulties in evaluating sustainability outcomes, and the need
for stronger governance mechanisms to ensure transparency and
ethical accountability (Akshita et al., 2024). For this reason,
sustainable marketing in digital environments cannot be
understood only as a matter of message delivery, platform use,
or technological adoption. Sustainable marketing in digital
environments also requires an explanation of how
communication links organizational practices, digital
infrastructures, stakeholder expectations, and value systems.
Existing studies often address sustainability communication in
a fragmented manner, focusing separately on technological tools,
consumer responses, organizational practices, or sustainability
outcomes. A fragmented treatment of sustainability
communication makes it difficult to explain how sustainable
marketing communication operates as a coordinated process
across organizational and societal contexts. Strategic
communication provides a useful basis for addressing the
fragmented treatment of sustainability communication because
strategic communication positions communication as an
integrative mechanism within organizational processes
(Falkheimer and Heide, 2014; Zerfass et al., 2018). From a
strategic communication perspective, communication is not
limited to message delivery. Communication involves the
coordination of objectives, practices, relationships, and meanings
across multiple stakeholder groups. Sustainable digital marketing
communication also requires a normative foundation because
sustainability claims become credible only when strategic goals
are connected with values that make organizational
responsibility socially meaningful. Prosocial values, including
altruism, cooperation, and social responsibility, provide a
foundation for aligning organizational communication with
broader societal expectations (Keltner et al., 2014; Kolm, 2006).
This article develops a conceptual framework for sustainable
digital marketing communication by treating sustainability
communication as a problem of value alignment. The
framework is not meant to replace stakeholder theory, CSR
communication, institutional perspectives, or sustainability
communication. It builds on these approaches and asks a
narrower question: when sustainability claims circulate through
digital marketing, how are they supported, questioned, or
weakened by organizational practices, organizational values,
societal infrastructures, and public expectations? Stakeholder
theory helps explain why sustainability communication must
respond to multiple stakeholder expectations (de Bussy, 2018;
Shah and Guild, 2022). CSR communication research shows that
credibility, transparency, engagement, and legitimacy shape how
responsibility claims are received (Arvidsson, 2010; Devin and
Lane, 2014; Ellerup Nielsen and Thomsen, 2018; Lock and
Schulz-Knappe, 2019). Sustainability communication widens the
discussion by emphasizing participation, cultural context, and
social change rather than corporate responsibility alone (Franz-
Balsen and Heinrichs, 2007; Gabai, 2025; Tölkes, 2018; Weder,
2025). Based on these discussions, the article proposes a value-
alignment framework for examining how sustainable digital
marketing communication links material practices, cultural
meanings, stakeholder relationships, and societal expectations.
2 Theoretical framework
2.1 Strategic communication
Strategic communication is not merely a functional activity
within organizations; rather, it represents a deliberate and goal-
oriented process through which communication is planned and

managed to support strategic objectives. It can be understood as
the purposeful coordination of communication practices to
influence stakeholder perceptions and behaviors in alignment
with long-term organizational goals (Zerfass et al., 2018). From
this perspective, communication is positioned not as a
peripheral function, but as a core organizational capability that
actively shapes both strategic formulation and implementation.
For sustainable digital marketing communication, this
perspective is relevant because sustainability messages are not
interpreted as isolated promotional claims. They are interpreted
in relation to organizational goals, stakeholder expectations, and
the perceived consistency between what organizations
communicate and what organizations do.
A defining characteristic of strategic communication lies in its
integrative nature. It encompasses both internal and external
communication processes, draws on insights from organizational
management and marketing strategy, and is therefore embedded
within broader strategic systems rather than treated as an isolated
activity (Falkheimer and Heide, 2014). Through such integration,
organizations are able to reinforce corporate identity, maintain
transparency, and construct coherent narratives across diverse
stakeholder groups (Falkheimer, 2014). Internally, strategic
communication enhances leadership effectiveness, supports
organizational change processes, and strengthens employee
engagement, thereby contributing to resilience and adaptability in
complex environments (Chiu, 2015; Martin, 2014). Externally,
strategic communication enables organizations to coordinate
messaging across platforms and audiences, ensuring consistency
in how organizational values and objectives are communicated. In
sustainable digital marketing communication, the connection
between internal conditions and external messaging is especially
important because public sustainability claims require support
from organizational practices, value commitments, and
stakeholder-facing narratives.
In sustainability contexts, the integrative function of strategic
communication becomes particularly critical. Strategic
communication serves as a mechanism for translating abstract
organizational values into actionable practices, enabling
alignment between environmental responsibilities, social
expectations, and business objectives (Articona and Torres,
2024). Adopting a systems perspective allows organizations to
identify points where sustainability principles can be embedded
into both digital infrastructures and organizational processes,
thereby enhancing long-term environmental and social
outcomes. Stakeholder engagement represents a central
dimension of sustainability-oriented communication.
Organizations must identify, segment, and actively engage
diverse stakeholder groups in order to foster trust, facilitate
innovation, and manage risks effectively (Beam et al., 2023;
Widén et al., 2014). This point is particularly important in
sustainability contexts, where stakeholder expectations are
dynamic and often contested, requiring continuous
communication and negotiation. For sustainable digital
marketing communication, stakeholder engagement also explains
why digital platforms cannot be treated only as channels of
message delivery; digital platforms are also spaces where
sustainability claims are interpreted, discussed, and evaluated.
Corporate social responsibility (CSR) communication further
illustrates the strategic role of communication within
sustainability-oriented organizational contexts. By embedding
CSR narratives within broader communication strategies,
organizations are able to enhance reputation, build customer
loyalty, and strengthen relationships with investors and
employees (Terchilă, 2025). CSR communication also helps
clarify why responsibility claims are not evaluated only by their
content. They are evaluated through credibility, legitimacy, and
the perceived relation between communication and
organizational conduct. Arvidsson (2010) shows that CSR
communication is part of how management teams present
responsibility to external audiences. From a legitimacy
perspective, CSR communication is tied to the need to justify
organizational conduct and maintain social acceptance (Ellerup
Nielsen and Thomsen, 2018). Credibility is especially important
because credible CSR communication can strengthen perceived
legitimacy (Lock and Schulz-Knappe, 2019). Engagement also
has a communicative dimension: organizations do not only
engage stakeholders, but also communicate what engagement
means and how it is practiced (Devin and Lane, 2014). At the
same time, CSR communication may become weak when firms
and stakeholders emphasize different CSR issues, creating
parallel conversations rather than shared understanding
(Gómez-Carrasco et al., 2021). For this article, CSR
communication is therefore treated as a related but narrower
field. It explains how organizational responsibility is
communicated and evaluated, while sustainable digital
marketing communication also requires attention to digital
infrastructures, cultural meanings, and wider
sustainability expectations.
Strategic communication is also closely integrated with
strategic planning and evaluation. Oliveira et al. (2021) discuss
the relationship between strategic communication and
organizational evaluation through the Balanced Scorecard. The
present article does not adopt the Balanced Scorecard as an
analytical tool. The relevance of this discussion is more limited
and more general: communication activities can be considered
in relation to organizational objectives, accountability, and
evaluation. For sustainable digital marketing communication,
this broader point is useful because sustainability claims require
coherence between organizational goals, communication
practices, stakeholder interpretation, and public accountability.
Strategic communication therefore provides a theoretical basis
for understanding how sustainability-oriented messages can be
coordinated with organizational practices and stakeholder
relationships in digital environments.
2.2 Prosocial values
Prosocial values provide another theoretical basis for
understanding sustainable digital marketing communication.
Prosocial values refer to motivations and behavioral orientations
that aim to benefit others and promote collective well-being.
They encompass constructs such as altruism, cooperation, social
responsibility, and concern for societal welfare (Keltner et al.,
2014; Muethel et al., 2011). Among these, altruism represents a
central component rooted in empathy and moral obligation, and
it plays a significant role in fostering social cohesion and
advancing the public interest (Flynn and Black, 2011; Kolm,

2006). Beyond individual behavior, prosocial values also operate at
an ideological level, shaping attitudes toward social justice,
environmental responsibility, and collective action (Nezlek,
2022). This conceptual expansion is particularly important for
sustainability communication, where ethical considerations
extend beyond immediate organizational interests toward long-
term societal and environmental outcomes.
Within organizational contexts, the integration of prosocial
values extends beyond traditional corporate practices and
becomes a foundational mechanism for aligning communication
strategies with sustainability objectives. Rather than treating
sustainability as a peripheral initiative, organizations can embed
prosocial principles into digital marketing and communication
activities, thereby reframing communication as a value-driven
process. Sustainable marketing frameworks provide useful
mechanisms for operationalizing these values. For instance,
models such as the 6Ps—product, price, place, promotion,
participation, and purpose—highlight the role of marketing in
creating shared value and fostering stakeholder engagement
(Kaufman et al., 2023). These approaches encourage
organizations to move from transactional relationships toward
value-based interactions grounded in shared social and
environmental concerns. From the perspective of the present
article, this shift suggests that communication is not merely a
tool for persuasion, but a process through which organizations
negotiate meaning, responsibility, and legitimacy
with stakeholders.
The integration of prosocial values into communication
practices also has important implications for consumer behavior
and broader societal engagement. When organizations
communicate sustainability commitments authentically, they are
more likely to shape public perceptions and encourage
sustainable behaviors (Narayan et al., 2025). In this context,
transparency and ethical communication become essential
conditions for ensuring that sustainability messages are credible
and resonate with increasingly conscious audiences (Ahmad Al
Serhan and Zhang, 2025). For sustainable digital marketing
communication, these arguments are relevant because digital
messages can increase the visibility of sustainability claims, but
visibility alone does not guarantee credibility. Credibility
depends on whether sustainability messages are perceived as
consistent with organizational practices, ethical responsibility,
and stakeholder expectations.
Digital technologies further enable the operationalization of
prosocial values through personalization and data-driven
communication strategies. By leveraging real-time insights into
consumer preferences, organizations can design communication
approaches that align with prosocial motivations and encourage
sustainable behaviors (Singh and Selvasundaram, 2024). This
alignment not only enhances engagement but also facilitates the
diffusion of sustainability practices across wider audiences. At
the same time, sustainable digital marketing communication
requires attention to the ethical meaning of personalization.
Personalized sustainability messages should remain transparent,
responsible, and consistent with the values they claim to
promote. In this sense, digital technologies are relevant not only
because they improve communication efficiency, but also
because they shape how prosocial values are translated into
communication practices.
Community engagement and collaborative initiatives also play
a critical role in amplifying the impact of prosocial values.
Partnerships with stakeholders and sustainability-oriented
organizations can strengthen the social and economic impact of
communication strategies, positioning organizations as active
contributors to sustainability-driven innovation (Deb et al.,
2024; Durmuş Şenyapar et al., 2024). From the perspective of
this article, the significance of prosocial values lies not only in
their normative implications but also in their capacity to
structure communication processes. Prosocial values provide a
lens through which communication can be understood as a
mechanism of alignment rather than mere information
transmission. They link ethical considerations with strategic
communication practices and enable sustainability to be
operationalized in a more coherent and value-driven manner.
This theoretical role prepares the ground for the following
framework, which examines sustainable digital marketing
communication across organizational and societal levels as well
as material and cultural dimensions.
Recent CSR and ethical communication studies help clarify
how prosocial values can become communicatively meaningful.
Consumers may engage with CSR initiatives when they see those
initiatives as connected with moral concern, shared
responsibility, and personal meaning (Song, 2022). CSR
symbolism can also shape consumer-brand identification and
trust, although symbolic expression alone is not sufficient if it is
not supported by credible organizational conduct (Rahman and
Chakraborty, 2026). Research on sustainability ratings further
suggests that external signals may help reduce greenwashing
concerns when they make ethical corporate communication
more verifiable (Parguel et al., 2011). These studies suggest that
prosocial values should not be treated only as abstract ethical
ideals. In the present framework, they function as evaluative
criteria through which stakeholders judge whether sustainability
communication appears responsible, cooperative, and credible.
2.3 Theoretical positioning
The proposed framework is positioned in relation to four
bodies of work: stakeholder theory, CSR communication,
institutional perspectives, and sustainability communication.
Stakeholder theory is relevant because it explains why
organizational responsibility cannot be defined only from the
perspective of managerial intention. Sustainability claims are
read by multiple stakeholder groups, and these groups may
support, question, or contest what organizations communicate
(de Bussy, 2018; Shah and Guild, 2022). This perspective
supports the stakeholder-facing side of the framework, especially
the link between organizational communication and societal
expectations. However, stakeholder theory alone does not
explain how material practices, cultural meanings, and digital
infrastructures interact when sustainability claims move through
digital marketing.
CSR communication provides a second point of reference. It
focuses on how organizations communicate responsibility, how
stakeholders evaluate the credibility of CSR claims, and how
such communication contributes to legitimacy. Existing studies
emphasize transparency, credibility, stakeholder engagement,

and legitimacy as recurring concerns in CSR communication
(Arvidsson, 2010; Devin and Lane, 2014; Ellerup Nielsen and
Thomsen, 2018; Lock and Schulz-Knappe, 2019). They also
show that firms and stakeholders may not focus on the same
CSR issues, which can weaken the communicative value of CSR
claims (Gómez-Carrasco et al., 2021). This literature is useful for
the present article because sustainable digital marketing
communication also depends on whether public-facing claims
are supported by organizational practices and stakeholder
recognition. The present framework extends this discussion by
asking how digital platforms, societal infrastructures, and
cultural narratives shape the interpretation of sustainability claims.
Institutional perspectives provide a third point of reference.
Sustainability communication is shaped not only by organizational
choice, but also by reporting expectations, regulatory environments,
field-level norms, and interorganizational positioning. CSR reports
can function as sites where organizations position themselves
within institutionalized fields of responsibility (Xu and Woo, 2023).
The shift from voluntary sustainability reporting toward more
formalized legal requirements also shows that sustainability
communication is increasingly shaped by institutional pressure
(Mangiuc and Kagitci, 2025). This perspective supports the
organizational–societal distinction in the proposed framework
because it shows that sustainability communication is produced by
organizations but constrained and evaluated within broader
institutional environments.
Sustainability communication provides the wider field in
which this article is located. Compared with CSR
communication, which often centers on corporate responsibility
and legitimacy, sustainability communication has a broader
social orientation. It addresses communication of, about, and for
sustainability, and it is concerned with participation, cultural
context, social learning, and behavioral change (Franz-Balsen
and Heinrichs, 2007; Gabai, 2025; Tölkes, 2018; Weder, 2025).
This broader orientation helps distinguish sustainability
communication from CSR communication and green marketing.
Green marketing may focus on environmentally oriented
products or services. Sustainability communication asks how
communication contributes to public understanding,
participation, and change. The present framework builds on this
distinction by treating digital marketing communication as one
site where organizational responsibility, material practice,
cultural meaning, and societal expectation meet.
Taken together, these approaches clarify why a value-alignment
framework is needed. Stakeholder theory explains the relational
context of responsibility. CSR communication explains how
responsibility claims are made credible or questionable. Institutional
perspectives explain the rules and expectations that shape
organizational communication. Sustainability communication
explains the wider social and cultural scope of sustainability-
oriented change. The contribution of the present framework is to
connect these insights and examine how alignment or misalignment
appears in sustainable digital marketing communication.
3 Sustainability dimensions
The 2 × 2 framework is organized around two analytical
distinctions: organizational vs. societal levels, and material vs.
cultural dimensions. The organizational–societal distinction is
used because sustainability communication is produced by
organizations but interpreted within wider stakeholder,
institutional, and societal settings. Stakeholder theory and CSR
communication research show that organizational responsibility
is evaluated through stakeholder expectations, credibility
judgments, engagement, and legitimacy concerns (de Bussy,
2018; Devin and Lane, 2014; Ellerup Nielsen and Thomsen,
2018; Shah and Guild, 2022). Institutional perspectives also
suggest that sustainability communication is shaped by reporting
expectations and field-level norms rather than by organizational
intention alone (Mangiuc and Kagitci, 2025; Xu and Woo,
2023). The material–cultural distinction is used because
sustainability claims depend not only on observable practices,
infrastructures, and reporting arrangements, but also on the
meanings through which stakeholders interpret sustainability.
Sustainability communication research is useful here because it
emphasizes participation, cultural context, and social change
rather than product promotion or corporate reputation alone
(Franz-Balsen and Heinrichs, 2007; Gabai, 2025; Tölkes, 2018;
Weder, 2025). These two distinctions allow the framework to
examine where alignment or misalignment may occur between
what organizations do, what they claim, how digital and
institutional systems support those claims, and how stakeholders
interpret them.
This dimensional structure is also consistent with multi-level
and multidimensional approaches to sustainability analysis.
Research on sustainability disclosure distinguishes among
macro, meso, and micro determinants, showing that
organizational communication is shaped by broader political,
legal, cultural, industry, media, and firm-level conditions (Ali
and Wilson, 2024). Studies of sustainability communication and
stakeholder engagement also indicate that organizational efforts
need to be understood in relation to stakeholder interpretation
and societal expectations (Agrawal et al., 2025). The material–
cultural distinction is supported by work showing that
sustainability is not only a matter of environmental or
operational performance, but also involves cultural dimensions
and socio-material practices (Fuentes and Fredriksson, 2016;
Vollero et al., 2020). The 2 × 2 structure therefore does not
simply classify sustainability topics. It identifies the main sites
where alignment or misalignment can occur.
At the organizational level, digital strategies influence both
material and cultural aspects of sustainability. From an
organizational–ma terial perspective, digital transformation can
support operational efficiency and environmental performance
through sustainable technologies and digital innovation
practices. When digital strategies are aligned with sustainability
governance, organizations can support resource optimization,
waste reduction, and digital circular economy practices,
thereby strengthening the material basis of sustainability-
oriented communication (Gül Ekşi and Kılıç, 2025). Proactive
sustainability strategies integrated into digital business models
can further strengthen corporate sustainability performance,
especially when strategic alignment and stakeholder
engagement are taken seriously (Dhyanasaridewi et al., 2024).
For sustainable digital marketing communication, the
organizational–ma terial dimension matters because credible
sustainability messages require some relationship to actual

organizational practices. Digital communication can make
sustainability claims more visible, but visibility alone cannot
provide credibility if the material basis of sustainability
remains unclear.
The organizational–cultural dimension concerns the values,
norms, and internal meanings that shape sustainability-oriented
communication. Digital strategies can contribute to
sustainability-oriented organizational cultures when ethical
principles, responsibility, and long-term thinking are embedded
into everyday practices. Corporate Digital Responsibility (CDR),
for example, emphasizes transparency, accountability, and
ethical digital practices, thereby shaping organizational values
and strategic orientations (Khani et al., 2025). Human-centered
digital leadership approaches associated with the Society 5.0
paradigm also show how sustainability principles can be
integrated into organizational culture, internal cohesion, and
sustainable corporate behaviors (Smuts and van der Merwe,
2025). In the context of sustainable digital marketing
communication, the organizational–cultural dimension explains
why sustainability communication cannot be understood only as
the external presentation of environmental or social initiatives.
Sustainability messages also depend on the cultural orientation
of the organization, including whether transparency,
accountability, and responsibility are treated as internal values
rather than external slogans.
At the societal level, digital strategies also operate across
material and cultural dimensions. From a societal–material
perspective, digital strategies support resource efficiency and the
development of circular economic systems. Digital circular
economy models can facilitate closed-loop processes that
minimize waste and optimize resource utilization, thereby
contributing to broader societal sustainability outcomes (Gül
Ekşi and Kılıç, 2025). The integration of digital and
environmental strategies, often described as the green and digital
twin transition, further highlights the systemic role of digital
innovation in climate neutrality and long-term environmental
sustainability (Kharb et al., 2024). For sustainable digital
marketing communication, the societal–material dimension is
important because digital marketing does not operate outside
material infrastructures. Platform systems, supply chains,
consumption environments, and circular economy practices
shape the social conditions under which sustainability messages
are received and evaluated.
The societal–cultural dimension refers to the collective values,
meanings, and public narratives through which sustainability
becomes socially understandable. Digital communication and
marketing practices within cultural and institutional contexts
contribute to the dissemination of sustainability narratives,
reinforce cultural identity, and encourage public engagement
(Avlonitou et al., 2025). Through digital storytelling and
interactive platforms, organizations can connect sustainability
objectives with societal values and forms of participation.
Sustainable digital marketing communication therefore depends
not only on organizational claims or digital tools, but also on
broader cultural understandings of environmental responsibility,
ethical consumption, and collective well-being. When
sustainability narratives resonate with societal values,
communication can support participation and shared
responsibility. When sustainability narratives lack cultural
resonance, digital visibility may not translate into trust
or engagement.
The four dimensions outlined above show that sustainable
digital marketing communication is multi-level and multi-
dimensional. Organizational material practices provide the
operational basis for credible sustainability claims, while
organizational cultural values shape the internal meanings that
guide communication. Societal material systems provide the
infrastructural and economic conditions in which sustainability
communication circulates, while societal cultural values shape
the public interpretation of sustainability narratives. The 2 × 2
framework therefore serves as an analytical tool for examining
how digital strategies generate sustainability-oriented
communication outcomes through the interaction of
organizational and societal contexts and through the relationship
between material and cultural dimensions. Effective sustainable
digital marketing communication requires attention to both
structural conditions and value-based orientations. The
framework presented in Table 1 summarizes these dimensions

5 Discussion
5.1 Positioning within existing literature
Recent studies have advanced sustainability communication
by emphasizing stakeholder engagement, governance
mechanisms, digital transformation, and the need for more
operationalizable models (Agrawal et al., 2025; Leal Filho et al.,
2025; Shabbir, 2025; Wang et al., 2025). Strategic
communication research also shows that sustainability-related
communication is closely tied to legitimacy, transparency, and
participation (Aggerholm and Thomsen, 2024). These studies
provide an important basis for the present article, but they do
not fully explain how organizational practices, cultural
meanings, digital infrastructures, and societal expectations
become aligned or misaligned in sustainable digital
marketing communication.
CSR communication research helps specify the
communicative side of this problem. Responsibility claims are
evaluated through transparency, credibility, stakeholder
engagement, and legitimacy. Arvidsson (2010) shows that CSR
communication is part of how organizations present
responsibility to external audiences. Ellerup Nielsen and
Thomsen (2018) review CSR communication from a legitimacy
perspective, while Lock and Schulz-Knappe (2019) show that
credible CSR communication can strengthen perceived
legitimacy. Devin and Lane (2014) further clarify that
engagement is not only an organizational activity but also
something constructed and communicated. These studies are
important for the present article because they show that
sustainability-oriented claims are not judged only by their
content, but also by how stakeholders evaluate their credibility
and relevance.
Sustainability communication expands this discussion beyond
corporate responsibility. It is concerned not only with how
organizations communicate responsibility, but also with how
communication contributes to participation, cultural
interpretation, and social change. Research on sustainability
communication emphasizes communication of, about, and for
sustainability, as well as the importance of cultural and local
contexts (Franz-Balsen and Heinrichs, 2007; Gabai, 2025;
Weder, 2025). Tölkes (2018) also shows that sustainability
communication research has developed across applied fields
where communication must address stakeholder understanding
and behavior rather than only promote sustainable products.
This broader orientation helps distinguish sustainability
communication from CSR communication and green marketing.
Greenwashing research clarifies why alignment is central to
sustainable digital marketing communication. The literature
identifies several mechanisms through which greenwashing risk
may emerge, including inconsistency between claims and
practices, selective disclosure, symbolic communication, limited
transparency, and stakeholder skepticism (Raman et al., 2025;
Vollero et al., 2016; Yilmaz and Abanoz, 2025). Studies of social
media and digital marketing also suggest that digital platforms
can amplify both sustainability claims and public skepticism
when stakeholders perceive a lack of credible support for those
claims (Balcarová et al., 2025; Vukasović et al., 2026). These
studies support the present article’s focus on value alignment.
The issue is not only whether an organization communicates
sustainability, but whether its digital sustainability claims are
connected with organizational practices, institutional
expectations, transparent evidence, and stakeholder interpretation.
The present framework therefore connects existing discussions
that are often treated separately. It uses the organizational–societal

5.2 Theoretical advancement
The theoretical contribution of this article lies in reframing
sustainable digital marketing communication as a value-
alignment problem. Existing approaches provide important but
partial explanations. CSR communication explains how
responsibility claims are communicated and evaluated.
Sustainability communication explains the wider social and
cultural scope of sustainability-oriented communication.
Stakeholder-oriented communication explains why responsibility
claims must be interpreted in relation to stakeholder
expectations. Greenwashing research explains why unsupported,
selective, or symbolic sustainability claims may produce
skepticism rather than trust. The proposed framework brings
these concerns together by examining how organizational
material practices, organizational cultural values, societal
material infrastructures, and societal cultural expectations
interact when sustainability claims circulate through digital
marketing. Prosocial values remain part of this model as the
normative basis for judging whether such alignment supports
responsibility, cooperation, and collective well-being.
The 2 × 2 sustainability dimensions framework and the 4 × 4
interaction structure extend this discussion by moving from
static classification to relational analysis. The 2 × 2 framework
distinguishes organizational and societal levels as well as
material and cultural dimensions. The 4 × 4 interaction structure
then shows how these dimensions affect one another. This
movement from classification to interaction is important
because sustainable digital marketing communication does not
operate only through single messages, single technologies, or
single stakeholder groups. Digital marketing communication
becomes sustainable when material practices and cultural values
are connected across organizational and societal contexts. The
framework therefore provides a way to examine why a
sustainability message may be visible but not credible,
technically efficient but culturally weak, or strategically planned
but poorly aligned with stakeholder expectations.
The framework also advances strategic communication
research by positioning communication as a coordinating
mechanism that links material practices with cultural values.
Prior studies have emphasized the importance of legitimacy,
transparency, and stakeholder engagement in strategic
communication (Aggerholm and Thomsen, 2024). The present
article extends this emphasis by placing these concerns within
digital sustainability processes. In sustainable digital marketing
communication, legitimacy and transparency cannot be
understood only as qualities of messages. Legitimacy and
transparency depend on the relationship between what
organizations do, what organizations communicate, and how
stakeholders interpret sustainability claims in digital
environments. Strategic communication therefore helps explain
the coordination of practices, messages, and stakeholder
relationships across multiple dimensions.
The incorporation of prosocial values adds a further
theoretical layer. Sustainability communication can be studied
through functional or technological questions, such as how
organizations communicate more effectively, how platforms
increase visibility, or how digital tools support stakeholder
engagement. These questions are important, but they do not
fully explain why sustainability communication requires ethical
credibility. By integrating prosocial values such as ethical
responsibility, collective welfare, and cooperation, the framework
provides a normative foundation for understanding sustainable
marketing communication. Prosocial values help explain why
sustainability claims are evaluated not only as persuasive
messages, but also as expressions of responsibility, trust, and
value consistency. This theoretical move is especially important
for sustainable digital marketing because digital communication
can amplify both sustainability narratives and doubts about
whether those narratives are consistent with organizational
practice.
5.3 Greenwashing as value misalignment
Greenwashing can be understood in this article as a form of
value misalignment. The literature identifies several recurring
mechanisms of greenwashing, including inconsistency between
claims and practices, selective disclosure, symbolic
communication, limited transparency, and stakeholder
skepticism (Raman et al., 2025; Vollero et al., 2016; Yilmaz and
Abanoz, 2025). The evaluation literature further suggests that
greenwashing can be examined through textual features,
verifiability, consistency between narratives and performance,
consumer perception, institutional pressures, and digital
platform practices (Daou et al., 2025; Okeke, 2026; Putri et al.,
2025; Vukasović et al., 2026). These dimensions are useful for
the present article because they show how greenwashing can be
studied without reducing it to a simple problem of
misleading wording.
The proposed framework helps specify where greenwashing
risk may emerge. At the organizational–material level, risk
appears when sustainability claims are not supported by
observable practices. At the organizational–cultural level, risk
appears when public-facing sustainability narratives are
disconnected from internal values or responsibility
commitments. At the societal–material level, risk appears when
organizational claims are not supported by reporting systems,
infrastructures, certifications, or institutional expectations. At
the societal–cultural level, risk appears when sustainability
narratives fail to resonate with public values or are interpreted
as symbolic rather than substantive. Transparency is important,
but transparency cues may not always produce trust if
stakeholders interpret them as incomplete, strategic, or
unsupported by action (Reck et al., 2022). Third-party
certifications may strengthen perceived authenticity and
credibility, but they do not remove the need for consistency
between claims and practices (Marschlich and Hurtado, 2024;
Parguel et al., 2011).

5.4 Research propositions
Conceptual articles often use propositions to connect
theoretical frameworks with future empirical testing. In
sustainability and CSR communication research, propositions
are commonly organized around the main dimensions of a
model, the relationships among key concepts, or the research
gaps identified by the framework (Goh et al., 2026; Matharu
et al., 2020). Following this approach, the propositions below are
offered as conceptual propositions rather than tested hypotheses.
The first proposition concerns the relationship between
organizational practice and organizational values. Sustainable
digital marketing communication is more likely to be perceived
as credible when organizational material practices are aligned
with organizational cultural values. This proposition follows
from the central argument of the article: sustainability claims
become more persuasive when they are supported by visible
practices and by internal commitments that make those
practices meaningful.
The second proposition concerns transparency and
verifiability. Transparency is more likely to strengthen
stakeholder trust when sustainability claims are supported by
observable practices, consistent communication, and verifiable
evidence. This proposition is important because transparency
alone may not always create trust. Its effect depends on whether
stakeholders interpret the disclosed information as complete,
relevant, and connected with actual organizational conduct.
The third proposition concerns stakeholder engagement.
Stakeholder engagement is more likely to support legitimacy
when digital marketing communication allows stakeholders to
interpret, question, and respond to sustainability claims. This
proposition treats engagement not as a simple communication
outcome, but as a relational process through which sustainability
claims are examined and negotiated.
The fourth proposition concerns greenwashing skepticism.
Greenwashing skepticism is more likely to increase when
sustainability claims are selective, symbolic, or weakly connected
to organizational practices. This proposition links the value-
alignment framework with greenwashing research by suggesting
that skepticism emerges when stakeholders perceive a gap
between what organizations claim and what they appear to do.
The fifth proposition concerns digital platforms. Digital
platforms can strengthen sustainability communication when
they support stakeholder feedback, data visibility, and
accountability. However, they may also intensify skepticism
when highly visible claims are not sufficiently supported by
credible evidence. This proposition reflects the double function
of digital environments: they can extend the reach of
sustainability communication, but they can also make
inconsistencies more visible.
The sixth proposition concerns prosocial values. Prosocial
values are more likely to strengthen perceived ethical credibility
when they are connected with transparent action, shared
responsibility, and stakeholder recognition. This proposition
clarifies that prosocial values do not operate only as abstract
moral ideals. They become meaningful in communication when
stakeholders can see how responsibility, cooperation, and
collective welfare are reflected in organizational practice.
These propositions can guide future empirical studies.
Quantitative research may test relationships among transparency,
credibility, stakeholder trust, legitimacy, greenwashing skepticism,
and stakeholder engagement. Qualitative research may examine
how stakeholders interpret alignment or misalignment across
organizational and societal contexts. Digital methods may also be
used to analyze sustainability reports, website content, platform
comments, and social media discussions in order to observe how
sustainability claims are circulated, questioned, and contested.
5.5 Implications for future research
The framework can guide future studies of how organizations
operationalize sustainable digital marketing communication
across industries, platforms, and cultural contexts. Future
research may first examine the propositions developed above by
testing relationships among transparency, credibility, stakeholder
trust, legitimacy, and greenwashing skepticism. Studies of digital
marketing and environmental management suggest that
stakeholder engagement can be linked with digital
communication and sustainability-related outcomes (Huynh and
Nguyen, 2025). Research on social media greenwashing also
suggests that trust-related reactions are central to how
stakeholders evaluate sustainability claims in digital
environments (Vukasović et al., 2026). These relationships can
be examined through surveys, experiments, content analysis, or
mixed-method designs.
Cross-cultural research is also needed because prosocial
values, sustainability expectations, and communication norms
vary across societies. Different cultural contexts may shape how
stakeholders interpret responsibility, authenticity, environmental
concern, and organizational legitimacy. A framework that
includes both societal and cultural dimensions can support more
context-sensitive analysis of sustainable digital marketing
communication. Longitudinal research would also be valuable
because sustainability-oriented communication strategies evolve
in response to changing stakeholder expectations, platform
environments, and regulatory conditions. Longitudinal studies
could show how organizations adapt sustainability
communication over time and how patterns of trust, skepticism,
and engagement develop across repeated interactions.
Future research may also examine the role of digital
infrastructures and data-driven communication in sustainability
communication. Digital platforms can support collaboration and
sustainability performance when they enable interaction across
organizational boundaries (Holopainen et al., 2026). Web-based
self-reporting systems may also support sustainability data
transfer and transparency for stakeholder use (Helbig and von
Höveling, 2024). At the same time, information,
communication, and media technologies can shape sustainability

5.6 Synthesis
The discussion above suggests that sustainable digital
marketing communication should be understood as an evolving
system rather than a fixed set of practices. Strategic
communication, digital technologies, and prosocial values do
not operate as separate elements. Strategic communication
provides a way to coordinate organizational practices and
stakeholder relationships. Digital technologies shape the
infrastructures through which sustainability messages are
circulated and evaluated. Prosocial values provide a normative
basis for judging whether sustainability communication remains
connected with responsibility, trust, and collective well-being.
The interaction among these elements requires organizations to
adjust communication strategies while maintaining coherence
with sustainability objectives and social expectations.
The framework developed in this article contributes to
sustainability communication research by connecting
sustainability communication, strategic communication, and
digital transformation within a value-based structure. In doing
so, the article responds to calls for more coherent and
operationalizable models in sustainability communication
research (Leal Filho et al., 2025). The central contribution is not
only the proposal of a 2 × 2 framework or a 4 × 4 interaction
matrix. The broader contribution lies in explaining how
sustainable digital marketing communication can be examined
as a relational system linking practices, values, infrastructures,
and public expectations. This perspective is useful for the
Sustainable Marketing in the Digital Era research agenda
because it positions digital communication as a site where
sustainability claims are coordinated, interpreted, and evaluated.
6 Conclusion
This article has developed a conceptual framework for
understanding sustainable digital marketing communication as a
multidimensional process shaped by strategic communication
and prosocial values. The central argument is that sustainability
in digital marketing cannot be understood only as a matter of
promoting green products, increasing message visibility, or
adopting digital tools. Sustainable digital marketing
communication also depends on how organizational practices,
stakeholder expectations, material infrastructures, and cultural
values are connected. From this perspective, communication is
not simply a channel through which sustainability is reported to
external audiences. Communication is part of the process
through which sustainability claims become visible,
interpretable, credible, and open to public evaluation.
The 2 × 2 sustainability dimensions framework provides one
way to organize this process. By distinguishing between
organizational and societal levels, and between material and
cultural dimensions, the framework shows that sustainability-
oriented communication operates across multiple domains at the
same time. Organizational material practices provide the
operational basis for sustainability claims, while organizational
cultural values shape the meanings and responsibilities that
guide communication. Societal material systems influence the
infrastructures and conditions through which sustainability
communication circulates, while societal cultural values shape
how sustainability narratives are interpreted by publics. The
4 × 4 interaction matrix further extends this framework by
showing that these dimensions do not operate separately.
Sustainable digital marketing communication emerges through
interaction among practices, values, infrastructures,
and expectations.
Strategic communication and prosocial values play
complementary roles in this framework. Strategic
communication explains how organizations coordinate
objectives, messages, stakeholder relationships, and sustainability
practices across different contexts. Prosocial values explain why
sustainable marketing communication requires more than
strategic consistency or persuasive effectiveness. Sustainability
claims also require ethical responsibility, public trust,
transparency, and value consistency. This is especially important
in digital environments, where platform visibility can amplify
both sustainability narratives and public skepticism. When
sustainability communication is disconnected from
organizational practice, digital visibility may weaken rather than
strengthen credibility. When sustainability communication is
connected with recognizable practices and shared values, digital
marketing can support more credible and socially meaningful
forms of stakeholder engagement.
The framework also has practical implications for
organizations that communicate sustainability in digital
environments. Organizations need to consider whether
sustainability messages are supported by material practices,
whether digital communication strategies remain transparent
and accountable, and whether sustainability narratives resonate
with stakeholder expectations and cultural values. This
perspective is useful for examining problems such as
greenwashing, fragmented sustainability messaging, and the
ethical use of personalization in digital communication. In
particular, the framework treats greenwashing as a risk of
misalignment among sustainability claims, organizational
practices, institutional supports, and stakeholder interpretation.
Sustainable digital marketing communication should therefore
be designed not only around reach, engagement, or persuasion,
but also around the consistency between public-facing claims
and the material and cultural conditions that support them.
As a conceptual analysis, the article does not empirically test
the relationships proposed in the framework. The value of the
framework lies in clarifying how key dimensions and
interactions can be understood and examined. Future research
may apply the framework to specific industries, platforms,
cultural contexts, or digital marketing practices. Comparative
studies could examine how sustainable marketing
communication differs across organizational sectors or national
contexts. Platform-focused studies could explore how
personalization, digital storytelling, reporting systems, or
stakeholder comments shape the perceived credibility of
