Rapport is essential for successful human interaction, representing the gratifying
experience of attunement and getting along well with a communication partner.
Notably, psychiatry has rarely treated rapport as an explicit measurable process,
despite its reliance on sustained interpersonal connection, sensitive information
disclosure, and therapeutic alliance. With reference to earlier conceptual work,
and drawing on interdisciplinary literature, we conceptualize rapport as the
dynamic coordination of behavioral, affective, and cognitive signals within
dyadic interaction. For successful communication, as opposed to disturbed
communication, it is recommended to situate rapport within broader
accounts of feedback, mutual ﬁt, efﬁciency, and ﬂexibility of communication.
Advances in multimodal interaction research (e.g., gaze, prosody, gesture,
posture, motion) provide options for objective validation of the subjective
rapport experience. These methods assess alignment and disruption of
communicative signals across participants. Applications span science
communication, education, marketing, psychotherapy, and psychiatry,
especially in cases involving communication disturbances such as autism
spectrum disorder. We outline an integrated framework of rapport and a pilot
study design for continuous real-time rapport measurement by human raters
or automated systems. Recognizing rapport as a measurable clinical process
grounds psychiatry within the broader science of human interaction research.

1 Introduction: rapport is more than a feeling –
A deﬁnable and dynamic construct
Successful interpersonal interaction depends on numerous experiential and behavioral
aspects of the interaction partners (including sensory, motor, cognitive, and emotional
aspects), but an essential factor is whether the interlocutors show mutual attentiveness,
understanding, openness, and generally interact positively and harmoniously – a
phenomenon called rapport. Rapport constitutes a central construct in the study of
human interaction, yet its deﬁnition varies across contexts. What unites these
approaches is the insight that rapport is an emergent property of the dyad as the basic
unit of the analysis, which arises between individuals, but is not a ﬁxed personality
trait of any of the interlocutors. It is consistently described as a positively valanced
relational state, i.e., being “in tune with” or “on the same wavelength” ( Bernieri, 1988,
p. 121).
In Jürgen Ruesch’s “Disturbed Communication” (1957), rapport was framed as
“gratiﬁcation” resulting from successful communication. Ruesch, premising that every
psychopathological state is a disorder of communication, presented an account of
successful (vs. disturbed) communication, namely:

Tickle-Degnen and Rosenthal (1990) subdivide rapport into
mutual attentiveness, positivity, and coordination. Mutual
attentiveness refers to sustained focus; positivity encompasses
warmth and receptivity; and coordination involves synchrony.
These components shift in relevance during a conversation:
attentiveness and positivity dominate the beginning, whereas
coordination becomes increasingly relevant later.
Recent conceptualizations try to integrate rapport’s dual
character as both a subjective state and a functional process. It
can be deﬁned either experientially as “people’s perceptions of
harmony, smoothness, and warmth in interpersonal relations”
(Spencer-Oatey and Franklin , 2009), or – functionally – as the
“invisible wave along which information can :f_low” ( St-Yves,
2006). In both cases, rapport enables efﬁcient and distortion-free
communication. A complementary perspective comes from
Gremler and Gwinner (2008) , who, across disciplines, identiﬁed
four broad categories of rapport-building behavior. These
categories re:f_lect how rapport is enacted in moment-to-moment
interaction, underscoring its observable and trainable dimensions:
• courteous behavior (e.g., politeness, smiling, expressing
concern for others’ well-being)
• common grounding behavior (e.g., establishing shared
interests or experiences)
• attentive behavior (e.g., eye contact, back-channel
responses)
• imitative behavior (e.g., mirroring posture, gestures, or
vocal patterns)
Beyond behavioral categories, a multidimensional ac count situates
rapport at the intersection of emotional, cognitive , and behavioral
processes. Rapport, as conceptualized by Murphy and Rodríguez-
Manzanares (2012) , is a multifaceted dyadic phenomenon
encompassing mutual attentiveness, respect, underst anding, and
openness, together with coordinated interaction and  movement.
These dimensions are mutually reinforcing, as affec tive resonance
motivates attentiveness, cognitive alignment sustai ns interaction,
and behavioral synchrony deepens connection, together producing
an experience perceived as positive, harmonious, and smooth. The
emergence of rapport therefore requires convergence  across its
observable constituents. Social context and environ mental setting
strongly shape how rapport manifests ( Gremler and Gwinner ,
2008; Murphy and Rodríguez-Manzanares , 2012). Encounter
structure, participant familiarity, and asymmetries  of expertise or
authority in:f_luence rapport emergence and sustainabi lity. This
contextual dependency explains the heterogeneity of  rapport
deﬁnitions across domains like psychotherapy, educa tion,
marketing, and science communication.
The conceptual relation between rapport and trust adds
another important layer of clarity. While rapport captures
immediate synchrony and alignment, trust implies vulnerability:
“the willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of
another, based on the expectation that the other will perform an
important action irrespective of control” ( Mayer et al. , 1995,
p. 712). Rapport often precedes and enables trust by creating an
atmosphere in which vulnerability feels safe. Trust, contrarily,
stabilizes rapport across relational ruptures. This distinction is
particularly salient in psychiatry, where patients disclose
sensitive and potentially stigmatized experiences under
conditions of inherent vulnerability.
Rapport is the resultant of successful communication: it
ensures feedback, ﬁt, efﬁciency, :f_lexibility, and gratiﬁcation as
rapport surrogate, while buffering against interference,
inefﬁciency, and frustration. In this way, rapport emerges as a
dynamic, context-dependent process that is simultaneously
experiential, observable, and functional – an indispensable
condition for meaningful human connection.
2 Seeing rapport in action: from
signals to shared experience
Rapport is experienced subjectively as a sense of connection,
yet it is of profound interest to assess, both for clinical as well
as training purposes, whether it may also be validated
objectively through observable markers and external ratings. It
can be hypothesized that rapport leaves measurable traces across
communication signals such as gaze, speech, intonation, gesture,
posture, and movement, which align with how third-party
observers perceive the quality of an interaction.
Gaze and eye contact have already been shown to serve as
robust indicators of connectedness ( Beck et al. , 2002; Harrigan,
1985; Mast, 2007). Sustained mutual gaze conveys attentiveness
and engagement ( Bavelas et al. , 2002; Cook, 1977), while
mismatched gaze behavior often signals disruption ( Bavelas
et al. , 2002; Jokinen et al. , 2013). Importantly, interpretations
must remain sensitive to cultural and clinical contexts, where
reduced eye contact may re:f_lect alternative communicative
norms rather than disengagement ( Hall et al. , 1995; Harrigan,
1985; Harrigan and Rosenthal , 1983). Speech and intonation
provide additional cues. Rapport is associated with convergence
in prosody, i.e., similarities in pitch, rhythm, and pacing, that
convey warmth and responsiveness ( Haskard et al. , 2008).
Abrupt or discordant changes in vocal patterns may, in turn,
indicate relational rupture. Gesture, posture, and bodily
alignment similarly impact rapport. Subtle mirroring of gestures
and coordinated body orientation likely improve experienced
rapport ( Bernieri, 1988; Dale et al. , 2020; Murphy and

listening (i.e., backchanneling, prosody), and afﬁliative
acknowledgement, and troughs linked to silence or overlapping
talk. Such reliability would suggest rapport to be not a hidden
and mysterious construct accessible only to experts, but a
publicly perceivable phenomenon that can be measured across
diverse observer groups.
3 Rapport in practice and application:
building trust across contexts
3.1 Psychotherapy and psychiatry:
interactional asymmetry and clinical
vulnerability
Psychotherapy can be considered a canonical reference case in
which rapport approximates its classical formulation.
Psychotherapeutic interaction is typically dyadic, face-to-face,
and sustained over time, allowing rapport to emerge through
direct feedback, attunement, and coordination. Here, rapport
closely aligns with the therapeutic alliance, a robust predictor of
treatment outcome ( Horvath and Symonds , 1991), while being
established under heightened vulnerability, constrained feedback,
and direct clinical consequences. Rapport enables disclosure of
sensitive experiences, fosters an environment of safety, and
supports treatment adherence ( English et al. , 2022; Sharkiya,
2023). Despite this, psychiatry has largely neglected rapport as
an explicit, measurable construct.
This omission is striking as patients often enter treatment in
states of heightened vulnerability, shaped by stigma, coercion, or
fragile trust in institutions ( Gaebel et al. , 2014). This makes
rapport an active therapeutic mechanism ( Gaebel et al. , 2014;
Opland and Torrico , 2025). Clinical encounters characterized by
attunement, conveyed by gaze, vocal tone, gesture, and narrative
alignment, create conditions in which patients can risk
disclosure and collaboratively construct meaning. Conversely,
failures of rapport manifest as ruptures in alliance,
disengagement, or non-adherence ( Gaebel et al. , 2014).
When conventional rapport markers are altered, clinicians risk
misinterpreting engagement. For instance, in autism spectrum
disorder, differences in eye contact, prosody, and nonverbal
communication ( Bloch et al. , 2024; Grice et al. , 2016; Jording
et al. , 2024; Wehrle et al. , 2023, 2024) can be misinterpreted
(Crompton et al. , 2020). Recognizing rapport as :f_lexible and
context-dependent prevents such misattributions: allowing
clinicians to adapt to the patient’s communicative style. Similar
dynamics occur in schizophrenia ( Chapellier et al. , 2022; Green
et al. , 2015), borderline personality disorder ( Zegarra-Valdivia
and Chino Vilca , 2019), or post-traumatic stress disorder ( Hall
and Werner , 2022), where disturbances in social cognition or
trust complicate relational alignment ( Santos, 2017; Walker,
2022). Norm-based rapport assessments risk pathologizing
functional interaction styles in “neurodivergent” and/or
psychiatric populations, a risk ampliﬁed by automated systems
trained on “neurotypical” data, making a :f_lexible, interaction-
speciﬁc conception of rapport a methodological and
ethical necessity.
From a phenomenological perspective, mental disorders
disrupt a person’s lived sense of connection – to oneself, others,
and one’s life world. Minkowski (1933) described this
connection as “moi – ici – maintenant” being a self that is
grounded in time and space. This idea can be enriched by
acknowledging that the self unfolds through its relation to
others. Self-experience becomes communicable only through the
responsiveness of another person, in a psychiatric context often
the clinician. Ruesch (1957) understood psychiatric illness as a
disturbance of communication in which reciprocity and
feedback are lost. Rapport may thus be viewed as the process
through which this reciprocity is constituted or restored, re-
establishing shared meaning within the therapeutic encounter
and anchoring both participants in a common “moi – toi – ici -
maintenant,” extending Minkowski’s wording and counteracting
alienation to enable recovery.
Rapport emerges through two interlinked layers of
interpersonal exchange (see Figure 1). The ﬁrst layer encompasses
the perception and production of behavioral cues (e .g., speech,
intonation, gaze, facial expressions, and gesture) and physiological
signals (e.g., heart rate variability, electroderma l response,
breathing rate), a measurable ground layer. These ﬁ nd their
dyadic counterparts in behavioral signatures such a s turn-taking,
:f_loor-time, or mutual silence. The second layer conc erns how
these cues, signals, and signatures are received an d give rise to
the experience of being noticed, understood, or “in  tune.” This
layer is experiential and captures the lived resona nce of
interaction—e.g., coordination, mutual presence, rhythmicity, a nd
synchrony—that cannot be reduced to observable behavior alone .
The two layers are inseparable: selective attention  and cue
awareness shape receptive experience, while recepti ve openness,
modulated by temporal and contextual factors, alter s the
weighting and meaning of speciﬁc cues.
Importantly, dominant conceptions of rapport in clinical and
interaction research are largely derived from Western, educated,
industrialized, rich, and democratic (WEIRD) populations
(Henrich et al. , 2010) from institutional contexts that privilege
speciﬁc communicative norms. Indicators such as sustained eye

3.2 Science communication as a
prototypical example of mediated
communication formats: expanding the
rapport paradigm
Science communication encompasses a range of formats
through which complex, uncertain, or contested information is
conveyed across asymmetries of expertise, including lectures,
media interviews, broadcasts, and online platforms ( Druckman
et al. , 2025). Effective communication exceeds technical accuracy
and depends on relational conditions that sustain attentiveness
and interpretability ( Hovland and Weiss , 1951). Research shows
that scientiﬁc messages are evaluated not only on factual or
logical grounds but also in relation to characteristics of both
interlocutors and the interactional context, including perceived
credibility, trustworthiness, and responsiveness ( Hovland, 1948;
Richmond and McCroskey , 1995; Trumbo and McComas , 2003).
These relational factors are especially salient in science
communication because interaction is frequently mediated by
technical formats, or third parties such as moderators, which
constrain direct reciprocal exchange.
Credibility refers to perceived expertise and reliability, while
trust involves a preparedness to accept vulnerability based on
these perceptions ( Besley et al. , 2018; McComas, 2001). Rapport,
by contrast, operates at the level of interaction itself, structuring
moment-to-moment communicative dynamics. This distinction
echoes Watzlawick et al.’s (1967) proposal that communication
comprises content and relationship aspects, with the latter
shaping the former and its interpretation. In science
communication, rapport functions less as a determinant of
persuasion than as a relational infrastructure that enables
openness, synchrony, and responsiveness, supporting
engagement without guaranteeing acceptance or belief. Actively
building such relational infrastructure can be particularly
challenging where immediacy and proximity between a
presenter and their audience are mediated by technical devices.

televised expert interviews, storytelling enhances emotional

formats that systematically reorganize the interactional structure
of face-to-face encounters. Formats can be distinguished
according to a small set of theoretically relevant interactional
dimensions: the number and conﬁguration of participating
parties, the degree of temporal coupling, the symmetry of
communicative activity, and the availability of feedback and
repair. These dimensions jointly determine which relational cues
can be produced, perceived, and attributed, and therefore how
rapport can emerge under conditions of limited reciprocity.

3.3 Feasibility studies and measurement
Rapport can be noticed and judged by external parti es, even
when they are not themselves involved in the intera ction
(Bernieri et al. , 1996). Observers are naturally able to point out
when two people appear “in tune” or when their inte raction
becomes strained. However, very little is known abo ut the speciﬁc
cues by which rapport is perceived, nor whether its  dynamics can
be tracked reliably over time. Among the central op en research
desiderates is the question whether these perceptio ns can be
quantiﬁed in real time, capturing the dynamics of r apport as
dyadic interactions unfold in time. This would chan ge the
concept of rapport from a general impression to a m easurable
construct tested across different contexts, and eve ntually applied
in clinical training.
A ﬁrst study would avoid the complexity of psychiatric
encounters and instead examine non-clinical dyads in structured
yet naturalistic conversations. Dyads could be asked to complete
cooperative tasks (e.g., a tangram puzzle or joint problem-
solving exercise to elicit semi-spontaneous communication
between interlocutors) alongside periods of open discussion,
thereby eliciting a range of interactional dynamics ( Spaniol
et al. , 2024). High-deﬁnition video and audio recordings would
provide material for human ratings and multimodal analyses.
Two rater groups would then assess rapport: trained
communication professionals, familiar with the construct and
skilled in identifying its behavioral manifestations, and naive lay
observers, given only a concise, accessible deﬁnition of rapport
(e.g., “the sense of connection, attentiveness, and harmony
between two people”).
Bente and colleagues (2020) demonstrated how motion
capture and synchrony analyses can relate movement data to
observers’ perceptions of rapport. Similarly, one could conduct a
study in which both groups use a continuous rating interface
(e.g., a slider) while viewing video segments ( Bente et al. , 2020),
adjusting the slider in real time to re:f_lect their perception of
rapport as it :f_luctuates across the interaction. The central
question concerns the degree of agreement among naive
observers. If untrained individuals converge in their moment-to-
moment perception of rapport, this would suggest that rapport
is a publicly perceivable phenomenon, accessible through
intuitive social perception.
To extend these ratings, multimodal behavioral coding could
link shifts in rapport judgments to observable events. This
continues Ruesch’s intuition of psychopathology as disturbed
communication, but his criteria were too coarse-grained for
empirical research. Candidate correlates include mutual gaze
and gaze aversion ( Cook, 1977; Rifai et al. , 2022), prosodic
alignment ( Haskard et al. , 2008; Park and Park , 2018), gesture
mirroring and posture synchrony ( Dale et al. , 2020), and

4 Outlook and conclusions: towards
future rapport research
Rapport is a dynamic, measurable process that sustains
communication across domains by bridging asymmetries of
knowledge, authority, or vulnerability and shaping whether
interactions become cooperative or strained. Rather than having
a unitary form, rapport is likely instantiated through context-
speciﬁc weightings of shared cues, coordination, and experiential
resonance, shaped by technological mediation and
communicative constraints. Future research should relate
subjective experiences of rapport to objective indicators and test
whether integrative multimodal measures provide greater
diagnostic or prognostic value than single modalities. Viewing
rapport as observable, teachable, and measurable redeﬁnes
psychiatry’s interpersonal foundations and offers a shared
framework for studying human connection, including
intercultural communication and human computer interaction.
