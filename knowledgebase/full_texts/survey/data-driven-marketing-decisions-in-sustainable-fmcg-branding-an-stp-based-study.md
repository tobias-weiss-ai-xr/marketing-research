ABSTRACT
Sustainability, ethical sourcing, transparency, and social responsibility are becoming important
factors influencing consumer brand preference and buying habits, causing a fundamental
shift in global consumer markets. Although sustainability has become a strategic issue in
the food, household and personal care product sectors, there is a lack of empirical research
that investigates the impact of sustainability-oriented consumer values on the Segmentation-
Targeting-Positioning (STP) framework, especially in emerging markets. This research has
taken up Mamaearth, a top personal care brand in India, to explore the operationalisation
of sustainable branding strategies through STP decisions. The study uses a mixed-method
exploratory design, combining quantitative survey data from 200 respondents who are familiar
with sustainable personal care brands with qualitative secondary data from sustainable
personal care brands’ consumer-facing communication, digital campaigns, and sustainability
disclosures. The results of the statistical analysis (reliability testing, Pearson correlation, linear
regression) show that the psychographic segmentation and effective implementation of STP
could be used as predictors of sustainable positioning of the brand; sustainable positioning of
the brand could be used as a predictor of consumer engagement; and the perceived brand
authenticity could be used as a predictor of sustainable brand loyalty. These results provide
further support to STP theory by showing how authenticity and ethical identity play a growing
role in sustainable consumer segmentation in emerging markets alongside demographic
classification. The research provides practical recommendations for FMCG businesses to
ensure they establish trust with consumers, implement digital targeting ethically, and ensure
their sustainability messaging is synchronised with tangible organisational action.

INTRODUCTION
Some of the key characteristics of the current marketing environment include sustainability
awareness, ethical consumption, environmental responsibility, and consumer scepticism of
the branding techniques of companies. In the last decade, sustainability shifted from the
fringes of corporate social responsibility to becoming a strategic imperative that influences
positioning, communication, product development, and the way customers are approached
(Belz & Peattie, 2012). Consumers no longer just look at price and product quality when it
comes to brands, but evaluating companies for their ethical practices, for being cruelty-free, for
their ecological responsibility, for transparency of ingredients, and for toxin-free formulations
has become more standard. In this context, the act of consuming is not only an expression of
individual identity, but it has also become a symbolic expression of personal identity, wellness
orientation and ethical convictions, which are symptomatic of the general changes taking
place in consumer culture (Firat & Venkatesh, 1995).
The Segmentation-Targeting-Positioning (STP) approach, which was first formalised by Smith
(1956) in reaction to the heterogeneity of consumer demand, is still the basic logic through

which companies take consumer insight and turn it into strategic marketing action. But with
the markets becoming more and more values-based as opposed to just demographic-based,
the validity of the classical STP logic for sustainability-oriented segments deserves attention
once more through empirical study. In line with Kotler’s overall thesis, Kotler et al. (2010) point
out that marketing needs to transcend from satisfying customers to improving societies, a
sentiment that is all the more important in sustainability-marketed markets where brand and
ethics, lifestyle, emotional identity and social acceptability begin to converge.
At the same time, marketing analytics is mainstream in the way businesses build and develop
their brand strategy. Iacobucci et al. (2019) show how marketing analytics has transitioned
from a technical function to a marketing strategic capability that allows companies to base
decisions on behaviour, and not intuition. France and Ghose (2019) also demonstrate that
data-driven marketing analysis helps companies identify hidden consumer motivators -
values, lifestyle preferences and aspirations - which are not easily identified in traditional
market research and thus enhances both segmentation accuracy and emotional positioning.
Collectively, this literature highlights that data-driven marketing has an important impact,
especially for sustainability-oriented branding, where trust and authenticity in the brand are
not easily derived from demographic data.
Indian FMCG market has seen significant growth in sustainability-oriented consumer
behaviour driven by urbanisation, increased education levels, internet penetration, wellness
consciousness, and the influence of social media. Among consumers, Millennial and Generation
Z consumers have been particularly drawn to environmentally conscious companies that
employ natural ingredients, a non-cruelty approach to production, environmentally friendly
packaging, and products free of toxins (Joshi & Rahman, 2015; Kumar & Ghodeswar, 2015).
One of the most prominent examples of this change is Mamaearth. Established in 2016 as
a personal care brand with a digital philosophy, Mamaearth has positioned itself as a brand
of family wellness, toxin-free formulations, and sustainability. The company’s extensive
digital marketing, influencer collaborations, emotional storytelling, and sustainability-focused
communication helped it make significant strides in the Indian personal care market (Kapoor
et al., 2022). In parallel, however, commercialisation of sustainability has also boosted an
increase in consumer mistrust of greenwashing and symbolic environmental marketing (Delmas
& Burbano, 2011). Consumers are losing trust in the authenticity of corporate sustainability
stories and whether they are really addressing environmental issues. That tension makes
Mamaearth an important example to explore when considering the use of the STP framework
in today’s context of digital branding.
SIGNIFICANCE OF THE STUDY
This study is significant in three ways. Academically, it provides empirical proof of the increasing
importance of psychographic and authenticity-based segmentation in sustainability-oriented
markets, where Indian evidence is scarce, extending the already existing STP theory, which is
traditionally based on demographic and geographic segmentation. It provides a framework for
FMCG decision-makers to help them calibrate their practice of sustainability communication

(ii) To analyse the influence of sustainability-oriented positioning on consumer trust and

LITERATURE REVIEW
STP Framework and Strategic Marketing
Segmentation, targeting, and positioning are one of the basic concepts of strategic marketing.
In view of the heterogeneous consumer demand, the concept of market segmentation was
developed by Smith (1956), and he suggested that the firm can cater to the market more
effectively by addressing the needs of different sub-groups of the market than by assuming
the market as homogeneous. Kotler and Keller (2016) and Kotler and Armstrong (2018) take
this reasoning one step further, suggesting that companies are not able to effectively serve
large and diversified markets without determining and targeting specific groups of consumers.
Traditional STP models have been based on geographic and demographic segmentation,

but in postmodern consumer behaviour, identity-based and psychographic segmentation are
gaining importance. Keller (2013) adds that positioning must be based on the consumers’
self-concept, and not just the observable characteristics thereof. The literature in recent years
suggests that it is not sufficient to simply profile consumers based on their demographic
characteristics in order to understand them; their ethical identity, environmental awareness,
wellness orientation, and perception of authenticity are becoming important factors affecting
sustainable consumption behaviour.
Sustainable Branding and Consumer Identity
As per the literature on sustainable branding, consumers nowadays create symbolic identities
with their brand selections. Consumer Culture Theory (Arnould & Thompson 2005) suggests
consumption is no longer solely an economic activity but rather a more cultural and emotional
practice. In this context, sustainable products convey responsibility, well-being, social bonds,
and morality. It is also noted that sustainable consumption products are often emotionally
enjoyable, symbolically communicative, and aligned with a particular lifestyle, as much as
being rationally considered, as suggested by Firat and Venkatesh (1995) in their description of
postmodern consumption. This is a broader view of green consumer behaviour than previous
and restrictive ideas that have been based on utilitarian considerations.
Authenticity and Greenwashing
One of the main issues in the literature of sustainable branding is authenticity. Delmas and
Burbano (2011) provide evidence that companies often make exaggerated claims about their
sustainability efforts to boost their brand. Once these claims are found out, the long-term result
is to engender a lack of trust among consumers and increase scepticism - a process known
as greenwashing. Beverland (2005) claims that brand authenticity is not an inherent feature of
a brand but a perceptual one, in which it is necessary to minimise the gap between what the
firm claims and what can be empirically demonstrated to achieve consistency over time and
therefore maintain brand authenticity. Sustainability is increasingly becoming a differentiator,
and consumers are looking for consistency between what a company says and how it acts in
practice, with any inconsistencies being penalised through public scrutiny.
Digital Sustainability Communication and Consumer Engagement
Digital media has revolutionised the way of communicating sustainability to consumers.
According to Dwivedi et al. (2021), social and digital media have made marketing research
more interesting as it allows companies to engage consumers in two-way communication
as opposed to one-way advertising. Social media storytelling, social media influencers,
wellness stories, and building online communities are all ways brands can create emotional
connections with younger audiences. Specifically, Kapoor et al.  (2022) demonstrate that
the use of influencer marketing can greatly boost consumer engagement by providing
perceived credibility and relatability in brand claims. But digital communication also
means that the same tools used to drive emotionally engaging sustainability storytelling

are also at the hands of the public to challenge misaligned or bogus environmental
claims.
Green Marketing Practice and Strategy
Ottman (2017) suggests that green marketing should be more than just communication;
companies need to adopt sustainability in their product design, sourcing and organisational
practice as consumers have come to value action more than words. This confirms Belz and
Peattie’s (2012) more comprehensive sustainability marketing conceptualisation, which sees
environmental and social responsibility as strategic marketing components throughout the
marketing mix, and not a communication add-on. All of this literature indicates that sustainable
positioning, where it is grounded in demonstration and not just story alone, is likely to last
longer.
Green Purchase Behaviour and the Attitude-Behaviour Gap
One of the frequently recurring issues in the green marketing literature is that there is a
disconnection between consumers’ professed environmental attitudes and their buying
behaviours. Carrington et al. (2010) report that pro-ecological buyers often do not act on their
positive attitudes towards ecology and the environment when purchasing a product, which has
direct consequences for how companies should act on survey results concerning purchase
intention. Joshi and Rahman (2015) and Kumar and Ghodeswar (2015) find price sensitivity,
availability, scepticism towards claims, and habit of purchasing as significant moderators of
the attitude-behaviour gap in the purchase of green products. This literature indicates that
purchase intention is a good proxy, but should be read with some caution as an indicator of
actual sustained purchase behaviour.
RESEARCH GAP
The review of literature reveals several important gaps:
(i)  Limited integration of STP theory with sustainable branding literature, particularly in
emerging-market contexts.
(ii)  Insufficient empirical studies examining sustainability-oriented positioning within Indian
FMCG markets.
(iii)  Limited research examining authenticity as an explicit positioning variable rather than
a background assumption.
(iv)   A lack of mixed-method studies integrating qualitative interpretation of brand
communication with quantitative consumer-response analysis.
(v)  Limited empirical evidence directly linking digital sustainability communication to
consumer purchase intention, as distinct from general brand engagement.

THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
The study incorporates four theories: STP Theory (Smith, 1956; Kotler & Keller, 2016),
Sustainable Marketing Theory (Belz & Peattie, 2012; Ottman, 2017), Consumer Culture Theory
(Arnould & Thompson, 2005; Firat & Venkatesh, 1995), and Authenticity Theory (Beverland,
2005; Delmas & Burbano, 2011). The integrated framework suggests that sustainability-
driven segmentation and authenticity-based positioning have a significant effect on consumer
engagement, purchase intention, and sustainability-based brand loyalty, while this effect is
moderated by the credibility of the digital sustainability communication.

RESEARCH METHODOLOGY
Research Design
The study uses a mixed-method exploratory research design that combines qualitative
interpretive analysis of brand communication with quantitative analysis of consumer response
to gain insights into both statistical relationships among constructs and the substantive content
of Mamaearth’s sustainability communication.
Data Collection
A structured questionnaire was used to collect responses from 200 respondents who have
an awareness of sustainable personal care brands. The secondary data was gathered from
sustainability reports, advertising, social media campaigns, company disclosures, industry
reports, and academic literature.
Sampling Technique
Unrepresentative, convenience sampling was used because of the difficulty of accessing
target groups and distributing the digital versions of the survey. This non-probability method
was suitable for the exploratory aims of this study but does not allow statistical generalisability
of the findings to the wider population.

FINDINGS AND DISCUSSION
Sustainability as Emotional Identity
The study indicates that data-driven marketing decisions are changing the way sustainability-
centric brands view segmentation, targeting, and positioning in the Indian FMCG landscape.
The findings are in line with Iacobucci et al. (2019) and France and Ghose (2019), which show
that companies like Mamaearth are progressively using behavioural and preference data
instead of using only intuition to understand and target their sustainability-focused audience.
The high STP-Positioning relationship (r = 0.741) and the significant variance explained (R²
= 0.549) demonstrate that the structured segmentation and targeting decisions contribute
meaningfully to strengthening a brand’s sustainability positioning.
The Positioning-Engagement relationship (r = 0.703, R² = 0.494) also shows that brands with
a more coherent and better targeted sustainability positioning get more positive attitudes
and more active consumer engagement than brands with a more generic positioning. This
reinforces the general theoretical statement that sustainable consumption is an emotional
and symbolic act, and not merely a utilitarian act of purchasing, that serves as a means of
building identity (Arnould & Thompson, 2005; Firat & Venkatesh, 1995).
The Authenticity Paradox
The strongest relationship is the Authenticity-Brand Loyalty relationship (r = 0.776, R2 = 0.602),
indicating that perceived authenticity is the single most important factor that drives sustainable

brand loyalty among the constructs analysed. The study also uncovers what might be termed
the “Authenticity Paradox”: while attempting to increase their sustainability messages as a
way to differentiate, sustainable brands are also courting greater consumer skepticism and
greenwashing scrutiny (Delmas & Burbano, 2011). Visibility, thus, is a strategic asset and a
reputational liability; the more a brand communicates about sustainability, the more it is likely
to come under fire for public scrutiny.
Qualitative Insights from Secondary Data
A qualitative analysis of the publicly available sustainability communication of Mamaearth
reveals three patterns that are recurring, aligning with the quantitative findings mentioned
above:
•  Trust signalling through specificity : communication that mentions concrete, verifiable
claims (named certifications or ingredient disclosures) seems to be more in line with the
authenticity construct than the use of sustainability terms in a broad and unverifiable
way.
•  Founder- and community-led narrative : personal, founder-led narratives on digital
channels seem to reinforce the emotional identity dimension of engagement as
mentioned in 10.1.
•  Visibility inviting scrutiny: During times of heightened sustainability marketing, we see
a greater amount of public discussion (including critical commentary) of the brand’s
environmental claims, as an example of the Authenticity Paradox.
The quantitative results show that authenticity (β = 0.776) is the most significant of the
constructs examined in predicting loyalty, while these qualitative patterns are directionally
consistent with and help to put into perspective those quantitative results. They should
nevertheless be considered as illustrative, rather than as statistically representative, until
systematic coding and reporting of them.
THEORETICAL CONTRIBUTION
The study has made five contributions in the field of marketing. First, it complements the
traditional STP theory because it shows that the increasing number of sustainability-oriented
consumers do not simply segment themselves according to their demographics, but in an
authenticity orientation and ethical identity. Second, it reveals that positioning for sustainability
is not only functional anymore, but also emotional and symbolic, as is suggested by the
Consumer Culture Theory (Arnould & Thompson, 2005; Firat & Venkatesh, 1995). Third,
the study proposes the term “Authenticity-Centred Sustainable Positioning”, which indicates
that sustainable brand positioning relies more and more on transparency, accountability and
consistency between internal and external activities of the organisation (Beverland, 2005).
Fourth, the research contributes to greenwashing literature (Delmas & Burbano, 2011) by
revealing that the Authenticity Paradox not only applies to individual claims, but to the level of
communication intensity. Lastly, the study extends the existing literature on emerging-market

MANAGERIAL IMPLICATIONS
The results have a number of implications for FMCG brand managers. First, companies
ought to focus on psychographic and values-based segmentation, not just on demographic
segmentation alone, when targeting the consumers who are interested in sustainability.
Second, since the connection between authenticity and loyalty is so strong, managers should
focus on ways to make authenticity more tangible by making it more specific and meaningful
with claims that can be backed up (Ottman, 2017). Third, companies must be wary of making
too many and too bold symbolic sustainability claims, as the Authenticity Paradox has been
proven: “Sustainability communication” should be measured in accordance with what the
organisation can back up, not what makes it feel good. Fourth, there is a need to create
transparent ESG communication systems and disclosure practices that can hold up to the
scrutiny of the public that digital visibility brings (Dwivedi et al., 2021). Lastly, as noted by
Carrington et al. (2010) and Joshi and Rahman (2015), there is an attitude-behaviour gap in
green purchasing; therefore, managers are advised to not use the purchase intention measured
by the survey as a direct indication of purchase behaviour and to include behavioural or
sales figures wherever possible. The power of influencers and digital storytelling persists as a
means of engagement (Kapoor et al., 2022), when backed up by action and not just narrative.
LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDY
The study did not cover the FMCG brand, which may have limited the generalisability of
the study to other sustainability brands or product categories. Convenience sampling is
appropriate for exploratory studies, but may have self-selection bias and restricts the statistical
generalisability of conclusions to the population of consumers. The quantitative analysis was
conducted on cross-sectional consumer perception data only, and there was no behavioural
tracking over time, so it was not possible to conclude whether these statements of engagement
and loyalty would result in sustained purchase behaviour over time. In line with the results of
the analysis of secondary data which is qualitative but not exhaustive, quantitative validation
of Hypothesis 4 is yet to be done The limitations of the study could be overcome by adding
cross-brand comparison and structural equation modelling in the future.
FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS
Future studies can be useful in comparing multiple brands of sustainable FMCG to test
the generalisability of the present findings, conducting longitudinal studies to analyse if
engagement and loyalty lead to sustained purchase behaviour, and using Structural Equation
Modelling (SEM)/Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) to validate the complete conceptual
model including the as yet unconfirmed Hypothesis 4 path with greater statistical rigour.

CONCLUSION
This research finds that the concept of sustainability is essentially changing the strategic
logic of segmentation, targeting and positioning in today’s consumer markets. As can be seen
in the case of Mamaearth, the success of sustainable branding is more and more relying
on psychographic segmentation, the construction of an emotional identity, the perception
of authenticity, and involvement with digital communities. Overall, the robust statistical
relationships found between STP implementation and sustainable positioning, between
positioning and consumer engagement, and, most importantly, between authenticity and
brand loyalty, validate that these factors are not trivial to sustainable branding strategy but
core to it. As sustainability positioning does so, it also attracts increased consumer scrutiny
and scepticism, as illustrated by the Authenticity Paradox. So sustainable brands need to go
beyond symbolic sustainability communication to tangible transparency and accountability.
The future of sustainable branding is not just in talking about sustainability, but in being a
sustainable brand in a verifiable and convincing manner.
