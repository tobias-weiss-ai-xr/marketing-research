Abstract
Digital marketing operates on an attention-extraction model, but evidence suggests
this model is failing. This systematic review synthesizes 31 empirical studies (2015–2026)
examining digital fatigue and consumer resistance across Google Scholar, Scopus, and
Web of Science following PRISMA guidelines. Key findings reveal three psychological
mechanisms: psychological reactance (Shoenberger, Kim, & Sun, 2021; Chen et  al.,
2019), self -determination violations (Chiu, Sun, & Ismailov, 2022), and emotional
exhaustion from information overload (Sheng et al., 2023). Critically, the literature reveals
a paradox: while many consumers avoid ads and pursue digital detox (Bograk ou &
Kontopoulou, 2025; Chen & Kent, 2025), digital burnout can paradoxically increase
platform use when driven by internet addiction (da Silva et al., 2024). Consumer resistance
manifests as ad avoidance, detox behaviors, and compulsive buying cycles. Thi s review
concludes that extraction -based engagement is unsustainable. We propose a novel
Integrated Consumer Fatigue Framework (ICFF) linking antecedents (overload,
saturation, privacy concerns), psychological mediators (reactance, exhaustion, low self -
control), and behavioral outcomes (avoidance, detox, or paradoxical continued use).
Moderators include age, self -control, product type, and internet addiction. Brands must
prioritize authenticity, autonomy, consumer well-being, and segment-specific strategies.
Keywords: attention economy, digital fatigue, consumer resistance, psychological
reactance, ad avoidance, digital burnout paradox, emotional exhaustion, consumer
well-being, digital marketing effectiveness

Introduction
The attention economy treats human attention as a scarce commodity to be
captured and monetized (Davenport & Beck, 2001). For nearly two decades,
digital marketing has relied on an extraction model —endless feeds, push
notifications, and algorithmic targeti ng. However, evidence shows this model is
reaching its limits. Globally, ad blocker usage exceeds 42%, and click through

Newman (2019) further argues that weak legal oversight has enabled dominant

Definitions. Digital fatigue is a multidimensional state of cognitive,
emotional, and behavioral exhaustion from prolonged digital interaction (Ceylan,
Basal, & Gayretli, 2024). Psychological reactance is a motivational state opposing
perceived freedom thr eats (Brehm, 1966; Shoenberger et al., 2021). Self
determination theory (SDT) posits that frustrated autonomy and competence
needs produce disengagement (Ryan & Deci, 2000; Chiu et al., 2022).
Algorithmic burnout refers to exhaustion from opaque recommenda tion systems
(Balaskas et al., 2025). The personalization–privacy paradox describes the tension
between desiring tailored content and resenting data exploitation (Cloarec, 2020).
Digital detox is intentional technology abstinence (Vaghefi & Turel, 2025; Ch en
& Kent, 2025).
Research Questions. This review answers three questions: (1) Why do
consumers experience digital fatigue? (2) How do they resist digital marketing?
(3) What should marketers do differently?
Scope. This review synthesizes evidence from multiple geographical
contexts (Greece, Thailand, Malaysia, China, United States, Portugal), considers
age-based segments (Generation Y, consumers aged 40+), and distinguishes
product types (utilitarian vs. hedonic) where evidence permits. Workplace digital
fatigue is excluded.
Contribution. Synthesizing 31 empirical studies from 2015 –2026 across
multiple databases, we provide the first integrated framework linking reactance,
SDT, algorithmic burnout, emotional exhaustion, and the privacy paradox to
consumer resistance behaviors.  Unlike prior reviews, we identify a critical
paradox in the literature (burnout sometimes increases platform use) and propose
an original conceptual model. Section 2 describes the methodology; Section 3
presents the conceptual framework; Section 4 analyze s psychological
mechanisms; Section 5 summarizes resistance evidence; Section 6 discusses
implications and limitations; Section 7 concludes.

This systematic review followed PRISMA guidelines (Page et al., 2021).
Search Strategy
Three databases were searched: Google Scholar, Scopus, and Web of
Science. The search period was January 2015 – June 2026. Manual cross
referencing supplemented the database search.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
Inclusion criteria: peer reviewed empirical studies, English language,
direct focus on consumer fatigue or resistance in digital marketing contexts.
Exclusion criteria: theoretical papers without data, workplace fatigue
studies, pre 2015 publications, non-English papers.
Selection Procedure
The initial search yielded 127 records (Google Scholar: 86; Scopus: 24;
Web of Science: 17). After duplicate removal (n=31), 96 records underwent
title/abstract screening. 54 records were excluded. 42 full text articles were
assessed for eligibility. 11 were excluded (irrelevant outcomes or methods). Final
N = 31 studies.

Causal Pathways in the ICFF
The ICFF proposes three primary pathways:
Pathway A (Reactance Pathway): Ad saturation + perceived manipulation →
psychological reactance → ad avoidance and detox behaviors (Shoenberger et al.,
2021; Chen et al., 2019; Guo & Jiang, 2024).
Pathway B (Exhaustion Pathway): Information overload + privacy concerns →
emotional exhaustion → either (a) withdrawal/avoidance OR (b) paradoxical
continued use when internet addiction is present (Sheng et al., 2023; da Silva et
al., 2024).
Pathway C (Self -Control Pathway): Personalization overload + low self -control
→ positive attitude toward targeted ads → impulse buying (Nyrhinen et al., 2024;
Ali et al., 2025). This pathway explains compulsive purchasing despite fatigue.

PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISMS: WHY CONSUMERS FATIGUE
Psychological Reactance
Shoenberger, Kim, and Sun (2021) found that threat-to-freedom messages
generate reactance, lowering ad attitudes. Chen et al. (2019) extended this by
identifying rational choice factors (negative -effect perceptions) as dominant
determinants of reactance to ward online personalized advertising. Critically,
affective factors (ownership, vulnerability) significantly influence these rational
choice factors. When consumers feel their data is owned or they are vulnerable,
reactance intensifies.
Emotional Exhaustion and Information Overload
Sheng et al. (2023) demonstrated that information overload from platforms
directly increases emotional exhaustion. This exhaustion mediates the relationship
between overload and social media fatigue. The shift of interpersonal
communication online means users constantly receive, access, and process
messages, advertisements, and news—creating cumulative cognitive burden. The
authors conclude that while social media offers benefits like increased access to
information and ease of contact, it also produces emotional exhaustion and
fatigue.
Self-Determination Theory Violations
Chiu, Sun, and Ismailov (2022) showed that autonomy and competence are
more important than relatedness in digital contexts. When digital environments
violate consumer autonomy (e.g., forced notifications, opaque algorithms), fatigue
accelerates. Jackson (2 025) confirmed that young people actively navigate
autonomy threats but remain vulnerable to emotional effects like social
comparison. While they use strategies to maintain autonomy, social media
continues to commodify their attention.
Algorithmic Burnout and the Personalization–Privacy Paradox
Balaskas et al. (2025) introduced "algorithmic burnout"—active resistance
to personalized digital persuasion. Cloarec (2020) explains the personalization –
privacy paradox: consumers value personalization but resent data exploitation. Ali
et al. (2025) added  that urgency and scarcity tactics, while effective short -term,
generate fatigue and ethical concerns when overused. Baek et al. (2026) reinforced
this: frequent promotional emails boost short -term sales but cause opt -outs and
reduced long-term value, recommending segmented, retention-focused strategies.

Nyrhinen et al. (2024) revealed that low self -control increases impulse
buying both directly and indirectly through positive attitudes toward targeted
advertisements and social network impulsiveness. Consumers with reduced self -
control become less resistan t to targeted marketing stimuli —a key mechanism
linking fatigue to compulsive purchasing. The findings indicate that low self -
control significantly increases impulse buying both directly and indirectly through
these mediators.

EVIDENCE OF CONSUMER RESISTANCE
Ad Avoidance
Onișor and Ioniță (2021) used eye-tracking with 121 respondents, finding
that users who gave the least attention to ads had the best ad memory —forcing
attention does not guarantee retention. Bograkou and Kontopoulou (2025)
demonstrated ad saturation as a negative environme ntal stimulus driving
avoidance. Guo and Jiang (2024) found that excessively frequent advertising
campaigns inevitably cause consumers to become fatigued, unresponsive, or even
irritated, recommending that firms proportionally reduce advertising expenditure
when fatigue is present.
Digital Detox Behaviors
Vaghefi and Turel  (2025) examined hedonic IT abstinence maintenance,
identifying self -efficacy and peer pressure as key factors. Yuting (2024)
confirmed detox intervention effectiveness. However, Chen and Kent (2025)
introduced a critical nuance: in China, digital detox apps like Forest reframe detox
as productivity enhancement rather than genuine well -being. Commercialization
and cultural context limit detox effectiveness, potentially reinforcing optimization
pressures rather than enabling meaningful disconnection. Feature s such as
gamification, self-tracking, and community engagement help reduce unintentional
device use but may also reinforce existing pressures.
The Paradox: When Burnout Increases Usage
da Silva et al. (2024) found a counterintuitive result: digital burnout has a
significant positive effect on digital consumer platform use, partially mediated by
utilitarian motivation. Crucially, burnout was driven by internet addiction (not
digital literacy), and effects were stronger among older consumers. This resolves
an apparent contradiction in the literature: fatigued consumers do not always
withdraw; addicted consumers continue using despite exhaustion.
Compulsive Buying and Self-Control Depletion
Sattayathamrongthian et al. (2025) found that social media buying pressure
leads to digital fatigue, which depletes self -control and drives compulsive
purchasing. Nyrhinen et al. (2024) confirmed that low self -control enables
impulsive buying by fostering positive attitudes toward targeted ads.

Maskun et al. (2025) found Generation Y experiences digital stress,

Born Digital Organizations' Response Strategies
Hafezieh and Pollock (2023) examined how "born digital" companies
organize to handle consumer demands, identifying three practices: pre -emptive
(predicting customer needs), reactive (responding quickly to changes), and
reflective & adaptive (continuously i mproving internal processes). These
practices represent organizational-level responses to consumer fatigue, explaining
how born digital organizations anticipate, respond to, and continuously adapt to
consumer demands.
AI and Intelligent Customer Service
Li et al. (2025) found that social influence, service quality, technical
characteristics, and personal perceptions all had significant positive effects on
college students' willingness to use AI-powered intelligent customer service in e-
commerce enterprises. Liu et al. (2025) demonstrated that product -focused
interactions enhance purchase intention for utilitarian products via perceived
value, whi le social interactions boost purchase intention for hedonic products
through social presence.

DISCUSSION
Summary of Findings
This systematic review of 31 studies yields four main conclusions. First,
psychological mechanisms driving fatigue are well-established: reactance
(Shoenberger et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2019), emotional exhaustion from
information overload (Sheng et al., 2023), autonomy violations (Chiu et al., 2022;
Jackson, 2025), and low self -control (Nyrhinen et al., 2024). Second, co nsumer
resistance is real and growing: ad avoidance, digital detox, and in some cases
compulsive buying. Third, a critical paradox exists: burnout sometimes increases
platform use when driven by internet addiction (da Silva et al., 2024) —fatigued
consumers are not a monolithic group. Fourth, age, self-control, product type, and
cultural context moderate all these relationships.
Theoretical Contributions
This review makes three theoretical contributions. First, we propose the
Integrated Consumer Fatigue Framework (ICFF) , the first model to integrate
reactance, emotional exhaustion, self -determination, and low self -control into a
single causal framework. Second, we identify and resolve the burnout paradox by
introducing internet addiction as a moderator. Third, we extend the

Limitations
This review has several limitations. First, while we included Scopus and
Web of Science, the search was limited to English -language publications,
potentially excluding relevant non-English research. Second, the ICFF is proposed
based on synthesis but requires empirical validation (e.g., structural equation
modeling or meta-analysis). Third, the review is cross -sectional in its synthesis;
longitudinal studies on fatigue progression remain rare. Fourth, quality appraisal
(MMAT) identified 4 low -quality studies; while retained, they received less
weight. Fifth, most studies are from Western or Southeast Asian contexts (Greece,
Thailand, Malaysia, China, US, Portugal); generalizability to other regions
(Africa, Latin America,  Middle East) is unknown. Sixth, the review excludes
workplace digital fatigue, a related but distinct phenomenon.

Conclusions
The attention economy is taking a measurable toll on consumers. This
systematic review of 31 studies reveals consistent findings across multiple
psychological mechanisms (reactance, emotional exhaustion, autonomy violation,
low self -control) and resistance behaviors (avoidance, detox, para doxical
continued use when addiction is present). The proposed Integrated Consumer
Fatigue Framework (ICFF) offers a unified model for understanding when and
why fatigue leads to withdrawal versus continued use.
The extraction model is unsustainable. The future of digital marketing
belongs not to those who capture the most clicks, but to those who earn the most
trust. However, this requires more than good intentions. Brands must
operationally reduce frequency, res pect autonomy, segment by age and self -
control, match interaction styles to product types, and support genuine digital
well-being—not productivity-masked detox. Without these changes, the attention
economy will continue eroding the consumer trust on which it ultimately depends.
As Newman (2019) argues, stronger legal reforms are also needed to build a fairer
and more balanced attention economy.
