Abstract
Marketing localization requires decisions about meaning, tone, cultural references, and the
relationship between language and visual presentation. However, claims that creative
translation necessarily produces trust or purchasing behavior frequently exceed  the evidence
available. This article develops a conceptual framework for examining the cognitive and
affective dimensions of marketing translation while distinguishing textual adaptation from
demonstrated audience effects. A focused narrative synthesis brings together twenty -one
publications from 2020 –2025 in translation studies, consumer research, international
marketing, and translation technology. The analysis separates five dimensions: claim
integrity, processing and comprehension, affective and social interpretation, multimodal
coherence, and outcome evaluation. It argues that effective localization cannot be reduced
either to literal correspondence or to unrestricted creative rewriting. Instead, the appropriate
degree of adaptation depends on the communicative purpose, audience, medium, and factual
commitments of the message. Hypothetical examples illustrate how alternative formulations
can be evaluated without treating preferred wording as proof of persuasion. The resulting
framework connects tr anslation decisions with explicit hypotheses and appropriate forms of
evidence. Its contribution is a transparent procedure for designing and evaluating localized
communication; it does not establish causal effects on consumers. The article concludes with
implications for professional practice, translator education, and future reception research.
Keywords: marketing translation, localization, transcreation, consumer cognition, affective
response, multimodal communication
1 Introduction
Marketing messages circulate through websites, product packaging, short videos,
service conversations, and mobile interfaces. Their translation involves more than finding
corresponding words. A formulation may communicate product information accurately while
sounding inappropriate for the interaction, and a memorable slogan may conceal an important
qualification. The central problem is therefore how to adapt communication for particular
audiences while retaining the factual commitments that make the message accountable. This

Localization is understood here as the adaptation of a communicative product to a
specified linguistic and usage environment. It can include verbal translation, layout, interface
elements, imagery, and audiovisual presentation. Transcreation refers more narrowly to
creative reworking guided by a communicative brief, although its professional and academic
definitions remain contested. Díaz-Millón and Olvera-Lobo (2023) document this conceptual
diversity, while Carreira (2022) examines practitioners’ understa ndings of transcreation as
both a service and a strategy. Neither term, by itself, demonstrates that a particular adaptation
is effective.
The psychological dimension also requires careful definition. Research on translators’
cognitive activity concerns how translations are produced; research on consumer reception
concerns how translated messages are understood and evaluated. These processes are related
but cannot substitute for one another. The breadth of cognitive translation studies described
by Alves and Jakobsen (2021) makes this distinction especially relevant. A translator’s
greater effort does not necessarily produce greater audience e ngagement, just as a quickly
produced translation is not necessarily difficult for readers.
This article asks how cognitive and affective considerations can guide marketing
localization without turning plausible interpretations into unsupported claims about consumer
behavior. Three subsidiary questions organize the inquiry: Which properties of a message
should remain stable during adaptation? Which psychological responses require direct
investigation? How can linguistic, visual, and technological decisions be evaluated within a
coherent workflow?
The article’s contribution is a decision framework connecting adaptation choices to
evidence. It distinguishes semantic and factual integrity from processing experience,
emotional interpretation, and behavioral outcomes. Rather than ranking literal translation and
transcreation as universally inferior or superior, it identifies circumstances in which different
solutions deserve testing. The discussion is a conceptual review, not a report of an original
experiment or a systematic assessment of all published research. Its proposed relationships
remain open to empirical evaluation.
2 Literature Review
2.1 Translation purpose and creative adaptation
Contemporary accounts of translation theory offer several ways to understand the
relationship between source and target texts. Functional approaches foreground
communicative purposes, while other approaches emphasize textual relations, interpretation,
social positioning, or uncertainty. Munday et al. (2022) and Pym (2023) provide recent
accounts of these theoretical alternatives. For marketing communication, the implication is
that evaluation must specify what a translation is expected to accomplish. A prod uct
description, contractual qualification, humorous headline, and service apology cannot all be
assessed by the same criterion.

92
Creative adaptation is consequently a choice requiring justification. Carreira’s (2022)
study highlights professional perspectives that resist a single rigid definition of transcreation.
Díaz-Millón and Olvera-Lobo’s (2023) review similarly supports explicit terminology rather
than assuming that  the label describes a uniform method. In this article, transcreation is
treated as a possible strategy within localization. Its value depends on the problem it
addresses, the changes it introduces, and the evidence used to evaluate those changes.
Torresi (2021) situates promotional translation within globalization and the
interaction between global and local communication. This perspective cautions against
equating localization with the removal of every foreign element. An international brand may
retain a recognizable name or stylistic feature while adapting explanations and interactional
conventions. The analytical issue is which elements carry necessary identity or information
and which create avoidable interpretive barriers.
2.2 Language processing and consumer interpretation
Consumer research provides evidence that language form can affect evaluations, but
such evidence is conditional. Packard and Berger (2021) examine concrete language in
customer service and connect it with perceptions that a speaker is listening. Their findings
make specificity relevant to service communication; they do not establish that every abstract
advertising expression should become concrete. Hüttl -Maack and Munz (2025) investigate
multilingual packaging and show why processing fluency deserves attent ion when several
languages share limited visual space.
Foreign-language content can also function symbolically. Hornikx et al. (2024)
distinguish theoretical perspectives concerning language as a symbol, language processing,
and accommodation. These perspectives help explain why linguistic familiarity is not the
only consideration. Retaining a foreign expression may communicate origin or identity,
whereas translating practical instructions may improve access. A single message can involve
both functions, making uniform treatment of all its elements inappropriate.
The distinction between textual properties and audience outcomes is particularly
important. Ho (2021) analyzes persuasive shifts in English –Chinese marketing translation,
identifying how evaluation is expressed through linguistic choices. Such analysis describes
the construction of persuasion within texts. It does not independently show that readers buy
more products. Cui (2021) brings cognitive poetics and reception evidence into advertisement
translation, illustrating how poetic features can be investigated rather than merely assumed to
have emotional effects.
2.3 Culture, affect, and multimodal communication
Cultural mediation requires attention to situations, communities, and relationships.
Katan and Taibi (2021) offer a framework for considering translation within cultural
contexts. For localization, this encourages inquiry into the intended audience rather than
assigning a fixed communicative personality to a nation. Age, language proficiency,
professional background, brand familiarity, and the purpose of an encounter can all become
relevant audience characteristics.

93
Visual interpretation likewise resists simple cultural dictionaries. Jonauskaite et al.
(2020) report shared patterns in color –emotion associations alongside variation associated
with linguistic and geographic proximity. Their findings support sensitivity to context, not a
universal rule that a color has one meaning in one region and an opposite meaning elsewhere.
In audiovisual communication, Díaz Cintas and Remael (2021) additionally show why
subtitles must be considered within temporal, spatial, and multimodal constraints.
Together, these sources establish a need for integration rather than a single
comprehensive causal theory. Translation scholarship explains adaptation choices, consumer
studies examine particular responses, and multimodal research identifies properties of the
communicative environment. The present framework connects these contributions while
preserving their different evidential roles.
3 Methodology
This article adopts a critical conceptual review based on a focused narrative synthesis.
Its design follows the principle that a conceptual contribution should make its selection of
theories and its reasoning explicit, as discussed by Jaakkola (2020). The purpose is to
organize a problem and develop propositions, rather than estimate an average effect or claim
exhaustive coverage.
The reference set comprises twenty -one publications dated 2020 –2025. Sources were
identified through targeted web searches of publisher and institutional records using
combinations of marketing translation, transcreation, localization, processing fluency, foreign
languages in advertising, emotion, and translation technology. Eligibility depended on
relevance to at least one component of the argument, identifiable publication details, and a
sufficiently clear account of the source’s purpose and evidence. Boo ks provide theoretical
context; empirical articles inform bounded claims about particular settings. Publication
indexing was not used as a measure of evidential strength.
Bibliographic details were checked against publisher  or institutional records. Where
access was limited to an abstract or authoritative summary, the discussion is restricted to the
design and conclusions explicitly described there. Informal brand anecdotes without a
traceable primary account were excluded. This procedure is selective and cannot rule out
language, access, or selection bias; it is not presented as a systematic review or meta-analysis.
The synthesis proceeds through three analytical operations. First, it separates
production processes, textual features, reported reception, and behavioral outcomes. Second,
it compares recurring concerns across the selected literature: factual continuity,
comprehension, affect, cultural interpretation, medium, and evaluation. Third, it organizes
these concerns into a practical framework and develops propositions for subsequent testing.
The examples below are constructed English -language illustrations of decision problems, not
observations from campaigns or evidence of translation quality in a specific language pair.
No participants were recruited, no proprietary campaign data were analyzed, and no original
effect estimates are reported.

4 Conceptual Analysis and Proposed Framework
4.1 Preserve claims while adapting their expression
The first dimension is claim  integrity: whether an adaptation retains the information,
qualifications, and commitments necessary for an accurate interpretation. Communicative
purpose can justify changes in rhythm, syntax, metaphor, or address, but it cannot establish
the truth of a stronger product promise. A localized message should therefore be evaluated
both for what it states and for what a reasonable reader may infer.
Consider a hypothetical subscription advertisement. Replacing “cancel during the first
month” with “try it without risk” changes more than tone. The second formulation may imply
freedom from financial or practical consequences that the first does not specify. Likewise,
“help available every day” does not necessarily mean immediate assistance at any hour.
Before assessing warmth or memorability, reviewers need to identify these differences in
commitment.
Kassawat (2024) compares Arabic transcreated slogans with a reference text,
attending to coherence and marketing purpose. This provides a useful point of contact
between creative adaptation and contextual accountability. A slogan should be considered
alongside the product description and the surrounding message, not evaluated as an isolated
attractive phrase. In the proposed framework, the product specification and approved factual
brief function as reference points.
Claim integrity is a threshold condition, not a numerical component that can be offset
by a higher persuasion score. An engaging adaptation that introduces an unsupported promise
requires revision even if readers prefer it. Table 1 illustrates how this principle changes the
discussion of apparently simple lexical substitutions.

4.2 Distinguish comprehension from perceived ease
The second dimension concerns processing and comprehension. Processing fluency
refers to experienced ease, whereas comprehension concerns the meaning a reader actually
constructs. Cognitive load concerns demands on cognitive resources and should be
investigated with appropriate measures rather than inferred from a reviewer’s impression.
These concepts overlap, but treating them as interchangeable obscures different causes of
communication failure.
A message can feel familiar while remaining inaccurate or incomplete. Conversely, a
technical term may require effort but preserve a distinction that a simplified paraphrase
removes. Localization should therefore identify which difficulties are unnecessary and which
arise from information that genuinely needs explanation. Shorter wording is useful only if the
information required for the task remains accessible.
Hüttl-Maack and Munz’s (2025) packaging research makes the arrangement and
familiarity of multiple languages an empirical issue. It does not justify removing languages
needed by the intended audience. The framework instead proposes testing visual hierarchy,
grouping, and access to explanations. A multilingual package and a mobile service interface
present different reading tasks, so conclusions should not be transferred without considering
those differences.
Packard and Berger (2021) similarly motivate attention to concrete language in a
particular communicative setting. A service reply that specifies the item, action, and next step
may help users understand what has been done. However, the present proposal is to measure
that understanding, not to classify concrete language as universally persuasive. A suitable
comprehension task would ask readers to explain the action available to them in their own
words.
This distinction also changes error diagnosis. If users understand a message but
dislike its tone, further simplification may not address the problem. If they like the wording
but misunderstand a condition, affective preference cannot count as successful localization.
Separate measures make those outcomes visible.
4.3 Treat affective response as a hypothesis
The third dimension addresses affective and social interpretation. A translator can
design wording intended to sound reassuring, respectful, playful, or prestigious. The intention
should be recorded as a hypothesis about a defined audience, rather than reported as an
achieved psychological effect. “The wording is intended to convey reassurance” and “the
wording increased trust” are different claims requiring different evidence.
Cui’s (2021) analysis of poetic advertising shows how repetition and imagery can
become objects of reception research. Ho’s (2021) study shows how translation may shift the
explicitness or intensity of evaluative language. Together, they support examining formal

96
choices and reception separately. More emphatic evaluation may be visible in a translated
text even when its consequences for particular readers remain unknown.
Social interpretation includes judgments about who appears to be speaking and how
that speaker positions the audience. A highly familiar address form might fit an established
community but seem intrusive in an unfamiliar service encounter. A restrained formulation
might communicate competence or appear distant. The framework does not assign either
response to an entire language community; it requires the intended relationship and situation
to be specified.
Foreignness can also be a deliberate resource. Following the distinctions developed by
Hornikx et al. (2024), a retained expression may signal origin, affiliation, or style as well as
present a processing challenge. A localized version should therefore be compared with
plausible alternatives, including selective retention. Complete domestication cannot be
assumed to preserve brand identity better than a mixed strategy.
Affective testing should distinguish liking, perceived warmth, trustworthiness, and
purchase intention. These outcomes can diverge. A humorous phrase may attract attention
while reducing perceived seriousness; a precise explanation may be less entertaining but
more useful. The framework consequently rejects a single undifferentiated category of
“emotional resonance” as a sufficient evaluation result.
4.4 Evaluate the whole communicative object
The fourth dimension is multimodal coherence: the relationship among words,
images, sound, layout, and interaction. A translation reviewed in a spreadsheet can appear
acceptable yet fail in the interface where users encounter it. Line breaks, proximity to images,
timing, and the visibility of qualifications influence what readers can reasonably connect.
Color illustrates the need for contextual evaluation. The cross -national findings of
Jonauskaite et al. (2020) resist a simple opposition between universal meaning and wholly
culture-specific meaning. For a localized campaign, a reviewer should examine the color
together with the product category, image, wording, and audience task. A general association
study cannot establish how a particular button or advertisement will be interpreted.
Audiovisual material introduces additional constraints. Díaz Cintas and Remael
(2021) discuss subtitling as a practice shaped by the interaction between verbal content and
the audiovisual medium. In promotional video, a proposed adaptation must be checked at its
actual display duration and in relation to speech, images, and on-screen information. A slogan
that works on a poster may be too demanding in a rapidly changing sequence.
The practical implication is to review a representative rendered object before release.
A mock-up should include the target language in its intended position, realistic text length,
and the relevant interaction. When the surrounding design changes, previous linguistic
approval may need reconsideration. Multimodal review does not replace translation
assessment; it supplies the context in which that assessment becomes meaningful.
A further  application concerns the order in which information is encountered.
Suppose a hypothetical advertisement presents a prominent promise on its opening screen and

harder to find. The problem belongs to the relationship between content and presentation, not
solely to the sentence -level accuracy of either passage. Reviewers should inspect the
complete route through which the audience receives the message.

Nayak and Nair (2025) provide a relevant example of a stronger behavioral design
through a randomized field experiment on language translation in mobile commerce chatbots.
Its methodological importance here is that language conditions and behavioral outcomes are
explicitly investigated. The existence of such a design does not imply that its results
generalize to packaging, subtitles, or every bilingual population.
Marketing text analysis also requires careful interpretation. Berger et al. (2020)
discuss using textual data for marketing insight. In localization evaluation, comments and
service conversations can help identify recurring difficulties, but automated sentiment labels
should not be treated as direct measurements of consumers’ internal emotions. Language
variety, context, irony, and the purpose of a comment may complicate interpretation.

Evaluation can proceed proportionately. Small exploratory  interviews may uncover a
serious ambiguity before a larger comparison is justified. A controlled study can then
examine whether revised wording changes comprehension or reported response. Where
behavioral testing is feasible, alternative versions should differ in clearly documented ways,
with relevant audience characteristics and exposure conditions recorded. Otherwise, changes
in outcomes may be attributed to translation when layout, pricing, or audience composition
also changed.
The framework therefore uses evidence as a chain of accountable inferences. It asks
what changed, what readers encountered, what was measured, and how far the conclusion
extends. This is more informative than approving a translation because it sounds fluent to a
bilingual reviewer.
5 Discussion
5.1 A practical localization workflow
The five dimensions can be implemented through a sequence of documented
decisions. The initial brief should identify the communicative task, intended audience,
medium, and desired response. It should also distinguish fixed factual content from elements
open to creative variation. For example, a headline may permit extensive rewriting while a
return period requires precise continuity. This distinction gives reviewers a shared basis for
evaluating alternatives.
The next stage is the preparation of a small number of justified options. Each option
should include a short explanation of its intended interpretation and any change in
explicitness, emphasis, or cultural reference. A back -translation may help stakeholders
inspect semantic differences, but it cannot by itself establish naturalness or audience
response. The target text must still be assessed as communication in its own setting.
Disagreements between reviewers can be informative. One reviewer may prioritize
immediate comprehension, another brand tone, and another consistency with a product
specification. Recording the criterion behind each judgment allows the team to distinguish a
factual problem from a stylistic preference. The framework does not eliminate such
judgments; it makes their grounds explicit and provides a way to decide which require
audience evidence.
Review then proceeds from claim integrity to comprehension, tone, and multimodal
fit. This order prevents an attractive formulation from receiving approval before its factual
implications have been examined. It also clarifies responsibility: product specialists verify
commitments, language professionals assess meaning and usage, designers examine
presentation, and audience research informs reception. The same person may fulfill several
roles in a small project, but the questions remain distinct.
An evaluation brief should state acceptance criteria before reviewers select their
preferred version. For a service instruction, these could include accurate identification of the
next action and recognition of the relevant condition. For a campaign headline, they could
include an intended interpretation without an unintended factual implication. The criteria

99
should remain proportionate to the task and should not be converted into arbitrary universal
thresholds. Where the available evidence cannot distinguish two acceptable options, a
documented professional judgment may be reasonable, provided that uncertainty is
acknowledged and further monitoring remains possible.
Finally, the chosen version and its supporting rationale should be retained alongside
unresolved uncertainties. After release, complaints, misunderstandings, or service requests
may indicate where further investigation is needed. Such observations are diagnostic signals
rather than automatic proof of a causal translation effect. Recording the version and context
makes subsequent evaluation possible.
5.2 Technology and human judgment
Translation technology changes the production environment but does not remove the
need for accountable decisions. O’Hagan (2020) surveys the relationship between translation
and technology, while Kenny (2022) emphasizes the knowledge users need to work with
machine translation. For marketing localization, these perspectives support a practical
division between generating candidate text and approving its meaning, suitability, and factual
implications.
Huang and Rust’s (2021) strategic framework for artificial intelligence in marketing
provides a broader account of how different marketing tasks may involve AI. It should not be
read as evidence that an automated system understands or guarantees a consumer’s emotional
response. In the present workflow, automated tools may assist drafting, terminology checks,
comparison, and the organization of feedback. Approval remains an evidential decision.
An apparently fluent output deserves the same questions as a human draft. Has a
qualification disappeared? Has a cautious statement become a guarantee? Does a revised
slogan fit the accompanying image? Does the wording rely on an unexamined assumption
about a community? A review record should identify the tool -assisted stages and the
substantive checks performed, rather than using fluency as a proxy for reliability.
5.3 Implications for research and translator education
The framework generates three propositions for future investigation. First, adaptation
that improves perceived ease should not be expected to improve decision quality unless it
also preserves relevant information. This proposition can be tested by comparing fluency
ratings with comprehension of qualifications. It predicts a possible divergence between
attractive wording and accurate understanding, not an inevitable conflict.
Second, the effects of culturally familiar or unfamiliar elements should depend on
their communicative function and audience interpretation. A retained expression that signals
origin may work differently from an untranslated instruction. Testing should therefore
distinguish symbolic elements from task -critical information and examine relevant language
experience. Third, the apparent advantage of a wording may change when it is evaluated
within its actual visual or audiovisual environment rather than as isolated text.
For translator education, these propositions encourage assignments that combine
creative drafting with explanation and evaluation. Students could produce alternative

100
versions, identify the factual commitments of each, and design a modest comprehension task.
They should be assessed on the quality of the reasoning and the appropriateness of the
evidence, alongside the quality of the language. Unsupported claims about national
preferences should become questions for investigation.
The article’s interdisciplinary value lies in connecting language analysis with
responsible inference. Translation choices remain linguistic and cultural acts, yet claims
about their consequences require methods suited to those consequences. Bringing these levels
together can make professional evaluation more precise without reducing translation to a
mechanical score.
5.4 Limitations and future research
The framework is a conceptual proposal derived from a selective recent literature
base. It has not been validated as an instrument or tested as a complete workflow. The
restriction to publications from 2020 onward provides a contemporary focus but excludes
direct engagement with many foundational works. Recent theoretical syntheses mediate that
background and cannot replace a full historical review.
The evidence also spans different language pairs, media, audiences, and study
designs. Findings from one setting cannot be pooled informally into a universal effect of
localization. Access to some sources was limited to authoritative summaries, and the review
does not establish the relative effectiveness of competing interventions. The hypothetical
examples illustrate reasoning only.
Future research should test the framework in specified language pairs and settings,
including contexts that receive less attention in international marketing research. Studies
could compare versions while holding product information and visual conditions stable, then
assess comprehension, interpretation, and behavior separately. Reporting null results, adverse
interpretations, and subgroup differences would help establish where adaptation is beneficial
and where restraint is preferable.
6 Conclusion
The psychological dimension of marketing translation is best approached through
explicit distinctions between communicative choices and demonstrated audience effects.
Translation can alter what a message makes salient, how easily it is processed, and the social
relationship it appears to construct. Those possibilities justify careful design and
investigation; they do not establish that creative adaptation necessarily generates trust or
purchasing.
This article proposes five connected dimensions for that work: claim integrity,
processing and comprehension, affective and social interpretation, multimodal coherence, and
outcome evaluation. Their central principle is that the degree of adaptation should follow the
task, audience, and evidence while preserving the message’s factual commitments. Neither
literal correspondence nor creative rewriting constitutes a sufficient standard on its own.
The resulting framework offers a structured basis for briefs, comparative review,
audience testing, and translator education. Its contribution is conceptual and procedural.

choices support particular outcomes in particular settings. A defensible localization decision
