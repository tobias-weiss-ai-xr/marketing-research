ARTICLE INFO ABSTRACT
Article History: This research aims to build an integrative model of World Class Education
(WCE) management at President University by synthesizing strategic
leadership, operational standardization, marketing -entrepreneurship, and
international cooperation. Using a qualitative, integrative document-based
case study design, data were drawn from three 2026 mini -research
manuscripts addressing leadership and operational standards, marketing -
entrepreneurship in education, and higher education internationalization,
enriched with  institutional documents, official websites, and relevant
literature. Analysis involved source mapping, thematic coding, pattern
matching, cross-script synthesis, and conceptual proposition development.
Findings show WCE is ineffective when managed as sepa rate programs:
strategic leadership guides vision, operational standardization ensures
quality consistency, marketing -entrepreneurship links market
understanding with value creation, and international cooperation expands
learning, research, cultural, and industry networks. The case's strengths lie
in its international positioning, English -language instruction, portfolio -
building curriculum, the Jababeka industrial ecosystem, entrepreneurial
incubation, academic mobility, and multicultural experience. Key ga ps
remain in cross -unit data integration, active partnership verification,
measurement of startup and alumni outcomes, and collaborative research
strengthening. The study's novelty is the 4I -WCE Integrative Model —
strategic initiation, operational instituti onalization, value innovation, and
impactful internationalization, positioning WCE as an integration platform
for institutional quality and evidence-based graduate competitiveness.

produce knowledge, innovation, and graduates who are able to work and create value. This
situation makes World Class Education (WCE) relevant, but vulnerable to being reduced to a
reputation slogan. WCE is not synonymous with global rankings or the number of international
students, but a combination of talent concentration, resources, effective governance, knowledge
productivity, learning quality, and impact that can be shown ( Salmi, 2009 ; Hazelkorn, 2024 ;
Altbach, 2016 ). Therefore, WCE is more appropriately understood as a quality management
architecture than a marketing label.
The concept of world -class developed from debates about globalization, ranking, talent
attraction, and knowledge production capacity. Salmi (2009)  identified the concentration of
talent, adequate resources, and governance that supports autonomy, vision, and flexibility as the
conditions for a world -class university. Hazelkorn (2024)  reminds that the model cannot be
copied mechanically because each university has a different national history, funding, language,
and mission. Hazelkorn (2024) also points out that rankings can influence institutional behavior,
but do not necessarily reflect student experiences or local contributions. On that basis, WCE is
formulated as a quality ecosystem that connects the quality of learning, knowledge productivity,
global experience, industry -social relevance, and adaptive governance. Universities can build
excellence through academic niches, industrial ecosystems, multicultural character, pedagogical
innovation, or regional contributions, with the main measure being consistency between mission,
process, evidence, and outcomes.
The ecosystem approach avoids the dichotomy between internationalization and national
quality. A global perspective can strengthen students' understanding of Indonesian and ASEAN
issues, while the use of English needs to be accompanied by academic support so as not to create
a participation gap. Industrial networking can improve employability, but it must be linked to
long-term learning goals, ethics, and capacity building. Its implementation require s leadership
that is able to set direction, align units, manage change, build trust, and ensure that global
ambitions are not separated from internal quality. The effectiveness of leadership is not solely
determined by the individual nature of the leader, but by the ability to form organizational
mechanisms so that the vision is consistently executed (Black, 2015; Groves et al., 2024). Strategic
leadership comes through decisions regarding curriculum, quality assurance, human resources,
digitalization, student services, research, partnerships, and organizational learning.
Strategic leadership combines long-term direction, environmental management, resource
alignment, cultural development, and organizational capacity building. Leadership is not only in
the rector, but also spread to the dean, head of study program, quality assurance, service unit
leaders, lecturers, and project managers. Paul & Ghosh, (2024)  emphasize setting expectations,
focusing on learning, collaboration, and human development. The term leadership superiority in
Murison's text, which includes visionary, transformational, spiritual, ethical, innovative, and

This leadership is assessed through the ability to formulate visions, communicate priorities, build

integrity, and collegial involvement. Leaders also need to manage the paradox between

The vision of leadership needs to be translated through operational standardization so
that it does not stop as normative direction. Standardization is required in academic procedures,
administrative services, evaluations, credit recognition, internships, data management, research,
service, human resources, partnerships, security, and follow-up improvements. ISO 21001 places
the needs of students and stakeholders, process  consistency, performance evaluation, and
continuous improvement as elements of the management system of educational organizations
(International Organization for Standardization, 2018). Standardization is not interpreted as
bureaucratization, but as the establishment of minimum quality limits, clarity of responsibilities,
indicators, documentation, and the ability of the organization to study the results. The principles
of ISO 21001 include a focus on learners, visionary leadership, people engagement, proc ess
approaches, evidence -based decisions, relationship management, social responsibility,
accessibility, and data protection (Silaeva & Semenov, 2018). The relationship between leadership
and standardization is two -way: leadership provides direction and legitimacy, while
standardization reduces dependence on certain figures and provides feedback for leaders
through SOPs and quality assurance.
WCE also requires the ability to create and communicate value. Educational marketing
that only pursues the number of applicants can create a gap between messaging and experience,
whereas evidence -based marketing connects market intelligence, value propositions, student
experience, quality of service, academic work, industry networks, alumni, and career outcomes
(Hemsley-Brown & Oplatka, 2016 ; Kotler et al., 2021 ). Digitization expands touchpoints, but
increases the risk of message inconsistencies and the use of superficial indicators such as
impressions or followers ( Rutter & Pickles, 2016 ; Peruta & Shields, 2017 ). Entrepreneurship
education forms the ability to recognize opportunities, create value, experiment, collaborate,
manage risk, and build networks. The impact depends on the quality of pedagogy and the depth
of experience (Nabi et al., 2017) , while ent repreneurial universities expand the university's
functions through knowledge transfer, innovation, incubation, and industry -government
collaboration (Guerrero et al., 2016; Etzkowitz & Zhou, 2017). Entrepreneurship is not limited to
business establishment, but includes workplace innovation, social projects, prototypes, applied
research, and community contributions.

Muzaki's script offers 6E Models: Explore, Express, Engage, Educate, Enable, and
Evaluate. Explore reads the needs of prospective students,  students, alumni, industry, and
society; Express drafts a clear and provable value proposition; Engage builds relationships
through experiences, community, and dialogue; Educate develops entrepreneurial competencies
through curriculum and projects; Enable  provides mentors, incubators, technology, networks,
and resources; and Evaluate measures funnel, satisfaction, competencies, startups, careers,
reputation, and impact. This model is circular because the results of the evaluation are used to
improve the ex ploration and design of values. Marketing -entrepreneurship integration
transforms marketing from claims -based promotion to experience - and outcome -based
communication. Student projects, prototypes, internship successes, industry joint research, and
alumni contributions are proof of value, while market needs information is used to improve
curriculum, mentoring, and career services. The integration must be accompanied by ethical data
governance so that academic data is not used manipulatively and job prospects are not conveyed
as certainty.
The next dimension is international cooperation, which is not only demonstrated by
foreign students, the use of English, or the signing of memorandums of understanding.
Internationalization is the process of integrating int ernational, intercultural, and global
dimensions into the goals, functions, and implementation of higher education ( Knight, 2004; de
Wit et al., 2015 ). Its implementation includes student and lecturer mobility, joint academic
programs, collaborative research, curriculum internationalization, global industry engagement,
and cultural experiences. Idham Kholid's manuscript identifies six forms of cooperation, namely
academic mobility, joint academic programs, research collaboration, curriculum
internationalization, global industrial cooperation, and cultural development. Students who do
not participate in mobility can still gain global experience through guest lecturers, multicultural
groups, collaborative online international learning, industrial projects,  and cultural activities.
Cooperation produces value when supported by resources, governance, credit recognition,
safety, mentoring, curriculum suitability, quality assurance, research ethics, supervision,
assessment, and evaluation of mutual benefits. The relationship with marketing also needs to be
maintained so that the number of countries or partners is not used without explaining the
activities and results.
President University is a relevant case because it is located in the Jababeka industrial
estate in Cikarang and positions its educational experience through the IMPACT framework:
International, Medium of Communication in English, Portfolio-Building Curriculum, Absolutely
Worth It, Culture, and Targeted Career Preparation (President University, 2026). The institution
also features international services, cultural activities, and entrepreneurial support through
Setsail BizAccel, as well as incubation integrated into the curriculum (President University, 2026).
The uniqueness of the locus lies in the opportunity to bring together a global academic orientation
with a national and multinational industrial environment. However, institutional documents

The synthesis of literature produces four propositions. First, strategic leadership is an
antecedent that provides direction, resources, and coordination for standardization, value
innovation, and internationalization. Second, operational standardization i s an
institutionalization mechanism that ensures consistency of experience and accountability. Third,
marketing-entrepreneurship is a mechanism for creating and communicating evidence -based
value. Fourth, international cooperation is a mechanism for expand ing talent, knowledge,
culture, and networks. The four form a WCE-mediated cycle as an integration platform through
indicators of learning quality, student experience, research productivity, innovation,
employability, inclusion, reputation, and social contribution. The weakness of one pillar can limit
the other: marketing without standardization creates a promise -experience gap,
internationalization without leadership and quality assurance results in symbolic MoU,
entrepreneurship without networking and evaluation only results in events, while SOPs without
innovation create bureaucracy. The research gap lies in the absence of a framework that connects
all dimensions as a single system. Therefore, this study aims to integrate the three source
manuscripts into  a WCE management architecture, identify the strengths and synergies
mechanisms at President University, formulate outcome -based indicators, and offer a 4I -WCE
Integrative Model. The theoretical contribution is to shift the understanding of WCE from
reputation to a management platform, while the practical contribution is in the form of a
framework that brings together leadership data, quality assurance, academic, marketing,
incubators, international offices, career centers, and alumni in a continuous improvement cycle.

METHODS
The research uses a qualitative approach with an integrative document-based case study
design. This design was chosen because the purpose of the research was not to test causal
relationships through new surveys, but to integrate evi dence, concepts and findings that were
previously spread across three academic manuscripts with the same locus. Document-based case
studies allow researchers to examine the relationship between institutional practices, industry
contexts, communication stra tegies, internationalization and governance without needing
unavailable data (Yin, 2018).
The main sources consist of: (1) Murison et al. (2026)  manuscript on leadership and
operational standardization; (2) Muzakki et al. (2026)  on WCE -based marketin g-
entrepreneurship; and (3) Kholid (2026) paper on WCE-based international cooperation. All three
manuscripts have a design that is not entirely the same. Murison's manuscript is primarily based
on literature studies; Muzaki's manuscript uses a rapid cas e study through re-analysis of visits,
observations, documents, and mapping of eight indicators ; while Kholid's manuscript uses
qualitative case studies with observations, interviews, documentation , and literature. These

Supporting data was obtained from institutional profiles, IMPACT pages, international
office information, Setsail BizAccel pages as well as literature on WCE, lead ership, education
management systems, higher education marketing, entrepreneurial universities and
internationalization. Official documents of institutions are treated as institutional self -
representation. This information is useful for checking the consistency of concepts and programs,
but is not treated as independent verification of all outcome claims. The numbers that appear in
the source manuscript are also not used as longitudinal data if they are not accompanied by a
time series or audit document.
The analysis was carried out through five stages. First, source mapping to identify the
objectives, methods, units of analysis, findings, limitations and contributions of each manuscript.
Second, coding is open to statements related to vision, SOPs, servic es, digitalization, marketing,
curriculum, incubators, partnerships, mobility, research, culture, outcomes and evaluation.
Third, axial coding to group code into four pillars: strategic leadership, operational
standardization, marketing -entrepreneurship an d international cooperation. Fourth, pattern
matching between case findings and theories. Fifth, integrative synthesis to formulate
relationship mechanisms, indicators, gaps and the 4I -WCE model. The flow of analysis adapts
reduction, presentation, conclusion drawing, and verification as suggested by Miles (2014).
Credibility is maintained through triangulation between manuscripts, comparison of
official documents with theoretical arguments, search for opposing evidence and audit of
limitations. A claim is categorized as relatively strong evidence if it appears consistent in more
than one source or has the support of an institutional program; as an indication when it appears
only on one manuscript; and as a verification agenda if related to numbers, long-term outcomes,
research productivity, active partnership status or startup performance. This strategy prevents
articles from turning institutional promotions into facts without scrutiny.
The main methodological limitations are the unavailability of raw interv iew data,
marketing funnel data, individual student data, audited partnership lists, integrated alumni
tracers, and startup longitudinal data. Therefore, the results of the study are conceptual models
based on case synthesis, not statistical testing. The m odel can be the basis for further research
through student and alumni surveys, cross -unit interviews, document audits, social network
analysis and SEM-PLS.

RESEARCH RESULTS
WCE Value Architecture at President University
The synthesis shows that WCE at President University is built through a combination of
global identity, use of the English language, portfolio -building curriculum, career preparation,
campus culture, proximity to industry, entrepreneurship programs, and international experience.
The IMPACT framework brings together most of those elements in an institutional value
proposition. At the conceptual level, IMPACT functions as a value architecture because it
connects global exposure, English language communication, proof of competence, consideration
of educational value, cultural experience and career orientation. This arc hitecture is more
powerful than a single slogan because it answers to the diverse motives of students.
However, value architecture has not automatically become a quality system. In order for
WCE to be substantive, each element needs to be translated into process and outcome indicators.
International needs to be measured through access and quality of global experience, not just the
student's country of origin. English medium needs to be measured through academic ability and
participation, not just a formal language of instruction. Portfolio-building curriculum needs to be
proven through the quality of work, assessment and industry recognition. Targeted career
preparation needs to be associated with quality internships, job placement, waiting times, career
mobility and the ability to create jobs. Culture needs to be assessed through belonging, inclusion,
well-being and cross-cultural interaction.
These findings position WCE as an integration platform between identity and evidence.
On the one hand, the architect ure of value helps the university explain its uniqueness to the
market. On the other hand, the value architecture should be an internal promise that is monitored
by quality assurance. The gap between messages and experiences has the potential to undermine
trust. Therefore, WCE management requires regular meetings between leaders, academics,
quality assurance, marketing, international offices, career centers, incubators and alumni to check
the consistency of promises-values-outcomes.

Strategic Leadership as a Director and Liaison
Murison's script affirms visionary, transformational, spiritual, ethical and strategic
leadership. The synthesis shows that the main contribution of leadership is not to personal
superiority, but to the ability to unite units that have different work logics. Marketing works with
the rhythm of recruitment and communication; academics focus on learning and assessment;
International Office manages partners and mobility; incubators encourage experimentation;
Career Center is related to i ndustry; Quality assurance requires documentation and evaluation.
Without a common direction, each unit can produce good activities that do not form institutional
outcomes.
Strategic leadership works through five mechanisms. First, establish a definition of WCE
that is operational and not just reputation -based. Second, develop cross -unit priorities and
allocate resources. Third, building a culture of collaboration and trust so that data can be shared
securely. Fourth, ensuring ethical accountability in comm unication, partnerships and data use.
Fifth, leading the organizational learning process based on dashboards and evaluations. The
mechanism is in line with the demands of leadership that sets expectations, focuses on learning,
encourages collaboration, and develops people (Ji et al., 2024).
The supporting factors that emerged were modern management systems, digital
technology, quality of human resources, professional culture and international networks.
Inhibiting factors include coordination complexity, t echnological change, global competition,
variations in unit maturity and limitations of sustainable innovation. This obstacle shows that
leadership must build distributed leadership. The top leadership sets the direction, but the
process owners in the stud y program and unit need to have clear authority, competence and
targets.
The spiritual and ethical dimensions are translated in trustworthy governance.
Universities need to avoid unverifiable claims, recruitment pressures that ignore the suitability
of pro spective students, the use of data without consent and partnerships that only pursue
ceremonies. The courage to convey limitations, improve services and stop programs that do not
have an impact are part of leadership integrity.

Operational Standardization as a Guarantee of Consistency
Operational standardization emerged as a bridge between the vision and the student
experience. Murison's manuscript mentions academic standards, administration, research and
service, student services, human resources , and gov ernance. Muzaki and Idham Kholid's
manuscripts extend the need for standardization to content management, student -alumni
relations, incubation, mobility, credit recognition, partnerships, joint research and cultural
activities. The synthesis shows that WCE  requires cross-functional SOPs, not just internal SOPs
of each unit.

The first example is the student journey. The value proposition conveyed at the awareness
stage must be translated into admissions, orientation, lectures, academic services, internships,
projects, mobility, graduation , and alumni. Each touchpoint requires information standards,
service time, responsibility, complaint mechanisms, data protection, and satisfaction evaluation.
The second example is partnerships. The MoU needs to be reduced to an MoA or implementation
arrangement, program owner, budget, target participants, recognition, output, outcome and
evaluation. The third example is startups. The incubation program requires selection standards,
mentoring, milestones, ethics, IPR, access to funding, reporting and post-program tracers.
Digitalization becomes a standardization reinforcement when systems are interconnected.
Data marketing, academic, student affairs, international offices, career centers, alumni and
incubators should not be in  silos. However, data integration should implement data
minimization, consent, role-based access and aggregate use for evaluation. Dashboards should
not turn into excessive individual oversight. The goal is to find out whether the institution's
promises are being fulfilled and which groups have not benefited.
The findings also show the risk of compliance without learning. Documents can be
complete but not used for decisions. Therefore, each audit needs to generate action owners,
deadlines, resources and fol low-up verification. Good standardization is a minimum viable
standard: strong enough to guarantee quality and safety, but leaves room for program
adaptation.

Marketing-Entrepreneurship: From Promotion to Proof of Value Creation
Muzaki's script mapped eight indicators and obtained a descriptive score of 1.63 on a scale
of 0-2. The score indicates a strong category within the internal framework of the manuscript, but
not a population statistic. The main strengths are in international positioning, the use of English,
the portfolio building curriculum, career orientation, proximity to the Jababeka ecosystem,
incubators, and industrial networks. These findings show that institutional attractiveness is built
by a combination of message and experience.
The 6E model helps explain the value creation cycle. Explore requires needs research and
journey analysis. Express requires consistent, proven messaging. Engage requires long -term
relationships with schools, parents, students, alumni, industry, and communities. Educate turns
entrepreneurship into competence through projects and reflection. Enable provides mentors,
spaces, technology, markets, partners and financing. Evaluate measures conversion, retention,
satisfaction, portfolio, efficacy, prototype, startup, survival, career, and impact. The cycle is not
linear; The entrepreneurship project generates evidence for marketing, while market feedback
improves the curriculum.

stopping at the reach of content, the number of leads or registrants. Entrepreneurship indicators
can stop at the number of activities and teams. Integrative models demand a relationship between

International Cooperation: From MoU to Outcome
The six forms of internationalization identified by Kholid show a relatively
comprehensive ecosystem. Academic mobility expands experience and networking; joint
academic programs support curriculum recognition and exposure; research collaboration
contributes to knowledge production; internationalization of the curriculum strengthens the
global perspective; global industry cooperation increases relevance; and cultural development
builds competence across nations. The use of English, the diversity of students and the location
in the industrial estate strengthen the supporting conditions.
The most visible strengths are in the global atmosphere, language, mobility, cultural
activities and industrial connectivity. The gap lies in collaborative research ev idence, co -
publications, impact evaluations and active partnership documentation. This is important
because a long-term academic reputation cannot be sustained solely by the campus experience.
Knowledge productivity, learning quality, innovation and a grad uate's track record continue to
determine the legitimacy of WCE.
The integrative model proposes a partnership lifecycle: needs exploration, partner
selection, suitability testing, implementing agreements, implementation, monitoring, evaluation
and follow-improve-stop decisions. Every partnership needs to have objectives, owners, target
participants, indicators, risks and mutual benefits. The partnership dashboard should distinguish
between MoUs, MoA, active programs, outputs, outcomes and review dates. Thus, the university
avoids the inflation of symbolic partnerships.
Internationalization at home also needs to be strengthened so that the benefits are not only
enjoyed by mobility participants. Guest lecturers, cross -border projects, collaborative online
international learning, multicultural groups, global -contextual literature and cultural activities
can expand access. However, the program should be designed with learning and reflection in
mind; Multicultural interaction does not automatically result in cross -cultural competence
without facilitation.

Cross-Pillar Synergies and Systemic Gaps
Cross-script analysis shows five synergistic pathways. First, strategic leadership sets the
direction and gives legitimacy to standardization. Second, standardization ens ures that the
marketing value proposition is consistently present in the student experience. Third,
entrepreneurship produces works, innovations and outcomes that are proof of marketing. Fourth,
international cooperation expands learning resources, mentors, markets, research and reputation.
Fifth, outcome data returns information to leaders to choose priorities and improve processes.
The path forms a cycle, not a one-way chain.
Three systemic gaps stand out. First, data silos. Each unit can have its own ind icators
without a consistent data identity and definition. Second, event orientation. Success is often seen
in the number of events, participants, or signings, not changes in competence and quality. Third,
reputational bias. Easy-to-communicate activities receive more attention than internal processes
such as language support, assessments, mentoring, audits and follow-ups. The 4I-WCE model is
designed to address all three gaps through governance, standardization, outcome metrics and
feedback loops.
The findings also show the need to distinguish between strong evidence, indications and
verification agendas. The existence of IMPACT, international office and Setsail BizAccel can be
verified as an institutional program. However, the impact on careers, startup su rvival, research
productivity and alumni contributions requires longitudinal data and audits. This article
therefore does not conclude that the entire WCE target has been achieved. Its contribution is to
show the foundations, mechanisms and areas of reinforcement.

DISCUSSION
WCE as an Institutional Mediator
The main contribution of the study is the placement of WCE as an institutional mediator,
rather than a stand -alone reputation variable. Leadership, SOPs, marketing -entrepreneurship
and international cooperation produce an impact when combined in student ex perience and
academic outcomes. In this logic, WCE is a platform that translates governance inputs into the
quality of learning, productivity, global competence, innovation and competitiveness of
graduates. This approach complements the framework of Salmi (2009) by adding value creation
mechanisms, standardization of experiences and data-driven feedback.
The model also explains why investing in one element doesn't always result in change.
The addition of international partners does not improve quality if the curriculum, recognition
and evaluation are not ready. Boosting promotions does not strengthen trust if service is not
consistent. Incubators do not produce an ecosystem if curricula, mentors, industry and tracers are
not connected. Leadership is not sustainable if decisions are not institutionalized in SOPs and
data. Thus, synergy becomes a condition that is more important than the number of programs.

From International Branding to Evidence-Based Reputation
The findings support the view that university brands are shaped by the accumulation of
experience and relationships. IMPACT provides a coherent differentiation narrative, but a long-
lasting reputation relies on evidence. Evidence does not have to be just ranking; it can be in the
form of portfolio quality, internship experience, recognized mobility, collaborative publications,
language skills, student well-being, enduring startups, innovation in the workplace, and alumni
contributions. Reputation is an outcome of quality, not a substitute for quality.
This shift has implications for marketing. The marketing unit needs to obtain aggregate
data on student success and outcomes, while the academic unit needs to understand the
expectations and risks of prospective students. Both must work within ethical and data protection
limits. Public communication should distinguish between facts, estimates, testimonials , and
aspirations. Transparency to limitations can build trust more strongly than overly broad claims.

Adaptive Standardization and Learner Culture
Standardization is often perceived as the opposite of innovation. The results of the
synthesis show that the two actually need each other. Innovations without standards are difficult
to replicate and evaluate; standards without innovation lose relevance. The  solution is adaptive
standardization: organizations set principles, quality limits, minimum data and risk mechanisms,
while giving experimentation room to program design. After the pilot, results are evaluated and
effective practices are institutionalized.

Inclusive and Relevant Internationalization
Impactful internationalization must reach more students. Physical mobility is important,
but cost and access limit participation. Internationalization at home through curriculum, COIL,
guest lecturers, multicultural groups, global-local projects and cultural activities can extend the
benefits. Design needs to ensure that the  use of English does not reduce the participation of
students who are still developing and that language support is available.
Local relevance also needs to be maintained. WCE does not mean importing the
curriculum in its entirety. A global perspective sho uld help students understand Indonesia's
industry, regional development, sustainability, inequality, technological ethics and ASEAN
opportunities. The proximity to Jababeka provides a unique opportunity to develop a university-
industry collaboration living  laboratory, but the university still needs to maintain educational
goals and social responsibility.

Implications for Institutional Research and Policy
Theoretically, the 4I -WCE model brings together the literature on leadership, quality
management, higher education marketing, entrepreneurial university and internationalization.
This integration shifts the unit of analysis from the program to the institutional architecture.
Methodologically, the model provides constructs and indicators for further research . Strategic
leadership can be measured through vision, coordination, trust, accountability and innovation
support; standardization through SOPs, digitalization, QA, service and follow -up; marketing-
entrepreneurship through 6E; international cooperation thr ough six forms of programs; WCE
through quality experience; and outcomes through the quality of the institution and the
competitiveness of graduates.
Practically, President University can build WCE portfolio management. Each major
program is recorded based  on objectives, owners, beneficiary segments, resources, risks,
indicators, outcomes and decisions. Portfolio reviews allow leaders to see duplication, symbolic
programs, access gaps and synergy opportunities. Data doesn't have to be perfect in the early
stages; Organizations can start from the minimum viable dashboard and iteratively improve the
definition.

Model Integratif 4I-WCE
The 4I-WCE Integrative Model is formulated from four institutional processes. The first is
Strategic Initiation. Leadership establishes the WCE definition, direction, priorities, resources,
ethics and collaboration mechanisms. The second I is Operational Institutionalization. The vision
translates into SOPs, quality assurance, data systems, services, HR competencies and follow -up
audits. The third is Value Innovation. Marketing -entrepreneurship connects the exploration of
needs, value propositions, engagement, value creation learning, supporting ecosystems and
evaluation. The fourth is Impactful Internationalization. Global cooperation is directed at
mobility, joint programs, research, curriculum, industry, culture and traceable outcomes.
The fourth process boils down to the WCE integration platform. This platform is not a
new organizational unit, but rather a governance mechanism that brings together goals and data.
Its components can be a cross -unit steering committee, dashboard, portfolio review, data
dictionary, outcome indicators, risk registers and decision logs. The WCE platform transforms
activity information into decisions: which programs are expanded, improved, merged or
discontinued.

platform that connects internal governance and external openness. Second, marketing and
entrepreneurship are united so that reputation is based on value creation. Third, standardization
and internationalization are brought together through outcome metrics, so that partnerships do
not stop at documents. This model can be tested at other universities, but the context of Jababeka
and the positioning of President University remain case-specific characters.

CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS
Conclusion
The research concludes that the management of WCE at President University can only be
understood in its entirety if strategic leadership, operational standardization, marketing -
entrepreneurship, and international cooperation are treated as one system. Leadership provides
direction and legitimacy; standardization transforms vision into a consistent experience;
marketing-entrepreneurship connects needs with value creation and communication; and
international cooperation expands knowledge, culture, research, industry, and networking. WCE
functions as an integration platform that translates the four pillars into institutional quality and
graduate competitiveness.
The foundation of the case is relatively strong on international pos itioning, the use of
English, the portfolio building curriculum, career preparation, proximity to the Jababeka
industrial ecosystem, entrepreneurial incubation, academic mobility and multicultural
experience. However, the foundation is not enough to declar e all WCE outcomes have been
achieved. The main gaps are in cross -unit data integration, indicator standardization, active
partnership audits, longitudinal measurement of startups and alumni, equitable access to
internationalization and collaborative research productivity.
The novelty of the research is the 4I -WCE Integrative Model: Strategic Initiation,
Operational Institutionalization, Value Innovation and Impactful Internationalization. The model
offers a shift from activity-based management to portfolio- and outcome-based management. Its
implementation requires the definition of shared indicators, cross -unit steering, minimum
dashboards, data governance, audits and feedback loops. Reputation is positioned as a
consequence of quality, not a substitute for quality.
The limitations of the research stem from document-based design, heterogeneity of source
manuscript methods and the unavailability of raw and longitudinal data. Therefore, the findings
are positioned as a case -based conceptual synthesis that requ ires empirical validation.
Nonetheless, the model provides a sufficiently operational framework to guide data collection,
governance strengthening and subsequent research.

Recommendations
Based on the findings and limitations, universities should build a cross-unit WCE
dashboard, audit SOPs and partnership lifecycles, integrate marketing data with student success
ethically, standardize startup and alumni tracers, expand internationalization at home,
strengthen joint research and publications, and use portfolio review for decisions to continue, fix,
integrate, or discontinue programs. Future research should test the 4I-WCE model through mixed
methods, SEM-PLS, longitudinal studies, cross-university comparisons, and partnership network
analysis to establish e mpirical validation and broader applicability across different higher
education contexts.
