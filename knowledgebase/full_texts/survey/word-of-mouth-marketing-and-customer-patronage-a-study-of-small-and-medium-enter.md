ABSTRACT
This study examined the effect of word of mouth marketing on customer patronage among Small and Medium
Enterprises (SMEs) in Katsina metropolis, Katsina State, Nigeria. The growing importance of SMEs to the Nigerian
economy, coupled with the high cost of traditional advertising, has made word of mouth marketing an essential
promotional strategy for small businesses, yet empirical research on this topic in  Northern Nigerian contexts
remains limited. The study was guided by three specific objectives: to examine the effect of source credibility on
customer patronage; to determine the effect of volume of word of mouth on customer patronage; and to assess the
effect of content richness on customer patronage. The study was anchored on the Theory of Reasoned Action, Social
Exchange Theory, and Source Credibility Theory. A quantitative cross -sectional survey research design was
employed, with 396 respondents sample d from four major commercial areas of Katsina metropolis using a multi -
stage sampling technique. A structured questionnaire with high internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha: 0.86–0.88)
was used for data collection. Data were analysed using descriptive stat istics, Pearson correlation, and multiple
linear regression. The findings revealed that source credibility had the strongest significant positive effect on
customer patronage (β = 0.328, p < 0.001), followed by volume of word of mouth (β = 0.254, p < 0.001 ) and
content richness (β = 0.214, p < 0.001). The three dimensions jointly explained 34.1% of the variance in customer
patronage (R² = 0.341, F = 67.723, p < 0.001). The study concluded that word of mouth marketing significantly
affects customer patronage  in Katsina metropolis, with source credibility being the most critical dimension. The
study recommended that SME owners cultivate relationships with credible community sources, systematically
encourage satisfied customers to share their experiences, train customers to share detailed recommendations, and
integrate word of mouth strategies into business operations.
Keywords: Word of mouth marketing, customer patronage, source credibility, volume of WOM, content richness,
Katsina metropolis.

1. INTRODUCTION

Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) constitute the
backbone of the Nigerian economy, contributing
significantly to employment generation, poverty
reduction, and gross domestic product. According to the
Small and Medium Enterprises Development Agency of
Nigeria (SMEDAN, 2024), SMEs account for
approximately 48% of Nigeria's GDP and employ over
60% of the nation's workforce. In Katsina State, SMEs
play an equally vital role, with the Katsina State Ministry
of Commerce and Industry (2025) reporting that over
85,000 registered SMEs operate across the state, with a
significant concentration in Katsina metropolis. These
enterprises range from retail shops and food vendors to
service providers and manufacturing concerns, serving
as the primary source of goods and services for the local
population. However, despite their numerical

dominance, many SMEs in Katsina metropolis struggle
to attract and retain customers in an increasingly
competitive marketplace, where traditional advertising
channels such as radio, televis ion, and print media are
often prohibitively expensive for small business owners.
In response to these marketing challenges, word of
mouth (WOM) marketing has emerged as a particularly
potent and cost-effective promotional strategy for SMEs,
especially in tight-knit communities like Katsina
metropolis where social networks are strong and
interpersonal trust is highly valued. Word of mouth
marketing refers to the informal, person -to-person
communication between consumers regarding their
evaluations of goods,  services, or businesses (Arndt,
1967). Unlike paid advertising, which is often viewed
with skepticism, WOM recommendations are perceived
as more credible, authentic, and trustworthy because
they originate from friends, family members,
neighbours, or colle agues who have no apparent
financial incentive to promote the business. In the
context of Katsina metropolis, where communal ties and
extended family networks (including the extended
kinship system known as  dangi) play a central role in
daily life, WOM mar keting may be particularly
influential in shaping customer purchasing decisions and
patronage behaviour.
The theoretical importance of WOM marketing has been
well established in the marketing literature. Scholars
have consistently demonstrated that positiv e word of
mouth leads to increased customer acquisition, higher
retention rates, and greater customer lifetime value
(Anderson, 1998; Trusov, Bucklin, & Pauwels, 2009).
Conversely, negative word of mouth can severely
damage a business's reputation and driv e customers
away. For SMEs operating with limited marketing
budgets, understanding the specific dimensions of WOM
that most strongly influence customer patronage is not
merely an academic exercise but a practical necessity for
survival and growth. However,  empirical research on
WOM marketing within the specific context of SMEs in
Northern Nigerian cities, particularly Katsina
metropolis, remains severely limited. Most existing
studies have focused on large corporations, e-commerce
platforms, or service industries in Lagos, Abuja, or Port
Harcourt, leaving a significant geographical and
contextual gap in the literature.
The justification for this study is therefore compelling
from multiple perspectives. From a practical standpoint,
SME owners and managers in Katsina metropolis require
empirical evidence to guide their WOM marketing
strategies. Understanding whether the expertise of the
recommender (source credibility) matters more than the
volume of recommendations (volume of WOM), or
whether the richness of i nformation provided (content
richness) outweighs both, enables resource -constrained
SMEs to focus their efforts on the most impactful WOM
drivers. From a theoretical perspective, this study tests
the applicability of the Theory of Reasoned Action
(TRA), Social Exchange Theory, and Source Credibility
Theory to the WOM –patronage relationship within a
Northern Nigerian cultural context, thereby extending
these frameworks to a previously under -researched
population. From a policy perspective, findings from this
study can inform the Katsina State Government's SME
support programmes, enabling the design of targeted
marketing training interventions that emphasise the most
effective WOM strategies for local businesses.
This study is guided by the following specific objectives:
(i) to examine the effect of word of mouth source
credibility on customer patronage among SMEs in
Katsina metropolis; (ii) to determine the effect of word
of mouth volume on customer patronage among SMEs
in Katsina metropolis; (iii) to assess the effect of word of
mouth content richness on customer patronage among
SMEs in Katsina metropolis. Correspondingly, the study
tests three hypotheses that each dimension of word of
mouth marketing (source credibility, volume, and
content richness) has a si gnificant positive effect on
customer patronage. The study focuses on customers of
SMEs operating in four major commercial areas of
Katsina metropolis: Kofar Guga Market, Central Market,
Kofar Kaura Market, and the New Market.

 2. LITERATURE REVIEW

 2.1 Conceptual Framework
 2.1.1 Word of Mouth Marketing
Word of mouth (WOM) marketing refers to the act of
consumers sharing information, opinions,
recommendations, or warnings about products, services,
or businesses with other consumers through informal,
person-to-person communication channels (Arndt, 1967;
Okonkwo & Eze, 2025). Unlike traditional advertising,
which is marketer -generated and paid for, WOM is

GUSAU JOURNAL OF BUSINESS ADMINISTRATION (GUJOBA) | Vol. 5, Issue 1 | 2026
 370
consumer-generated and typically perceived as more
credible, trustworthy, and unbiased because the
communicator has no direct financial interest in the
transaction (Nwakamma & Okafor, 2025). In the digital
age, WOM has expanded beyond face -to-face
conversations to include electronic word of mouth
(eWOM) through social media platforms, messaging
applications (such as WhatsApp and Telegram), online
reviews, and customer forums (Babatunde & Aliyu,
2026). However, in the context of Katsina metropolis,
where internet penetration is still developing and face -
to-face social interactions remain highly valued,
traditional oral WOM continues to play a dominant role
in consumer decision-making (Usman & Bello, 2025).
For the purpose of this study, word of mouth marketing
is conceptualised as a multi -dimensional construct
comprising three specific dimensions: source credibility,
volume of WOM, and content richness.
Source Credibility refers to the extent to which the
consumer perceives the person providing the WOM
recommendation as knowledgeable, trustworthy, and
unbiased (Hovland, Janis, & Kelley, 1953; Adegbuyi &
Ogunyemi, 2025). A credible source is someone whom
the receiver believes has accurate information about the
product or service (expertise) and will not deliberately
provide misleading information (trustworthiness). In the
Katsina metropolis context, credible  sources may
include close family members, respected community
elders, experienced business owners, or satisfied
customers who have used the product or service
themselves (Ibrahim & Sani, 2026). Source credibility is
particularly important in high-trust, collectivist societies
like Northern Nigeria, where recommendations from
trusted community members carry more weight than
anonymous online reviews or mass media advertisi ng
(Muhammad & Abubakar, 2025).
Volume of WOM refers to the quantity or frequency of
word of mouth communications that a consumer receives
about a particular business, product, or service (Liu,
2006; Adewale & Olaniyi, 2025). High volume of WOM
can take the form of many individuals recommending the
same business, the same individual recommen ding the
business repeatedly over time, or widespread community
discussion about the business. In Katsina metropolis,
volume of WOM may manifest as a business being "the
talk of the town" or being mentioned favourably in
multiple social gatherings, mosque congregations, or
market interactions (Suleiman & Yusuf, 2026). High
volume signals social proof-the idea that if many people
are recommending a business, it must be good -and can
create a bandwagon effect that drives customer
patronage (Ogundele & Fasasi, 2025).
Content Richness refers to the level of detail, specificity,
informativeness, and vividness of the information
conveyed in the word of mouth communication (Herr,
Kardes, & Kim, 1991; Ekwueme & Nwachukwu, 2025).
Rich content includes specific details  about product
features, pricing, quality, customer service experiences,
location, and availability, rather than vague statements
such as "it's good" or "I liked it." Rich WOM helps
potential customers form clear expectations, reduces
perceived risk, and f acilitates confident purchasing
decisions (Akinola & Ogunbiyi, 2026). In the Katsina
metropolis context, rich WOM might include detailed
accounts of a positive experience at a particular tailoring
shop, including specifics about price, turnaround time,
fabric quality, and the tailor's attitude (B ashir & Rabiu,
2025).
 2.1.2 Customer Patronage
Customer patronage refers to the frequency, consistency,
and volume of purchases that a customer makes from a
particular business over time (East, Hammond, &
Lomax, 2008; Oladipo & Adebayo, 2025). Patronage is
a behavioural outcome measure that goes beyond mere
attitude or intention; it captures actual purchasing
behaviour (Adeleke & Ogunyemi, 2026). High customer
patronage is manifested in repeat purchases, larger
basket sizes, shorter intervals between purchases, and a
demonstrated preference for one business over
competitors (Ogunbiyi & Adeleke, 2025). For SMEs in
Katsina metropolis, customer patronage is the ultimate
indicator of business success, as it directly translates into
revenue, profitability, and sustainability (Musa &
Abdullahi, 2026). Patronage is influenced by multiple
factors, including product quality, price, location,
customer service, and, as this study posits, word of
mouth marketing (Bello & Suleiman, 2026).
 2.2 Theoretical Framework
This study is anchored on three complementary theories:
the Theory of Reasoned Action, Social Exchange
Theory, and Source Credibility Theory.
Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) , developed by
Fishbein and Ajzen (1975) and r ecently applied in

GUSAU JOURNAL OF BUSINESS ADMINISTRATION (GUJOBA) | Vol. 5, Issue 1 | 2026
 371
consumer behaviour contexts by Okafor and Nwankwo
(2025), posits that an individual's behavioural intention
is determined by two factors: attitude toward the
behaviour (the individual's positive or negative
evaluation of performing the b ehaviour) and subjective
norm (the perceived social pressure to perform or not
perform the behaviour). Subjective norm is directly
relevant to word of mouth marketing, as it captures the
influence of referent others -people whose opinions the
individual values-on the individual's behavioural
intentions (Ajzen, 2020; Adeyemi & Ogunyemi, 2025).
When a customer receives a positive WOM
recommendation from a valued referent (such as a family
member or close friend), the subjective norm favouring
patronage is s trengthened, increasing the customer's
intention to patronise the recommended business (Ismail
& Bello, 2025). TRA provides the theoretical
justification for hypothesising that WOM, particularly
from credible sources, affects customer patronage:
WOM shapes  subjective norms, which in turn shape
behavioural intentions and actual beha viour
(Muhammad & Usman, 2025).
Social Exchange Theory , rooted in the work of Homans
(1958) and Blau (1964) and extended by contemporary
scholars such as Cropanzano and Mitchell (2005) and
Abdullahi and Lawal (2026), posits that human
relationships are governed by a rational calculation of
costs and benefits. Individuals engage in interactions and
relationships that provide them with maximum benefits
at minimum costs (Ekeh, 1974; Hassan & Zayd, 2025).
In the context of word of mouth, when a consumer
receives a WOM recommendation that leads to a
satisfactory purchase experience, both the recommender
(who gains social approval and gratitude) and the
receiver (who gains a positive con sumption outcome)
benefit from the exchange (Blau, 2017; Usman & Bello,
2025). Conversely, a poor recommendation leads to
costs (disappointment, wasted money) and damages the
social exchange relationship (Lambe, Wittmann, &
Spekman, 2001; Babatunde & Usman , 2025). Because
individuals value their social relationships and
reputations, they are motivated to provide accurate,
helpful WOM recommendations, and receivers are
motivated to act on recommendations from those with
whom they have ongoing exchange relati onships
(Cropanzano, Anthony, Daniels, & Hall, 2017). Social
Exchange Theory thus explains why WOM is generally
credible and influential, and why high volume of WOM
from multiple exchange partners increases patronage
(Ogunbiyi & Adeleke, 2025).
Source Cred ibility Theory , developed by Hovland,
Janis, and Kelley (1953) and updated by Pornpitakpan
(2004) and Adegbuyi and Ogunyemi (2025), identifies
two primary dimensions of source credibility: expertise
(the perceived knowledge, skill, or experience of the
source) and trustworthiness (the perceived honesty,
objectivity, and sincerity of the source). Highly credible
sources are more persuasive than low-credibility sources
because receivers are more likely to accept and act upon
information from sources they con sider knowledgeable
and honest (Wilson & Sherrell, 1993; Okafor & Eze,
2025). In the context of WOM marketing, when the
recommender is perceived as an expert (e.g., someone
who has extensive experience with similar products) and
trustworthy (e.g., someone with no hidden agenda or
incentive), the receiver is more likely to form positive
attitudes toward the recommended business and to act on
those attitudes by patronising the business (Ismail, 2025;
Bello & Suleiman, 2026). Source Credibility Theory
provides the theoretical foundation for this study's first
hypothesis regarding the effect of source credibility on
customer patronage (Muhammad & Abubakar, 2025).
 2.3 Empirical Review
Several empirical studies have examined the relationship
between word of mouth  marketing dimensions and
customer patronage or related outcomes such as
purchase intention, brand loyalty, and customer
retention, with an emphasis on the most recent literature
(2023–2026).
In a study conducted among 380 consumers of retail
SMEs in Kano State, Abubakar and Bello (2025)
investigated the effect of word of mouth on purchase
intention using a quantitative survey design. The study
employed multiple regression analysis and found that
WOM credibility (β = 0.43, p < 0.001) and WOM
volume (β = 0.36, p < 0.001) both significantly predicted
purchase intention, with credibility having a stronger
effect. The study recommended that SME owners
encourage satisfied customers to share detailed positive
experiences through both face -to-face interactions and
social media platforms. However, the study focused on
purchase intention rather than actual patronage and was
conducted in Kano, not Katsina, limiting direct
applicability to the current study context.

Similarly, Ogunbiyi and Adeyemi  (2025) examined the
relationship between word of mouth marketing and
customer loyalty among 420 consumers of food and
beverage SMEs in Lagos State. Using structural equation
modelling, the study found that WOM quality (β = 0.39,
p < 0.001) and WOM source expertise (β = 0.35, p <
0.001) had significant positive effects on customer
loyalty, with satisfaction serving as a partial mediator.
The study recommended that SMEs focus on delivering
exceptional service experiences that customers will want
to share with others. However, the study was conducted
in Lagos, a mega -city with vastly different social
dynamics and consumer behaviour patterns compared to
Katsina metropolis.
In a study specifically focused on Northern Nigeria,
Ibrahim and Sani (2026) investigated  the influence of
word of mouth on consumer purchase decisions for
household goods in Kaduna metropolis. Using a sample
of 350 consumers and hierarchical regression, the study
found that WOM from family members (β = 0.38, p <
0.001) and close friends (β = 0.32, p < 0.001) was
significantly more influential than WOM from
acquaintances or online reviews. The study also found
that content richness moderated the relationship between
WOM volume and purchase intention, such that high
volume combined with rich con tent produced the
strongest effects. This study is particularly relevant as it
was conducted in a neighbouring Northern Nigerian city,
though it focused on purchase intention rather than
patronage and did not include Katsina specifically.
Internationally, Trusov, Bucklin, and Pauwels (2009)
conducted a longitudinal study of a social networking
site and found that WOM referrals had a significantly
greater impact on customer acquisition than traditional
marketing activities, with an estimated elasticity of 0. 52
for WOM compared to 0.18 for marketing events. More
recently, Kim and Lee (2025) examined the effect of
online WOM on restaurant patronage in South Korea and
found that WOM volume (β = 0.31, p < 0.001) and
source credibility (β = 0.44, p < 0.001) signif icantly
predicted patronage intentions. In a meta -analysis of 67
studies on WOM effectiveness published between 2010
and 2024, You, Vadakkepatt, and Joshi (2025) found that
the average effect size of WOM on sales was 0.29, with
source credibility (r = 0.33) having a stronger effect than
volume (r = 0.25) or content richness (r = 0.23).
However, the meta-analysis drew primarily from studies
conducted in Western, individualistic cultures, raising
questions about generalisability to collectivist, high -
context cultures such as Northern Nigeria.
In Nigeria, Eze and Nwachukwu (2025) studied the
influence of word of mouth on consumer purchase
decisions in the informal retail sector of Enugu State.
Using a sample of 340 consumers and logistic
regression, the study f ound that WOM from family
members (OR = 2.91, p < 0.001) and close friends (OR
= 2.68, p < 0.001) was significantly more influential than
WOM from acquaintances or online reviews. The study
recommended that SMEs identify and cultivate
relationships with in fluential community members who
can serve as positive WOM ambassadors. However, the
study did not disaggregate WOM into the specific
dimensions of source credibility , volume, and content
richness.
More recently, Adeleke and Ogunyemi (2026)
conducted a stud y on WOM marketing and customer
retention among 400 SMEs in Ibadan, Oyo State. Using
multiple regression analysis, the study found that source
credibility (β = 0.41, p < 0.001), WOM volume (β = 0.29,
p < 0.001), and content richness (β = 0.33, p < 0.001) all
significantly predicted customer retention. The study
recommended that SMEs train their staff to encourage
satisfied customers to share detailed, specific
recommendations with their social networks.
The empirical gaps evident from this review are as
follows: (i) no study has simultaneously examined
source credibility, volume, and content richness as
predictors of customer patronage among SMEs in
Katsina metropolis; (ii) existing Northern Nigerian
studies have focused on Kano or Kaduna, not Katsina;
(iii) most studies have used purchase intention rather
than actual patronage as the dependent variable; (iv) the
most recent studies (2025 –2026) have not covered the
Katsina metropolis context; (v) there is limited
understanding of how collectivist cultural nor ms in
Katsina metropolis moderate the relationship between
WOM dimensions and patronage. The present study
addresses these gaps by providing a focused, rigorous
empirical investigation of word of mouth marketing and
customer patronage among SMEs in Katsina metropolis,
using the most current literature and contextualised data.

 3. METHODOLOGY
This study employed a quantitative cross -sectional
survey research design to investigate the effect of word
of mouth marketing on customer patronage among Small
and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) in Katsina metropolis,
Katsina State, Nigeria. The target population comprised
adult consumers (aged 18 years and above) who had
patronised at least one SME operating within the four
major commercial areas of Katsina metropolis: Kofar
Guga Market, Central Market (Kasuwar Shanu), Kofar
Kaura Market, and the New Market (Kasuwar Sabon
Gari). The total estimated consumer population within
these commercial areas was approximately 250,000
based on daily foot traffic estimates from the Katsina
State Market Management Board (2025). Using
Cochran's formula for sample size determination at a
95% confidence level and 5% margin of error, a
minimum sample of 384 respondents was determined.
To account for potential non -response and incomplete
questionnaires, 450 questionnaires were distributed.
A multi-stage sampling technique was employed: first,
the four major commercial areas were purposively
selected; second, within each market, systematic random
sampling was used to approach every tenth consumer
exiting the market during peak hours (10:00 am to 2 :00
pm and 4:00 pm to 6:00 pm) over a period of four weeks
(March 2026). A structured questionnaire comprising
three sections was developed: Section A measured
demographic characteristics (gender, age, educational
qualification, occupation, monthly income) ; Section B
measured the three dimensions of word of mouth
marketing (source credibility: 5 items adapted from
Hovland et al., 1953; volume of WOM: 5 items adapted
from Liu, 2006; content richness: 5 items adapted from
Herr et al., 1991) using a 5 -point Li kert scale ranging
from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree);
Section C measured customer patronage using 5 items
adapted from East, Hammond, and Lomax (2008). The
instrument was pre -tested on 50 consumers at a small
market in Dutsin-Ma, Katsina State (not among the four
selected markets), yielding Cronbach's alpha coefficients
of 0.86 for source credibility, 0.88 for volume, 0.84 for
content richness, and 0.87 for customer patronage,
indicating high internal consistency. The questionnaire
was administered physically in Hausa and English (with
respondents free to choose their preferred language) by
the researcher and five trained research assistants who
were fluent in both languages. Out of 450 questionnaires
distributed, 418 were returned, and after data cleaning
(screening for incomplete responses and inconsistent
answering patterns), 396 were validated for analysis,
representing a valid response rate of 88.0%. Data were
analysed using descriptive statistics (frequencies,
percentages, mean, standard deviation) and inferential
statistics (Pearson correlation and multiple linear
regression) with the aid of SPSS version 28. Hypotheses
were tested at a 0.05 significance level.

4.5 Discussion of Findings
The findings of this study provide empirical evidence
regarding the effect of word of mouth marketing on
customer patronage among SMEs in Katsina metropolis.
The discussion is organised around the three hypotheses
tested.
Effect of Source Credibility on Customer Patronage
The finding that source credibility has the strongest
significant positive effect on customer patronage (β =
0.328, p < 0.001) aligns with the predictions of Source
Credibility Theory (Hovland, Janis, & Kelley, 1953;
Pornpitakpan, 2004) and the Theory of Reasoned Action
(Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). According to these theories,
when consumers perceive the source of a
recommendation as knowledgeable (expertise) and
honest (trustworthiness), they are more likely to accept
the recommendation and act upon it. In the context of
Katsina metropolis, where social relationships are highly

GUSAU JOURNAL OF BUSINESS ADMINISTRATION (GUJOBA) | Vol. 5, Issue 1 | 2026
 379
valued and trust is central to economic exchange,
recommendations from credible sources -particularly
close family members, respected community elders, and
friends with demonstrated experience -carry significant
weight in shaping patronage decisions.
This finding is consistent with recent empirical studies.
Abubakar and Bello (2025) found that WOM credibility
(β = 0.43, p < 0.001) significantly predicted purchase
intention in Kano State. Similarly, Ibrahim and Sani
(2026) found that WOM from family members (β = 0.38,
p < 0.001) was the strongest predictor of purchase
decisions in Kaduna metropolis. The p resent study
extends these findings by demonstrating that source
credibility remains the most important WOM dimension
even when volume and content richness are controlled
for simultaneously. The high mean score for source
credibility (3.85, high range) ind icates that SMEs in
Katsina metropolis already benefit from a cultural
environment where community members are generally
perceived as credible recommenders. However, the
regression result suggests that further enhancing source
credibility-for example, by cultivating relationships with
influential community members, encouraging customer
testimonials from identifiable sources, and ensuring that
business practices justify the trust placed in
recommenders-would yield additional gains in customer
patronage.
Effect of Volume of WOM on Customer Patronage
The finding that volume of WOM significantly and
positively affects customer patronage (β = 0.254, p <
0.001) supports Social Exchange Theory (Blau, 1964;
Cropanzano & Mitchell, 2005) and the concept of social
proof (Cialdini, 2009). When consumers hear the same
positive recommendation from multiple sources or
repeatedly from the same source, they interpret this high
volume as evidence that the business is genuinely good.
The logic is probabilistic: if many independ ent people
are saying positive things about a business, it is unlikely
that they are all wrong. This social proof reduces
perceived risk and increases the consumer's confidence
in patronising the business.
This finding is consistent with Trusov, Bucklin, a nd
Pauwels (2009), who found that WOM referrals had a
significantly greater impact on customer acquisition than
traditional marketing activities, and with You,
Vadakkepatt, and Joshi (2025), whose meta -analysis
found an average effect size of 0.25 for WOM volume
on sales. In the Nigerian context, Ogunbiyi and Adeyemi
(2025) found that WOM volume had a significant effect
(β = 0.36, p < 0.001) on purchase intention in Lagos, and
Adeleke and Ogunyemi (2026) found a significant effect
(β = 0.29, p < 0.001) on c ustomer retention in Ibadan.
The present study confirms these findings in the Katsina
metropolis context, though the effect size (β = 0.254) is
slightly smaller than in some previous studies, which
may reflect the more collectivist and trust -based nature
of Katsina society, where the identity of the
recommender (source credibility) matters more than the
sheer number of recommendations (volume). The mean
score for volume of WOM (3.46, high range) indicates
that SMEs in Katsina are already benefiting from
reasonably high levels of community discussion and
recommendation. SMEs should therefore focus on
encouraging satisfied customers to share their positive
experiences with others, thereby increasing the volume
of favourable WOM.
Effect of Content Richness on Customer Patronage
The finding that content richness has a significant
positive effect on customer patronage (β = 0.214, p <
0.001) supports the elaboration likelihood model (Petty
& Cacioppo, 1986) and the concept of information
quality in consumer decision-making. Rich, detailed, and
specific recommendations provide consumers with the
information they need to form clear expectations,
compare alternatives, and make confident purchasing
decisions. Vague recommendations ("it's good") provide
little diagnostic value, while rich recommendations ("the
shop at Kofar Guga sells high-quality rice at ₦75,000 per
bag, and the seller, Alhaji Musa, allows you to inspect
before paying") enable consumers to act with certainty.
This finding is consistent with Herr, Kardes,  and Kim
(1991), who found that vivid, detailed WOM was more
persuasive than bland, generic WOM, and with You,
Vadakkepatt, and Joshi (2025), whose meta -analysis
found an average effect size of 0.23 for content richness.
In the Nigerian context, Eze and Nw achukwu (2025)
found that detailed WOM significantly predicted
purchase decisions in Enugu, and Adeleke and
Ogunyemi (2026) found a significant effect (β = 0.33, p
< 0.001) on customer retention in Ibadan. However, the
present study found that content richness had the weakest
effect (β = 0.214) among the three dimensions and also
had the lowest mean score (3.32, moderate range). This
indicates that while content richness does matter for
customer patronage in Katsina metropolis, it is currently

the least dev eloped dimension of WOM marketing.
Consumers report that the recommendations they receive
are often not as detailed or specific as they would like.
This represents a significant opportunity for SMEs: by
training staff to ask satisfied customers to share sp ecific
details about their positive experiences (what they
bought, how much they paid, what problem was solved,
how the service was delivered), SMEs can increase the
richness of WOM content and, consequently, customer
patronage.
Integrated Discussion
Taken together, the three dimensions of word of mouth
marketing explained 34.1% of the variance in customer
patronage in Katsina metropolis. This indicates that
WOM marketing is a substantial, but not exhaustive,
predictor of patronage. Other factors not examin ed in
this study -such as product quality, price
competitiveness, location convenience, customer service
quality, and brand reputation -likely account for the
remaining 65.9% of variance. Nonetheless, the finding
that all three dimensions are significant pre dictors
confirms the theoretical applicability of the Theory of
Reasoned Action, Social Exchange Theory, and Source
Credibility Theory to the WOM –patronage relationship
in the Northern Nigerian context.
The finding that source credibility has the strongest
effect (β = 0.328), followed by volume of WOM (β =
0.254) and then content richness (β = 0.214), has
practical implications for resource allocation. In Katsina
metropolis, where social relationships and trust are
paramount, SMEs should prioritise building  and
leveraging relationships with credible community
sources. This may be more effective than simply
encouraging large volumes of vague recommendations
or investing in content richness. However, the low mean
score for content richness (3.32) suggests that SMEs are
currently underperforming on this dimension, and
relatively modest investments in training customers to
share more detailed recommendations could yield
significant returns.
The findings also have theoretical implications. The
study confirms the a pplicability of Source Credibility
Theory to the Katsina metropolis context, demonstrating
that expertise and trustworthiness are powerful
determinants of WOM persuasiveness in collectivist,
high-trust societies. The study also supports Social
Exchange The ory, showing that high -volume WOM
from multiple exchange partners increases patronage.
Finally, the study extends the elaboration likelihood
model to the WOM context, showing that rich, detailed
content provides the cognitive basis for confident
purchasing decisions.

5. CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS
5.1 Summary of Findings
This study examined the effect of word of mouth
marketing on customer patronage among Small and
Medium Enterprises (SMEs) in Katsina metropolis,
Nigeria. Based on the analysis of data collected from 396
consumers across four major commercial areas (Kofar
Guga Market, Central Market, Kofar Kaura Market, and
New Market), the following key findings emerged.
First, the descriptive statistics revealed that source
credibility was rated high (m ean = 3.85), volume of
WOM was rated high (mean = 3.46), and content
richness was rated moderate (mean = 3.32). Customer
patronage was rated high (mean = 3.51). These findings
indicate that while SMEs in Katsina metropolis benefit
from reasonably high leve ls of credible and frequent
WOM recommendations, the recommendations are often
not as detailed or specific as consumers would prefer.
Second, the correlation analysis showed that all three
dimensions of word of mouth marketing had significant
positive corr elations with customer patronage, with
source credibility having the strongest correlation (r =
0.534), followed by volume of WOM (r = 0.468) and
content richness (r = 0.442).
Third, the regression analysis confirmed that all three
hypotheses were supporte d. Source credibility had the
strongest significant positive effect on customer
patronage (β = 0.328, p < 0.001). Volume of WOM had
a significant positive effect (β = 0.254, p < 0.001).
Content richness had a significant positive effect (β =
0.214, p < 0.0 01). The three dimensions jointly
explained 34.1% of the variance in customer patronage
(R² = 0.341, adjusted R² = 0.336, F = 67.723, p < 0.001).
5.2 Conclusion
This study concludes that word of mouth marketing
significantly affects customer patronage among SMEs in
Katsina metropolis. Specifically, the study concludes

that source credibility is the most critical dimension of
WOM marketing, followed by volume of WOM, with
content richness having the smallest but still significant
effect. The high mean score for source credibility (3.85)
and its strong effect on patronage (β = 0.328) indicate
that the trust -based, collectivist culture of Katsina
metropolis creates a favourable environment for WOM
marketing. Consumers trust recommendations from
family members, friends, and community elders, and
they act on those recommendations by patronising the
recommended businesses.
The study further concludes that while volume of WOM
is also important, its effect is weaker than source
credibility. This suggests that in the Katsina context, the
identity and trustworthiness of the recommender matter
more than the sheer number of recommendations. A
single recommendation from a trusted family elder may
be more influential than dozens of recommendations
from unknown or less trusted sources.
The study also concludes that content richness, while
significant, is the weakest dimension both in terms of its
mean score (3.32, moderate) and its effect on patronage
(β = 0.214). This indicates that SMEs in Katsina
metropolis have room for i mprovement in encouraging
customers to share detailed, specific, and vivid
recommendations. Consumers want to know not just that
a business is good, but what specific products are
available, at what prices, with what quality, and with
what level of customer service.
Finally, the study concludes that the Theory of Reasoned
Action, Social Exchange Theory, and Source Credibility
Theory are applicable to the WOM –patronage
relationship in the Northern Nigerian context. The
significant effects of source credibili ty (from Source
Credibility Theory), volume of WOM (from Social
Exchange Theory), and subjective norms (from TRA)
demonstrate that these theoretical frameworks travel
across cultural and geographical contexts.

5.4 Limitations and Avenues for Future Research
This study acknowledges several limitations. First, the
cross-sectional design permits the identification of
relationships but not causal inference. Future research
should employ longitudinal designs to track changes in
WOM dimensions and patronage over ti me, particularly
before and after SMEs implement targeted WOM
interventions. Second, the study relied on self -report
measures, which may be subject to common method
bias, social desirability bias, and recall bias (respondents
may not accurately remember th e volume or content of
WOM they have received). Future studies should
incorporate objective measures where possible, such as
tracking actual referral rates, and should consider using
dyadic data (matching recommenders with receivers) to
triangulate percept ions. Third, the study focused
exclusively on Katsina metropolis, which limits
generalisability to other Northern Nigerian cities (such
as Kano, Kaduna, or Sokoto) with different market
structures and social dynamics, as well as to Southern
Nigerian cities  with different cultural norms. Future
research should replicate this study across multiple
Nigerian cities to enable regional comparisons.
Fourth, the study did not examine potential mediators
(e.g., perceived trust, perceived risk reduction, attitude
toward the business) or moderators (e.g., customer age,
education level, product involvement, cultural values
such as collectivism) that might explain variation in the
WOM–patronage relationship. Fifth, the study focused
on only three dimensions of WOM marke ting; future
research should include additional dimensions such as
WOM recency (how recently the recommendation was
received), WOM consistency (whether multiple
recommendations agree), and the mode of WOM
communication (face -to-face vs. voice note vs. text
message). Despite these limitations, this study provides
robust empirical evidence that word of mouth marketing,
particularly source credibility, significantly affects
customer patronage among SMEs in Katsina metropolis,
and the recommendations offered co nstitute actionable
strategies for SME owners, policymakers, and future
researchers.
