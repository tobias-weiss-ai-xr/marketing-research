# AI-DRIVEN STRATEGIC MARKETING, CUSTOMER INSIGHT CAPABILITY, AND FIRM PERFORMANCE: A CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK WITH A NONLINEAR TURBULENCE CONTINGENCY

Abstract
Artificial intelligence is now used across marketing research, segmentation, targeting, customer engagement and
resource allocation. Adoption alone, however, does not explain how firms turn analytical output into better
performance, or why returns vary acr oss markets. Prior research has linked analytics and AI capabilities to
performance. It has tested environmental moderators, but it has rarely separated the production of customer
understanding from the actions that follow, and its findings on turbulence a re inconsistent. This integrative review
develops a conceptual framework in which customer insight capability, defined as the organizational ability to turn
customer evidence into validated, shared understanding that can inform decisions, partly mediates the relationship
between AI -driven strategic marketing and firm performance. Market turbulence is placed on the insight -to-
performance path, and we propose that its moderating effect is curvilinear: the value of insight capability should
peak at intermediate turbulence and decline when customer evidence becomes outdated faster than the firm can act
on it. Defining that threshold relative to the firm’s insight -to-action cycle makes the boundary testable rather than
open-ended. It also predicts that faster -responding firms reach peak returns at higher levels of turbulence. Five
propositions and a corollary specify these relationships. The framework offers one explanation for the mixed
moderation results in market orientation and analytics research and sets out an agenda for longitudinal, quasi -
experimental and comparative work. The propositions are conceptual and have not been estimated. For managers,
the framework suggests judging AI by the quality and use of the customer insight it supports, by incremental
outcomes, and by how quickly decisions can be renewed as markets change.

Keywords
Artificial intelligence; strategic marketing; customer insight capability; market turbulence; firm performance;

1 Introduction
Artificial intelligence has become relevant for strategic marketing because it can help in making decisions on the
most valuable customers, the value to be offered to them, and the resources to be allocated —predictive Modelling
for Purchase Patterns and At trition Estimation. Arora et al. (2025) and Li et al. (2024) demonstrate that language
models can also simulate survey respondents and be used in qualitative research. Generative systems support
communication and help explore different value propositions. Huang and Rust (2021) categorize the applications of
AI according to marketing research, marketing strategy and action, and Davenport et al. (2020) develop implications
for marketing strategy, consumer behavior and responsible practice. One simple question  that arises from these
developments is: why do companies with broadly similar technologies have different marketing results? An
algorithm, data volume or automation will be the technology -based solution. The answer is not quite complete, as
in other organizations, the same system may result in very different decisions being made. One company might use
a model's results along with customer interviews and service history before repositioning the model. Another could
take a prediction for granted and not look into the assumptions or make it a campaign that customers find intrusive.
Both firms use AI. The difference lies in their ability to create customer understanding.
The distinction between information and insight is central here. Information describes observations. Insight is a
defensible interpretation of those observations that can inform a consequential decision. A list of customers with
high predicted attrition is information. Understanding that the attrition reflects an unresolved service problem in a
particular segment is insight. Whether the firm then acts on that understanding is a separate question, and keeping
it separate matters for the argument that follows . Customer insight capability concerns the organizational routines
through which customer evidence is interpreted, validated, shared and made available to decision makers. Existing
research already connects technology -related capabilities with performance.  Germann et al.  (2013) found that
deployment of marketing analytics was associated with higher return on assets, and that the association was
stronger in more competitive industries and where customer preferences changed quickly. Wamba et al.  (2017)
examined dynamic capabilities as a mechanism linking big data analytics capability to performance, Mikalef et
al. (2019) studied environmental conditions in a related model, and Mikalef and Gupta (2021) extended capability
reasoning to AI. This paper does not claim that the link between AI -related capability and performance is new. Its

contribution is narrower and, we argue, more testable. It makes three moves that distinguish it from the closest
prior models.
First, it separates three things that earlier models tend to combine: the strategic use of AI, the organizational
production of customer understanding, and the actions taken in response. Analytics and dynamic capability
constructs usually include items about acting on data, which makes it hard to tell whether firms fail because they
lack understanding or because they cannot act. Second, it locates market turbulence on the insight -to-performance
path and proposes a curvilinear rather than linear contingency. This matters because the evidence on turbulence is
inconsistent. Germann et al.  (2013) report amplification under fast -changing preferences, whereas Jaworski and
Kohli (1993) and the meta -analysis by Kirca et al. (2005) found little support for turbulence as a moderator of the
market orientation–performance relationship. A relationship that rises and then falls across turbulence levels could
produce both patterns, depending on the range a sample covers . Third, it defines the upper boundary of useful
turbulence relative to the firm’s insight-to-action cycle, turning a vague “too much change” caveat into a measurable
condition and predicting that faster-responding firms reach peak returns at higher levels of turbulence.
The review addresses three questions. How should AI-driven strategic marketing be distinguished from AI adoption
and routine automation? Through what mechanism might customer insight capability transmit the performance
implications of AI use? Under what co nditions should market turbulence strengthen or weaken the value of
customer insight? The aim is to build an explanation that can be tested, not to estimate the average return on AI
investment. In this respect, the paper follows the integrative marketing –performance reasoning of Morgan (2012).

2 Review approach and scope
The paper is an integrative narrative review with conceptual model development. Snyder (2019) distinguishes
review types by objective, and Jaakkola (2020) asks authors of conceptual articles to make their design and
reasoning explicit. Following both, the review combines theory synthesis with model building. It does not claim the
exhaustive retrieval, reproducible screening or quantitative aggregation of a systematic review or meta -analysis.
Sources were identified through keyword searches of scholarly sear ch engines and publisher platforms, and each
bibliographic record was checked against the publisher’s page or an institutional repository. Search themes covered
artificial intelligence and strategic marketing, marketing analytics and performance, customer insight and customer
experience, market orientation, marketing agility, organizational knowledge, and environmental turbulence. A
second round of targeted searches during revision in October 2026 looked specifically for two bodies of work:
empirical tests of environmental moderation in market orientation, analytics and capability research, and marketing
research using large language models. Foundational publications were retained where they define the constructs
used here. The cited literature spans 1990 to 2025.
Sources were included if they had a contribution to a construct definition, mechanism, boundary condition or the
review design. It includes conceptual papers, reviews, organizational surveys, archival studies, and customer -level
experiments. For each sourc e, only claims that could be substantiated in the published article are cited. This is a
purposive selection, and no database export, duplicate -removal count or formal quality score is reported. The
analysis progressed from definitions to mechanisms to com peting explanations. It separated AI resources from
strategic application and customer data from customer insight capability. It then inquired into which of the
organizational routines might bridge these constructs with performance and whether turbulence increases the value
of insight, decreases the reliability of insight, or does both in varying quantities. Lastly, it translated the reasoning
into a set of simple propositions. Purposive selection may lead to the exclusion of conflicting evidence and the
selection of existing theories. To minimize this risk, this revision specifically looked for null and contrary moderation
results, which are discussed in Section 5.6. The studies included here are also analyzed at different levels, making it
impossible to draw firm-level conclusions from customer-level responses or general analytics capabilities from firm-
level conclusions to support the analytics mediation of the proposed marketing.

3 Construct clarification
3.1 Unit of analysis
The framework is specified at the level of the strategic business unit: the firm in single -business companies, or the
business unit in diversified companies. Strategic marketing choices, customer markets and performance outcomes
are usually defined at this level, and turbulence in one product market may not describe a whole diversified group.
For brevity, the paper uses “firm performance” throughout, but every construct should refer to the same unit and
comparable time periods. Where only corporate-level outcomes are available, researchers need to justify how unit-
level effects aggregate.
3.2 AI-driven strategic marketing
Strategic marketing is the use of coordinated marketing analysis and decision -making processes using AI tools to
guide market selection, value proposition design, customer management and allocation of resources in marketing.

The use should be strategic, with a connection to organizational priorities and related decisions. If the decisions are
based on a chatbot or automated email schedule that is not connected to the rest of the system, it is not an isolated
solution and there fore not qualified. The definition relates to how AI is used in strategy development, rather than
whether decisions are made automatically. While AI capability is defined by Mikalef and Gupta (2021) as the
complementary resources that enable an organization to deploy AI effectively, the construct differs in that capacity
is not just about the resources an organization has; it is also about how well they can utilize AI tools to solve
problems. An AI company could have robust capabilities and use AI technology primarily for business operations
without impacting their marketing strategy. It is also in contrast to online marketing. Digital channels need not rely
on learning -based systems,  and AI can support pricing, portfolio, and distribution decisions outside digital
communication (Kannan & Li, 2017; Verhoef et al., 2021). Strategic intensity should then be based on the extent of
the use of AI, its integration and the relevance of the de cisions made, rather than on technology spending or the
number of software subscriptions or enthusiasm among managers.
Predictive and generative systems need to be distinguished. Predictive AI makes predictions or patterns from data.
With generative AI, content is generated, and candidate interpretations are produced. This trend in consumer
research, as well as the implica tions arising from this shift to consumer research, is explored by Hermann and
Puntoni (2024), Grewal et al. (2025) and Kshetri  et al. (2024), respectively. The most relevant development is the
use of large language models within marketing research itself for this framework. For brand similarity and attribute
ratings, Li et al. (2024) identified agreement between perceptual data f rom the models (more than three -quarters
of the time) and data from human surveys. Human researchers and language models can complement each other in
qualitative and quantitative research, enhancing efficiency and depth, though Arora et al. (2025) emphasize the need
for strong human supervision. The research reveals that Generative AI has now permeated beyond just the
communication of customer understanding to its creation. They also show the importance of checking evidence for
fluent output before it informs strategy.
3.3 Customer insight capability
Customer insight capability is an organization’s repeatable ability to turn customer evidence into validated, shared
understanding that can inform decisions. We propose four dimensions: integrating relevant evidence, interpreting
what it means for customer s, validating interpretations, and making insight accessible to decision makers. These
dimensions form an analytical definition, not a validated scale. The concept is not new to marketing. Said et
al. (2015) studied how four organizations generate and use customer insight and found that insight takes many
forms, comes from varied sources, and requires more than dissemination to be used. Their attention to how insight
is formatted, partly automated and actively steered through the organization supports the p rocess view adopted
here. Day (1994) earlier identified market sensing and customer linking as distinctive capabilities of market-driven
firms, and McColl-Kennedy et al. (2019) show how qualitative and quantitative experience data can be combined to
identify root causes. The contribution here is not the concept itself but its placement between strategic AI use and
performance, and the exclusion of action from its scope.
Evidence integration concerns connecting transactional, behavioral, service and qualitative data (Balducci &
Marinova, 2018; Erevelles  et al., 2016). Interpretation concerns explaining patterns in terms of customer needs,
constraints and experiences. Validation concerns whether an interpretation is reliable enough for the decision at
hand. Accessibility concerns whether the relevant managers can find and understand the insight when they need it.
Changes to prices, products or service delivery are downstream uses of insight. They are deliberately excluded from
the construct so the mediator does not include the outcomes it is meant to expla in. Insight capability also differs
from data quality, analytical sophistication and customer orientation. Accurate data can go unused. Sophisticated
analysis can reveal complex patterns without explaining their commercial meaning. Customer orientation expresses
priorities, whereas insight capability describes the routines that produce usable knowledge. Keeping these apart
avoids a model in which several variables are different labels for the same favorable organizational trait.
3.4 Market turbulence
Market turbulence is the speed and volatility of the changes in customer preferences, customer composition and
purchasing behavior in the market served by the unit. It is different from technological turbulence and competitive
intensity (Jaworski & Kohli, 1993). A market may be very competitive but with few customers' needs changing
quickly; and the other way round is possible too: a few customers may be involved in a stable market, but having
their priorities change rapidly.
For the curvilinear argument presented in Section 6, the turbulence should be compared with the organization's
velocity. We thus introduce another relative measure: the ratio of the rate at which customer evidence becomes stale
and the time that elapses between the receipt of a customer signal and the marketing action taken, the so -called
insight-to-action cycle time. Turbulence is “excessive” for a particular firm when there is more evidence of
turbulence than the firm can validate and act upon. Both of them can be measured without the performance. Segment

3.5 Firm performance
Firm performance includes financial and market results that are the measure of the effectiveness of organizational
activity. The financial indicators are profit, revenue growth, and return on marketing investment. Retention,
acquisition efficiency and relative market position are market indicators. This outcome should be distinguished from
process outcomes like reporting speed or prediction accuracy as outlined in the marketing productivity agenda of
Rust et al. (2004). A faster analysis can contribute to p erformance, but it may not prove it. Performance is multi -
dimensional and time -varying. A short -term surge in sales may be accompanied by reduced margins, excessive
discounting, or later discontent. Evaluation questions should therefore include whether the re is incremental value
after implementation and operating costs of AI -informed marketing, and empirical applications should identify the
expected outcome change and explain why.

4 Theoretical foundations
4.1 Resource-based reasoning
The resource-based view focuses on the differences in performance as a result of the differences in the resources,
which are valuable, rare, hard to imitate and not easily substituted (Barney, 1991). When thinking about AI-powered
marketing, it implies that popular software alone will not be the key to a lasting advantage. Similar technologies may
create different value because of proprietary customer evidence, the collective wisdom of the customer,
synchronized procedures and established customer relations hips. Germann et al. (2013) followed this reasoning,
adding the upper echelons theory to account for returns to marketing analytics. This paper suggests that insight
capability can complement AI as an organizational capability. A capability -building argume nt is presented by
Vorhies and Morgan (2005), who describe how firms develop marketing capabilities through learning. Competitors
may be able to purchase similar software but may struggle to create a similar process for identifying, validating, and
sharing customer knowledge. But that is not necessarily a positive attribute, and it's not impregnable to being
duplicated. Its worth is determined by the ability to enhance decisions and the relevance of its routines in the face
of changing markets.
4.2 Dynamic capabilities
Dynamic capabilities are related to organizational renewal under changing conditions (Teece et al., 1997) and
involve largely the capacity to sense, seize and reconfigure (Teece, 2007). Sensing is the major component of
customer insight capability. The ability to act is also treated as a separate construct, as it is related to other capacities
that are needed to seize and reconfigure, as captured in the framework as insight -to-action cycle time. Eisenhardt
and Martin (2000) suggest that in moderately and highly dynamic markets, dynamic capabilities manifest in various
ways. The routines are simpler, more experiential and less predictable in the outcome in moderately dynamic
markets.
In contrast, in high -velocity markets they become simpler, more experiential and less predictable in outcome. A
similar contingency test was conducted by Schilke (2014), and they showed that the relationship between dynamic
capabilities and competitive adv antage was strongest in the intermediate range of environmental dynamism, but
weakest in the extremes. While the finding is not directly applicable to the curvilinear proposition that follows, it is
the nearest empirical analogue for it, as it relates to alliance and product development capabilities, rather than
customer insight.

4.3 Market orientation and organizational knowledge
Market orientation links the generation, dissemination and use of market intelligence to organizational behavior
(Kohli & Jaworski, 1990), and Narver  and Slater (1990) connected it to profitability. Customer insight capability
adapts the generation and dissemination parts of this logic to a setting where AI now produces or processes much
of the evidence. It is narrower than market orientation, which al so includes organization -wide responsiveness.
Knowledge-based reasoning explains why this conversion depends on coordination. Grant (1996) emphasizes
knowledge integration within the firm, and Cohen and Levinthal (1990) link the use of external knowledge t o prior
related knowledge. Marketing managers need enough domain knowledge to question AI outputs and enough
analytical literacy to understand their limits. Systems widen access to evidence, but the organization still has to
recognize its significance and combine it with expertise that the data do not contain. The theories play distinct roles.
Resource-based reasoning explains differences in capability endowments. Dynamic capabilities explain renewal
under change and why their value may depend on the pace o f change. Market orientation and knowledge -based
reasoning explain the movement from evidence to shared understanding.

5 Critical synthesis of the literature
5.1 From AI applications to strategic decisions
The focus of AI in marketing research has shifted from discussing specific tools to the integration of AI applications
with marketing strategies and decisions. Huang and Rust (2018) examine AI in service, while Huang and Rust (2021)
link AI to marketing re search, strategy, and action. The field is extremely varied as evidenced in the reviews by
Verma et al. (2021), Mustak et al. (2021), and Mariani et al. (2022). The scope of the agenda has again expanded with
the advent of generative AI: The use of generative AI has expanded to content production, personalization and
customer engagement (Kshetri et al., 2024) and to using language models as research tools (Arora et al., 2025; Li et
al., 2024). This literature sets the breadth. It ’s not so much about the organizational process which generates the
returns for the firm. Decision support and decision quality are two important concepts.
While AI can reduce the expense of considering numerous options, it can also perpetuate the wrong objective. De
Bruyn et al. (2020) speak of these challenges, along with knowledge creation and transfer challenges. A targeting
system that optimizes for immediate conversions might prioritize customers who would have purchased without it.
A content system can generate content rapidly without much variation. These are illustrative scenarios and not
reported cases but demonstrate the need for a capability of assessing customer meaning and commercial relevance
in the framework. Interactions between decisions are also elements of strategic use. Targeting, positioning, pricing
and channel selection must work together, and an algorithm that enhances one aspect of the process could affect the
others – for instance, by making a product more visible to promotion -sensitive customers who may otherwise not
have viewed it at such low prices. The framework thus focuses on integrated use of AI and clear decisions on what
is beyond management control.
5.2 Analytics capability and organizational outcomes
Analytics research provides the closest empirical evidence for capability-based explanations. Germann et al. (2013)
surveyed senior executives in Fortune 1000 firms. They found that greater deployment of marketing analytics was
associated with higher retur n on assets, with stronger associations in more competitive industries and where
customer preferences changed quickly. Wamba et al.  (2017) examined dynamic capabilities as a mechanism
between big data analytics capability and performance. Mikalef  et al. (2019) studied analytics capability, dynamic
capabilities and innovation under different environmental conditions, and Akter et al.  (2016) highlighted the
alignment between analytics capability and business strategy. Mishra et al.  (2022), using text from annual reports,
found that firms’ AI focus was associated with higher net operating efficiency and profitability. That result is
consistent with a direct efficiency pathway that does not run through customer understanding.

These comparisons have a methodological consequence. Analytical capacity is typically broad, including
infrastructure, technical skills, and managerial use. One knowledge process is related to customer insight capability.
An apparent mediation might be the result of duplicated constructs if the two are measured using similar constructs
related to effective data use and better decisions. A defensible test should include its own measures of strategic AI
use, validated customer understanding, response velocity , and performance. It should be skeptical of strong
associations based on a single respondent. According to Wedel and Kannan (2016), marketing analytics is in a long
tradition of the research into resource allocation, personalization and privacy. This paper asks the question of which
routines are useful in making strategic customer decisions based on their analytical output. A Prediction can make
an Allocation Rule work better. Still, the firm might not understand why the customers are responding, and an
Interpretation can help to deepen the understanding without being a good predictor. It is not assumed that
predictive accuracy goes hand-in-hand with explanatory understanding and financial value.
5.3 Customer experience and actionable understanding
Customer Experience research highlights the interpretation of evidence in interactions and over time. Lemon and
Verhoef (2016) arrange CX along the journey, and McColl -Kennedy et al. (2019) integrate the use of experience
analysis with managerially relevan t insight. A signal can have different meanings depending on where and when it
appears in the relationship, and can be communicated through a variety of actors, including products and employees.
Patterns identified by AI, therefore, require interpretation in the context of the image. Negative language in feedback
can be related to delivery issues, lack of clarity about the use of a product or dissatisfaction with the communication.
All of these are treated as the same sentiment, which masks the specific ans wer each one needs. Patterns can be
interpreted with qualitative evidence and knowledge gained on the frontline, but managerial intuition should be
checked.
5.4 Customer responses and responsible marketing
Customer-level research warns against equating personalization with benefit. Aguirre et al.  (2015) show that how
information is collected and whether trust -building cues are present shape reactions to personalized advertising.
Puntoni et al. (2021) examine consumer experiences of AI across data capture, classification, delegation and social
interaction. Acceptance of algorithmic advice is context-dependent: Dietvorst et al. (2015) document aversion after
observed errors, Logg et al.  (2019) identify conditions favoring algorithmic judgment, Longoni and Cian (2022)
show that utilitarian and hedonic goals shape responses to AI recommendations, and Luo et al. (2019) find in a field
experiment that disclosing a chatbot reduced purchases. These studies operate at the customer level, but they give
plausible reasons why technically successful applications meet resistance. The framework treats responsible data
practice as an en abling condition for insight capability. Understanding obtained through practices customers find
unacceptable can damage the relationships on which future evidence depends, and it can exclude groups whose
behavior is poorly represented in the data. Governa nce should cover evidence provenance, representativeness,
permitted uses and the consequences of acting on a classification.
5.5 Adaptation and response speed
Day (2011) discusses adaptive marketing capabilities, including market learning and experimentation, and
Kalaignanam et al.  (2021) develop the concept of marketing agility and its antecedents. Both separate detecting
change from responding to it. An organi zation may produce insight quickly but lack the authority, budget or
coordination to change course. When the response window is short, faster sensing has limited value. This is the
reason the framework treats insight -to-action cycle time as a distinct cons truct rather than folding it into insight
capability.
5.6 Mixed evidence on turbulence as a moderator
The evidence on turbulence as a moderator is less settled than often assumed. Jaworski and Kohli (1993) found that
the market orientation–performance relationship held across levels of market turbulence, competitive intensity and
technological turbulence. Kirca et al. (2005) reached a similar conclusion in their meta -analysis: only a minority of
studies found that market turbulence strengthened the relationship, and sign tests did not support competitive
intensity or technological turbulence as moderators. By contrast, Germann et al. (2013) found that rapidly changing

One reading of this record is that turbulence does not matter. Another, which this paper develops, is that most
studies tested a linear interaction when the underlying relationship is curvilinear. If the value of a sensing capability
rises with turbulence up to a point and then falls, a sample concentrated in the low -to-moderate range would show
amplification, a sample spanning the full range could show little net interaction, and a sample concentrated in highly
turbulent markets could show attenuation. Thi s explanation is offered as a conjecture to be tested. Measurement
differences and construct breadth could also account for the inconsistent results.

6.1 AI-driven strategic marketing and customer insight capability
AI can be strategically applied to help with insight capability by providing a broader evidence base, more frequent
analysis, and identifying patterns that should be explored. It can correlate the service feedback with behavioral
change or segments that do not match the existing classifications. LMs can also provide an interview guide, generate
open-ended answers, and suggest possible explanations for the answers (Arora et al., 2025). All of these reduce the
cost of interpretation production. Whether this e nhances understanding depends on whether the organization
affirms the interpretation and can use it.
The proposed relationship is thus based on access to evidence, analytical skills, and routines to challenge outputs. If
they are not present, the number of unexamined claims could just be ramped up. With generative systems, this risk
is acute, since their output, whether it is correct or incorrect, is fluent. Organizational design is also important.
Shrestha et al. (2019) explain examples of integrated human and AI decisions, and Raisch and Krakowski (2021)
elaborate on the conflict between automation and augmentation. Both allow specific accountability for interpreting,
validating, and escalating analytical results.
P1: AI-driven strategic marketing is positively associated with customer insight capability when AI-supported evidence
is integrated with organizational routines for interpretation and validation.
6.2 Customer insight capability and firm performance
Customer insight capability can improve performance by helping managers identify profitable needs, avoid
misdirected spending and find the causes of dissatisfaction. A validated explanation of attrition may direct resources
toward an underlying service problem rather than repeated incentives. Understanding the different reasons behind
price sensitivity may lead to a better -matched product or channel offer. The mechanism is better decisions, not the
possession of more information. Insight affects performance only if the firm acts on it, so this relationship depends

on how quickly and how well the firm responds. Rather than building that condition into the proposition, the

P2: Customer insight capability is positively associated with subsequent firm performance.
6.3 Partial mediation through customer insight capability
The combination of P1 and P2 suggests an inverse relationship. AI -driven strategic marketing creates and supports
customer understanding, and that understanding influences decisions that affect performance. The mediator —the
customer insight capability —is the step that describes the knowledge conversion between AI -supported strategic
activity and business outcomes. It is hoped that mediation will be partial. Not everything good about AI requires
better customer understanding: AI can reduce processing costs, and allocation systems can improve efficiency by
leveraging existing customer knowledge within the firm. These kinds of correlations are reported by Mishra and his
colleagues (2022), and task -level experiments demonstrate productivity improvements from gen erative AI in
professional writing (Noy & Zhang, 2023) and customer support (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025). AI is not the only way
to gain insight, either; research and customer interaction can do the trick, too. The model thus maintains the direct
path in addition to the indirect path.
P3: Customer insight capability partially mediates the relationship between AI -driven strategic marketing and
subsequent firm performance.
6.4 Market turbulence as a curvilinear second-stage moderator
Turbulence should affect how much performance value a given level of insight capability creates. In stable markets,
existing knowledge stays useful for longer, so continuous updating adds less. As preferences change faster, firms
with strong insight capabi lity are better placed to notice emerging needs and revise established choices, and
outdated assumptions become more costly for firms without that capability. This is consistent with the amplification
reported by Germann et al.  (2013). The same reasoning i mplies a limit. When change is very rapid, evidence loses
relevance quickly, the time available to respond shrinks, and implementation capacity is overwhelmed. An insight
can be valid when produced and outdated when acted on. Eisenhardt and Martin (2000) a rgue that in high-velocity
markets capability outcomes become less predictable, and Schilke (2014) found weaker capability –advantage
associations under high dynamism. We therefore propose that the moderating effect is curvilinear rather than linear.
P4: The positive association between customer insight capability and subsequent firm performance follows an inverted-
U pattern across levels of market turbulence: it is strongest at intermediate turbulence and weaker at low and very high
turbulence.
6.5 Response speed and the location of the peak
P4 alone leaves open where the peak lies, making it easy to explain away any result. The definition in Section 3.4
addresses this. The decline in the value of insight should begin when customer evidence goes out of date faster than
the firm can validate an  interpretation and act on it. That point depends on the firm. An organization with a short
insight-to-action cycle can keep using insight under faster change than one that takes months to respond. This gives
a prediction that does not appear in earlier ca pability models and that distinguishes the present argument from a
general claim that “too much change is harmful”.
P5: The level of market turbulence at which the insight–performance association peaks is higher for firms with shorter
insight-to-action cycle times.
6.6 Conditional indirect relationship
If P1 holds and turbulence changes the performance value of insight capability (P4), the indirect effect of AI -driven
strategic marketing on performance should also vary with turbulence. This follows from P1 and P4 and is stated as
a corollary rather than a separate proposition.
Corollary: The indirect relationship between AI -driven strategic marketing and subsequent firm performance through
customer insight capability is strongest at intermediate market turbulence.

strategic marketing, M customer insight capability, W centered market turbulence and Y subsequent performance,

6.7 Rival explanations and boundary conditions
Several alternative possibilities might explain the suggested connections. High -performing companies might also
invest more in AI and customer research, which could lead to reverse causality. All the organizational variables could
be explained by leadership quality or already existing marketing competence. AI is not the primary driver of digital
maturity, as it can enhance evidence availability without it. The intensity of competition or the changing technology
can have an impact that is misinterpreted as c ustomer-market turbulence. Mixed moderation results without any
curvilinearity may be due to differences in measurement, as noted in Section 5.6. These alternatives should not only
be presented in a limitations section, but should affect the empirical desi gn.
The model includes no moderators, but rather boundary conditions: data representativeness and managerial
expertise and governance. Testing the model would be difficult if it included all of them. They identify assumptions
that have to be tested in empirical applications.

7 Theoretical and managerial implications
7.1 Theoretical implications
The first implication is a more precise account of where AI-related strategic value comes from. Separating the use of
AI in marketing decisions from the capacity to produce validated customer understanding explains why firms with
similar technical resource s can get different returns. It also lets researchers compare firms with similar tools but
different interpretive and coordination routines.
The second concerns capability boundaries. Restricting insight capability to the production and accessibility of
understanding, and measuring response speed separately, keeps the mediator from containing the actions it is
meant to explain. It also opens a richer process model in which AI-informed strategy supports insight, insight guides
adaptation, and adaptation affects outcomes.
The third is a curvilinear view of environmental change, with the threshold defined relative to the firm. Turbulence
raises the need for current understanding but also shortens its useful life. Treating these two effects as operating
together, rather than choosing between them, gives one possible explanation for the inconsistent moderation
findings in market orientation and analytics research, and P5 provides a way to check that explanation.
7.2 Managerial implications
Managers should start from a strategic decision that needs better customer understanding, such as which segment
deserves investment, why customers are leaving, or whether a revised value proposition will work. The evidence
required and the acceptable level of uncertainty should follow from that decision. Choosing systems before defining
the decision problem tends to produce attractive dashboards with no clear route to value. Organizations need a
practical process for validating insight. Analysts can identif y patterns, frontline staff can explain them in context,
and managers can test their commercial implications. This requires shared definitions and clear responsibility for
accepting or rejecting an interpretation. Validation should include looking for disc onfirming evidence and
comparing against simpler explanations. Where generative tools summarize customer evidence, high -impact
decisions should rest on summaries that can be traced back to the underlying data. Performance should be judged
on incremental outcomes. Managers can compare AI-informed decisions with the existing approach, a holdout group
or a staged rollout, using metrics that match the decision objective, such as contribution margin, retained customer
value or acquisition efficiency. More content, faster responses, or higher predictive accuracy indicate operation al

In relatively stable markets, less frequent updating may be enough.

8 Future research agenda
8.1 Construct development and measurement
The priority is to develop measures that keep the framework’s variables apart. Items for AI -driven strategic
marketing should capture integration into market selection, positioning, relationship strategy and resource
allocation. Items for customer insight capability should capture evidence integration, interpretation, validation and
accessibility. They should avoid statements such as “we produce insights that improve performance”, which build
the outcome into the mediator. Researchers should also decide whe ther the dimensions form a composite or a
common-factor construct. Cycle time and evidence decay need their own measures, ideally drawn partly from
records rather than perceptions. Qualitative interviews and expert review can refine the construct domain be fore
survey work. Different informants are likely needed, since executives can report strategic integration while analysts
and customer -facing managers see insight routines. Measurement invariance should be established before
comparing sectors or countries.
8.2 Temporal ordering and causal identification
For a longitudinal design, use a measure of the AI-driven strategy before an incremental increase in insight capability
and evaluate performance over a reasonably extended time. Multiwave  designs can help to control for temporal
ordering, but not for confounding. Archival outcomes and multiple informants minimize reliance on one manager's
evaluation; past performance, marketing skills, and investments should be kept constant. Quasi -experimental
comparison designs may be possible with staggered deployments. Still, the potential for comparison is limited by
the need to control for selection into adoption and the need to control for pre -existing trends. Field experiments
could help determine whether the effectiveness of the AI-informed decisions is improved when there is a validation
step, allowing for the distinction between the algorithm's contribution and the organizational interpretation of the
decision. Assumptions remain central to the me diation claims, as mediation is not the random assignment of a
mediator.
8.3 Turbulence and context
For tests of P4 and P5, samples must cover a broad spectrum of turbulence; otherwise, a curvilinear relation can not
be identified in a narrow range. Distinguish customer -market turbulence from technological turbulence and
competitive intensity, and keep market and unit definitions consistent. A comparison of the industries can
demonstrate if the peak is moving in line with purchase frequency, decision reversibility or implementation time.
The retailer who is making weekly promotions could hit their apex at a much steeper slope than the manufacturer
who is redesigning a product platform. Additionally, existing data sets with quadratic interaction terms could be re-
analyzed to see whether the interaction effect with mixed moderation reported in Section 5.6 is curvilinear. The
focus on emerging economies should be directed to the wide variation in data coverage, management resources, and
access to skills among firms, but not based on country labels. Business-to-business environments also play a role, as
customers' knowledge may be limited to a few account relationships, which are difficult to capture in data.
8.4 Generative AI, adverse outcomes and capability renewal
Failures as well as successes: poor segment classification, over -investment, too much discounting, customer
mistrust, and slow responses. With Generative AI, a particular concern is whether it is possible that valid but
unsupported interpretations can get into a decision process —and whether such validation routines can find them.
However, the emergence of language models that can predict survey answers (Li et al., 2024) and facilitate
qualitative studies (Arora et al., 2025) increases the need to ask this q uestion, as synthetic evidence can replace
actual customer contact. The study of problem detection and routine changes by the firms would take the model
beyond the first deployment.

9 Limitations and conclusion
This review elaborates the conceptual explanation through purposeful selection of the literature. It doesn't measure
effect sizes, show mediation, or find an empirically established turbulence threshold. The studies that it relies on
employ different const ructs and levels of analysis to varying degrees, making it difficult to compare them directly,
and the explanation for the mixed results of moderation is only a suggestion. The propositions are based on
assumptions regarding the quality of evidence, interp retation and implementation. The main argument is that the
strategic marketing value of AI partly stems from the capability for customer insight, which benefits from market
turbulence in a curvilinear fashion, linked to the speed of the firm's action. The value of technological output is added
to when firms can translate the output into validated understanding, which can guide viable decision -making. The
