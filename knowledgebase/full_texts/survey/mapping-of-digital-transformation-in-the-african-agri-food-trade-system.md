ABSTRACT
Background: Digital trade is an important component of modern food systems as it enhances efficiency, business
transactions, traceability and regulatory compliance. In the African continent, digital transformation of agri-food
systems is needed, as reflected in the continental initiatives to advance digital trade. Agriculture is a key driver
for economic development for the continent. However, there is currently no coherent synthesis of literature on
the state of digitalisation supporting agri-food trade in Africa.
Scope and approach: This study systematically reviews scientific and grey literature to examine how Africa's
digital landscape aligns with agri-food digital trade requirements. It maps trends, drivers, and barriers to digi-
talisation in the African agri-food sector, digital solutions, and technologies.
Key findings and conclusions: The analysis reveals steady growth, but several imbalances between countries, value
chains, and actors. The study identifies five categories: digital extension services, fintech, supply chain man-
agement, digital farm management and digital marketing, and the underlying technologies. Existing studies do
not provide a coherent digital trade framework based on compliance with trade and digital standards, which is
consistent with the fact that Africa's paperless and streamlined trade processes, the goal the continent is aspiring
to, are far from being realised. Digital transformation in Africa is occurring in a fragmented manner across
different domains rather than as part of an integrated digital ecosystem aligned with trade requirements and is
therefore insufficient to address fundamental barriers in Africa's agri-food trade systems. The findings suggest
that unlocking Africa's agri-food trade potential requires a systematic digital trade ecosystem approach.
1. Introduction
Africa plays a strategic role in global agri-food systems, accounting
for more than 60% of the worldwide uncultivated arable land, with
agriculture playing an essential role in economic development and
livelihoods across the continent (Choruma et al., 2024). Despite this
potential, Africa accounts for less than 5% of global agri-food trade,
being hindered by low value addition, productivity, and trade barriers
(Bou¨et & Odjo, 2019). However, African agri-food trade is largely sha-
ped by informal markets (i.e., decentralised and unregistered trade ex-
change), which often remain outside formal digital trade infrastructure
(Zougb´ed´e et al., 2026). As global trade increasingly shifts towards
digital paperless trade (i.e., adoption of electronic formats to expedite
trade processes), digital information exchange and compliance are
becoming essential for global trade participation (OECD, 2025).
Increased digitalisation in agri-food systems presents opportunities to
unlock Africa's trade potential by strengthening and complying with
regional and global trade integration (Duval et al., 2024; FAO, 2024a;
Tsan et al., 2019). Digital transformation (DT) refers to a profound
socio-technical change of key business operations that affects products,
processes, organizational structures and management concepts (Medina
et al., 2017). In agri-food systems, DT is changing food systems and
digital trade (i.e., sale of goods conducted over computer networks)
through digitalisation and standardisation, creating new opportunities
to advance food security, safety and trade through traceability, trade
compliance and authenticity, changing the way agriculture is being
practiced (Bernard, 2011; IMF et al., 2023; Matt et al., 2015; Vahdanjoo
et al., 2025; Wolfert et al., 2023). These developments are urgently
needed to cope with the increasing pressure on Africa's food systems.
This pressure is amplified by Africa's population growth, vulnerability to
climate change, and dependence on food imports. Recent disruptions to
global supply chain and grain markers during COVID-19 and the
Russia-Ukraine conflict highlight the need to strengthen resilience and
improve regional food integration in Africa (Asem - Hiablie et al., 2023;
FAO, 2024b). Currently, the continent is a net importer of agri-food
products compared to other global regions, constraining its food

accounts for less than 20% of the continental trade exchange annually,
offering potential to better align regional food production within the

continental initiatives, such as Agenda 2063, the Comprehensive Africa

Beyond these commitments, African agri-food exporters face several
non-tariff measures (NTMs), including sanitary and phytosanitary pro-

licensing requirements) trade frictions, affecting market access and

infrastructural constraints (e.g., agro-processing, hard infrastructure),
alongside market access restrictions (Mkansi et al., 2025; Sadeu et al.,

which are increasingly traded globally, show how digitalisation, and
thus improved information flow, enables trade and is expected to
continue growing (Jouanjean, 2019). In many African agri-food value
chains, limited digitalisation reduces transparency and information
sharing. This constrains trade and market opportunities, which
increasingly depend on rising information and traceability
requirements.
Global trade systems increasingly rely on digital systems to facilitate
exchange of information (Azmeh et al., 2020). Effective information
exchange among all actors is a critical component in trade; digitalisation
and standardisation assist in streamlining these processes (Pace &
Gephart, 2017). However, Africa's digital landscape to support trade
remains limited and fragmented, constraining trade and thus the
transformation of African economies (Choruma et al., 2024; Duval et al.,
2024; Tsan et al., 2019). Alignment with global developments - such as
the increasing adoption of global trade systems supporting cross-border
paperless trade - in which modernising and digitalisation support Afri-
can trade systems (OECD, 2025). Evidence suggests digitalisation im-
proves decision-making, productivity, revenue and market linkages,
while digital food standards enhance transparency, traceability and
compliance with trade standards. Additionally, access to continent-wide
and high-value external markets, which are a key source of revenue, is
negatively affected when the necessary information, documentation or
certification cannot be provided or made available in time (European
Commission, 2023; Hartmann et al., 2021). However, unequal access
to digitalisation could reinforce digital divide and power asymmetry
among stakeholders who are unable to adopt (Bahn et al., 2021; Sanusi
et al., 2025). Understanding agri-food trade requirements is therefore
critical to ensure compliance with regional and international markets.
Current literature focuses on parts of the digital landscape, including
specific countries, products, or technologies, but there is a scientific gap
in linking digitalisation with trade in Africa, despite increasing interest
in the topic given the recent regional commitments (Arthur et al., 2024;
FAO, 2024a; Tsan et al., 2019). This study addresses this gap by sys-
tematically synthesising academic literature on the digital trans-
formation of African agri-food trade systems, complemented by grey
literature. This review contributes by synthesising fragmented evidence
on Africa's digital landscape and what trends, drivers and barriers in-
fluence digitalisation, and how these developments relate to digital
agri-food trade. Consequently, there is a gap between the increasing
adoption of digital agri-food solutions and their integration into inter-
operable, cross-border digital trade systems, highlighting priorities for
future research and implementation.
To achieve this objective, this research addresses the following main
research question:
How does Africa's evolving digital landscape contribute to digital
trade of agri-food products?
This research is divided into the following sub-research questions.
1. What types of digital agricultural solutions and technologies are
currently being adopted to support agri-food trade in Africa?
2. What trends, drivers and barriers influence the transformation to-
wards digital trade systems in Africa?
The remainder of the paper is organised as follows: Section 2 offers a
theoretical background of trade-related digitalisation. In Section 3, the
methodological design of the systematic literature review is outlined in
detail. Section 4 presents the results and analysis of this study, followed
by the interpretation of the findings and potential future research di-
rections. In Section 5, a comprehensive discussion of the contributions
and limitations inherent to this paper is provided. To conclude, Section 6
summarised the key findings of this study.
2. Background
2.1. Trade as an economic development driver
Global trade is a key driver of international economies and revenue
streams for households, businesses, and nations. In Africa, expanding
agri-food trade is considered a strategic priority for driving economic
transformation, considering the continent's agricultural backbone.
However, the continent currently accounts for less than 5% of global
agri-food trade. Intra-African trade currently accounts for less than 20%
of Africa's annual agri-food exports, signalling significant potential for
collaboration and, thus, growth moving forward (FAO, 2024b;
Zougb´ed´e et al., 2026). As trade volume and markets increase, efficient
information exchange becomes a critical component (Bou¨et & Odjo,
2019).
The AfCFTA supports recent agreements on agri-food trans-
formation. Founded in 2018 and ratified by 54-African countries, it aims
to eliminate trade barriers, boost intra-African trade, and improve har-
monisation in the African market, thereby promoting commodity-based
industrialization and an internationally uniform African free trade area
(African Union, 2025). To achieve these objectives, uptake in digital-
isation (e.g., tools and technologies) and standardisation (streamline
processes such as reporting, and information exchange) to improve
agri-food production and facilitate trade, play an essential role in this
shift towards modernised agri-food systems, as shown by the AfCFTA
Digital Trade Protocol (DTP), Digital Agriculture Strategy 2023-2030
(DAS) and the recently renewed CAADP Strategy 2026-2035. The shift
towards paperless trade to automate and streamline trade processes -
thus reducing transaction costs, time, and improving revenue - has
received increased attention in recent trade strategy development plans
in the EU and Africa, such as the AfCFTA DTP (Duval et al., 2024).
2.2. Digitalisation and paperless trade
Digitalisation of trade-related processes, the so-called paperless trade
(PT), though not mandatory, is a de facto standard for efficient cross-
border trade, efficient information exchange, and can minimise trans-
action costs (OECD, 2025). PT is dependent on the digital landscape,
encompassing of the environment created by digital technologies, ser-
vices, and networks, which enable the required interaction among
actors.
Paperless trade involves five key steps: (1) digitalisation in trade-
related documents, (2) trade processes, (3) adoption of digital technol-
ogies (e.g., Electronic Data Interchange EDI), (4) standardisation of data
elements (e.g., GS1, UN/CEFACT standards), and (5) regulatory and
legal frameworks supporting PT (OECD, 2025). The state of paperless
and cross-border paperless trade, however, remains at an early stage
globally, with the lowest adoption globally of cross-border paperless
trade (e.g., interoperability between national trade systems), as reported

To facilitate trade, shipments of goods, alongside information ex-
change about the produce, play a crucial role in ensuring efficient ex-
change of goods and monetary funds between actors before it reaches
the end consumers (European Commission, 2025). In the African
context, intermediaries such as middlemen, brokers, cooperatives and
auctions play a significant role in facilitating agri-food trade (e.g., cash
crops such as coffee and tea) between countries and continents (Archer
& Elliott, 2021; Canwat, 2023). In the case of international trade,
(smallholder) farmers sell their produce to these facilitating actors, who
in turn trade with international procurement actors or via direct export
contracts. Depending on the destination country, the produce, and the
country of origin, (inter)national food laws and customs regulations
require specific documentation to be included in the shipment. For
instance, the following documentation must be provided before a
product enters the EU common market (European Commission, 2025).
1. Business registration and legal certificates issued in the com-
pany's country of origin.
2. Export licenses issued by the domestic authority in charge of trade-
related matters, such as the South African Revenue Service (SARS) or
Rwanda's National Agricultural Export Development Board (NAEB).
3. Certificates of Origin and Quality verifying the production loca-
tion and compliance with food quality standards.
4. Sanitary and Phytosanitary (SPS) certificates confirming food
safety and animal/plant health compliance.
5. Customs documentation, including a commercial invoice, pack-
aging list, bill of lading, and the previously mentioned certificates.
6. Sharing of relevant Business-to-Business (B2B) information
accompanying the physical goods flow; facility and product master
data as well as transactional data of shipping (e.g., receiving, storage,
goods handling, transport, sales).
The required data exchange for cross-border trade can be structured
into three data elements to enable digital exchange of information
among agri-food actors (GS1, 2017).
1. Master data: source of common business data used for all systems,
applications, and processes of an entire company.
2. Transactional data: a record of business transactions (e.g., order,
invoice, and dispatch advice, upon completion of a transfer of
ownership).
3. Visibility event data: records of the completion of business process
steps involving physical or digital entities. Each event captures
which objects and subjects participated in the process, when the
process took place, where the objects were and will be afterward, and
why (i.e., in what business context the process occurred).
2.3. Role of digitalisation and standardisation in trade participation
Digitalisation and standardisation provide the foundation for inter-
operable (trade) systems in relation to efficient exchange of agri-food-
specific information (OECD, 2025). These interoperable systems
enable interconnected digital networks and frameworks (i.e., ecosys-
tems) of agri-food actors across supply chains, who communicate
through a universal language. These digital trade ecosystems offer po-
tential improvements by enabling the trade of goods, services and data
using digital technologies, streamlining processes and collaboration to
conduct trade faster, more securely, and more efficiently (Jouanjean,
2019). Digital technologies make previously considered financially
and technically infeasible options (e.g., access to information, markets,
services) now possible, particularly in developing countries, leading the
way for a fundamental change in the way agriculture is practiced
(Trendov et al., 2019). Nevertheless, the diverse and heterogeneous
nature of food production poses challenges to the effective exchange of
food-related information required for agri-food trade (FAO, 2017).
While digitalisation enables the creation and exchange of information,
standardisation addresses this issue by establishing a common bench-
mark, set of rules, and thus a common communication language, to
facilitate trade.
Internationally recognised standards offer regulations and guidelines
to establish a central reference point, providing a universal language
that allows different technologies, economies and systems to intercon-
nect and is thus a crucial element in cross-border trade (FAO, 2017).
This removes technical trade barriers and improves trade efficiency by
establishing a single, accepted set of rules on, for example, testing
methods, certification, and document requirements at customs. Addi-
tionally, this common set of rules benefits (food) safety and the envi-
ronment. The Codex Alimentarius, established by the World Health
Organisation and the FAO in 1963, is an example of a global collection of
food safety standards, guidelines and code of practices (FAO & WHO,
2025). For example, the HACCP framework encompasses guidelines
for setting up a control and assurance system for food safety hazards in
food production processes and is increasingly required for domestic and
international food trade (FAO & WHO, 2023). HACCP identifies critical
control points affecting food safety. These can be systematically mapped
by their severity and likelihood within the production process. Digi-
tizing the required test procedures and the associated data collection
streamlines the necessary information exchange for facilitating
agri-food trade. Additionally, the Electronic Data Interchange (or EDI
for short) allows an efficient exchange between trading partners of these
documents, for example, to follow a standardised digital format such as
XML or EDIFACT, which are compatible with both parties’ information
systems (UNECE, 2025). As trade increasingly becomes more connected
and dependent on digital information systems, digitalisation and
standardisation to guide an efficient flow of information exchange in-
crease throughout the trade ecosystem.
3. Methodology
A systematic literature review (SLR) was conducted, following
Kitchenham et al., with PRISMA used for transparency and reporting
purposes (see Fig. 1) (Kitchenham & Charters, 2007). Grey sources from
reputable organisations (e.g., African Union, United Nations) com-
plemented the academic sources. The review process consisted of
forming relevant research questions (as described before), search
criteria, search strategy, data extraction method, and analysis and
reporting of the findings. Duplicate records (n = 9) across the four da-
tabases were identified based on DOI and title matching and were sub-
sequently removed while retaining one copy for review. The primary
screening and data extraction were performed by one researcher, with
two additional researchers involved in the screening selection, thematic
categorisation, and eligibility assessment during the SLR process. Dif-
ferences in judgement between the researchers were resolved through
discussion and consensus. Further details on the data extraction, eligi-
bility assessment and search queries are provided in the supplementary
material. The search strategy was designed to maximise the identifica-
tion of potential sources covering digital agri-food trade in Africa given
the broad and interdisciplinary nature of the topic. In total, the search
consisted of eight iterations, with exportation of the sources done
through the database-specific export functionality in the final iteration.
Sources in this study include 52 articles deemed eligible across
Scopus (n = 15), Web of Science (n = 3), IEEE Xplore (n = 4) and Sci-
enceDirect (n = 30), with additional reports from reputable grey sour-
ces, including the Technical Centre of Agricultural and Rural
Cooperation (CTA), Organisation for Economic Co-operation and
Development (OECD), Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and the
United Nations (UN) to complement the academic findings (excluded
from the SLR and Results section). English-language written sources

4. Results
This section presents an overview of the studies included in the re-
view, followed by the empirical findings. The literature search, con-
ducted using the broadest possible search string, yielded relatively few
primary studies explicitly focusing on digitalisation in African agri-food
trade systems. Therefore, studies were included that addressed the
foundational building blocks of digitalisation (e.g., infrastructure,
technologies, standards, enabling conditions) in African agri-food trade
systems, or that focussed on the digital transformation of agri-food value
chains and market participation with a view to that goal. This approach
captures the broader digital foundations upon which trade can be
facilitated, while acknowledging that not all forms of digital agriculture
can be considered digital trade. Accordingly, the findings are organised
into 5 main sections: (1) geographic and temporal distributions of the
studies and actors, (2) digital landscape identifying key technologies and
services, (3) key trends and developments, (4) barriers to digitalisation,
and (5) adoption drivers.
4.1. Geographical, time, stakeholder distributions of the studies
An increase in scientific interest in the topic of digital trade in agri-
food chains in Africa can be observed since 2020, with most studies
(n = 45) published in the last 5 years, matching recent interest and
(domestic) commitments to digitalisation set by governments in Africa
(Fig. 2). This growing interest is also associated with the COVID-19
pandemic, which exposed vulnerabilities in Africa's food systems and
accelerated the adoption and exploration of digital solutions, not only in
trade systems, but also across diverse areas of the economy (Bahn et al.,
2021). Institutional reports and websites (grey literature) also served as
imported sources for this study. These sources likewise indicate a similar
trend, reflecting increased attention following the pandemic and recent
trade agreements and commitments on digitalisation within the African
Union trading bloc.
Both scientific and grey literature indicate major regional differ-
ences, with West and East Africa being mentioned far more often in the
literature compared to North and Southern Africa, except South Africa
(Fig. 2). Many of the studies are primarily clustered around countries
with well-established digital innovation hubs such as Ghana, Nigeria
and Kenya, which offer a favourable environment for tech start-ups and
adoption of digital solutions for agriculture and trade (Njuguna et al.,
2025). In addition to studies focusing on the individual countries, 30
studies covered multiple regions in Africa, of which 15 took a
continent-wide approach.
There was also a significant imbalance in the attention paid to actors
relevant to the digitalisation of trade. The analysis of the studies
revealed the emphasis on farmers and specifically the smallholder
farmers (70% of the papers, n = 37) and Producers (81% of the studies,
n = 43). Institutional Actors are represented in 40% of the papers
(n = 21), followed by Market Actors (32% of the papers, n = 17). The
remaining actors, particularly Technology and Start-Ups, were
mentioned fewer than expected (n = 16; 30%). Research and Academic
(n = 13; 25%), Financial Actors (n = 6; 12%), and other actors (NGOs,
communities, trainers, etc.) are represented in 12% of the papers (n = 6).
The following list represents how these actors are generally defined in
the studies.
• Producers, consisting of smallholder farmers, producers, farm
owners, and farming communities.

4.2. African digital landscape
The following section presents studies that deal with foundational
building blocks of digitalisation in African agri-food trade systems. The
African digital landscape is presented along two dimensions in the
studies: digital technologies and digital services.
The following main categories were found in the literature, where
digital technologies provide the foundation for digital solutions, tools,
and services used in agricultural practices.
• Communication Technologies: these include mobile phones, SMS,
chat applications, internet platforms, network coverage (2G/3G/4G/
5G), Interactive Voice Response (IVR), and USSD (Unstructured
Supplementary Service Data). These were considered crucial in
connecting farmers, providing access to markets, and in digital
extension services that offer knowledge, services, and support to
farmers (Arthur et al., 2024; Baumüller et al., 2023; Hartmann et al.,
2021; Asem - Hiablie et al., 2023; Mazwane et al., 2023).
• Fintech: examples are mobile money, digital banking, digital
finance, mobile payments, insurance and other financial services.
Fintech is considered pivotal in financial inclusion among (remote)
smallholder farmers and also as a driving force behind digital
adoption in Africa (Bahn et al., 2021; Choruma et al., 2024;
Mazwane et al., 2023; Mkansi et al., 2025; Sanusi et al., 2025).
• Precision and Smart Farming Technologies: these technologies
include Internet of Things (IoT) devices, sensors, drones (UAV),
smart irrigation systems, precision farming, Low Earth Orbit (LEO)
satellites, robotics and automation (sensors/computer vision).
Although less prevalent among smallholder farmers, these technol-

(traditional) farming practices (Arthur et al., 2024; Faysse & Simon,

• Data Analytics Technologies: examples are Big Data, Geographical
Information System (GIS), Machine Learning, AI, cloud computing,
and data analytics. These technologies offer informed decision-

Dooyum Uyeh et al., 2023; Sanusi et al., 2025).
• Supply Chain Management (SCM): encompasses hardware, soft-

efficiency, transparency, and integration of the value chain through
traceability and information sharing among all actors, from input
supplier to farmer, processor to retailer (Abate et al., 2023; Arthur
et al., 2024; Choruma et al., 2024; Kieti et al., 2022).

4.3. Emerging patterns in Africa's digital agri-food landscape
4.3.1. Growth of a fragmented agri-food ecosystem
Availability of digital tools and services for African farmers has seen
a steady increase on the continent recently, with the CTA report (2019)
indicating that two-thirds of the 390 identified digital solutions were
established during the first three years of the CTA survey (Tsan et al.,
2019). Additionally, the COVID-19 pandemic resulted in supply chain
shocks, exposing Africa's food system vulnerabilities (e.g., food import
dependence) and led to greater emphasis on shorter supply chains,
sustained local food production, and the role of digitalisation
(Abdelrahman & Chunping, 2022; Bahn et al., 2021; Christiaensen et al.,
2021; Asem - Hiablie et al., 2023). Renewed interest since 2020 is re-
flected in increased academic interest and funding of projects, primarily
from foreign institutes in the USA, Europe and Asia (Arthur et al., 2024;
Bahn et al., 2021).
However, this distribution of digitalisation interest is skewed to a few
countries in South, West and North Africa (Arthur et al., 2024). This
skewed interest leads to another recurring pattern of uneven
geographical adoption. Fewer reports and insights can be obtained from
the Sahel and Central African region as well as the Horn of Africa (Arthur
et al., 2024). Smaller African countries in the MENA region have little to
no documented knowledge on digitalisation in agriculture, with conflict
areas being more practically oriented with their digital solutions (Arthur
et al., 2024; Bahn et al., 2021). Moreover, the digital divide within
countries and communities is cited as an important challenge to
enhancing the sector through digitalisation (Abate et al., 2023; Achieng,
2023; Arthur et al., 2024; Bahn et al., 2021; Kudama et al., 2021; Mkansi
et al., 2025). Despite these developments, there is a growing trend in the
emergence of new digital tools and solutions to address these con-
straints, driven by increased coverage, internet accessibility, and mobile
phone adoption (Addison et al., 2024; Arthur et al., 2024; Choruma
et al., 2024). More recently, Njuguna et al. observed a shift from
generic digital tools - low-cost information exchange - towards more
farm-specific and data-driven solutions. These newer tools offer tailored
advice to farmers through the use of diagnostics and sensor-generated
data (Njuguna et al., 2025). These challenges extend across the
agri-food system, as evident in the domain of trade and certification
standards.

4.3.2. Trends in trade, standards and infrastructure
A continued trend is the growing interest of agri-food actors in
accessing export and regional markets to improve economic opportu-
nities. A study on how agricultural small-and medium-sized enterprises
(SMEs) perceived export market access in South Africa concluded that
there is a strong desire to enter export regional markets due to better
prices and support, but both internal (finance, know-how) and external
barriers (lack of support, infrastructure & underdeveloped value chains)
pose barriers to entry (Mkansi et al., 2025). The fragmented trade
landscape among African countries, consisting of complex required
standards, licenses, documentation, and trade barriers, hinders the
continent's ability to boost intra-African trade (Akrong et al., 2025;
Assoua et al., 2022; Dooyum Uyeh et al., 2023; Faysse & Simon, 2015;
Kouty & Lemma, 2025; Nelson & Tallontire, 2014). These barriers are a
recurring theme and showcase why intra-African trade is only a small
part of the total trade in African agricultural products (Abate et al.,
2023; Mkansi et al., 2025). Nevertheless, party members' political
commitment and capacity are significant drivers of its success in
boosting trade growth, regional integration among members, and uni-
fication on the continent. Certification standards play a key role in
facilitating trade, and complying with (international) standards on food
quality and safety, with digitalisation enabling easier exchange and
verification of information. Certification standards show mixed, but
important impacts for African farmers. A study by Sadeu et al. on cocoa
certification schemes concluded that certified farmers (e.g., UTZ, Fair
Trade, Rainforest Alliance) gained higher income through better yields
versus non-certified farmers (Sadeu et al., 2023). Certified firms also had
improved access to external markets, and offered better contractual
agreements and working conditions compared to uncertified farms, ac-
cording to a paper by Akrong et al. on the GlobalG.A.P. certification on
mango farms in Ghana (Akrong et al., 2025). At the same time, certifi-
cation does not lift farmers out of poverty, and the premium it offers
plays only a small role compared to socio-contextual factors (Akrong
et al., 2025; Sadeu et al., 2023). Wage inequality between men and
women still exists, regardless of the degree of equality in tasks (Akrong
et al., 2025). Finally, the certified benefits are often a spillover effect
from training through the participation of the (farm) owners in specific
farmer-based organisations (FBOs), rather than the certification stan-
dards (Akrong et al., 2025).
Evidence from literature regarding benefits directly linked to digital
tools for smallholder farmers includes (Arthur et al., 2024; Choruma
et al., 2024; Njuguna et al., 2025).
1. Improved market access, (farming) knowledge, and lower trans-
action costs outside local markets,
2. Improvements in record-keeping, transparency and access to large
buyers,
3. Higher profitability.
However, despite these potential benefits, the literature highlights
several barriers to digitalisation among users (see Table 3).
4.4. Barriers to digitalisation in African agri-food systems
4.4.1. Structural constraints to digital access: digital literacy, infrastructure
and economic feasibility
The expansion of digitalisation across Africa has created new op-
portunities for improving agri-food systems. However, limited digital
and technical capacity of farmers and agri-food actors hinders the
adoption of digital agricultural technologies (A. Bateki et al., 2021;
Asem - Hiablie et al., 2023; Choruma et al., 2024; Eitzinger et al., 2019).
Access to these digital tools is often unevenly distributed across the
continent and within regions, resulting in skewed continental digital
literacy, especially among older farmers in remote areas who are often
unfamiliar with these digital agri-food tools (Abate et al., 2023;
Choruma et al., 2024; Eitzinger et al., 2019; Kudama et al., 2021). In
addition, many digital tools were conceived as not user-friendly; in-
terfaces are often poorly translated and overly complex, making them
difficult to control. In combination with the associated cost (member-
ship, adoption hardware), these factors reduce perceived utility and
limit adoption (Bahn et al., 2021; Baumüller et al., 2023; Choruma et al.,
2024; Faysse & Simon, 2015). Mobile network coverage, connectivity,
and mobile phone adoption on the continent have greatly improved
recently, offering opportunities to channel digital services to (rural)
farmers. However, (mobile) network speed and reliability, high internet
costs, limited access to electricity in rural areas, and the high investment
cost of capable hardware hinder the adoption of digital solutions by
farmers (Choruma et al., 2024; Njuguna et al., 2025; Osabohien, 2024;
Sanusi et al., 2025). These infrastructure constraints hinder the real-time
exchange of digital market participation, digital financial services, and
information, thus mitigating the exchange benefits of digital solutions
for farmers in agriculture (Baumüller et al., 2023; Choruma et al., 2024;
Sanusi et al., 2025).
The emergence of digitalisation in Africa has created new opportu-
nities for start-ups and entrepreneurs to provide solutions to actors in the
agri-food chain. However, the long-term economic effects of their

4.4.2. Social and institutional inequalities in adoption and design
Social and institutional inequalities limit equal adoption and shared
benefits from digital agri-food solutions, resulting in barriers such as
digital divides, reinforcing (market) power inequalities, exclusion from
adoption, and gender inequality, especially among women (Bahn et al.,
2021; Choruma et al., 2024; Nelson & Tallontire, 2014; Sanusi et al.,
2025). Institutional structures often reinforce these inequalities by
following the interests of more powerful, corporate actors, while
limiting participation and representation of smallholder farmers and
workers (Faysse & Simon, 2015; Nelson & Tallontire, 2014). Similar
patterns have been observed in agri-food certification and standards,
which, despite the social and sustainability objectives, pay insufficient
attention to workers’ representation and socio-contextual factors
(Akrong et al., 2025; Gather & Wollni, 2022; Sadeu et al., 2023).
Additionally, the design of digital interfaces in agri-food apps and pro-
grams often insufficiently reflects the needs and experiences of small-
holder farmers, particularly older and more digitally illiterate users with
limited experience of digital hardware (Bahn et al., 2021; Choruma
et al., 2024; Njuguna et al., 2025). Examples include challenges in
navigating complex menus, limited or no options for local languages,
and reliance on text-heavy interfaces, creating significant barriers for
new users, especially those with low literacy and digital literacy skills
(Choruma et al., 2024). These design limitations are further amplified by
socio-contextual factors including inconsistent mobile connectivity and
limited access to reliable devices (Kabbiri et al., 2018; Kudama et al.,
2021). A Human-Centred Design approach that addresses the local
socio-contextual factors of smallholder farmers, including limited digital
skills and infrastructure constraints, should be taken into consideration
to improve the usability and effectiveness of digital agri-food solutions
for smallholder farmers (Choruma et al., 2024; Eitzinger et al., 2019).
4.5. Adoption drivers for digital agri-food systems
4.5.1. Enabling adoption through capacity building, infrastructure and
sustainable business models
Despite these constraints, targeted bottom-up training and knowl-
edge sharing programs adapted to local cultural context and language
represent key drivers to address two main barriers: digital literacy &
human capacity constraints (Abate et al., 2023; Choruma et al., 2024;
Tsan et al., 2019). Such initiatives focus on building skills, awareness
and confidence among (smallholder) farmers, while also helping to
incentivise and attract the next generation of farmers who are more
accustomed to digital tools (Asem - Hiablie et al., 2023; Choruma et al.,
2024; Sanusi et al., 2025; Schouten & Bitzer, 2015). Adoption barriers
often indicate the mismatch between the rapid pace of digital de-
velopments and the readiness of users, entrepreneurs, and governmental
agencies (Abate et al., 2023; Arthur et al., 2024; Tsan et al., 2019).
Therefore, education and awareness initiatives should recognise and
address users’ unfamiliarity and discomfort with digital tools, show-
casing the practical benefits and improving digital literacy in the process
while strengthening resilience to climate shocks (Abate et al., 2023;
Choruma et al., 2024; Eitzinger et al., 2019; Sanusi et al., 2025). Beyond
human capacity constraints, digital agri-food solutions require reliable
infrastructure to provide connectivity and electricity to function.
Despite the impressive uptake of mobile phone adoption and connec-
tivity across the continent recently, limited access to electricity and
affordable connectivity still hinder broader adoption (Abate et al., 2023;
Choruma et al., 2024; Sanusi et al., 2025). Telecommunication com-
panies have been a large contributor to digital adoption, providing
internet services and mobile networks, including to farmers in rural
areas (Abate et al., 2023; Adolwa et al., 2025). However, limited and
slow internet access, combined with high costs, poses barriers for
farmers to fully leverage digital tools, restricting their ability to access
real-time information necessary for decision-making and market
participation (Adolwa et al., 2025; Choruma et al., 2024; Osabohien,
2024).
Continuous investment in electricity infrastructure (e.g., power sta-
tions/transformers) and internet connectivity (e.g., subsea cables, data
centres, mobile networks) is therefore essential to keep up and drive
adoption, aligning with the digitalisation commitments by African
member states (Arthur et al., 2024; Choruma et al., 2024; Sanusi et al.,
2025; Tsan et al., 2019). Without opportunities to charge devices or
connect to the internet, phones and subsequently software applications
will not work. At the same time, grid extension across the continent is
unlikely to happen soon, emphasising the importance of solar-powered
solutions and batteries to provide electricity to homes, farms and busi-
nesses (Abate et al., 2023; Njuguna et al., 2025). To align with the local
rural context and the limited availability of mobile network coverage,
offline- or low-data functionality should be incorporated in digital
agri-food solutions (Abate et al., 2023; Arthur et al., 2024; Eitzinger
et al., 2019). In addition to physical infrastructure, further in-
vestments in soft infrastructure (public services, regulatory frameworks
and (intangible) systems to support physical network infrastructure) are
required to ensure that infrastructure translates to sustainable adoption
of digital tools (Abate et al., 2023; Choruma et al., 2024).
A large share of digital agriculture solutions have emerged recently,
but many of these solutions operate through donor-funded projects or
pilots and remain highly dependent on public funding (Abate et al.,
2023; Bahn et al., 2021; Baumüller et al., 2023). Hence, these solu-
tions often lack long-term sustainable business models, leading to un-
certainty and market distortion at the scaling stage (Abate et al., 2023;
Choruma et al., 2024).
Success stories of more mature existing solutions tap into the po-
tential of providing public-private partnerships (PPP), such as DigiFarm
and iCow in Kenya (Abate et al., 2023; Arthur et al., 2024). These
companies joined forces with local telecommunication companies, of-
fering benefits to their users (digital extension services to farmers) while
mobile network providers gained subscribers and increased use of their
platforms. These examples illustrate the importance of incorporating
digital agri-food solutions within local commercially viable ecosystems.
Developments should therefore target clear revenue models that target
the full potential of the market, with examples such as
subscription-based or fee-for-services models only reaching a small
fraction (Abate et al., 2023; Sanusi et al., 2025). Consequently, public
funding will remain a crucial enabler for the development of digital
solutions, especially when these solutions 1) have strong public good
characteristics, 2) target users with limited purchasing power, and 3) are
implemented in local contexts where traditional practices and risk
aversion constrain willingness to pay (Abate et al., 2023; Choruma et al.,

4.5.2. Governance, trust and social embeddedness in technology adoption
Dynamic policies and regulations that target digital innovations are
essential to ensure and enable sustainable competitive market envi-
ronments while protecting consumers. Key areas include digital prop-
erty rights, data ownership, and privacy-related regulations, which must
be regularly updated to fit the quickly evolving digital landscape.
Enabling regulatory policies and environments can lead to rapid digital
expansion, as showcased by the digital-friendly policies in Kenya and
Ghana (Abate et al., 2023; Njuguna et al., 2025). As of 2025, 76% of the
African Union has dedicated data protection and privacy legislation in
place, and 78% have implemented e-transaction laws to ensure reliable
online commercial transactions (Abate et al., 2023). However, specific
regulation targeting digitalisation in agri-food systems is not widespread
and remains uneven across the continent. A study investigating the ef-
fects of sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) requirements on Cameroon's
cocoa export concluded that SPS measures are not as impeding as
originally thought and instead could enhance trade and market access
for exporting products (Assoua et al., 2022). However, weak capacity to
comply with renewed SPS regulations requires strengthening regulatory
and institutional frameworks that are adapted and aligned with global
best practices. In addition, the government should intensify relation-
ships with stakeholders (farmers/organisations, local and institutional
regulatory actors and exporting firms) and prioritise capacity building
through knowledge sharing (agricultural extension services) and
financial/technical assistance to farmers and exporters to ensure SPS
compliance in importing markets (Assoua et al., 2022).
The effective use of a technology depends on the recognition of a
global application standard to ensure interoperability and industry-wide
consistency (Asem - Hiablie et al., 2023; Nelson & Tallontire, 2014;
Schouten & Bitzer, 2015). Regulatory incentives should address existing
power imbalances and structures within (domestic) agri-food systems. A
power asymmetry is generally present in the agri-food chain, with un-
derrepresented groups, such as smallholder farmers and women, having
limited influence on decision-making, leading to digitalisation policy
initiatives not reflecting local needs and realities (Adolwa et al., 2025;
Akrong et al., 2025; Choruma et al., 2024; Faysse & Simon, 2015).
Special care should be taken by governments and local authorities to
align these local realities in their strategies.
The adoption and use of digital agri-food solutions is strongly
influenced by integration within social channels, such as workers' or-
ganisations, local communities, self-organising communities and farmer
schools (Abdulai et al., 2023; Munthali et al., 2018). First, social influ-
ence and peer-based learning play a significant role in adoption.
Word-of-mouth communication and collective learning within local
communities affect farmers’ willingness to innovate or experiment with
new solutions (Abdulai et al., 2023; Munthali et al., 2018). Second,
prioritising legitimacy and trust is crucial (Kieti et al., 2022; Munthali
et al., 2018). Governmental actors and companies should build part-
nerships (e.g., PPP) that reflect local realities and practices and incor-
porate trusted intermediaries (Assoua et al., 2022; Sanusi et al., 2025).
In this context, user experience and usability should be prioritised over
the addition of new features. Third, socially embedded solutions must
address gender inequality and power asymmetries while remaining in
line with cultural norms and practices (Abate et al., 2023; Akrong et al.,
2025; Arthur et al., 2024; Choruma et al., 2024; Daum et al., 2022).
D4Ag solutions affect everyday practices in (rural) farming, including
planning, husbandry activities, decision-making, and market participa-
tion (Abdulai et al., 2023). However, existing gender roles and unequal
access to financial resources and digital tools (e.g., mobile phones) limit
women and other marginalized group from accessing and using digital
solutions equally (Abdulai et al., 2023). Digital solutions risk reinforcing
existing inequalities for users who already possess digital skills and
decision-making power without these considerations, highlighting the
importance of engaging underrepresented groups and women in the
design and deployment process (Adolwa et al., 2025; Eitzinger et al.,
2019). Finally, digital adoption strategies should prioritise differences
in socio-economic characteristics, including age, education, income, and
digital familiarity (Choruma et al., 2024; Osabohien, 2024). Additional
segmentation within D4Ag solutions, which considers different user
groups and varying levels of digital literacy, can improve uptake and
inclusivity among farmers (Abdulai et al., 2023; Choruma et al., 2024;
Daum et al., 2022). To summarise, technology acceptance is shaped by
trust and perceived usefulness, social integration in communities and
networks, human-centred design, and accessibility.
5. Discussion
5.1. Key findings
This study synthesises findings on the digital transformation of Af-
rican agri-food systems and its relevance to trade. This approach cap-
tures the broader digital foundations upon which trade can be
facilitated, while acknowledging that not all forms of digital agriculture
can be considered digital trade. The literature indicates growing interest
and adoption of digital innovations for agri-food in recent years,
alongside growing publications and continental initiatives. Several en-
actments of continental and national digitalisation initiatives were
launched (or updated) recently to promote the growth and adoption of
digital initiatives to boost agricultural transformation and trade on the
continent. However, the literature identifies persistent challenges in
human capacity, infrastructure, regulation, and unsustainable business
models. The identified digital agri-food solutions include digital exten-
sion services, supply chain management, fintech, digital farm manage-
ment tools and digital marketing.
Key adoption drivers identified across the literature include user-
centred development programs and capacity building, infrastructure
investments, supportive policy and regulatory incentives, and social and
institutional embeddedness. Evidence from the literature is concen-
trated on specific digital solutions, countries and actors, with few studies
addressing digital agri-food trade explicitly.
This study contributes by synthesising fragmented evidence on
Africa's digital landscape and what trends, drivers and barriers influence
digitalisation, and its relevance to agri-food trade. Rather than focusing
on individual countries, value chains or solutions, it provides a conti-
nental overview of the digital solutions, trends, drivers and barriers
shaping digital trade. In particular, the findings indicate a gap between
the increasing adoption of digital agri-food solutions and their integra-
tion into interoperable, cross-border digital trade systems, highlighting
priorities for future research and implementation.
Despite the growing work in Africa's digital agriculture, compara-
tively few studies have combined technological, institutional, and socio-
economic perspectives. A digital trade ecosystem provides a framework
for considering how digital technologies, data standards, agri-food ac-
tors and institutional frameworks interact to enable trade. This
perspective is essential to identify the enabling conditions needed for
digital transformation and support efficient cross-border trade that the
continent strives for.
5.2. Digitalisation as an enabler for food system transformation
According to the FAO, the continent remains a small player in agri-
food trade and is still highly dependent on food imports from outside
Africa, with intra-Africa trade contributing to less than 20% of the
average yearly export of foodstuffs (FAO, 2024b). Smallholder farmers,
responsible for 90% of Africa's agri-food production, often face limited
access to external markets and low value addition practices. Evidence
from the UN Trade Digitalisation Index, African Development Bank and
FAO showcase how Africa's agri-food system is characterised by a low
adoption rate of (digital) paperless trade, trade barriers that limit trade
opportunities (e.g., non-tariff trade barriers), a predominantly informal

agri-food market, and a fragmented digital landscape to enable trade,
despite the export potential opportunities (African Development Bank
Group, 2026; Duval et al., 2024; FAO, 2024a). The continent's ambi-
tion under continental initiatives, alongside domestic digitalisation
policies and agendas by African countries, indicates the fundamental
shift of transforming the continent through its agricultural backbone,
with digitalisation of the sector as a key driving force (Addison et al.,
2024). The continent aims to incorporate more value-addition prac-
tices in the AVC, boost intra-African trade to become less dependent on
foreign food imports with the increasing population, and build resilient
value chains to reduce the adverse effects of climate change. Recent
continental initiatives, including AfCFTA and CAADP, highlight the
importance of digitalisation and standardisation in the AVC to build
towards better integration of trade-related practices and international
compliance (certificates, trade documentation, traceability) (Kieti et al.,
2022).
5.3. Main digitalisation developments in African agri-food systems
Rapid growth in mobile connectivity, reaching 47% unique mobile
phone users in 2024, together with infrastructure investments and
COVID-19-related digital uptake, expanded opportunities for digital
services to agri-food actors (Arthur et al., 2024; Bahn et al., 2021; GSMA,
2025; GSMA AgriTech, 2020). Despite these improvements, the included
studies report uneven adoption of digital agri-food solutions across
Africa's agri-food system, potentially constraining Africa's integration in
global agri-food trade participation and paperless trade adoption. Dig-
ital solutions are distributed unevenly, with digital hubs in West and
East Africa offering primarily digital extension services and market
linkage solutions to agri-food actors, with limited trade-specific solu-
tions. The fast-evolving digital landscape in Africa favours simple
adaptive solutions over complex commercial solutions, highlighting the
importance of public support and smart finance partnerships in enabling
a gradual shift towards sustainable business models, given the current,
primarily donor-driven, pilot projects of digital solutions (Njuguna
et al., 2025). Despite progress in national digitalisation strategies and
continental frameworks, regulatory gaps (e.g., intellectual property
rights and consumer and business protection), and limited institutional
digitalisation frameworks constrain digitalisation and digital trade up-
take (ESCAP, ECA & ECLAC, 2024).
Current literature on digitalisation in Africa's agri-food systems has
largely focused on farm production and market access, with compara-
tively limited evidence on integrated digital trade systems. This in-
dicates a gap between the development of digital agricultural potential
and their integration into digital trade systems. Future research there-
fore needs to examine how these solutions, trade processes, and agri-
food actors can be connected within a digital trade ecosystem, offering
new perspectives on how digitalisation can enhance Africa's role in
cross-border trade markets.
5.4. Digitalisation, standards and trade integration
Digitalisation has the potential to improve various aspects within the
agri-food chain, including enabling trade and information flow
(Choruma et al., 2024; Hartmann et al., 2021). However, relatively few
included studies have examined the relationship between digitalisation
and agri-food trade in Africa. This gap is increasingly important as
global trade requirements move towards digital paperless trade,
requiring stronger digital trade capabilities to support Africa's global
trade participation (OECD, 2025). For African exporters, international
regulations, certification requirements, and changing market standards
increasingly require digital information to comply with regulations.
Standards and certifications, such as organic, Fair Trade or production
standards (e.g., GlobalG.A.P., ISO), play an increasingly important role
in addressing environmental and health concerns by consumers
(Ditlevsen et al., 2019). Africa's most important agri-food trade partner,
the EU, announced upcoming requirements under the Green Deal
(European Commission, 2023). These requirements bring new demands
on agricultural processes and affect African farmers looking to trade
directly. On top of these upcoming regulations, certification of food
safety and quality generally requires (digital) upkeep in various agri-
cultural processes that benefit from digital implementations. Without
shared technical or data standards, customs authorities and other
cross-border agencies are unable to efficiently process and/or exchange
digital trade-related data, limiting the effectiveness of digital trade
facilitation. Continental frameworks and commitments such as the
AfCFTA DTP should be enforced by member states within domestic
policymaking to prioritise incorporating global and continental appli-
cation standards into the agri-food chain. The AfCFTA offers smallholder
farmers and traders new market opportunities for intra-African trade by
benefiting from the reduced tariffs on many African goods, with the
AfCFTA Rules of Origin being such an example, bringing preferential
treatment and trade-facilitation opportunities. Digital solutions enable
options that previously seemed financially and technically unfeasible,
leading the way for a fundamental change in the way agriculture is
practiced (Trendov et al., 2019).
5.5. Limitations
This study has several limitations that should be considered. First,
the search strategy, inclusion and exclusion criteria, assessment strat-
egy, and the choice of databases may have limited the scope of the
findings due to the selection of keywords, eligibility criteria, and Bool-
ean construction. Second, the emerging nature of trade digitalisation in
Africa limits the evidence of impacts on trade volumes and participation,
with limited generalizability given the concentrated cluster of included
countries in the papers. To address this limitation, reputable grey
sources were included. However, the inclusion of grey sources in-
troduces variability in the methodological assessment, which was
addressed by excluding these findings solely in the results section. Third,
the rapidly evolving nature of initiatives and policies in the African
digital agri-food landscape results in the exclusion of developments that
have occurred after the completion of the data collection and assessment
stage, which are disregarded in this paper. Fourth, no formal quality and
risk-of-bias assessment was conducted due to the scope and heteroge-
neous nature of the studies. A single assessment was therefore not
considered appropriate. Finally, the limitations of the included external
grey data sources (e.g., Digital Agri Hub) should also be taken into
consideration.
5.6. Recommendations and future research
Recommendations: Based on this review and in particular the
thematic analysis (Table 3), a key priority for stakeholders is to move
beyond isolated digital agricultural initiatives towards a more connected
and interoperable digital agri-food trade system. As global trade systems
increasingly rely on digital processes, this transition requires affordable
and interoperable infrastructure, supportive regulatory frameworks and
greater alignment with digital trade compliance requirements. It also
requires scaling up proven, sustainable digital agri-food business models
supporting trade to move beyond short-term, donor-funded projects,
while ensuring that agri-food solutions are locally adapted and user-
centric. At the same time, targeted (digital) capacity building and
tailored coordination among relevant stakeholders are needed to
strengthen Africa's digital trade capabilities and support the scaling up
of successful initiatives. These efforts should prioritise accessibility and
inclusion, particularly for smallholder farmers and other underserved
groups facing barriers to digital adoption.
Future research: This review highlights important gaps in the evi-
dence on digital agri-food trade in Africa. Future research is needed to
strengthen evidence on the long-term impact of digital agri-food trade
digitalisation. Specifically, how existing digital agricultural systems can

research on digital documentation, digital standards and certification,

6. Conclusion
This study examined the evolving nature of agri-food digitalisation in
Africa and its relevance to trade and global market integration. The
trends identified through the thematic classification indicate growing
adoption and interest in digital agri-food solutions, supported by con-
tinental and national digitalisation initiatives aimed at promoting
agricultural transformation on the continent. In addition, Africa's
adoption of digital paperless trade remains below the global average,
which may constrain participation in global trade opportunities that
increasingly rely on digital information exchange. Nevertheless,
growing connectivity, digital initiatives and continental commitments
showcase the digital transformation potential when aligned with local
realities. The barriers identified include a fragmented digital landscape
relevant to trade, constrained by a lack of human capacity, infrastruc-
ture, regulation, and unsustainable business models. The drivers include
user-centred development programs to address digital literacy, infra-
structure investments, supportive policy and regulatory incentives, and
solutions that are socially embedded in existing agricultural practices
and value chains.
Only limited literature has addressed how digital agri-food solutions
connect with trade compliance and digital standards, which is consistent
with the fact that Africa's paperless and streamlined trade processes, the
goal the continent is aspiring to, are far from being realised. As Africa
seeks to bring economic transformation and move beyond isolated
digital agri-food applications, digitalisation and global value chain
integration, both increasingly relying on digital information exchange,
are becoming increasingly important enablers to operationalise their
commitments. Future research should examine how existing digital
agricultural systems in Africa can be connected to interoperable
paperless trade systems, particularly through digital documentation,
digital standards and certification, and cross-border interoperability.
Insights into interconnected digital trade are therefore essential to un-
derstand how digitalisation and standardisation can enhance Africa's
role in cross-border trade markets.
