Abstract
Sensory mapping, the process by which a sensory stimulus elicits a consumer response, is central to effective sensory
marketing. Yet, research on the topic remains fragmented. First, the prevalence of construct proliferation, where a lack of
distinction between a continuously growing array of similar terms, raises questions about how different types of sensory
mappings should be defined. Second, sensory mappings have often been studied in isolation, even though they are inter -
related in practice, where one stimulus can diverge into multiple responses and multiple stimuli can converge on a single
response. Third, sensory stimuli are prone to mixed outcomes, enhancing or harming consumer evaluations. This paper
addresses these challenges in two steps. First, a typology delineating sensory mappings based on sensory stimuli (physical
vs. lexical) and consumer responses (sensory vs. semantic) is developed, yielding four key variants: sensory correspon -
dence, semantic correspondence, sensory communication, and semantic communication. Second, a conceptual model is
provided with propositions explaining how these sensory mapping types can be combined and how stimuli intensity and
individual differences act as boundary conditions that explain mixed outcomes. Together, these frameworks underpin a
future research agenda that offers directions to advance sensory marketing theory and practice.

evaluation · Sensory experience

adverse outcomes for brands.
In response, we propose a theory of sensory mappings
that outlines the interconnected pathways through which
different sensory stimuli influence consumer responses and
evaluations. In doing so, our analytical framework offers a
three-fold contribution to sensory marketing research. First,
we offer a delineating theoretical contribution via a typology
that distinguishes four types of sensory mappings: sensory
correspondence, semantic correspondence, sensory commu-
nication, and semantic communication. Second, we develop
a conceptual model with propositions that explain how
these types can be combined and identifies stimuli intensity
and individual differences as boundary conditions. Lastly,
we provide structured guidance on how sensory market -
ing scholars can formulate theoretically grounded research
questions via a future research agenda. Practically, we offer
insights into how managers can prevent sensory marketing
failures and integrate different types of sensory mappings
when designing multisensory experiences.
The roadmap of the paper is structured as follows. We begin
by reviewing the theoretical foundations and key challenges
of sensory marketing research. We then develop a typology of
four sensory mapping types, followed by a conceptual model
that integrates these mappings and outlines key propositions.
We conclude with a discussion of theoretical and managerial
implications, along with a future research agenda.
Background
Foundations of sensory mappings in the sensory
marketing literature
Sensory mapping spans several disciplines. Originally, the
notion of “mapping the senses” emerged from neuroscience,
most notably with the “somatosensory homunculus,” a top-
ographic representation linking different sensory organs to
distinct brain regions (Penfield & Boldrey, 1937). Essen -
tially, this depiction illustrated how sensory perception is
organized through systematic mappings between sensory
stimulation and cortical responses. Building on this stimu -
lus–response relationship was the study of synesthesia, a
neurological phenomenon in which stimulation of one sen -
sory pathway triggers involuntary experiences in another
(Spence & Deroy, 2013). The few individuals with syn -
esthesia, known as synesthetes, “hear” colors or “smell”
sounds due to the atypical activation of these pathways
(Bien et al., 2012). This term inspired concepts such as syn-
esthetic correspondences (Crisinel & Spence, 2010; Melara
& O’Brien, 1987) and synesthetic metaphors (Yu, 2003),
which emerged in the fields of psychology and linguistics
to describe sensory associations at the physical and lexical
levels, respectively. Eventually, these concepts travelled
downstream to the marketing domain, where scholars intro-
duced constructs such as crossmodal communication (Hagt-
vedt & Brasel, 2016) and multisensory experiences (Velasco
& Obrist, 2021) to explain how sensory stimuli shape con -
sumer perceptions and behavior.
Across disciplines, the term “crossmodal correspon -
dences,” from which much of our current understanding
of sensory mappings is derived, has become a dominant
umbrella term (Spence, 2011). Since the term demonstrates
how physical stimuli can evoke perceptions of otherwise
inaccessible sensory attributes, its relevance has grown in
marketing. This is particularly true for digital and expe -
riential marketing contexts, where taste, touch, and smell
are not always directly experienced (Petit et al., 2019). For
example, Le Labo uses images of raw ingredients such as
sandalwood and rose to communicate scent profiles on its
website.
Taken together, the evolution of sensory mappings
reflects a shift from neurological synesthesia, which affects
only a small fraction of the population, to more generalized
sensory mappings experienced across all consumers that can
be strategically leveraged through marketing. Throughout
this evolution, the cartographic nature of sensory perception
persists, emphasizing structured pathways from stimuli to
responses. Thus, we retain the term “mapping” in our theo-
rization, which we motivate via three challenges in sensory
marketing research: construct proliferation, unaccounted
interrelationships, and mixed outcomes.

Intramodality Sensory responses can arise both within a
single modality (intramodal) and across modalities (cross -
modal). However, prior conceptualizations of sensory map-
pings in marketing have largely focused on crossmodal
correspondences (Bhatia et al., 2021; Wörfel et al., 2022),
with limited attention to intramodal mappings despite their
role in shaping consumer perceptions. For example, intra -
modal correspondences that show how colors influence per-
ceived product shape (Chen et al., 2015) and product size
(Hagtvedt & Brasel, 2017) have meaningful implications
for packaging design.
Lexical stimuli Sensory language plays a key role in prod-
uct names, descriptions, and reviews because it supplements
inaccessible sensory attributes (Cascio Rizzo et al., 2023;
Fenko et al., 2010). For instance, HogWine’s tagline “Like
sweet Banjo music to your tongue” evokes gustatory pleasure
prior to physical consumption (Bolognesi & Strik-Lievers,
2020; Strik-Lievers, 2017). While prior reviews acknowledge
the metaphorical use of sensory language (e.g., describing
someone with a “cold shoulder”; Wörfel et al., 2022), they
offer limited insights into how such lexical stimuli systemati-
cally elicit specific sensory or semantic consumer responses,
or how brands can design them strategically. This gap is cor-
roborated by Pandey and Tripathi’s (2025) review of the last
four decades of sensory marketing research, which finds that
only three studies investigated lexical stimuli.
Semantic responses Meaning, symbols, and identity are
central to how consumers evaluate offerings and brands.
However, the term “semantic” has been used in varied ways.
For example, semantic congruence describes how different
sensory features are associated via meaning (Almadori et
al., 2021; Laurienti et al., 2004), semantic knowledge refers
to learned regularities that allow consumers to interpret
stimuli (Schwarz, 2004), and semantic priming involves
manipulating memory following sensory exposure to influ -
ence consumer behavior (De Luca & Botelho, 2020). In
all, there is less emphasis on meaning-related responses
and more on mechanisms that facilitate the processing of
sensory information. While some studies do link sensory
features to connotative meanings (Labrecque et al., 2025;
Lowe et al., 2025), existing frameworks offer limited guid -
ance in doing so (Luffarelli et al., 2021).
 Taken together, our interdisciplinary review suggests that
advancing the literature requires clearer delineation of sen -
sory mappings based on the pathways linking different sen-
sory stimuli (physical vs. lexical) and consumer responses
(sensory vs. semantic).
Challenge 2: Unaccounted interrelations
Sensory mappings are frequently examined in isolation,
despite longstanding critiques that “there is no single,
theoretically interesting way in which senses interact,” but
rather that they form “complexes that interact in many the -
oretically interesting ways” (Fulkerson, 2014, p. 10). True
enough, sensory mappings rarely operate independently in
practice. Instead, multiple stimuli and pathways simulta -
neously shape consumer responses and evaluations. For
example, darker hues (physical stimuli) and descriptions
of harvest conditions (lexical stimuli) can jointly influ -
ence perceptions of taste (sensory response) and luxury
(semantic response), which in turn affects quality evalua -
tions of wine. Despite this complexity, prior research has
largely adopted a reductionist approach. Pandey and Tripa -
thi’s (2025) review documented a research focus on single
stimulus–response relationships within a single modal -
ity, reinforcing the limited understanding of how map -
pings combine in real-world contexts. While some studies
address this issue through the lens of multisensory experi -
ences, they typically focus on the concurrent use of mul -
tiple physical stimuli, but not the generation of multiple
consumer responses (Petit et al., 2019; Wörfel et al., 2022).
As a result, the field lacks a systematic understanding of
how combined sensory mappings may reinforce or conflict
with one another. This makes it unclear whether ineffec -
tive sensory marketing strategies stem from poor design
or merely a failure to account for other coinciding sensory
mappings present to consumers.

Challenge 3: Mixed outcomes
Effective sensory marketing hinges on the context-dependent
nature of sensory mappings. Even identical sensory stimuli can
produce varied consumer responses depending on situational
and individual factors. One concern is the stimulus itself.
Drawing on the Yerkes–Dodson law and optimal stimulation
level theory (Yerkes & Dodson, 1908), there is an assump -
tion that moderate levels of sensory arousal produce the most
favorable consumer responses (Steenkamp & Baumgartner,
1992). This implies that both insufficient and excessive sen-
sory stimulation can be detrimental. For example, in online
environments, a lack of access to tactile, olfactory, or gusta-
tory inputs can result in negative or insufficient processing of
product information (Petit et al., 2019). Conversely, excessive
stimulation in shopping environments can cause confusion
and discomfort for consumers (Doucé & Adams, 2020). How-
ever, inconclusive empirical findings in sensory marketing
research suggest that this is not always the case. For example,
in visual complexity studies, some find that highly saturated
colors signal product potency, while others perceive such
saturation as overloading stimuli, especially when they are
already burdened with stimulation in other modalities (Groth
et al., 2024; Labrecque et al., 2025). This variability is further
compounded by individual differences in optimal stimulation
levels, such that what is optimal for one consumer may be
overstimulating for another (Velasco et al., 2026).
Beyond intensity, the alignment between sensory stimuli
and consumers’ expectations also produces inconsistent
outcomes. First, individual differences in sensory process -
ing can reverse the direction of effects. For instance, while
blue is widely associated with trust, innovation, and posi -
tive brand perceptions (Labrecque & Milne, 2012), it elicits
disgust and reduced appetite when applied to food contexts,
as natural blue foods are rare and trigger perceptions of
artificiality (Spence, 2021). Second, how sensory stimuli
are interpreted can vary across cultures, which complicates
the global applicability of sensory marketing strategies.
Cross-cultural research examining crossmodal correspon -
dences between colors, shapes, and tastes across China,
India, Malaysia, and the United States found that while
some associations were consistent (e.g., pink-sweetness),
others varied significantly based on ethnic background
(Wan et al., 2014). For example, Chinese (British) con -
sumers expect red packaged crisps to have tomato (spicy)
flavors. Similarly, stinky tofu is a beloved street food in
Taiwan associated with comfort and nostalgia for locals,
yet its pungent odor can be unpleasant for those unfamiliar
with the dish, illustrating how sensory perception is some -
times shaped by cultural learning rather than being univer -
sally understood.
Importantly, there is an assumption that incongruence
between sensory stimuli and consumer expectations uni -
formly leads to negative outcomes (Krishna et al., 2010;
Sundar & Noseworthy, 2016). If this were true, rebranding
would be impossible since there will always be a mismatch
between newly redesigned sensory stimuli and how con -
sumers previously recognized the brand. This assumption is
problematic because mismatches can generate positive out-
comes. For example, as part of a broader rebranding effort
to communicate speed and convenience, Visa enhanced its
brand value by reinventing the sound, animation, and hap -
tic vibration used in its point-of-sale experience (Zha et al.,
2022). Incongruence here functioned as a mechanism for
repositioning rather than a source of consumer resistance.
Taken together, these mixed outcomes highlight the dif -
ficulty of establishing universal guidelines for sensory mar-
keting design. They also raise an unresolved question: How
do such boundary conditions operate across different types
of sensory mappings? To address this, we first turn to the
dimensions that delineate distinct sensory mapping types.
Dimensions of sensory mappings
To address the first challenge of construct proliferation, we
specify the dimensions of sensory stimuli and consumer
responses to delineate sensory mappings.
Sensory stimuli: Physical vs. lexical
Sensory stimuli are cues that draw on sight, sound, smell,
taste, and touch to elicit consumer responses. While dis -
tinctions across the five senses are relatively straightfor -
ward, ambiguity arises in how stimuli are conceptualized in
prior research. One source of ambiguity lies in metaphors.
Although commonly associated with language (Zhao et al.,
2022), metaphors can also manifest in physical objects. This
includes visual metaphors, which refer to “highly structured
images in which the viewer is pushed to construct meta -
phorical transfers from a source to a target domain, cued
by the image itself” (Bolognesi & Strik-Lievers, 2020, p.
4323). Put simply, they are depictions that convey abstract
qualities, such as how Kleenex using images of puppies to
convey the softness of its tissues. Essentially, metaphors
manifest both physically and linguistically. A second source
of ambiguity comes from the term ‘linguistic.’ Although it
primarily refers to the semantic content of words, it may
also encompass phonology (i.e., how words sound; Poga -
car et al., 2015) and typography (i.e., how words appear
visually; Park et al., 2022; Venkatesan et al., 2022). Both
elements refer to physical features rather than the semantic

content of language. The ambiguity in sensory marketing
design stemming from the overlaps between physical and
lexical stimuli have been applied without distinction in prior
research, which further leads to challenges in disentangling
their underlying mechanisms towards consumer responses.
We therefore dichotomize stimuli as either physical or lexi-
cal and distinguish them based on the nature of sensory acti-
vation (stimulate vs. simulate) and their relationships with
consumer responses (bidirectional vs. unidirectional).
Physical sensory stimuli  are cues that directly stimulate
sensory organs, such as with ambient scents and tempera -
ture in-store atmospherics. A key characteristic of physical
stimuli is correspondence, which refers to the bidirectional
relationship between a physical stimulus and a consumer
response (Spence, 2011). For example, in vision–sound cor-
respondences, consumers can match visual shapes to audi -
tory features, and hearing those sounds similarly evokes
those shape associations (Chen et al., 2016). Likewise,
green hues can signal sustainability (Lim et al., 2020), while
consumers seeking sustainable products may attend more
closely to green cues. Another characteristic of physical
stimuli is polarity (Imschloss & Kuehnl, 2019; Wang et al.,
2020; Yang et al., 2022), where stimuli vary with responses
along a continuum. For example, darker (vs. lighter) prod -
ucts are associated with perceptions of heaviness and dura -
bility (vs. lightness and ease of use) (Hagtvedt, 2020). Taken
together, the bidirectionality and polarity characteristics
suggest that processing mappings between physical sensory
stimuli and consumer responses are automatic to an extent.
Lexical sensory stimuli  are cues that indirectly simulate
sensory experiences using words and phrases. Their key
characteristic is communication, which reflects a primar -
ily unidirectional relationship from stimulus to response, as
commonly observed in advertising and branding (Smirnova,
2016). This unidirectionality implies that lexical sensory
stimuli are more consistently mapped onto specific inter -
pretations. When reversed, the underlying message alters
and is often less intuitive. For example, a “sweet melody”
communicates the auditory pleasantness of music, but the
reverse phrasing “melodic sweetness” is less intuitive and
conveys taste (Kumcu, 2021). Although less common, lexi-
cal stimuli may sometimes exhibit polarity. For example, a
frozen silence versus warm chatter describes interactions
using contrasting temperatures Overall, the unidirectional
nature of lexical stimuli suggests that their mappings to
consumer responses are asymmetric rather than recipro -
cal. Since these mappings are less automatic than the ones
depending on physical stimuli, the underlying psychologi -
cal processes are prone to boundary conditions like indi -
vidual differences.
Consumer responses: Sensory vs. semantic
Consumer responses refer to the outcomes of processing
sensory stimuli, which later shape evaluations. To distin -
guish consumer responses that remain within the sensory
domain from those that extend beyond it, we categorize
them as either sensory or semantic.
Sensory consumer responses are perceptions within the
sensory domain, including those within the same modality
(intramodal) or across modalities (crossmodal). For example,
colors can influence both perceptions of product size (visual-
visual) (Hagtvedt & Brasel, 2016) and weight (visual-touch)
(Hagtvedt, 2020). Sensory responses allow consumers to eval-
uate the functional value of products (Wiedmann et al., 2018),
which can, in turn, enhance product learning and recall (Boltz
et al., 2016). In wine consumption, for example, consumers’
understanding of crossmodal descriptors regarding a wine’s
“body” or “aroma” (Latour & Deighton, 2019) helps justify
their premium pricing, as heightened sensory information
increases perceived value for money (Wiedmann et al., 2018).
Semantic consumer responses  are perceptions that
extend beyond the sensory domain, including emotions and
meaning-based interpretations (Shahid et al., 2022). This
can include the agitation or surprise among museumgo -
ers engaging with art (Joy & Sherry, 2003) or consumers’
perception of rugged versus sophisticated brand personali -
ties (Melzner & Raghubir, 2023). As semantic responses
contribute to the hedonic and symbolic value of products,
they are instrumental in driving deeper engagement with an
offering (Granulo et al., 2021). For example, Chinese con -
sumers preferred auspiciously named foods due to the cul -
tural importance of “fortune” (Youn et al., 2021). Semantic
responses also allow brands to differentiate themselves even
when products are homogeneous (Smith & Keller, 2021).
For example, mere pieces of paper were rated as more pleas-
ant when smooth (vs. rough) textures were matched with
feminine (vs. masculine) odors (Krishna et al., 2010).
In sum, sensory responses pertain to properties in the five
senses, while semantic responses involve properties related
to meaning-making. Therefore, depending on the type of sen-
sory stimulus and response, different underlying mechanisms
are to be expected, which we explain in the following section.

Type 1: Sensory correspondence
Sensory correspondence refers to the mapping between a
physical stimulus and a sensory consumer response. This
includes crossmodal correspondences, such as how sound

& Brasel, 2016), and intramodal correspondences, such as
how sound volume shapes perceptions of auditory distance
(Spence & Sathian, 2020). As the most extensively stud -
ied type, sensory correspondences have direct implications
for marketing design that involves specification of sensory
properties at different touchpoints. For example, because
high-pitched (vs. low-pitched) sounds are linked to smaller
(vs. larger) sizes (Bien et al., 2012), a snack brand might use
higher-pitched crunch sounds in ads to emphasize product
lightness and bite-sized portions.
A key characteristic of sensory correspondences is the
automaticity of mapping. Sensory correspondences are
often processed rapidly and require minimal cognitive
effort, as evidenced by response latency methods such as
the speeded classification task (De Luca & Botelho, 2020),
eye-tracking (Hagtvedt & Brasel, 2016), and implicit asso-
ciation tests (Crisinel & Spence, 2010). Although there is
ongoing debate regarding the extent to which these methods
fully capture automatic processing, findings such as faster
visual attention (Hagtvedt & Brasel, 2016) and improved
recall (De Luca & Botelho, 2020) nonetheless demonstrate
their practical relevance in the design of retail spaces and
catalogs.
A second characteristic of sensory correspondences
is their presumed universality across population groups,
stemming from their prelinguistic nature. These correspon -
dences are believed to emerge prior to, and independently
of, language (Ramachandran & Hubbard, 2001). Even pre-
verbal infants can reliably associate auditory pitch with
spatial height and shape sharpness (Walker et al., 2010). As
a result, sensory correspondences are often assumed to be
broadly consistent across cultures and do not require lexical
sensory stimuli, although they may reinforce them (Ćwiek
et al., 2022; Lowe & Haws, 2017; Wan et al., 2014). While
we note that sensory-impaired consumers (e.g., color-blind)
naturally experience sensory correspondences differently,
the universality assumption provides a useful foundation
for designing sensory cues that generalize across cultur -
ally diverse audiences. For example, the Bouba/Kiki effect
is established across cultures as people consistently match
rounded-sounding words like “bouba” with round shapes
and sharp-sounding words like “kiki” with spiky shapes
(Ćwiek et al., 2022). Marketers have leveraged the Bouba/
Kiki effect in brand naming, where plosive sounds (e.g., “p”
and “k”) convey distinct sound-symbolic qualities that help
differentiate leading brands from generic ones (Pogacar et
al., 2015).

Type 2: Semantic correspondence
Semantic correspondence refers to the mapping between a
physical stimulus and a semantic consumer response (Hagt-
vedt & Brasel, 2016). In marketing contexts, these mappings
are particularly useful for conveying abstract meanings and
evoking emotional or symbolic associations. For example,
since consumers associate higher spatial placement with
greater power, brands seeking to project authority may need
to refrain from placing their logo lower on packaging or
advertisements (Sundar & Noseworthy, 2014).
A key characteristic of semantic correspondences is that
they can be processed relatively automatically, although to
a lesser extent than sensory correspondences. Evidence for
this can be found in response latency studies, such as the
implicit associations between brand personality traits and
tactile cues (Lee, 2022), as well as computational approaches
linking visual features (e.g., color, complexity) to perceived
brand personality (Peng et al., 2024). As semantic responses
are more abstract than sensory responses, they require a
greater degree of cognitive processing (Hagtvedt & Bra -
sel, 2016). This difference is rooted in perceptual learning
(Spence, 2011), where consumers’ prior experiences shape
how they interpret a given stimulus, leading to variability
in associated meanings. Take sonic branding, for example,
in which consumers learn to instinctively associate specific
auditory cues (e.g., jingles) with particular brands (Spence
& Keller, 2024). For familiar consumers, these associations
are retrieved quickly and with little effort, whereas unfamil-
iar consumers may perceive the same stimulus as ambigu -
ous or meaningless. While semantic correspondences can
become automatic over time, their strength depends on prior
learning and exposure.
A second characteristic of semantic correspondences is
that they are not universally experienced. Unlike sensory
correspondences, which are often assumed to be prelin -
guistic, semantic correspondences are shaped by learned
associations. This can be explained through classical con -
ditioning, whereby repeated pairings of a neutral stimulus
with a meaningful one cause it to elicit a similar response
over time (Bart et al., 2014; Pornpitakpan, 2012). As a result,
semantic meanings attached to stimuli can vary across indi-
viduals and contexts. This is particularly useful for product
line extensions and rebranding, as frequent exposure to new
physical stimuli promotes consumer acceptance by weaken-
ing existing semantic correspondences (Ernst, 2007; Uno &
Yokosawa, 2022).
Type 3: Sensory communication
Sensory communication refers to the mapping between
a lexical stimulus and a sensory consumer response. This
includes crossmodal expressions, such as “heavy odors,”
and intramodal expressions, such as “sweet–sour taste,”
which can manifest in written forms (e.g., slogans and
product descriptions) or in verbal interactions (e.g., sales
assistants speaking about product sensations). In terms of
processing, consumers extract the underlying message from
these expressions through embodied cognition—the process
by which abstract thoughts are grounded in simulations of
concrete sensory experiences (Phillips et al., 2023).
A key characteristic of sensory communication is that the
sensory response must be explicitly mentioned in the lexical
stimulus. Conceptual Metaphor Theory explains that this is
because consumers must map information from a tangible
source domain to an intangible target domain (Lakoff &
Johnson, 1981). That is, understanding sensory information
is anchored in how something feels, looks, sounds, tastes, or
smells (i.e., embodied cognition) (Phillips et al., 2023). In
its simplest form, sensory communication combines a mod-
ifier drawn from the source domain (e.g., an adjective) with
a head representing the target (e.g., a noun) (Werning et al.,
2006). This modifier–head relationship allows consumers
to characterize sensory associations expressed through lan -
guage by drawing on perceptual similarities and interactions
(Martino & Marks, 2001), with comprehension then arising
from the embodiment of key sensory features (Indurkhya,
1991).
Another characteristic of sensory communication is
the vividness of sensory imagery that language can evoke
(Elder & Krishna, 2022; Refaie, 2013). A central concept
here is directionality, which refers to the typical order in
which sensory qualities transfer between words, generally
proceeding from touch, to taste, smell, sound, and finally,
sight (Williams, 1976). For example, “silky taste” seems
more intuitive than “loud taste.” Directionality has practical
implications: offerings that depend on weaker senses face
a narrower range of usable lexical stimuli, a constraint that
is amplified when those senses are physically inaccessible,
such as in social media advertising. However, research
across Turkish, German (Werning et al., 2006), Korean (Jo,
2019), Mandarin (Yu, 2003), and English (Strik-Lievers,
2015) suggests that directionality varies with culture (Wan
et al., 2014; Werning et al., 2006), highlighting that lexical
sensory stimuli will differ across consumer segments.
Type 4: Semantic communication
Semantic communication refers to the mapping between
a lexical stimulus and a semantic consumer response. In
marketing, words drawn from different sensory modali -
ties provide the basis for concrete interpretations (Krishna
& Schwarz, 2014; Melzner & Raghubir, 2023) of abstract
qualities such as brand personality, identity, or symbolic

value (Simner, 2007; Strik-Lievers, 2017). More generally,
semantic communication conveys meaning in ways that
are more vivid and memorable than purely semantic terms
(Akpinar & Berger, 2015). For example, since words like
“hot” and “red” signal anger (Liu, 2023), a brand like Angry
Goat can leverage these dual meanings in its “Red Arma -
dillo Hot Sauce” to both reinforce a brand personality trait
and broaden the appeal of its products.
A key characteristic of semantic communication is that the
target semantic response does not need to be explicitly stated
and can instead be implicitly conveyed. For example, Crystal
Pepsi’s slogan, “You’ve never seen a taste like this,” maps
visual and gustatory cues onto the concept of novelty without
directly stating the word. This implicit communication is pos-
sible because semantic responses are not as strongly rooted in
bodily sensations, given their focus on higher-order meaning.
As such, semantic communication does not retain the same
degree of embodied cognition as sensory communication.
A second key characteristic is that semantic communica-
tion is driven by how consumers interpret the stylistic sen -
sory language that brands use (Cascio Rizzo et al., 2023).
As semantic communication is not strictly bounded by the
directionality principle, it affords brands greater creative
flexibility in applying lexical sensory stimuli. A notable
example is Buckley’s cough mixture, with the slogan “It
tastes awful. And it works,” which uses an unpleasant gus -
tatory cue to counterintuitively convey product efficacy.
Conceptual Metaphor Theory (Lakoff & Johnson, 1981)
suggests that because semantic communication is less reliant
on embodied cognition, it requires richer contextual infor -
mation to convey meaning effectively. For example, Dove’s
“Real Beauty” campaign does not rely on a single slogan.
Instead, it builds meaning through layered sensory language
across touchpoints, using words like “soft” and “natural” in
descriptions, advertisements, and packaging to map tactile
cues onto an intended semantic response of authenticity and
self-worth. Without this contextual scaffolding, these words
in isolation would default to their literal sensory meaning.
Collectively, the four mapping types differ not only by
the stimulus and response, but also by their underlying
mechanisms. Correspondences are driven primarily by the
perceptual processing of physical sensory stimuli, whereas
communications require consumers to interpret lexical stim-
uli to construct meaning. Within these broader categories,
sensory correspondences rely primarily on automatic pro -
cessing, whereas semantic correspondences depend more
heavily on perceptual learning. Likewise, sensory com -
munication operates through embodied cognition, whereas
semantic communication relies on contextual interpretation.
Together, these distinctions demonstrate that sensory map -
pings represent distinct pathways through which sensory
stimuli shape consumer responses.
Conceptual model for sensory mappings
While our typology distinguishes four sensory mapping
types to provide guidance on the configuration of sensory
marketing stimuli, it does not account for the mechanisms
underlying when and why sensory marketing strategies suc-
ceed or fail. Supporting the cartographic nature of sensory
mappings, we develop a conceptual model specifying sen -
sory stimuli as the design starting point, consumer responses
as experiences, and consumer evaluations as destinations
(Fig. 1). The model also illustrates how different sensory
mappings are combined and identifies the boundary condi -
tions of their effectiveness.
Interrelated sensory mappings
To address the second challenge of unaccounted interrela -
tions, we theorize how sensory mappings are combined.
While different sensory stimuli and consumer responses can
be linked in a limitless number of ways, we identify two
main combinations: divergent and convergent sensory map-
pings. We ground each proposition theoretically using key
concepts, practically using real-life cases, and empirically
using sensory marketing research.
Divergent sensory mappings occur when a single stim -
ulus maps onto multiple responses. Most research shows
how a stimulus can elicit positive consumer evaluations via
a specific sensory or semantic response (Biswas & Szocs,
2019; Spence, 2011; Sunaga et al., 2016). However, theo -
retical tension arises when considering unexpected sensory
marketing failures, where a sensory stimulus counterpro -
ductively produces mixed or negative evaluations. The key
concept underlying this tension is unintended consumer
responses, which emerge when a physical or lexical stimu -
lus diverges beyond its intended mapping.
 For a physical stimulus, divergent sensory mapping involves
a combination of sensory correspondences and/or semantic
correspondences. For example, empirical evidence shows that
the color red diverges into sweetness perceptions (Spence,
2019) and excitement as a brand personality trait due to its
longer wavelength relative to other hues (Labrecque & Milne,
2012). While this reflects a beneficial case with positive
responses, there are instances where multiple diverging cor-
respondences produce unintended outcomes. Consider Cad-
bury’s 2013 redesign of its Dairy Milk bar, which involved
replacing rectangular chunks with rounded ones to modern-
ize the brand (semantic correspondence). Customer sentiment
was negative, with many protesting a non-existent increase in
sweetness (Spence, 2013), likely due to the well-documented
sensory correspondence between curved shapes and sweet

intended effects of one correspondence conflicted with the
unintended effects of another, ultimately resulting in ambiva-

P1 A physical stimulus diverges into several sensory and
semantic consumer responses via a combination of sen-
sory correspondences and semantic correspondences.
For a lexical stimulus, divergent sensory mapping includes
a combination of different sensory communications and/or
semantic communications. For example, empirical evidence
shows that the term “juicy” diverges into the direct sensory
experience of consuming a meal (sensory communication)
(Cascio Rizzo et al., 2023) while also increasing its perceived
usefulness and enjoyment (semantic communication) (Yao &
Chen, 2025). However, there are instances where lexical stim-
uli convey unintended meanings. In 2010, Snack Factory’s
Pretzel Crisps faced backlash for the slogan “Tastes as good as
skinny feels,” which was designed to map tactile comfort onto
taste (a sensory communication). However, it instead became
associated with eating disorders (a semantic communication)
due to its resemblance to model Kate Moss’s controversial
mantra, “Nothing tastes as good as skinny feels,” from the
year prior. Here, divergence suggests that the intended effect
of one type of communication was overwritten by the unin-
tended effects of the other, creating unhealthy semantic associ-
ations and, consequently, unfavorable consumer evaluations.
P2 A lexical stimulus diverges into several sensory and
semantic consumer responses via a combination of sen-
sory communications and semantic communications.
Convergent sensory mappings  occur when multiple
stimuli map onto a single consumer response. The key
mechanism underlying convergence is sensory congruence,
whereby stimuli that are aligned with one another may
produce super-additive effects, such that their combined
impact exceeds the sum of their individual effects (Chen
et al., 2022; Krishna et al., 2010). However, convergence
does not always yield positive outcomes. When stimuli are
incongruent, they may result in sub-additive effects (where
the combined effect is weaker than expected) or even sub -
tractive effects (where the combination produces negative
outcomes). We argue that such negative effects often arise
from the misapplication of multisensory stimuli or from a
failure to account for other ambient stimuli in the environ -
ment. These issues cannot be resolved without understand -
ing how several stimuli converge and interact on a shared
consumer response.
 For a single sensory response, convergent sensory mapping
involves combinations of sensory correspondences and/or
sensory communications. For example, Burger King’s 2020
“Moldy Whopper” campaign paired the slogan “The beauty of
no artificial preservatives” with imagery of a moldy burger. The
term “beauty” evokes desirable taste expectations associated

Magnum’s 2026 campaign combined stylized pink ice cream

indulgence (BBC News, 2026). These cues were reinforced

the act of biting into the product. These multimodal cues
are congruent and collectively construct a strong semantic
response of indulgence. However, this effect changed in the
presence of other convergent mappings. When this cam -

shaping perception (Ballouli & Bennett, 2014), prompting
consumers to reallocate their cognitive resources to pro -

with unaccounted competing environmental stimuli.
P4 Multiple stimuli converge onto the same semantic
response via a combination of semantic correspon -

In cartography, travelers can reach the same destina -
tion through multiple routes, many of which intersect and
overlap. Likewise, P1 through P4 show that effective sen -
sory marketing design depends not on a single pathway, but
on understanding how divergent and convergent sensory
mappings jointly shape consumer responses within a broader
marketing environment. Although these propositions are
presented separately for conceptual clarity, they should not
be interpreted as a discrete causal system. Rather, our con -
ceptual model is intended as an analytical framework that
disentangles the network of simultaneously operating and
overlapping sensory mappings linking physical and lexical
stimuli with sensory and semantic consumer responses.
Boundary conditions
To address our third challenge of mixed outcomes, we iden-
tify two boundary conditions and explain how they oper -
ate across different sensory mapping types. As illustrated in
Fig. 1, these conditions include stimuli intensity and indi -
vidual differences, which operate at the point of design and
experience, respectively.
Stimuli intensity refers to the strength of sensory cues.
As stimuli intensity is largely within managerial control, we
position it at the point of design in our conceptual model
(Fig. 1). The inherent differences between physical and lexi-
cal stimuli suggest that stimulus intensity varies in both its
configuration and effects on consumer responses. To pro -
vide a unified theoretical lens, we ground our arguments in
processing fluency, defined as the ease with which informa-
tion is processed (Lee & Labroo, 2004).
 Physical stimuli intensity refers to the quantity and magni-
tude of physical sensory inputs, such as brighter hues, louder
sounds, stronger scents, richer flavors, and softer objects. It
primarily involves bottom-up processing, whereby percep -
tion is driven directly by sensory information rather than
prior knowledge or expectations (Chen, 2024). Prior research
often assumes a positive, linear relationship between physi-
cal stimulus intensity and consumer responses, as stronger
stimuli enhance arousal and perceptions such as product
potency or brand power (Labrecque et al., 2025; Ringler et
al., 2021). However, this assumption holds only for a mod -
erate range of intensity. At higher levels of physical stimuli
intensity, sensory overload occurs due to excessive stimula-
tion, which then overwhelms consumers’ cognitive resources
and reduces processing fluency (Krishna, 2012). Often, this
prompts consumers to disengage from the experience alto -
gether to alleviate discomfort (Doucé & Adams, 2020; Groth
et al., 2024). For example, consumers may avoid virtual
reality (VR) environments due to dense visual stimulation
that induces disorientation (sensory correspondence) and
discomfort (semantic correspondence) (Martins et al., 2017).

At lower levels of physical stimuli intensity, sensory
deprivation occurs due to the limited availability of sen -
sory information (Petit et al., 2019), ultimately reducing
processing fluency and forcing consumers to compensate
by attending to other stimuli (Krishna, 2012). For example,
with haptic blocking (e.g., “Do Not Touch” signs), con -
sumers compensate for the lack of tactile stimulation with
visual exploration (sensory correspondence), which has
been shown to enhance engagement with a broader range
of museum exhibits (Liu et al., 2026). Similarly, dining
in complete darkness can enhance empathy toward visu -
ally impaired staff (semantic correspondence), resulting in
more favorable attitudes and advocacy intentions (Nguyen
& Nguyen, 2025). These studies demonstrate that sensory
overload and deprivation are not necessarily disadvanta -
geous for brands. They merely diminish processing fluency
and, therefore, the magnitude of consumer responses. Nev -
ertheless, to reconcile the conflict between sensory overload
and deprivation, we suggest that stimulus intensity ranges
from low to high, with moderate levels of stimulation maxi-
mizing consumer responses.
P5 Physical stimuli will display an inverted U-shaped rela-
tionship with (a) sensory and (b) semantic consumer
responses at higher levels of stimuli intensity.
Lexical stimuli intensity refers to the intuitiveness of sensory
language. Unlike physical stimuli, lexical stimuli require
more interpretation to extract the underlying message
from language (V on Wallpach & Kreuzer, 2013). As such,
lexical intensity operates along an intuitive-to-counterintu -
itive continuum. At the intuitive end, concrete and familiar
expressions (e.g., “sharp parmesan” or “smooth chocolate”)
improve processing fluency by requiring minimal cognitive
effort to evoke vivid mental imagery (Yoo & Kim, 2014).
At the counterintuitive end, abstract and layered descriptions
(e.g., “a fragrant lavender-honey syrup topped with a vibrant
cloud of whipped mascarpone and a crunch of pistachios”)
combine multiple sensory dimensions in ways that demand
greater cognitive effort. While such descriptions may initially
enrich the imagined sensory experience by providing addi -
tional details (Petrova & Cialdini, 2005), their effectiveness
diminishes as the cognitive load required to process them
increases. That is, as lexical cues become more complex,
they become less intuitive, forcing consumers to expend a
greater degree of cognitive resources to interpret them, thus
reducing processing fluency (Hu & Krishen, 2019).
P6 Lexical stimuli will exhibit positive but diminish -
ing returns on (a) sensory and (b) semantic consumer
responses at higher levels of structural complexity.
Individual differences refer to the heterogeneity of cog -
nitive processing between consumers that shapes their
responses and evaluations. The key concept underlying
these differences is schemas, which refer to the structured
mental representations of prior knowledge and experience
that shape how consumers perceive, interpret, and respond
to new information (Meyers-Levy & Tybout, 1989). Within
the scope of top-down processing, this prior knowledge
and experience systematize into brand-related categories
(e.g., brand personality) (Sundar & Noseworthy, 2014) and
product-related categories (e.g., typicality) (Stayman et al.,
1992) over time. When exposed to a new sensory stimulus,
these categories act as a reference point that shapes con -
sumer responses by triggering product expectations (De
Luca & Botelho, 2020; Gaillet et al., 2013) and brand rec -
ognition (Wang et al., 2022). For example, in 2019, Doritos
removed its logo and name from its ads, yet consumers still
recognized it through its red colors and triangles. Because
schemas create reference points during perception, we posi-
tion individual differences as an experience-level variable
occurring after exposure to sensory stimuli (Fig. 1). In this
section, we identify prior experiences and culture as key
sources of individual differences and explain how they oper-
ate for each sensory mapping type leading up to consumer
evaluations.
 Prior experiences shape how physical stimuli are inter -
preted by activating mental representations of correspon -
dences previously encoded in memory (Elder & Krishna,
2022). As these memories act as reference points, the same
physical stimulus can result in polarizing evaluations. Take
Crystal Pepsi as an example: one who has never tasted cola
has little knowledge of the association between color and
flavor (sensory correspondence) or the brand (semantic
correspondence). Upon tasting it, quality evaluations of its
dark-caramel and dark-Pepsi associations embed into con -
sumers’ schemas within the category of cola drinks. When
exposed to Crystal Pepsi’s lack of color, perceived flavor is
lowered, and brand identity is compromised due to schema
incongruity, leading to lowered quality perceptions.
Culture also shapes how physical stimuli are interpreted.
As such, not all sensory correspondences are equal across
consumers. For example, although Dutch and Turkish
speakers share similar thickness–pitch associations, they
differ in height–pitch associations (Dolscheid et al., 2020).
This implies that, while a car advertisement pairing a large
vehicle with a low-pitched soundtrack may be effective in
both contexts, an added emphasis on the vehicle’s vertical
height may allow the ad to perform better in the Netherlands
than in Turkey. Similarly, culture embeds meaning and sym-
bols within certain physical sensory stimuli (Yao & Chen,

2025). Often, these semantic correspondences are encoded
deeply in consumer cognition due to a naturally high level

Even without direct product exposure, this can bias evalu -

P7 Individual differences derived from prior experience
and culture shape the relationship between physical
sensory stimuli and consumer evaluations.
Prior experiences shape consumer learning and alter how
they interpret lexical stimuli. As opposed to simply read -
ing, consumers learn more efficiently when lexical stimuli
gained from prior experiences are used to showcase their
knowledge (Maciel & Wallendorf, 2017). This is appar -
ent in aestheticized markets, which are often laden with
ambiguous and figurative sensory language (Suárez-Toste,
2017). For example, beer aficionados exhibit their profi -
ciency by describing craft beer flavors with specific phrases
like “sharp taste of molasses” (Maciel & Wallendorf, 2017).
Similarly, in tourism reviews, customers describe their sen-
sory experiences (V on Wallpach & Kreuzer, 2013) using
lexical stimuli to report on their bodily sensations (Fenko
et al., 2010). Overall, the more consumers learn to use and
interpret lexical stimuli, the more likely they are to under -
stand and appreciate products and services (Latour, 2019).
Culture also shapes how lexical stimuli are interpreted.
In wine culture, for example, sommeliers were more suc -
cessful in improving customers’ learning and memory by
using words related to spiky and smooth shapes to describe
the taste structure of wine, which is notoriously challenging
to explain (Latour & Deighton, 2019). To those unfamiliar
with wine culture, sensory communication of this nature can
enhance abstractedness because the associations between
senses are not encountered in everyday experiences. As
a result, the mental imagery evoked by such descrip -
tors, which depends on whether the sensory properties are
mapped intuitively (El Refaie, 2013), is reduced, inhibiting
overall evaluations. Additionally, different cultures attach
unique meanings to lexical stimuli. For example, Chinese
consumers prefer auspiciously named foods as they repre -
sent “fortune” (Youn et al., 2021).
P8 Individual differences derived from prior experience
and culture shape the relationship between lexical sen -
sory stimuli and consumer evaluations.
In cartography, the same path may be easier or harder for
a traveler to traverse depending on the surrounding condi -
tions. Similarly, P5 through P8 demonstrate that sensory
mappings are inherently context-dependent. Rather than
assuming that the relationships proposed in Fig. 1 are fixed,
stimuli intensity and individual differences provide nuance
to our analytical framework by explaining how the same
sensory mapping can be strengthened or weakened, which
then produces different consumer responses and evaluations.
Discussion
To address construct proliferation, unaccounted interrela -
tionships, and mixed outcomes within sensory marketing
research, we propose a theory and analytical framework
that distinguishes different types of sensory mappings and
explores how their combinations and boundary conditions
impact consumer responses and evaluations. In doing so,
we offer theoretical and managerial implications for sensory
marketing research and practice.
Theoretical implications
Typology We provide an interdisciplinary literature review
(Table 1) that, to our knowledge, is the first to showcase the
proliferation of sensory mapping constructs. This motivates
the development of a typology (Table 2) that underpins our
delineating theoretical contribution. Specifically, our work
differs from prior conceptualizations that have focused on
individual sensory stimuli (e.g., multisensory stimuli, sen -
sory triggers, stimuli) (Krishna, 2012; Pandey & Tripathi,
2025; Petit et al., 2019) and responses (e.g., emotional reac-
tions, sensory experiences) (Bhatia et al., 2021; Wörfel et
al., 2022) by introducing four distinct types of sensory map-
pings as the conceptual bridges between them. In addition
to distinguishing the underlying mechanisms of each type,
we open up avenues to advance sensory marketing research
beyond its traditional emphasis on physical stimuli towards
lexical stimuli, which has largely been absent in prior work
(Pandey & Tripathi, 2025). These efforts allow us to respond
to calls for “research exploring the effect of verbal informa-
tion on sensory perception” (Krishna, 2012, p. 347). Overall,
our typology serves as the foundation for advancing different
subfields of sensory marketing as it integrates how physical
and lexical stimuli generate multisensory experiences.
Conceptual model In practice, physical and lexical stimuli
are combined simultaneously: ads pair slogans and images,
and packaging pairs product photos and descriptions. Yet,
there is little guidance on how the bottom-up sensory

perception from direct stimulation and the top-down seman-
tic processing of textual information collectively shape
consumer responses. We address this gap with a concep -
tual model (Fig. 1) that illustrates how physical and lexical
sensory stimuli diverge (P1–P2) and converge (P3–P4) on
consumer responses. By discussing how sensory responses
(often associated with functional value) and semantic
responses (often associated with affective and symbolic
value) are triggered by sensory stimuli, we respond to calls
to move beyond the prior separation between functional and
emotional dimensions of sensory marketing effects (Wörfel
et al., 2022).
To enrich our theorization, we identify boundary condi -
tions at the point of sensory marketing design and sensory
experience. At the point of design, we show how stimuli
intensity shapes the effectiveness of sensory stimuli on
consumer responses. For physical stimuli intensity, some
studies find a positive linear relationship with consumer
evaluations (Becker & Jaakkola, 2020; De Luca & Botelho,
2020; Hagtvedt, 2020; Imschloss & Kuehnl, 2019; Krishna
et al., 2010), while others document diminishing returns at
higher levels of intensity (Cowan & Ketron, 2019; Doucé &
Adams, 2020; Krishna, 2012). We reconcile these conflicts
through a curvilinear relationship where moderate levels of
physical stimulation are most effective in generating con -
sumer responses (P5). In doing so, we extend prior work
on curvilinear effects in specific sensory domains (e.g.,
olfaction; Zhang & Spence, 2023) more generally towards
all sensory modalities. More importantly, we expand the
concept of stimuli intensity, which has primarily focused on
physical stimuli, toward lexical stimuli intensity, conceptu-
alized along an intuitive–counterintuitive continuum (P6).
Whereas physical stimuli vary in arousal, lexical stimuli
differ in the degree of cognitive effort required to mentally
simulate sensory information. Thus, we show that lexical
complexity is beneficial up to a certain point as the underly-
ing message can become too counterintuitive. This positive
but diminishing effect further substantiates our theoretical
delineation contribution as it highlights differences in how
physical and lexical stimuli are cognitively processed and
shaped, which then reinforces the need to treat various stim-
uli and their sensory mappings differently.
At the point of experience, we show how individual dif -
ferences derived from prior experiences and culture shape
the effectiveness of sensory stimuli on consumer responses
and evaluations (P7–P8). By explaining the polarization
of responses and evaluations from diverse consumer back -
grounds, we extend prior research on the cultural depen -
dence of sensory perception (Bremner et al., 2013; Chen et
al., 2016; Ćwiek et al., 2022). Specifically, by proposing that
prior experiences shape future sensory experiences, we offer
a longitudinal perspective on sensory mappings that is largely
absent in current sensory marketing research. This answers
calls to investigate how embedded associations can be updated
or corrected when new contextual information becomes salient
(De Luca & Botelho, 2020). Through both ethnic and con -
sumer cultures, we also provide insights into how meanings
are contextualized within sensory stimuli (e.g., differences in
directional use of lexical stimuli across cultures).
Both our typology and conceptual model provide an
integrated analytical framework that clarifies the structure,
interrelations, and boundary conditions of sensory map -
pings, advancing the conceptual precision and explanatory
power of sensory marketing research.
Managerial implications
Our research also provides guidance for managers to avoid
marketing design failures and plan for effective multisen -
sory experiences. To explicate both, we follow Ulaga et al.’s
(2021) approach of drawing managerial relevance directly
from research propositions.
Preventing sensory marketing failures  Our propositions
caution managers against actions that may harm the brand,
waste resources, or provoke backlash. P1 and P2 caution
against unintended consumer responses arising from the
divergence of sensory stimuli. Managers should avoid over-
emphasizing intended consumer responses and instead be
aware of other associations with that same sensory stimu -
lus. This includes understanding the broader context of the
brand’s offering, such as the industry in which it operates.
For example, while the color red can convey excitement
(Spence, 2019) and may be effective for a candy brand due
to red’s association with sweetness (Labrecque & Milne,
2012), managers should rely less on it in wellness contexts
since it can signal artificiality or even danger.
P3 and P4 caution against sub-additive or subtractive effects
arising from the convergence of stimuli to an intended con-
sumer response. Managers should note potential conflicts
among stimuli within the offering itself. For example, a man-
ager pairing a black color scheme meant to convey authority
with a rounded typeface associated with playfulness would
risk diluting the intended brand personality. Additionally,
managers need to consider conflicting stimuli in the con -
sumer’s vicinity by evaluating the locations of exposure. For
example, in retail environments where competing brands are
presented together, managers must account for how rivals’
stimuli may weaken the focal brand’s intended positioning.
P5 and P6 caution managers against incorrect levels of
stimulus intensity that may cause consumers to disengage.
In most cases, a “Goldilocks” approach is warranted, where

managers identify stimulus intensity that is neither too high
nor too low, but just right. However, this logic reverses if
there is a negative relationship between consumer response
and evaluation. For example, in medical contexts where
injections are painful yet necessary, reducing pain-related
responses is vital. Since the sight of needles elicits fear and
pain (Kılıç et al., 2021), managers might consider depriving
visual input or overloading other senses through audiovisual
distractions such as VR or television (Le May et al., 2021).
P7 and P8 caution managers against using the same sen -
sory marketing strategies across all consumers. Even though
it is tempting given the prospect of saving costs, it could end
up eroding profit margins. Managers should thus account
for individual differences by ensuring that psychographic
segmentation encompasses the sensory experiences that cus-
tomers might have been exposed to. For example, managers
should not engage audiences with ASMR (Autonomous Sen-
sory Meridian Response) despite its popularity with younger
customers unless their target customer associates it more with
comfort than disgust. Similarly, managers for global brands
should plan for cultural biases before implementing sensory
stimuli. For example, white is often associated with purity in
Western markets, but represents death in some Asian cultures,
which can polarize evaluations when used for products.
Becoming a sensory marketing cartographer  Our concep-
tual model suggests that effective sensory marketing also
requires a holistic application of sensory mappings, from
stimuli to evaluations. Managers can do this by drawing on
all propositions simultaneously.
Consider a multisensory fine-dining experience. Restaurant
managers might consider how a heavy leather menu diverges
into the perceived richness and luxury of dishes (P1). Upon
reading the menu, descriptors like “crisp pancetta shards”
also diverge into anticipation of the food’s mouthfeel along-
side perceptions of culinary craftsmanship (P2). The addi -
tion of dim candlelight and velvet furniture converges into
the perceived density of red wine (P3), while the somme -
lier’s refined language and a hand-engraved wine list signal
that the establishment is upscale and curated (P4). However,
when ambient scents are too subtle or too overpowering,
both sensory pleasure and upscale inferences are dimin -
ished (P5a, P5b). Likewise, descriptors like “sumptuously
aromatic heritage carrots with cloud-like chèvre foam,”
although fancy, become increasingly counterintuitive with
every additional sensory word, yielding diminishing returns
on anticipated taste and perceived sophistication (P6a, P6b).
Ultimately, how diners respond to these stimuli depends on
their individual interpretation. The leather menu, for exam-
ple, can either be perceived as a deliberate material choice
or as cumbersome (P7). Similarly, a food critic may relish
“crisp pancetta shards” as evocative whereas a more casual
diner may find the language less meaningful (P8).
We wish to emphasize that it is not necessary to draw on
all propositions in every context. Some advertisements, for
example, rely solely on physical stimuli and therefore need
not consider propositions pertaining to lexical cues. The goal
is to ensure that all relevant pathways from stimuli to con -
sumer evaluations are accounted for in the planning process.
Future research agenda
In Table 3, we present a future research agenda organized
around three themes (i.e., individual, interrelated, and mod-
erated sensory mappings) corresponding to the three research
challenges (i.e., construct proliferation, unaccounted inter -
relations, and mixed outcomes). Each subtheme is grounded
theoretically using our typology or conceptual model, fol -
lowed by research directions that enable future scholars to
develop their own research questions.
Theme 1: Individual sensory mappings
Theme 1 focuses on the configurations of specific sensory
stimuli for specific consumer responses, providing insights
for stimulus design. Future research on sensory correspon -
dences (Type 1) can examine the effectiveness of physical
stimuli in eliciting sensory responses across modalities. By
examining differences between crossmodal and intramodal
processing, future research would uncover the distinct per -
ceptual mechanisms that underly sensory processing. Future
research on semantic correspondences (Type 2) can exam -
ine how new physical stimuli can be used to unlearn prior
semantic associations. This could generate insights into the
mechanisms through which new sensory experiences reshape
established semantic associations, which are vital for brand
image, repositioning and product line extensions. For the
design of lexical stimuli, future research on sensory com -
munication (Type 3) can examine the effectiveness of using
lexical cues to shape crossmodal and intramodal sensory
responses. For example, scholars can explore how the direc-
tionality of sensory transfer between words varies with syn-
tactic structure (e.g., adjective–noun vs. noun–noun). These
insights would reveal the mechanisms through which lexical
stimuli activate mental sensory representations and help mar-
keters design engaging product descriptions. Future research
on semantic communications (Type 4) can identify various
formats of explicit and implicit target semantic responses and
their link to sensory language. Such work could uncover the
mechanisms through which lower-order sensory language
translates into higher-order semantic evaluations, which is
crucial for branding and creating symbolic value.

on sensory marketing. Additionally, it would be valuable to

properties of products and services.
