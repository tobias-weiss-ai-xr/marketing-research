# A comprehensive analysis of FinTech (1968–2025): a bibliometric approach

Mohammed R. M. Salem1* and Shahida Shahimi1
Abstract
This study provides a comprehensive bibliometric analysis of FinTech research spanning from 1968 to 2025, using
2760 articles indexed in the Web of Science database. It aims to uncover major publication trends, core theoretical
frameworks, emerging topics, and the intellectual structure of FinTech scholarship. Employing VOSviewer and Har-
zing’s Publish or Perish software, this study maps co-occurrence networks, citation structures, and thematic clusters.
It analyzes document types, source distribution, geographical contributions, keyword evolution, and the top 10
most cited papers in FinTech literature. The analysis reveals a significant surge in FinTech research since 1968, driven
by the growing impact of digital finance innovations. The top three countries contributing to FinTech publications
are the USA, England, and China. Dominant publication outlets include the International Journal of Bank Marketing
and the Journal of Financial Services Marketing. Key research themes have evolved across three distinct periods: early
banking and innovation (1968–1999), customer satisfaction and trust (2000–2011), and bank performance and digi-
tal adoption (2012–2025). Emerging topics include blockchain, mobile banking, crowdfunding, and Internet bank-
ing. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), along with its extended versions (TAM2, TAM3, UTAUT), is identified
as the foundational theoretical framework in this field. The co-citation and keyword cluster analysis confirm the cen-
trality of trust, risk, satisfaction, and performance in shaping FinTech outcomes. These findings not only synthesize
FinTech’s academic development but also inform future research by identifying intellectual gaps and high-impact
trends. The study highlights the growing integration between FinTech and consumer behavior and calls for deeper
exploration into regulatory, ethical, and cybersecurity issues affecting FinTech adoption. Beyond the banking sector,
the thematic patterns uncovered particularly in areas such as blockchain-based supply chain finance, crowdfunding
ecosystems, and AI-enabled embedded financial services signal substantial strategic implications for non-financial
firms. These include enhanced liquidity management, decentralized capital access, and data-driven business model
innovation across diverse industries such as manufacturing, retail, and digital commerce.

a longitudinal, theory-linked, and conceptually compre -
hensive synthesis of the FinTech domain. Moreover, sev -
eral prior bibliometric reviews concentrate narrowly on
subsets such as mobile payments [11–13] or blockchain
applications [37], often neglecting the broader theoreti -
cal and conceptual underpinnings that drive the FinTech
discourse. There is also insufficient cross-comparison
between mature and emerging economies, and limited
integration of interdisciplinary perspectives spanning
finance, technology, and regulation [24]. To bridge these
gaps, the present study employs a bibliometric method -
ology aimed at mapping the intellectual structure and
thematic trends of FinTech research. By offering a multi-
decade, multi-thematic synthesis, it contributes to a
more integrated and theory-informed understanding of
the field’s progression. This approach enables a system -
atic exploration of the concepts, themes, and theoreti -
cal foundations that shape the field, providing valuable
insight into its development and trajectory.
The core problem addressed by this paper is the
absence of an integrative bibliometric synthesis that
identifies emerging research hotspots, thematic shifts,
and the dominant intellectual drivers behind FinTech
development. FinTech is increasingly recognized as a
strategic mechanism for addressing structural inefficien -
cies in financial systems by enhancing operational per -
formance, regulatory adaptability, and customer-centric
innovation [1, 38]. Moreover, traditional financial institu-
tions face growing pressure to embrace FinTech tools to
enhance profitability, regulatory compliance, and service
innovation in line with evolving consumer expectations
[22]. This study uses bibliometric tools to systematically
explore the growth trajectories, co-citation networks,
keyword co-occurrence clusters, and influential theo -
retical frameworks that shape FinTech research. Unlike
narrative reviews, which are susceptible to subjective
bias, bibliometric analysis provides empirical objectivity
by quantifying scholarly output and mapping intercon -
nections across thousands of documents [30, 66]. This
approach enables a systematic and transparent inves -
tigation of the field’s evolution, providing evidence-
based insight into its intellectual structure and strategic
trajectory.
The field of FinTech research Has progressed beyond
its early exploratory phase and now exhibits substantial
empirical and theoretical development. Between 2010
and mid-2025, over 2,500 peer-reviewed articles on Fin -
Tech have been published across disciplines including
finance, economics, information systems, and strategic

Bibliometric analysis for FinTech research
Bibliometrics and citation analysis are widely recognized
as essential methodologies within the field of information
science, offering robust tools for evaluating the struc -
ture and dynamics of scholarly communication (Gómez-
Jáuregui et  al. 2014). Bibliometric analysis involves the
application of mathematical and statistical techniques
to examine various forms of academic output, including
journal articles, books, and other communication media
[19, 47], Pritchard 1969. Among the most frequently
used bibliometric indicators are citation counts, publi -
cation impact, authorship patterns, and country-level
contributions, all of which are instrumental in assessing
the intellectual output and influence of research domains
(Ahmi and Mohammad 2019). Numerous studies have
employed bibliometric techniques to analyze the con -
ceptual structures of academic disciplines and to identify
emergent themes within established bodies of knowledge
[25, 37, 43].
To facilitate the analysis and visualization of scholarly
networks, this study utilizes VOSviewer [66], a widely
used software tool for constructing bibliometric maps
and exploring disciplinary relationships. This approach
is particularly effective for identifying the core intellec -
tual concerns and thematic clusters within a research
field. Co-citation analysis, which reveals the strength of
relationships between publications through shared refer -
ences, is employed in this study to uncover both strong
and weak intellectual ties. This method has been exten -
sively validated in prior research [11–13, 36, 43] (Shiau
et al. 2017). In addition, the study uses Harzing’s Publish
or Perish software [30] to compute citation metrics and
other bibliographic performance indicators. Specifically,

three tools are adopted in this analysis: VOSviewer to
construct and visualize bibliometric networks; Microsoft
Excel to calculate frequency distributions and to generate
relevant graphs and charts; and Harzing’s Publish or Per -

To ensure transparency and replicability, the bibliomet-
ric process followed a structured, multi-phase approach.
The procedure began with the selection of Web of Sci -
ence as the primary data source, given its consistent
indexing criteria and high-quality metadata. A compre -
hensive keyword strategy was developed by synthesiz -
ing search terms from academic thesauri, expert input,
and prior FinTech-related reviews [37]. The search query
was operationalized using Boolean operators to extract
records containing relevant FinTech terms in titles,
abstracts, and author keywords. Following data retrieval,
a refinement phase was conducted to remove dupli -
cates, non-peer-reviewed documents, and records that
were conceptually irrelevant to the research scope. The
resulting dataset was cleaned and standardized to ensure
analytical precision. VOSviewer was then employed to
construct keyword co-occurrence networks and citation-
based clusters. Harzing’s Publish or Perish software was
used to extract bibliometric indicators and author-level
citation data, while Microsoft Excel supported the tabu -
lation and visualization of frequency distributions.
The study selected co-citation analysis and keyword
co-occurrence mapping over co-authorship and bib -
liographic coupling due to their stronger alignment with
the study’s objectives. Co-citation is well-established for
identifying the intellectual foundations of a field [62, 73],
while keyword co-occurrence reveals thematic structures
and research trends [19, 66]. These methods are most
appropriate for addressing RQ2 and RQ3, which focus
on theoretical frameworks and emerging hotspots. This
approach reflects best practices in bibliometric research
aimed at conceptual mapping [7]. Finally, the study pur -
sued theoretical alignment by interpreting emergent
clusters in light of established conceptual frameworks,
primarily the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)
and its derivatives [69]. This integration of empirical
mapping with theory-guided interpretation allows for
deeper insights into the intellectual structure of FinTech
research.
Literature review
Foundational literature on financial innovation (Pre‑2015)
The evolution of FinTech cannot be fully understood
without engaging with the foundational literature on
financial innovation that predates the 2015 boom. Mer -
ton [45] advanced the notion of financial innovation as
a functional response to systemic inefficiencies, such as
information asymmetry and illiquidity. Tufano [65] later
offered a comprehensive taxonomy of financial innova -
tion, distinguishing between product and process inno -
vations that fundamentally alter capital allocation. Allen
and Santomero [5] emphasized the role of technological
advancements in reshaping intermediation processes,
while Frame and White [20] provided empirical classifi -
cations that anchor FinTech within a broader continuum
of financial evolution. These seminal works laid the con -
ceptual groundwork for understanding FinTech not as a
disruptive anomaly, but as the logical extension of long -
standing structural transformations in finance.
Recent advances in FinTech scholarship (2022–2023)
Recent FinTech literature published in top-tier jour -
nals has refined the theoretical and empirical contours
of the field. For example, Chen, Huang, and Lin [16], in
the Journal of Finance, reveal how decentralized finance
(DeFi) creates novel systemic risks through cross-plat -
form liquidity dynamics. Frost et  al. [21] explore the
implications of central bank digital currencies (CBDCs),
highlighting the tensions between digital innovation and
macro-financial stability. Rai and Tarafdar [58] investigate
algorithmic credit scoring within platform economies,
finding that institutional logics mediate both consumer
behavior and firm-level outcomes. These contributions
represent a new wave of FinTech scholarship rigorous,
data-driven, and policy-relevant thereby enhancing the
contemporary relevance and depth of the present review.
Source of data
Selection of database
Scopus, Google Scholar and Web of Science database are
considered the main databases for the citation indexes
and academic literature. These databases provide impor -
tant and sufficient stability of coverage to be used for
detailed cross-disciplinary comparisons [30]. For both
Scopus and Web of Science, the coverage is almost simi -
lar. However, Google Scholar includes software, books,
book chapters and publications in journals not included
in the former. The search conducted by this study on Fin-
Tech in Google Scholar returned more than 91,500
results. However, the quality control process in Google
Scholar is not strong and it brings any data available in
related websites. For this reason, this study chose Web of
Science database to start and do the analysis. This choice
reflects the need for curated, high-quality bibliographic
metadata and peer-reviewed content, which Web of Sci -
ence offers with greater consistency and replicability.
This study selected Web of Science over Scopus due to
its more stringent journal selection process, standard -
ized bibliographic metadata, and superior compatibility
with co-citation and co-authorship analysis tools such as

Keyword development strategy
This study conducted a comprehensive search across
multiple fields namely, title, abstract, and author key -
words using the term FinTech and its synonymous vari -
ants, including financial technology, digital finance,
e-finance, and digitization. To ensure a methodologically
rigorous search, the study employed a structured key -
word formulation strategy comprising four distinct steps.
The study followed four main steps to form out the main
search string and keywords. First step is looking for Fin -
Tech synonyms from Websites like (Saurus) and others.
Second step is collecting synonym keywords from WOS
database. Third step is taking an expert opinion about the
proper keywords to be used in such topic. Last step is col-
lecting keyword synonyms from other Q1 and Q2 articles
in same related topic. Finally, the researcher along with
the expert prepared the search string for the keywords
and refined it to get suitable articles for this analysis.
Search string formulation
To ensure comprehensive retrieval of FinTech-related
literature, we developed a structured Boolean search
query applied across titles, abstracts, and author key -
words in the Web of Science Core Collection. The query
combined multiple synonym sets related to FinTech
(e.g.,"digital finance,""e-finance,""mobile banking"), tech -
nology (e.g.,"blockchain,"" AI,""cloud computing"), and
financial services (e.g.,"bank,""lending,""payment sys -
tems"), while excluding irrelevant terms. The full search
string is provided in Appendix A for transparency and
reproducibility.
Index scope and temporal coverage
This study employed a structured search strategy across
multiple indexes within the Web of Science Core Col -
lection, including the Science Citation Index Expanded
(SCI-EXPANDED), Social Sciences Citation Index
(SSCI), Arts & Humanities Citation Index (A&HCI),
Conference Proceedings Citation Index-Science (CPCI-
S), Conference Proceedings Citation Index-Social Science
& Humanities (CPCI-SSH), and Emerging Sources Cita -
tion Index (ESCI). This study first selected all the years
until 2025; however, the study repeated the same search
for each year to analyze the time evolution. The search
retrieved more than 3527 documents before refining.
This ensured a longitudinal understanding of FinTech
research trends across time and across various discipli -
nary lenses.
Screening and exclusion criteria
Web of Science does not include reviews, working
papers, non-indexed journals. To ensure thematic con -
sistency, a refinement process was undertaken. The study
followed four main steps to form out main search string
and keywords. To present the relevant topic, the study
used and focused on the title of the articles as this is sig -
nificant with the research area and the aim of research
paper. In contrast to scoping or systematic reviews that
follow the PRISMA protocol to successively narrow the
literature toward a small set of highly focused studies,
bibliometric analysis is designed to process and interpret
large volumes of scholarly output. The goal is not to min -
imize the dataset but to retain a broad, thematically rel -
evant corpus suitable for algorithmic examination using
tools such as VOSviewer and Bibliometrix [7, 19]. As
such, refinement in bibliometric research does not rely
on rigid exclusion frameworks but instead applies quality
control to ensure dataset validity and relevance.
This study followed four academically grounded refine -
ment steps. First, duplicate records were removed to
prevent analytical bias in co-authorship or co-citation
networks. Second, non-peer-reviewed publications
including editorials, conference abstracts, and gray litera-
ture were excluded to maintain academic rigor. Third, the
dataset was limited to English-language, indexed journal
articles to ensure methodological consistency. Fourth, a
manual thematic screening was conducted on titles and
abstracts to eliminate documents that did not clearly
align with the conceptual domain of FinTech. This refine-
ment approach ensured that the final set of 2760 records
was both high-quality and sufficiently comprehensive to
enable robust bibliometric mapping and network analy -
sis. These criteria are summarized as follows:

Analytical software and rationale
To analyze and interpret the intellectual architecture
of the 2760-article corpus, this study employed two
widely recognized bibliometric tools: VOSviewer (ver -
sion 1.6.20) and Harzing’s Publish or Perish (version 8).
VOSviewer was selected due to its advanced visualization
of similarities (VOS) clustering technique, which pro -
duces high-fidelity bibliometric maps by optimizing node
positioning based on normalized link strength [66]. This
algorithmic precision enables the generation of keyword
co-occurrence networks, co-citation structures, and bib -
liographic coupling maps with a high degree of interpre -
tive clarity. Importantly, VOSviewer efficiently processes
datasets exceeding 10,000 records without compromising
computational performance, which is particularly criti -
cal given the size and complexity of our data. Moreover,
the software’s capacity to generate interactive, zoomable,
and color-coded visualizations has made it the preferred
tool in numerous bibliometric studies within finance and
technology domains [19, 47].
In parallel, Harzing’s Publish or Perish (PoP) was
employed to extract citation-based metrics and validate
author-level and article-level indicators from the Web
of Science database. PoP’s integrated search and batch
export features facilitated the computation of citation
indices (e.g., h-index, g-index, i10-index) and enabled tri-
angulation of bibliometric metadata for improved accu -
racy and reproducibility [30]. While alternative platforms
such as Bibliometrix [7], CiteSpace [14], or Gephi offer
comparable analytical functions, VOSviewer’s superior
performance in visualizing large-scale bibliometric struc -
tures and PoP’s unique ability to consolidate multi-source
citation data render this combination particularly robust,
efficient, and methodologically sound for the aims of
this study. Together, these tools meet current standards
for computational transparency, analytical rigor, and
replicability in science mapping and strategic literature
synthesis.

Underpinning theories
A central theoretical framework underpinning FinTech
adoption research is the Technology Acceptance Model
(TAM), originally developed by Davis and later expanded
by Bagozzi, Davis, and Warshaw [10]. This model is
prominently featured in the co-occurrence maps across
the second and third periods of this study, underscor -
ing its enduring influence. Over time, TAM has evolved
through several iterations, including TAM2, TAM3, and
the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology
(UTAUT), each incorporating additional constructs to
better explain user acceptance of technological systems
in increasingly complex contexts [68, 69].
Technology acceptance model (TAM)
TAM explains users’ acceptance and actual use of tech -
nology through three core constructs: perceived useful -
ness (PU), perceived ease of use (PEOU), and behavioral
intention (BI), which together predict actual system use.
PU refers to the extent to which an individual believes
that using a system enhances their job performance,
while PEOU denotes the degree to which the system is
perceived as free of effort. These two perceptions jointly
shape the user’s attitude and intention to use the technol-
ogy. Later research emphasized the influence of external
variables such as social norms and user demographics on
these perceptions [40, 50]. Thus, the model provides a
parsimonious yet powerful framework to explain individ -
ual-level technology adoption behaviors.
Unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT)
To address limitations in the original TAM, Venkatesh,
Morris, Davis, and Davis [69] developed UTAUT, inte -
grating elements from eight prominent models of user
acceptance. UTAUT introduces four core predictors:
performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influ -
ence, and facilitating conditions, which are moderated
by gender, age, experience, and voluntariness of use. This
model offers enhanced explanatory power, accounting
for up to 70% of the variance in behavioral intention and
approximately 50% in actual system use [69]. UTAUT’s
holistic approach makes it particularly suitable for study -
ing FinTech adoption, where sociodemographic and con-
textual factors often shape user engagement with digital
financial services.

Technology acceptance model 3 (TAM3)
Building upon its predecessors, TAM3 was introduced by
Venkatesh and Bala [68] to further unpack the anteced -
ents of PU and PEOU. TAM3 identifies four categories
of determinants: individual differences (e.g., age, per -
sonality), system characteristics (e.g., interface design),
social influence, and facilitating conditions. This nuanced
framework reflects the maturity of technology use by
2008, particularly in banking contexts, where user experi-
ence, trust, and perceived risk became critical adoption
factors (Karahanna et  al. 1999, Koufaris 2002). TAM3
remains a dominant model in recent FinTech studies,
offering refined tools to analyze how users evaluate and
interact with emerging financial technologies.
Theoretical integration and application in FinTech
The sequential evolution from TAM to UTAUT and
TAM3 reflects the increasing complexity of user interac -
tions with technology, particularly in the financial sector.
Early FinTech systems faced adoption barriers due to user
unfamiliarity and trust issues. As banking technologies
became more integrated into daily life, user expectations
and behavioral drivers also evolved. The prominence of
keywords such as user acceptance, customer satisfac -
tion, trust, and internet banking in the bibliometric
maps (Figs.  1 and 2) aligns closely with the theoretical
constructs of TAM-based models. Notably, recent stud -
ies have confirmed the robustness of these models in
explaining FinTech adoption [15, 52, 71]. In light of these
findings, this study confirms that TAM and its extensions
continue to offer valuable explanatory frameworks for
understanding how users interact with FinTech. How -
ever, given the increasing systemic complexity of digital
finance, there is a growing need to complement these
models with broader institutional and ecosystem-level
theories. This will be further discussed in the following
subsection on theoretical integration.
Integration of theoretical and empirical insights
The co-occurrence and cluster analyses presented in this
study align meaningfully with the core constructs of the
Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and its exten -
sions. Empirically, the prominence of keywords such as
user acceptance, perceived risk, trust, and behavioral
intention reinforces the theoretical emphasis on how
individuals evaluate and adopt fintech innovations. The
evolution of fintech research from technology-driven
banking solutions to consumer-focused performance
and service quality mirrors the conceptual shift embed -
ded in TAM3, where external variables and perceived
usefulness increasingly shape user behavior. This con -
vergence between theory and empirical trends highlights
the robustness of TAM-based frameworks in explaining
fintech adoption patterns, and underscores the value of
bibliometric methods in validating theoretical models
across time.
While the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and
its extensions (TAM2, TAM3, UTAUT) offer valuable
insights into user-level technology adoption behaviors,
they fall short in capturing the broader institutional,
structural, and ecosystem-level dynamics characterizing
the relationship between FinTech and traditional bank -
ing. Specifically, the interaction between FinTech and
incumbent financial institutions is not merely a mat -
ter of end-user acceptance but represents a dynamic,
co-evolutionary process involving phases of disruption,
adaptation, integration, and eventual symbiosis [29, 35].
These interactions occur across multiple levels techno -
logical, organizational, regulatory, and societal which
necessitates the use of more comprehensive theoretical
frameworks. To address this complexity, Technology-
Institution Co-Evolution Theory offers a more suitable
lens. This framework explains how technological inno -
vations and institutional structures mutually influence
and evolve in tandem over time [49, 51]. In the context
of FinTech, banks adapt their strategies, infrastructures,
and regulatory engagements in response to the disruptive
potential of emerging technologies, while FinTech firms
simultaneously adjust their business models to align with
institutional norms and market structures. This mutual
shaping reflects a deeper, system-level transformation not
accounted for by TAM’s individual behavioral constructs.
Moreover, the application of Ecosystem Theory par -
ticularly digital business ecosystem models provides
a holistic understanding of how FinTech innovation
unfolds within an interconnected network of actors
including banks, technology providers, regulators, and
consumers [2, 34]. This perspective emphasizes co-
dependence, knowledge flows, platform dynamics, and
institutional interoperability as drivers of innovation
and integration. Rather than viewing technology adop -
tion as a linear process, ecosystem theory conceptual -
izes FinTech as part of a co-constructed environment
where value creation emerges through collaborative and
competitive interactions across the ecosystem.Therefore,
while TAM remains a foundational model for under -
standing consumer adoption behavior, its integration
with institutional and ecosystem-based theories would
enhance the explanatory power and analytical depth of
FinTech–banking research. Future studies should con -
sider adopting a multi-theoretical approach to more
effectively analyze the evolving nature of FinTech, par -
ticularly its systemic impact on financial institutions, reg-
ulatory environments, and industry architectures.

problems that call for coordinated efforts by regulators,
legislators, and FinTech businesses. In order to maintain
the FinTech industry’s sustainable growth and its ongoing
contribution to the development of the financial sector,
it is imperative that these issues be resolved. Therefore,
more study on these hot-button issues is needed, with a
focus on how to get over ethical conundrums, cybersecu -
rity risks, and regulatory obstacles in the FinTech sector.
Implications for policymakers and firms
The findings of this study carry substantial implications
for policymakers and financial institutions navigating the
evolving FinTech landscape. The sustained rise of block -
chain, digital identity, and mobile payment themes from
2012 onward highlights the urgency of developing adap -
tive, technology-neutral regulatory frameworks. Policy -
makers should institutionalize regulatory sandboxes and
innovation hubs to support supervised FinTech experi -
mentation while maintaining market integrity and con -
sumer protection [8, 75]. Furthermore, as FinTech firms
increasingly operate across borders, harmonizing data
privacy and security standards particularly between
frameworks like the EU’s GDPR and China’s PIPL can
mitigate compliance fragmentation and facilitate global
digital finance integration [53].
For firms, particularly incumbent banks, the thematic
evolution observed in this study underscores three man -
agerial imperatives: investing in AI-powered regtech
systems for dynamic compliance and fraud detection,
integrating blockchain-based Know-Your-Customer
(KYC) infrastructure to reduce onboarding friction [52]
and establishing dedicated FinTech strategy units to fos -
ter innovation absorption and organizational agility [15].
These measures translate bibliometric trends into action -
able strategies that can enhance resilience, competitive -
ness, and regulatory alignment within an increasingly
digitalized financial ecosystem.
Implications for business beyond the financial sector
While the bibliometric clusters predominantly stem from
the finance and banking literature, the emerging themes
bear significant relevance for non-financial enterprises.
First, blockchain-integrated supply chain finance mecha -
nisms enable firms in manufacturing, logistics, and trade
to shorten cash conversion cycles and improve working
capital efficiency transformations that are already being
operationalized by leading supply networks [70]. Second,
the rise of embedded financial services, particularly AI-
powered Buy-Now-Pay-Later (BNPL) solutions and digi -
tal wallets, is reshaping customer acquisition, retention
strategies, and revenue models in the retail and e-com -
merce domains. These tools offer seamless financial
integration with existing enterprise systems, facilitating
modular service deployment [64]. Third, platform-medi -
ated crowdfunding and peer-to-business financing have
expanded capital accessibility for startups and SMEs, fos -
tering more inclusive and decentralized innovation pipe -
lines [56]. Accordingly, managers across industries must
not only monitor FinTech advancements but also develop
integrative strategies that leverage financial technologies
as core components of digital transformation, customer
analytics, and risk-sharing mechanisms.
Conclusion
This study has undertaken a comprehensive bibliomet -
ric investigation into the evolution of Financial Technol -
ogy (FinTech) research from 1968 to mid-2025, utilizing
a dataset of 2760 peer-reviewed publications extracted
from the Web of Science Core Collection. Through the
integration of co-citation mapping, keyword co-occur -
rence analysis, and theoretical interpretation, the study
systematically traces the field’s developmental trajectory
and intellectual architecture. The analysis confirms that
FinTech research has experienced a substantial surge in
scholarly interest over the past decade, evolving from a
nascent discourse centered on technological experimen -
tation in banking into a mature, multi-thematic domain
characterized by the convergence of digital innovation,
consumer behavior, institutional performance, and regu -
latory transformation.
Thematically, the study identifies three distinct
phases shaping the FinTech research landscape. The
first phase (1968–1999) is predominantly anchored in

infrastructural and institutional innovation, where the
emphasis is placed on banking technologies and financial
systems design. During the second phase (2000–2011),
the research agenda shifts toward customer-centric con -
structs such as trust, satisfaction, and consumer behavior,
marking a paradigmatic reorientation toward human–
computer interaction and perceived risk. The third phase
(2012–2025) evidences a significant intensification of
interest in performance optimization, service quality,
and behavioral adoption, accompanied by a heightened
focus on blockchain, mobile and internet banking, and
the strategic role of FinTech in enhancing organizational
efficiency. The transition across these phases reflects
a maturation of the field, both in terms of theoretical
sophistication and empirical scope.
In response to the first research question (RQ1), the
findings delineate a robust and exponential growth in
FinTech scholarship, particularly since 2018, with the
USA, England, and China emerging as the most pro -
lific contributors. Publication trends indicate a discipli -
nary pluralism, with leading contributions disseminated
through finance, banking, marketing, and information
systems journals, thus affirming FinTech’s cross-sectoral
academic significance. Regarding the second research
question (RQ2), the Technology Acceptance Model
(TAM) and its derivatives (TAM2, TAM3, UTAUT)
have been empirically validated as the dominant theo -
retical frameworks guiding FinTech research. The recur -
rence of constructs such as perceived usefulness, ease of
use, behavioral intention, and trust across co-occurrence
clusters substantiates their explanatory power in predict -
ing FinTech adoption behavior. The third research ques -
tion (RQ3) reveals that current research hotspots are
strongly aligned with blockchain technology, crowdfund -
ing platforms, mobile banking ecosystems, and internet-
based financial services underscoring the shift toward
decentralized finance and user-driven service models.
The theoretical implications of this study are profound.
The alignment between empirical findings and TAM-
based constructs confirms the durability and adaptability
of this model in capturing behavioral dimensions of Fin -
Tech use. Nevertheless, the emerging complexities such
as data ethics, cybersecurity threats, and algorithmic bias
necessitate an augmentation of existing models to incor -
porate sociotechnical and institutional variables. The
coalescence of theoretical constructs with empirical key -
word trends further affirms the potential of bibliometric
analysis as a robust methodological apparatus for theory
validation and development in financial research.
Practically, the findings hold substantial implications
for financial institutions, regulatory bodies, and technol -
ogy developers. For banking institutions, the evolving
discourse emphasizes the critical importance of aligning
technological innovation with consumer trust and service
quality to achieve sustained competitive advantage. Reg -
ulators are urged to reconfigure existing policy frame -
works to accommodate the agility and borderless nature
of FinTech innovations without compromising consumer
protection or systemic stability. Technology developers,
in turn, must address growing concerns around data pri -
vacy, digital inclusivity, and platform transparency to fos-
ter adoption and ethical alignment. The findings extend
beyond the financial domain and illuminate how emerg -
ing financial technologies can enable strategic innova -
tion, liquidity optimization, and enhanced consumer
engagement across a broad spectrum of business sectors.
Despite its methodological rigor, the study is not
without limitations. The exclusive reliance on the
Web of Science database, while ensuring data reliabil -
ity and standardization, may underrepresent emerging
or regional studies indexed in alternative repositories
such as Scopus or Dimensions. Moreover, the inherent
limitations of bibliometric tools in capturing qualitative
nuances and latent constructs underscore the need for
complementary methodological approaches. As such,
future research should adopt multi-source bibliometric
triangulation and incorporate qualitative content analy -
sis, thematic synthesis, and network ethnography to
deepen the interpretive richness of FinTech scholarship.
Finally, several promising avenues for further inves -
tigation emerge from this study. Future research should
extend bibliometric inquiries to underexplored regions,
particularly developing economies where FinTech adop -
tion patterns are shaped by distinct socioeconomic and
infrastructural variables. Additionally, more granular
studies are needed to empirically validate the conceptual
underpinnings of emerging FinTech phenomena such
as decentralized finance (DeFi), digital currencies, and
algorithmic credit scoring. Particular attention should
also be paid to the ethical, legal, and behavioral dimen -
sions of FinTech adoption, especially concerning mar -
ginalized populations and data-vulnerable communities.
By addressing these critical gaps, scholars can contrib -
ute not only to the theoretical advancement of FinTech
studies but also to the formulation of more equitable and
resilient financial ecosystems.
Research agenda and future questions
The bibliometric results of this study reveal six dominant
thematic clusters blockchain and DeFi, AI and automa -
tion in finance, regtech and compliance, digital trust and
user behavior, sustainability-linked FinTech, and embed -
ded financial services for SMEs. While these domains
exhibit growing scholarly attention, several conceptual
and empirical gaps remain. To advance the intellectual
maturity of the field, future research should develop

Page 18 of 20Salem and Shahimi  Future Business Journal          (2025) 11:233
theory-informed, context-specific, and methodologically
diverse inquiries. First, the blockchain and DeFi clus -
ter highlights a lack of empirical investigation into gov -
ernance risks and regulatory fragmentation. A relevant
question is: How can decentralized protocols align with
macroprudential regulatory objectives in cross-border
contexts? Second, while AI is extensively applied in credit
scoring and advisory systems, concerns remain around
algorithmic fairness and transparency. Future work
should examine: To what extent do AI-driven FinTech
tools reinforce or mitigate structural financial biases?
Third, the RegTech (Regulatory Technology) cluster
shows strong technical evolution but limited institu -
tional theorization. There is a need to clarify: How do
variations in national regulatory capacity shape RegTech
adoption trajectories? Fourth, trust-related research
remains over-reliant on TAM and UTAUT frameworks,
with little attention to cultural or institutional modera -
tors. A critical question is: How do sociocultural dynam -
ics influence digital trust formation in FinTech adoption
across regions? Lastly, the sustainability and SME-related
clusters remain under-integrated into mainstream Fin -
Tech discourse. Future studies should explore: What role
does FinTech play in operationalizing ESG compliance
for SMEs through embedded finance tools? By ground -
ing future investigations in these unresolved tensions, the
field can progress toward greater theoretical consolida -
tion, empirical generalizability, and policy relevance.
In addition to these thematic opportunities, future
research should also address methodological limita -
tions related to bibliometric design. The current study’s
exclusive reliance on the Web of Science database, while
methodologically rigorous, may introduce coverage bias
particularly in terms of language, geography, and citation
recency. Non-English and regionally focused studies may
be underrepresented, and citation lag may obscure the
visibility of recent yet impactful work. To improve inclu -
sivity and robustness, future bibliometric analyses should
consider triangulating data from multiple sources such as
Scopus, Google Scholar, or Dimensions.
Ethical considerations
This study relies exclusively on secondary bibliometric
data retrieved from the Web of Science database. No
human participants or sensitive personal information
were involved. All intellectual property rights have been
respected through accurate citation of original sources.
A transparent and systematic literature selection process
was followed to minimize bias and ensure objectivity in
the analysis.
