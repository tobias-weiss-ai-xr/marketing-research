Abstract
Purpose  Green marketing has become essential for addressing environmental imperatives while meeting consumer
demands. This study conducts a systematic literature review and bibliometric analysis examining how green market-
ing practices influence green purchase intention. Synthesizing evidence from 52 empirical studies, this review identi-
fies research gaps, develops three testable propositions, and proposes a strategic research agenda.
Methodology  Following PRISMA guidelines, systematic searches were conducted in Scopus and Web of Science
(January 2010–August 2024), yielding 1283 documents. Fifty-two empirical studies met rigorous inclusion criteria
for thematic synthesis. Bibliometric analysis using VOS Viewer examined publication trends, geographic distribution,
and keyword co-occurrence.
Findings Publication volume grew 27-fold from 5 articles (2010) to 138 (2024). India is the most productive country
(99 documents) but ranks ninth in citations per document (14.7), while China (72; 78.4) and the USA (61; 92.7) achieve
higher impact. Eight thematic clusters emerged; "purchase intention" (178 occurrences), "green marketing" (167),
and "green purchase intention" (107) dominate. Green labeling, green advertising, and integrated green marketing
mix consistently and positively influence green purchase intention. Green packaging evidence is mixed. Green trust
and green perceived value consistently mediate these relationships. Critically under-researched pathways include
green packaging → green trust (one study), green labeling → green perceived value (one study), and green marketing
mix → green perceived value (one study).
Originality/Value This review synthesizes fragmented evidence into an integrated framework revealing unknown
pathways supported by only one or two studies; develops three testable theoretical propositions (P1, P2, P3);
empirically validates fragmentation via bibliometric cluster disconnects; and proposes a strategic, methodologically
informed research agenda.
Keywords Green advertising, Green labeling, Green marketing, Green marketing mix, Green packaging, Green
purchase intention

Introduction
Rising consumer awareness of environmental issues—
global warming, plastic pollution, and resource deple -
tion—has fundamentally transformed consumption
patterns [13, 64]. This consciousness has driven a para -
digm shift toward green products among marketers and
consumers [66]. Green marketing encompasses the pro -
motion of products based on environmental benefits,
including being recyclable, biodegradable, and energy
efficient [65]. The American Marketing Association
defines green marketing as promoting products designed
to reduce negative environmental impacts. Rising green
demand has motivated firms to adopt green marketing
strategies that enhance brand image and social respon -
sibility [12]. Green purchase intention refers to consum -
ers’ willingness to purchase environmentally sustainable
products [70].
Despite decades of academic attention, the green
marketing literature remains fragmented. Most studies
examine isolated practices—green labeling, packaging,
or advertising—without considering interactive effects
on consumer behavior [37, 83]. This fragmentation limits
theoretical development and constrains practical guid -
ance. Prior research has examined green labeling [76],
green packaging [62], green advertising [55], and the
green marketing mix [40]. Scholars have also explored
related concepts such as green brand equity [2] and the
role of environmental knowledge in shaping purchase
decisions [32, 47]. Early conceptual work by Shil [77]
traced the evolution of environmental marketing, while
Kinoti [43] examined green marketing intervention strat-
egies for sustainable development.
This review reveals a specific problem: Although green
trust and green perceived value are frequently invoked
as mediators, empirical evidence for specific pathways
remains thin. The green packaging → green trust pathway
has one study; green labeling → green perceived value
has one study. Without understanding these mecha -
nisms, managers cannot know which practices build
trust versus enhance value, and theorists cannot specify
boundary conditions. Furthermore, the literature has
paid insufficient attention to how green skepticism [22,
48] and greenwashing perceptions [2, 68] moderate these
relationships. Alhamad et  al. [4] provided a conceptual
framework linking green marketing strategies to green
purchase intentions through green perceived value and
green trust, but empirical testing remains limited.
Research questions
• RQ1:  What are the publication trends, geographic
distribution patterns, and intellectual structure of the
green marketing and green purchase intention litera -
ture from 2010 to 2024?
• RQ2: What is the nature, consistency, and methodo -
logical quality of evidence regarding direct relation -
ships between green marketing practices and green
purchase intention?
• RQ3:  To what extent do green trust and green per -
ceived value mediate relationships between green
marketing practices and green purchase intention,
and which pathways remain under-investigated?
• RQ4:  What are the most critical research gaps,
and what specific, actionable directions for future
research can advance theoretical and empirical
understanding?

Theoretical background and literature review
Theoretical foundations
To understand how green marketing influences con -
sumer behavior, three complementary perspectives are
drawn upon: Signaling Theory, Attitude–Behavior–Con -
text (ABC) Theory, and the Theory of Planned Behavior
(TPB). Table  1 summarizes core propositions, applica -
tions, and limitations.
Signaling theory
Signaling theory [14] addresses information transfer
under asymmetry—a condition characterizing green
markets where consumers cannot easily verify "cre -
dence attributes." In green marketing, firms signal envi -
ronmental quality via credible practices like third-party
eco-labels. However, this review reveals signaling has
been applied statically, overlooking: (a) signal degrada -
tion over time, (b) signal noise from competing claims,
and (c) feedback effects from purchase experience. No
reviewed study has examined these phenomena. Exten -
sions of signaling theory in green marketing have been
proposed by researchers examining how green brand
credibility mediates the relationship between green
advertising and purchase intentions [33, 73]. The role
of trust in signaling effectiveness was also explored by
Hart and Saunders [25] in the context of electronic data
interchange adoption.
Attitude–behavior–context (ABC) theory
ABC theory [23] posits that behavior results from atti -
tude–context interaction. Green marketing practices rep-
resent contextual factors facilitating attitude–behavior
translation. However, the literature has examined only
one direction—context shaping attitudes—while ABC
originally proposed bidirectional effects and moderation
of attitude–behavior relationships. No reviewed study
has tested whether green marketing moderates the atti -
tude–intention link. Several studies have applied ABC
theory to understand green consumer behavior in spe -
cific contexts, such as organic food purchasing [59] and
local food consumption [20]. Boztepe [9] examined green
marketing’s impact on consumer buying behavior, pro -
viding early empirical support for ABC applications.
Theory of planned behavior
TPB [1] proposes that behavioral intention is shaped by
attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral con-
trol. In green contexts, purchase intentions are shaped
by attitudes toward green purchasing, social pressure,
and perceived access. However, the 52 studies apply -
ing TPB have treated components as static antecedents
rather than dynamic mediators. No study has examined
whether repeated green advertising exposure changes
attitudes or whether improved distribution enhances
perceived behavioral control. Prior applications of TPB
to green purchasing include Kalafatis et  al. [38], Yadav
and Pathak [90], and Yazdanpanah and Forouzani [91], all
of which confirmed the predictive validity of the model
while noting its static limitations. Karatu and Mat [39]
extended TPB by examining the mediating effects of
green trust and perceived behavioral control on green
purchase intentions in Nigeria.
Empirical review of green marketing practices
Green labeling and green purchase intention
Eco-labels are symbols indicating environmental com -
pliance [76]. Research consistently demonstrates that
eco-friendly information on product labels significantly
influences purchase intentions [8, 26, 75]. From signal -
ing theory, eco-labels function as credible signals reduc -
ing information asymmetry. Of 52 reviewed studies, eight
examined green labeling → green purchase intention,
all reporting significant positive associations (β = 0.12–
0.58). The effectiveness of eco-labels has been shown to
depend on consumer trust in the certifying body [80] and
the perceived credibility of the label [15, 83]. Rahbar and
Wahid [67] investigated green marketing tools’ effect on
consumer purchase behavior, finding that eco-labels sig -
nificantly influenced purchase decisions.
Green packaging and green purchase intention
Green packaging reduces waste and uses recyclable mate-
rials [3]. From ABC theory, packaging represents a con -
textual factor facilitating or hindering attitude–behavior
translation. Panda et al. [62] found positive associations,
but Kong et  al. [45] reported no direct effect for elec -
tronics (high involvement). This inconsistency suggests
product involvement as a potential moderator. Four stud-
ies examined this relationship, most reported positive
associations, but conflicting findings highlight the need
for further research. The role of packaging in consumer
decision-making has been extensively studied, with
researchers noting that packaging communicates prod -
uct attributes and influences perceived product quality
[19, 86]. Auliandri et al. [7] explored green packaging as
a business strategy among young consumers in emerging
markets.
Green advertising and green purchase intention
Green advertising promotes products while emphasiz -
ing environmental responsibility [41]. From TPB, green
advertising shapes attitudes by providing product benefit
information and influences subjective norms by signal -
ing socially valued behavior. Seven studies examined

this relationship, with consistent positive associations
(β = 0.18 to 0.51) [55, 88]. Recent research has also exam-
ined how green advertising receptivity mediates the
relationship between corporate social responsibility and
organizational customer consumption [58]. Li and Shan
[49] and Youyou and Baoshun [92] both investigated the
influence mechanism of green advertising on consumers’
purchase intention for organic foods, finding serial medi -
ation through green perceived value and green trust.
Green marketing mix and green purchase intention
The green marketing mix comprises green products
(environmentally friendly attributes), green price (pre -
mium pricing justified by value), green place (low-car -
bon distribution), and green promotion (environmental
communication). Seven studies examined relationships
with green purchase intention, most reporting positive
associations (β = 0.20–0.55). Early conceptualizations of
the green marketing mix were proposed by researchers
examining how firms can integrate environmental con -
siderations into each element of the marketing mix [27,
69]. More recent studies have examined the green mar -
keting mix in specific country contexts, including Bang -
ladesh [31], Sri Lanka [40], and Nigeria [94]. Mahmoud
[51] and Mahmoud et  al. [52] examined the impact of
green marketing mix on purchase intention, finding sig -
nificant direct and mediated effects through environmen-
tal knowledge. Hello and Al Momani [29] studied green
marketing’s relationship to purchase decisions among
university students in Saudi Arabia.
Green trust and green purchase intention
Green trust is consumers’ confidence in a firm’s envi -
ronmental credibility [12]. From signaling theory, trust
develops when signals are perceived as credible over
time. False claims raise skepticism, negatively impact -
ing trust and purchase intention [48]. Six studies exam -
ined green trust → green purchase intention, consistently
finding positive associations. The concept of green trust
builds on broader theories of trust in marketing, includ -
ing the role of trust in electronic commerce [28] and
inter-organizational relationships [25]. Recent research
has integrated green trust with green perceived value and
green product attributes to predict purchase intentions
[10, 35, 46]. Jamal et  al. [36] developed an integrated
model of brand trust for green marketing, finding that
green marketing promotion success depends on trust-
building mechanisms.
Green perceived value and green purchase intention
Green perceived value is a customer’s appraisal of the
overall benefit of a green product [63], encompassing
functional, emotional, and social dimensions. Research
consistently demonstrates positive impacts on purchase
intentions [13, 72]. Seven studies examined this relation -
ship, all reporting positive associations. The dimensional-
ity of green perceived value has been explored in various
contexts, including organic food [72], energy-efficient
appliances [32], and green new products [50]. Studies
have also examined how green perceived value mediates
the relationship between green advertising and purchase
intention [89, 92] and between green packaging and pur -
chase decisions [34]. Doszhanov and Ahmad [18] exam -
ined customers’ intention to use green products through
the impact of green brand dimensions and green per -
ceived value.
Mediation mechanisms
Fourteen studies explicitly tested mediation effects. Seven
found that green trust mediates relationships between
green marketing practices (particularly labeling and
advertising) and purchase intention. Five found that green
perceived value mediates these relationships. Two exam -
ined serial mediation (practices → GPV → GT → GPI).
These findings confirm that green marketing influences
purchase intention both directly and indirectly through
cognitive and affective evaluations. The mediating role of
green trust has been examined in studies of green brand
equity [2] and green word of mouth [55]. Similarly, green
perceived value has been shown to mediate the effects of
green marketing on purchase decisions in various emerg-
ing economy contexts [6, 18, 93]. Guerreiro and Pacheco
[24] found that green trust, consumer brand engagement,
and green word of mouth mediate purchasing intentions.
Shrestha [78] analyzed green marketing tools’ influence
on consumer purchase intention in Kathmandu, finding
partial mediation through perceived value.
Research conceptual framework
Figure 1 presents the conceptual framework guiding this
review. The framework positions four green marketing
practices (green labeling, green advertising, green pack -
aging, integrated green marketing mix) as antecedents
influencing two mediating mechanisms—green trust
and green perceived value—which shape green purchase
intention. The framework integrates signaling theory,
ABC theory, and TPB. Notably, the framework excludes
feedback loops, moderators, direct effects without medi -
ation, and practice interactions—exclusions that repre -
sent high-priority future research directions.

Methodological characteristics

No longitudinal studies were identified. Studies were
conducted in 23 countries: Asia (n  = 34), Europe
(n = 8), North America (n  = 6), and other regions

Theoretical implications
Extending signaling theory
The 52 reviewed studies have treated signaling as static—
ignoring signal degradation, noise, and feedback effects.
No study has examined how credibility erodes over time,
how competing signals confuse consumers, or how pur -
chase experience reshapes signal interpretation. Future
research should employ longitudinal designs, within-
subjects signal inconsistency tests, and experimental
greenwashing manipulations. Table  11 summarizes the
overlooked phenomena in signaling theory applications.
Extending ABC theory
The literature has examined only one direction (con -
text → attitudes)—while ABC originally proposed bidi -
rectional and interactive effects. No study has tested
whether green marketing moderates the attitude–inten -
tion link, whether prior attitudes moderate responses
to green marketing, or reciprocal influences over time.
Future research should use longitudinal designs, moder -
ated mediation models, and experimental-causal-chain
designs. Table  12 presents the untested propositions of
ABC theory in green marketing research.

Developing Propositions for Future Research
Proposition 1 (P1): The contingent role of  green packag ‑
ing The relationship between green packaging and
green purchase intention is mediated by green trust,
moderated by product involvement and product cate -
gory. For low-involvement products, green packaging has
expected significant positive effects; for high-involvement
products, effects are weak or non-significant. This propo-
sition extends signaling theory by identifying boundary
conditions for green packaging effectiveness, recogniz -
ing that packaging signals may be more influential for
low-involvement products where consumers rely on
peripheral cues [86]. Recommended method: factorial
experiment manipulating packaging type and product
involvement (N ≥ 400).

Proposition 2 (P2): The Credibility Gradient
of  Eco‑Labels Third-party certified eco-labels have
stronger effects on green perceived value and purchase
intention than self-declared claims, mediated by green
trust. This effect is stronger in regulatory contexts with
strong enforcement. This proposition builds on signal -
ing theory by conceptualizing signal credibility as a con -
tinuous rather than binary construct, recognizing that
different certification mechanisms convey varying levels
of credibility [15, 80]. Recommended method: Cross-
national experiment comparing certified versus self-
declared versus no label.

serial mediation model (practices → GPV → GT → GPI)
explains more variance than parallel models. For inexpe -
rienced green buyers, the serial pathway is stronger; for
experienced buyers, direct effects dominate. This propo -
sition extends TPB by reconceptualizing it as a dynamic
process model where the relative importance of cognitive
(value) and affective (trust) pathways shifts with consumer
experience [13]. Recommended method: Longitudinal
SEM with multi-group analysis. Table 14 summarizes the
three propositions for future research.

Conclusion
This systematic review examined relationships
between green marketing practices and green pur -
chase intention, synthesizing 52 empirical studies
(2010–2024). Bibliometric analysis revealed substantial
growth, with India, China, and the USA being the most
productive countries. Eight thematic clusters emerged.
Synthesis demonstrates that green labeling, green
advertising, and marketing mix positively influence
purchase intention, mediated by green trust and green
perceived value. However, critical gaps exist: Green
packaging evidence is mixed; pathways from green
packaging → trust, green label → perceived value, and
marketing mix → perceived value have only one to two
studies each; all 52 studies are cross-sectional.

Limitations and future research directions
Limitations
This review has several limitations that should be
acknowledged.
• Database coverage.  Although Scopus and Web
of Science are comprehensive academic databases,
some relevant studies published in other databases or
in non-indexed journals may have been excluded.
• Language restriction.  Including only English-lan -
guage publications may introduce bias, particularly
given the significant volume of green marketing
research emerging from non-English speaking coun -
tries.
• Publication bias.  The predominance of studies
reporting positive findings may reflect publication
bias, as journals tend to publish studies with statisti -
cally significant results [5, 81].

Future research directions
Under‑researched relationships
Table 18 presents the priority relationships requiring fur-
ther investigation.
Cross‑cultural comparative research
Researchers should examine whether relationships vary
across cultural dimensions [30, 74], conduct direct cross-
cultural comparisons with measurement invariance test -
ing, investigate under-researched regions (Africa, Latin
America, Middle East, Eastern Europe), and examine
how national environmental policies moderate effec -
tiveness. Prior research has shown that green marketing
effectiveness varies significantly across national contexts,
with factors such as collectivism, uncertainty avoid -
ance, and long-term orientation moderating consumer
responses to green claims [42, 85]. Nekmahmud and
Fekete-Farkas [57] examined determinants of green pur -
chase intention in a developing nation, highlighting the
need for cross-cultural replication.
Longitudinal and experimental designs
Researchers should employ longitudinal designs (three
or more waves over 6–12 months), experimental designs
(laboratory and field), and experience sampling methods
to establish causality and examine dynamic processes.
As noted by several scholars, the dominance of cross-
sectional survey research has limited our understand -
ing of how green marketing effects evolve over time and
how consumers update their beliefs based on purchase
experience [24, 39]. Chandon et al. [11] provided a ben -
efit congruency framework that could inform experimen-
tal designs in green marketing research.
Moderating variables and boundary conditions
Systematic investigation is needed of consumer charac -
teristics (environmental concern, knowledge, skepticism,
values), product characteristics (involvement, type), pur -
chase context (channel, social context, time pressure),
and cultural/regulatory contexts. The moderating role of
environmental knowledge has received some attention
[52], but many other potential moderators remain unex -
plored. Similarly, the role of green skepticism in attenu -
ating green marketing effectiveness requires further
investigation [22, 48].
Methodological advancements
Required advancements include meta-analytic synthesis,
standardized measure development, objective behavioral
measurement (scanner data, transactions), multi-level
modeling, and registered reports to reduce publication
bias. The field would benefit from adopting best prac -
tices from related disciplines, including pre-registration
of studies and replication of key findings [11]. Addition -
ally, the use of fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis
(fsQCA) offers promise for understanding complex con -
figurations of green marketing practices [54].
Emerging topics
Future research should address digital green marketing
(social media, influencers), greenwashing effects (skepti -
cism, recovery strategies), generational differences (Gen -
eration Z, Millennials, Generation X, Baby Boomers),
circular economy marketing (product-as-service, reman -
ufacturing), and artificial intelligence-powered personali-
zation. Research on green marketing in specific sectors,
such as organic food [57] and energy-efficient appliances
[32], should also be expanded to develop sector-specific
theories and best practices.
