Abstract
Research on Internet memes continues to be largely rooted in Euro-American theories of digital culture
and semiotics, while the existing body of Indian meme research remains largely empirical and
descriptive, focusing on political satire and “results” of memes without a grounded interpretive theory
of the ways in which Indians interpret memes. This paper fills that gap by suggesting rasa theory and
its related concept of the sah ṛdaya (literally, one who has heart), described in Bharata Muni's
Natyashastra as the central theory for analyzing meme reception and virality in India. Meme theory
(Shifman, 2014), the theory of visual culture studies (Mirzoeff, 2009) and the theory of semiotics
(Barthes, 1972) have also been taken in as complementary but secondary tools. The Panchatantra and
the folk visual tradition of Patachitra are presented as conceptual parallels that illustrate the same
logic of prepared, participatory reception, rather than as historical predecessors of the meme. The
framework is then elaborated on the concept of brand identity arguing that a brand meme works only
if the consumer is similarly prepared for it, which is backed by a brand narrative theory (Escalas, 2004;
Kapferer, 2008) and empirical Indian study on meme marketing (Sharma, 2018). The paper ends by
reflecting on the limits of this contribution and suggests directions for future research.
Keywords: Memes, Visual Culture, Indian Knowledge Systems, Indian Aesthetics, Rasa
Introduction
Every day, an Indian smartphone screen is packed with memes - a Bollywood still with a diatribe about
inflation, a cricketer's 'look' reworked to poke fun at a political announcement and a brand's Instagram
post taking a dig at a trendsetter template. Thes e compact forms of humour, satire, and commentary
have become important ways for Indians to navigate news, politics, and consumer culture online. Meme
scholarship has evolved in the Euro-American academic context, and it can be traced back to Dawkins'

(2006) biological metaphor of the meme as a self-replicating unit of culture, and the redefinition of the
internet meme as a cluster of digital objects sharing content, form, and stance that are created and
circulated by multiple participants, with reciprocal awareness. (Shifman, 2014) The research on memes
in India has increased significantly, albeit on a narrower empirical trajectory, for example, in the fields
of studying political satire (Kulkarni, 2017; Kweera, 2025) and the study of marketing outcomes
(Sharma, 2018), without providing a theoretically grounded explanation of why Indian audiences
interpret, enjoy, and reshare memes in particular ways.
This is the gap this paper adresses. Instead of importing western meme theory without making any
adaptations or assuming that Indian meme culture is a localised version of a global template, it suggests
rasa theory - in particular the concept of the sahṛdaya, the aesthetically formed spectator from Bharata
Muni's Natyashastra - as a more theoretically accurate, and India-based, description of how the meme
has a meaning that is completed by an audience. Rasa theory and the concept of the sahṛdaya constitute
the paper's primary analytical framework while other analytical work, such as meme theory, visual
culture studies and semiotics are still useful, and are kept as supporting theories. The fable structure of
the Panchatantra and the folk art of Patachitra ar e then introduced as conceptual parallels, rather than
as historical ancestors of the digital meme, a distinction carefully drawn by the paper since the parallels
can easily be interpreted as continuity.
It suggests that a brand meme is only successful if it aligns with a reader-consumer who is ready to read
and enjoy what the brand is saying.
Meme Theory: Foundations and a Conceptual Gap
In The Selfish Gene, Dawkins first used the word meme, which refers to an idea, tune or piece of
behavior that is passed from person to person like a gene is passed on in a population (Dawkins, 2006).
Shifman's influential redefinition (2014) shifted the focus of meme scholarship from the meme as one
viral object to the meme as a cluster of items that share a content, form, and attitude, and that are created,
circulated, imitated, and transformed by multiple users.
Studies of memes in India have increased in quantity and scope, albeit with a more limited and empirical
approach. One specific strand has explored the role of memes in the marketing of Indian brands, aiming
to understand the reaction of Facebook user in I ndia in response to brands on their meme feed via
netnographic techniques (Sharma, 2018). This literature is empirically rich, but primarily descriptive;
it demonstrates that Indian memes work, and sometimes how, but does not inquire into how viewers are
able to complete the meaning of a meme in the first place, a question that is taken up by rasa theory,
with the vocabulary that this paper develops as its lens.

Visual Culture and Semiotics as Complementary Lenses
Before going to rasa theory, two other frameworks are useful, but here are used as auxiliary rather than
core ones. Mirzoeff (2009) sees the study of visual culture not as an academic field, but as a way of
comprehending the organisation of the everyday th rough images, suggesting that visual media have
become a central feature of the understanding and communication of contemporary life. Barthes's
(1972) semiotic description of myth introduces a second dimension; the image has a literal denotation
and a seco nd order of connotation that can come to appear natural. A still of an actor's expression
conveys only a face, but when used repeatedly with a caption, a connotative meaning also emerges
which can be quickly identified by competent viewers.
This is a helpful, but general statement. The Indian aesthetic theory, discussed in the next section,
describes this requirement more precisely, not only that a code is required, but also the kind of spectator
who can hold it; for this reason, rasa theory cannot be limited to the theory of semiotics or of visual
culture, but is used as the paper's main lens.
Rasa Theory and the Sahṛdaya: The Primary Lens
One of the most systematically developed Indian theories of aesthetic experience is rasa theory that is
linked with the Natyashastra (Bharata Muni, 1996), an ancient Sanskrit treatise on dramaturgy.
According to rasa theory, a dramatic or artistic work aims to create a certain kind of feeling, or rasa, in
the spectator's mind. Importantly, rasa is not only in the work itself; it can be experienced only if the
work is presented to one who is properly attuned to it through a process of cultivation, namely, a
sahṛdaya, literally one who has heart, a person with cultivated sensibility who can recognise and relish
the emotional and cultural cues within the work. The audience, then, is an active part in the construction
of meaning, and without this spectator the standard of an artful performance will do nothing to produce
rasa.
This concept parallels the way memes work online. The meme is not a fixed, meaning -entire entity;
instead, its humor or critique only comes into play when it comes into contact with an audience with
the cultural, political, and linguistic literacy to extra ct the multiple layers of the references, the
equivalent of the sahṛdaya in the digital space. If the audience is not familiar with a Bollywood quote,
or a cricket moment, it doesn't make any sense to them. Rasa theory provides a more specific mechanism
underpinning Shifman's (2014) notion of a meme: the stance is never received as content, but as stance,
by a sahṛdaya-like viewer who is already primed to appreciate it. If a meme is read that way, it is not
just the algorithm of a platform that gets it to go viral in India, but also the aesthetic sensibility that
already exists in the eyes of those who view the meme and whose reference points it completes.
It also redefines the participative and remixing nature of the meme and what Shifman (2014) considers
to be at the core of the definition of the meme. Both Rasa and meme theory place the spectator as a

participant who completes the work; the meme theory goes beyond that, making the audience a producer
as well as a receiver. Both rasa theory and meme culture re -frame what is meant to be relished as co -
authorship, but they are both built upon the same prem ise: meaning is not inherent in the artefact, but
completed and, in the case of a digital artefact, extended, with an engaged, prepared audience.
The Panchatantra and Patachitra as Conceptual Parallels
It is essential to be clear about the things that these comparisons do not assert. They do not claim that
the internet meme originated in the Panchatantra or Patachitra scroll painting, or that either tradition
foreshadowed digital media. Instead, both are  discussed here as conceptual equivalents: as both
examples of the principle already stated above, 'a small cultural artefact needs an attentive, competent
audience to make sense of it. This is done to prevent overstepping the mark and ascribing a direct l ine
of descent that is not supported by the evidence. The Panchatantra is a compilation of five Sanskrit niti-
shastra tales, each of which is shorter, with lessons on proper behaviour, diplomacy and government
summarised in succinct, memorable episodes set on animals that form the core of the tales and are
interwoven within a frame-story structure (Olivelle, 1997). Like the audience of a story in Panchatantra,
a meme's viewer will need to identify the various stock roles in the story before it can be understood by
the watcher: the smart jackal, the stupid croc odile, etc. The recognisability of the character is not
different from the meaning of the story; it is a condition for the story's meaning to be fulfilled by the
listener, just as the stock template of a meme is only a means of expressing the attitude of a meme that
the viewer understands. A similar parallel can be drawn from the scroll painting and singing of Bengal,
done with the help of an itinerant painter-performer known as ‘patua' and a long scroll of painted panel,
which stretched by panel is depicted while the story is sung by the patua. This tradition relies on a live,
socially-embedded audience: Without the sung narration of the Patua, the motifs of a scroll, for instance,
the didactic yama pattis that show punishments in the afterlife, are hard to understand without the
context of the performance, that is, without the patua's story being told to an audience. Chakraborty
(2023) also highlights the difference between loka (the folk/folkish domain) and shastra (elite/elite -
knowledge domain), and how shastra makes its way into the popular, low barrier visual domain of loka,
where it is carried and disseminated amongst the masses, much like a meme. Chakraborty (2023) also
records Patachitra's own recent passage into the digital realm, where the scroll no longer accompanies
live performanc e, and is instead increasingly presented as an image instead of a sung narrative,
highlighting its tradition of relying on the presence of a prepared, present audience, where without that
something is lost. They illustrate how the need to have the audience prepared for the performance is
repeated in seemingly unconnected Indian cultural genres, thus providing some justification for the use
of rasa and the sahṛdaya as a possible tool for the analysis of Indian meme reception, in general.

Rasa, Sahṛdaya, and Brand Identity: Extending the Framework
The paper's most important original contribution is its attempt to extend the rasa/sah ṛdaya framework
to the domain of brand identity, which is a crucial part in the way memes circulate in India, but has been
tackled in the main by quantitative outcomes rather than also by interpretive theory. This is a structure
that is similar to the sah ṛdaya relationship in rasa theory, in which the work of art relies on a receiver
who has the capacity to complete it. If applied to memes this means that a brand meme is a compressed
story that will only create a self -brand connection if the viewer already has the cultural reference that
the meme appeals to - which is not a property of the image itself, but rather a sah ṛdaya-like condition
of preparedness. This kind of phenomenon can be observed in the empirical studies of Indian meme
marketing. In Sharma's (2018) netnographic study of the Facebook Indian audience, the researcher used
the concept of the internet meme to explore the potential of using such memes to engage Indian users,
concluding that humour is not universal and that brands can engage Indian audiences if the memes are
linked to an existing cultural vocabulary shared by the audience. This is not just a marketing best
practice, but an example of the same rasa mechanism - an Indian brand meme elicits a positive brand
association only if it is presented to the consumers with the proper or adequate preparation, with the
ability to recognize and enjoy it in a sah ṛdaya manner. The reframing provides an explanatory
framework for the Indian findings of meme-marketing that thus far have been lacking.
Discussion: Sharpening the Contribution
The argument presented here claims a specific and limited point: one that can be stated explicitly. It's
not as if the internet meme had been invented by the most ancient storytellers like the Panchatantra or
the Patachitra, or as if the classical thinkers  of India predicted digital media; The technology through
which memes circulate is a product of contemporary digital culture - networked platforms, algorithmic
circulation, and instantaneous reach - and can be well described by existing meme theory (Dawkin s,
2006; Shifman, 2014). The premise is narrower: the concept of sah ṛdaya, which is part of rasa theory,
is the most precise available term for a mechanism which is gestured at by meme theory (stance), visual
culture theory (the image-event), and semiotics (connotation); that a compact cultural artefact does not
have any meaning until it is inserted into an audience able to add to its meaning. It is illustrated in older
Indian media, in the Panchatantra and the Patachitra, and in brand identity literature it is in operation in
the commercial sphere, but no evidence is provided in any of them of historical descent.
The sharp framing is important because of two things. First, the mechanism of reception proposed here
is not simply a translation of Western meme theory; it is grounded in Indian aesthetics and offers an
alternative conceptual basis for understanding meme reception. Second, it provides Indian meme -
marketing research, which has been strong on outcomes and weak on explanation, with a specific and

testable explanative claim; that the success of a meme and/or a brand -meme in India should correlate
with the extent to which audience members already share the reference points a meme needs.
The framework also has clear boundaries, which should not be blurred. Rasa theory was created for
formal performance, and trained aesthetic judgement, while meme culture is anonymous, and not
always characterized by the formal refinement as classical theory would consider an accomplished piece
of art; the use of the sahṛdaya concept with an anonymous audience of memes stretches a term that was
designed for a different kind of spectatorship. In addition, the moral -instructive message of the
Panchatantra and the religious and communal function of the Patachitra are different in tone from many
contemporary memes, which are satirical, ephemeral and nihilistic. These aren't incidental side notes,
but rather legitimate tensions that signal important avenues for fu ture research, like studies on the
connection between self-reported cultural familiarity with memes and actual engagement with them as
the model suggests.

Conclusion
This paper has contended that rasa theory, and more specifically the notion of the sahṛdaya, provides a
precise, India-based primary lens for understanding the reception of memes in India - a lens that is
currently lacking from an empirically rich and thin Indian body of meme scholarship. Meme theory,
visual culture studies, semiotics, and rasa theory collectively point toward the importance of the viewer
in completing a meme's meaning. They were not conceptualized as historical predecessors of the meme,
but rather as conceptual parallels that illustrate the same mechanism in older Indian media, and the
framework was extended, as this paper's central original contribution, to brand identity, providing the
existing Indian meme-marketing findings (Sharma, 2018) with an explanatory grounding they have so
far lacked, by connecting them to established brand narrative theory (Escalas, 2004; Kapferer, 2008).
The boundaries of the framework, its basis in formal aesthetic judgement, rather than decentralised
digital circulation, are very real and should inform, not deter, empirical audience-reception research that
the argument of this paper now enables one to design.
