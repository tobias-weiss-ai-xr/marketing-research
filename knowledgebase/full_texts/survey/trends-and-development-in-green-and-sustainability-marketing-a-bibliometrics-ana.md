Abstract
The recent global environmental crisis and climate change have prompted greater interest among global citizens in sus-
tainability issues. Consumers are demanding more to be done by product and service suppliers to ensure sustainability.
This has sparked research and academic interest in understanding how various organisations position themselves from
a sustainability perspective. This study emerges from the need to understand trends and developments in sustainability
marketing. To this end, this study uses bibliometric data from the Scopus database to examine this development. The data
were analysed using Microsoft Excel and VOSviewer. The study found significant growth in the literature on green and
sustainable marketing which is indicative of the subject over the past couple of years. In many respects green marketing
is seen by many enterprises as playing a central role in shaping consumer perceptions and brand loyalty in many mar -
kets. However, green marketing and sustainability face various challenges such as misleading claims and greenwashing
which threaten consumer confidence in sustainability efforts. Overall, this study found that integrating sustainability
into marketing strategies fosters environmental responsibility, builds consumer trust, and enhances business perfor -
mance. In addition, frameworks such as the theory of planned behaviour (TPB) provide a foundation for understanding
consumers’ green attitudes and adoption behaviour. Greenwashing can be addressed by transparency, education, and
regulatory compliance. Finally, practical recommendations include fostering international cooperation, developing tar-
geted capacity-building initiatives, and leveraging sustainability marketing to achieve the United Nations Sustainable
Development Goals (SDGs). This study provides a comprehensive framework for advancing green marketing research and
practice, offering pathways for organisations to contribute to global sustainability while achieving long-term profitability.

2  Methodology
This study adopted a bibliometric analysis of Scopus-indexed material which included the study of bibliometric data
published in Scopus. Bibliometric data analysis is a well-accepted research approach which allows the study of large
volumes of datasets in a quantitative manner [38]. The Scopus database was used in this study because it is a reliable
database which contains data from various fields [39].Academics generally agree that Scopus and Web of Science are
two of the most prestigious databases in the world [40]. These two databases cover published documents and citation
databases in all fields of science; thus, they are suitable for bibliometric analyses. Hence, the search keywords in the
Scopus database were Green Marketing" OR sustainability *marketing. The search covered material published over three
decades, spanning the period between 1991 and 2023. Specifically, a document search was conducted in August 2024.
Finally, 1576 documents were reserved for further analysis after removing book reviews, other publications, and non-
English journal articles. Moreover, it has been argued that quality outcomes are mostly documented in journal outlets
[41], probably because of the review process. However, the removal of non-English journal articles is acknowledged as
a limitation of this study. Furthermore, the abstract of the studies was read, 15 documents were found not related to the
study frame and they were removed, and the remaining documents were 1561 documents, as shown in Fig. 1.
A descriptive analysis was conducted to track trends in scientific publications. Determine the annual publications and
the country leading the way in green and sustainability marketing research using the Scopus database. Data from 1561
documents were analysed using Microsoft Excel. The primary study fields of green marketing and sustainability marketing
were identified by performing a co-occurrence keyword analysis. Clusters of thematically connected published literature
are mapped through the scientific review process known as bibliometric analysis, which examines all prior research on
a subject. Future scholars interested in researching the same topic could use this as a guide.
To assess the content of each of the selected publications, keywords were analysed through an analysis of the co-
occurrence of keywords. In the 1561 documents in the study sample, 5362 keywords were used only once. For VOSviewer
to deliver a keyword co-occurrence network capable of being visually analysed and observed, the minimum number of
occurrences of a keyword used was adjusted to 15 in VOSviewer, thus thresholding keywords with fewer than 15 occur-
rences, as in other studies [42]. Of the 5362 keywords, only 88 met the threshold.

3.2  The global leading authors in green and sustainability marketing
The leading authors in green and sustainability marketing (see Fig.  4) focused most of their studies on greenwash and
green brand trust and factors that influence green purchase intention.
3.2.1  Greenwashing and green brand trust
Most scholars have focused on greenwash and green brand trust [45– 47]. Greenwashing occurs when an organisation
makes a misleading claim about the environmental benefits of its product [48], and greenwashing has emerged as a
critical issue in sustainability discourse among prolific writers. Thus, as consumer awareness of environmental issues
increases, understanding the dynamics and consequences of greenwashing is essential for operators, policymakers,
and researchers. It has been argued that greenwash negatively impacts consumers’ green purchase behaviour, with
green brand image and loyalty partially mediating this relationship [46]. In addition, greenwashing has a dual impact
on consumer attitudes, while paradoxically increasing environmental awareness and sustainable behaviour intentions
[49], and negatively affecting consumer trust, green word-of-mouth (WOM), and green purchase intentions [46, 50–52].
Thus, it reduces trust, happiness, brand legitimacy, and heightens perceived risks [51, 53, 54].
Thus, educational strategies such as greenwashing literacy, higher education initiatives, and tools for detecting mis-
leading claims are crucial for mitigating the negative effects of greenwashing [55]. Andreoli and Batista [56] emphasised
the limited standalone effectiveness of education initiatives, advocating for stronger regulatory measures. Regulatory
measures are sacrosanct, although their enforcement remains insufficient [57, 58]. Effective strategies to mitigate green-
washing include timely responses to misinformation [ 59] and consumer empowerment through information dissemi -
nation [48]. Thus, while greenwashing can inadvertently drive environmental awareness, its overall negative effects on
consumer trust, purchase intentions, and brand equity demand a multipronged approach. Thus, integrating education,
regulatory measures, and ethical marketing practices can mitigate the negative effects of greenwashing.

3.2.2  Factors that influence green purchase
Most prolific scholars have focused on identifying factors that influence green product purchases [46, 60]. It has been
stated that promoting consumer purchase behaviour for green products drives environmental sustainability [61]. The
extended theory of planned behaviour (TPB) has been captured as a critical variable in purchase intention behaviour in
the green marketing literature [62, 63]. Thus, several scholars have identified knowledge and awareness of environmental
issues [64–66], environmental concerns [64, 65, 67], consumer attitudes and values towards the environment [68, 69],
social influence [65, 70, 71], perceived behavioural control [72, 73], green product characteristics [67, 74], religion [44],
ethical and self-identity of a customer towards the environment [70, 73], and the application of structured marketing
strategies [67, 68] as factors that influence green purchase behaviour.
Thus, educating consumers about green brands; organising campaigns that emphasise the environmental impact of
green products; aligning green products with consumers’ ethical values; and applying a well-structured green market -
ing strategy that integrates pricing, promotion, and placement can drive consumer adoption of green products. Thus,
subsequent studies can test the effects of TPB, environmental concern, green perceived value, social influence, self-
image in environmental protection, and perceived environmental responsibility on green purchase intention. Investing
resources to increase green perceived value and decrease green perceived risk can improve green trust and purchase
intentions [47].

3.3.1  Consumer attitudes towards environmental protection
Attitudes serve as key determinants of green purchase behaviour. Several scholars have emphasised that positive atti-
tudes towards green products and practices significantly influence purchase behaviour. For instance, Margariti et al.
[52] found that positive attitudes towards green products increased purchase intentions. In addition, Sheikh et al. [75]
demonstrated that attitudes are strong predictors of green purchasing behaviour, although factors such as price sen-
sitivity and perceived risks can moderate this relationship. Furthermore, Ahmad et al. [76] showed that environmental
attitudes mediate the relationship between environmental awareness and green purchase behaviour, serving as a bridge
between knowledge and actions. These findings underscore that attitudes are not merely isolated constructs, but are
shaped by external factors, including marketing strategies, social norms, and individual values. The distinction between
specific and general attitudes is crucial for predicting green behaviour. For instance, Tan and Lau [77] found that specific
attitudes, such as green purchase attitudes, are stronger predictors of behaviour than general environmental attitudes.
This highlights the importance of targeting specific attitudinal dimensions in green marketing efforts to drive behaviour
change effectively.
Furthermore, attitudes towards green products are shaped by several factors, such as environmental knowledge [78,
79]; marketing efforts [80]. Green marketing strategies rely heavily on shaping and leveraging positive consumer atti-
tudes to promote eco-friendly behaviours. For instance, Sahioun et al. [81] suggested that green marketing can enhance
favourable consumer attitudes, leading to increased purchases of green products. Cerri et al. [82] emphasised the role
of ecolabels and information in forming positive attitudes and fostering green purchases. Invariably, Shieh et al. [83]
showed how environmental attitudes influence green advertising design, which, in turn, positively impacts purchase
intentions. These findings highlight the bidirectional relationship between green marketing efforts and consumer atti-
tudes, demonstrating that attitudes not only influence behaviour but are also shaped by effective marketing strategies.
While attitudes are strong predictors of behaviour, the attitude–behaviour gap persists as a significant challenge.
Studies have found discrepancies between consumers’ self-reported environmental attitudes and their actual behav -
iors [52, 84]. Most people with a positive attitude towards the environment may not purchase eco-friendly products.
For instance, some consumers value green practices, but are often unwilling to pay extra to purchase green products
[85]. In addition, concerns about greenwashing reduce the impact of positive attitudes on behaviour [52]. Furthermore,
materialistic customer values negatively impact environmental attitudes [86]. Additionally, social desirability bias can
distort self-reported attitudes [87]. Similarly, Singhal and Malik [88] revealed that income and demographic factors influ-
ence attitudes toward green products.
3.3.2  Green products and supply chain management
This thematic area emphasises the importance of sustainable supply chain practices in green business. Thus, green
innovation exerts the most significant influence on green supply chain management (GSCM) practices [89]. Mohamed
et al. [90] establish a link between green supply chain management practices (GSCM) and business performance (BP).
Furthermore, Tantan and Akdağ, [91] explore the effect of green supply chain management (GSCM) practices on three
sustainability dimensions; environmental, social, and economic. They found that GSCM practices were positively sig-
nificant for economic and social performance but not for environmental performance. Furthermore, “innovation and
recycling” in developing new products supports sustainability. Green marketing means integrating environmental con-
siderations into all aspects of new product development [92]. Thus, Firms should engage in environmental new product
development (ENPD) activities [93]. Thus, Green supply chain management (GSCM) has emerged as a key approach for
enterprises seeking to become environmentally sustainable [ 94]. Hence, Hong and Guo [ 95] opined that cooperation
among supply chain partners can lead to environmental improvement.
Studies have emphasised innovation as the backbone of green product development. Scholars have postulated that
collaborative environmental research and design (R&D) aligns manufacturers and retailers in optimising green product
development and reducing emissions [96]. In addition, Li et al. [89] state the strategic importance of green R&D in reduc-
ing environmental impacts during production and distribution. Importantly, regulations have been shown to play a
central role in encouraging green practice. For example, Zhu et al. [94] emphasised that effective policies are fundamental

3.3.3  Sustainable marketing strategies and corporate social responsibility (CSR)
Sustainability marketing focuses on the integration of sustainability into marketing strategies. Sustainability marketing
helps businesses achieve sustainability in their commercial and manufacturing operations while also having a significant
impact on their reputation [105]. Brand love has the potential for long-term influence only if sustainability marketing is
used as the backbone [106]. In addition, sustainability marketing messages appeal to consumers’ underlying sustaina-
bility-oriented awareness, responsibility, and obligations to predict their behavioural responses [107]. Corporate social
responsibility initiatives and sustainability marketing in organisations can promote ethical and sustainable consumer
behaviour [108]. Thus, perceived trust in social media is an antecedent of consumers’ sustainable purchasing attitudes,
which in turn influences their sustainable purchase intentions [109]. Thus, social media enhances sustainability market-
ing, competitive advantage, and organisational performance [110]. Moreover, the modern tourism industry’s secret is
its emphasis on sustainability [111].
Furthermore, the practice of marketing and selling products due to their beneficial effects on the environment is
known as "green marketing" , the product is eco-friendly [112]. Thus, it has been recorded that most companies con-
stantly review their production processes followed by suppliers to ensure their compliance with environmental specifi-
cations [113]. This suggests that companies gain a market edge by engaging in or adopting green marketing strategies.
Furthermore, green marketing supports environmental sustainability and encourages consumer behaviour that aligns
with environmental value. Thus, strategic green marketing orientation has a significant and positive influence on envi-
ronmental sustainability [114, 115]. The development of a green economy has become a global consensus [116]. This
has awakened consumer awareness of sustainable consumption, and more companies have realised the significance of
green marketing in improving brand performance [51]. Furthermore, Environmental concerns, green promotional initia-
tives, green customer trust, and green marketing challenges are considered green marketing practices [117]. Specifically,
research has shown that consumers are becoming more aware of environmental concerns and how their purchase habits
impact the environment [118]. Environmental concerns significantly and positively affect the intention to purchase green
products [79]. In addition, sustainable marketing practices result in enhanced brand trust [119], and consumer trust in
green labelling influences their green product satisfaction and perceived value [120]. Furthermore, the quality of green
information strongly predicts green brand love and brand trust among consumers of natural or organic products [121].

consumption behaviour through the lens of planned behaviour (TPB). Sabbir et al.  [126] studied the determinants
of consumers’ e-waste recycling behaviour using the theory of planned behaviour. They found a significant positive

4  Managerial implications
The findings of this study offer noteworthy managerial implications for organisations aiming to excel in green and
sustainable marketing. Managers can use these insights to optimise strategies, build consumer trust, and contribute
to environmental sustainability. The steady growth in green marketing research, particularly in countries such as
the United States, India, and China [37, 43], signals increasing global interest in green and sustainability marketing.
Thus, managers and organisations in emerging markets, especially Africa and South America, should recognise this
opportunity to position themselves as pioneers in underrepresented regions. Moreover, proactive investment in
green initiatives and research can help bridge global disparity and capture untapped markets. In addition, collabora-
tive international efforts, such as knowledge sharing and partnerships with global leaders in green and sustainable
marketing, can facilitate the development of tailored solutions for local markets.
Furthermore, as greenwashing undermines consumer trust [46, 47], companies must ensure the authenticity of
their sustainability claims. Organisations should invest in robust verification systems such as third-party certifica-
tions and transparent reporting mechanisms to reinforce brand trust. In addition, educating consumers through
greenwashing literacy initiatives [ 55] can improve their ability to discern genuine efforts from misleading claims,
further strengthening brand loyalty. Incorporating regulatory compliance and ethical marketing practices is crucial
for mitigating the negative impacts [57].
Environmental awareness, social norms, and perceived product value [67, 68]. In addition, leveraging eco-labelling
[82] and product quality [103] and emphasising affordability can encourage consumer adoption of green products.
However, bridging the attitude–behaviour gap, addressing cost-related concerns, and reducing perceived risks [52,
85], can further enhance green purchase intentions.
Moreover, managers should embrace Green Supply Chain Management (GSCM) practices to integrate sustainability
across production and distribution processes. Green innovation, a critical driver of GSCM practices, positively affects
business performance [90, 91]. In addition, investing in closed-loop supply chain systems and green technologies
can reduce operational costs and improve resource efficiency, yielding long-term benefits [103, 104]. Thus, managers
should realise that collaborative partnerships among supply chain actors are crucial for achieving environmental
improvement [95].
Organisations should align their marketing strategies with sustainability goals by integrating Corporate Social
Responsibility (CSR) initiatives into their branding efforts. CSR and sustainability marketing can enhance brand trust
and positively influence consumer behaviour [108]. Leveraging social media platforms for sustainability marketing
has been shown to improve consumer engagement and competitive advantage [109, 110]. Thus, sustainability mes-
sages that appeal to consumer values foster long-term brand loyalty [106, 107].
Finally, the lack of green marketing research and initiatives in developing regions highlights the need for targeted
capacity-building programs [43]. Organisations in these underrepresented regions should collaborate with academic
institutions, policymakers, and international organisations to foster green marketing research and practice [94, 98]. By
engaging in green and sustainability marketing, organisations can not only contribute to environmental sustainabil-
ity, but also strengthen their market position, improve stakeholder relationships, and achieve long-term profitability.

5  Theoretical implications
This study contributes to the body of knowledge by identifying critical thematic areas in green and sustainability marketing,
and their interrelationships. First, it reinforces the importance of the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) as a robust framework
for understanding consumer intentions and behaviours toward green products [62, 63]. The theory of planned behaviour
highlights the role of attitudes, social influence, and perceived behavioural control in shaping green purchase behaviour.
In addition, integrating behavioural theories such as TPB, Innovation Resistance Theory (IRT), and the stages of behavioural
change (SOC) model provides a nuanced understanding of the barriers and enablers of green consumption [127].
Additionally, this research expands on the concept of greenwashing and its dual impact on consumer behaviour. While
greenwashing raises environmental awareness, it also undermines consumer trust and purchase intentions, calling for a bal-
ance between ethical marketing practices and regulatory interventions [46, 55]. Furthermore, the findings underscore the
strategic importance of integrating sustainability into supply chain management, innovation, and corporate social respon-
sibility [90, 94]. By linking green marketing strategies to business performance and consumer trust, this study provides a
theoretical basis for future research on the long-term impacts of sustainable practices.
6  Practical implications
This study on green and sustainability marketing has significant practical implications. For instance, findings on green con-
sumer behaviour emphasise the need for structured marketing strategies that align with consumer values and responsible
consumption. Businesses can promote SDG 12 by adopting ethical marketing practices, increasing consumer awareness of
green products, and fostering trust through transparency [47, 79], which will promote responsible consumption and pro-
duction. For example, addressing greenwashing through education and regulatory measures ensures that consumers make
informed and sustainable consumption choices [55].
The growing interest in green marketing research reflects heightened global awareness of climate change, which aligns
with the SDG 13. Thus, by integrating green practices into supply chains and product development, organisations can mitigate
environmental impacts [89]. Moreover, policies and innovations in green supply chain management directly support climate
action by encouraging businesses to reduce emissions and resource wastage [94, 95]. The integration of sustainability into
corporate strategies and supply chains underscores its potential to foster green economic growth which ties with SDG 8.
Organisations that adopt green marketing and sustainable practices will achieve long-term benefits such as brand loyalty
and competitive advantage, which contribute to economic sustainability [100, 115].
This study highlights the disparities in green marketing research between developed and developing nations. This calls
for increased collaboration and investment in regions with low participation, particularly in Africa and parts of South America
[37, 43]. Strengthened international partnerships can bridge knowledge gaps, expand green initiatives, and create a globally
inclusive framework for sustainable development, which ties with SDG 17. Green products and marketing efforts aimed at
influencing consumer attitudes toward sustainability will lead to sustainable cities, communities, and ties with SDG 11. Eco-
friendly consumption and waste management practices contribute to sustainable living environments [79, 91]. Moreover,
encouraging eco-friendly product development, recycling, and closed-loop systems through technology helps industries
evolve sustainably, thereby supporting environmental and economic goals [97, 103]. Investments in green technologies
and innovation in supply chain practices align with the SDG 9. Policymakers can draw on the study’s findings to design
and enforce regulations that encourage sustainable practices, while addressing disparities in research and practice across
regions. Policymakers can promote inclusivity and equity in green marketing research and implementation by fostering
international collaboration and capacity-building initiatives. Institutions and educators can incorporate the study’s insights
into curricula to equip future professionals with the knowledge and skills needed to drive sustainable practices. Overall, this
study provides a comprehensive framework for advancing green and sustainable marketing research and practice, paving
the way for a more equitable and environmentally conscious global economy.
7  Conclusion
This study highlights the growing global interest and academic engagement in green and sustainability marketing,
as evidenced by the steady increase in the number of publications from 2009 to 2023. This trend reflects heightened
awareness of environmental issues, increased research funding, and greater global attention to sustainable practices

8  Limitations of the study
Bibliometric analysis relies on procedural decisions, such as choosing documents based on language, field of study, and
type of documents, which may exclude relevant papers from the analysis. In addition, our study was obtained exclu -
sively from the Scopus database, and other databases were not considered. However, a multi-source database might
provide a broad overview of the investigation in this field and a better comprehension of the subject area. This study
was limited to papers written in English and documented in journal outlets, which may have led to the exclusion of some
high-quality papers. Also, this study is limited by the following keywords "Green Marketing" OR "Sustainab* Marketing"
(August 2024). The inclusion of additional keywords may have led to the inclusion of additional studies. A bibliometric
approach provides an eminently descriptive analysis and may lack content analysis to increase the explanation capacity
and provide a deeper analysis of the results and implications.
