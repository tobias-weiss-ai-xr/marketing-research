Abstract
The latent nature of many phenomena we study in marketing necessitates the use of multi-item scales to adequately reflect
construct richness. Ensuring that these scales are both valid and reliable provides a critical foundation for subsequent
research. In pursuit of this goal, we propose a refined scale-development process model that builds on existing standards
while emphasizing the role of theory for creating meaningful, valid, and novel scales. Further, we use a “methods-in-
use” approach to systematically review scale-development papers published in premier marketing journals over the past
25 years to identify areas both where marketing scholars appropriately use established guidelines, but also where ele -
ments are executed with insufficient rigor. This leads to recommendations to help scholars avoid some common pitfalls
we observed in reviewing and evaluating extant scale-development research. Finally, drawing on our framework in con -
junction with observations of current scale development practices as well as recent methodological advances (e.g., the
emergence of AI), we delineate best practices for creating high-quality scales.

Importantly, we emphasize a fundamental issue that has
not, in our view, been given adequate treatment—the appro-
priate role of theory in scale development. Most scholars
note the importance of theory, but in practice quantitative
elements (e.g., item reliability, convergent validity) receive
more attention. In contrast, theory-related elements (e.g.,
domain sampling and nomological validity) receive sub -
stantially less emphasis. Previous reviews of scale develop-
ment have done a good job of refining its various elements
(e.g., MacKenzie et al., 2011), but no previous paper has
emphasized theory’s role in each stage of the process.2
We rectify this imbalance by explicitly calling out and
articulating key theory-related stages in the scale development
process and by identifying best-practices associated with their
assessment, building on past frameworks. Our fundamental
rationale is that without sufficient theory guidance across multi-
ple steps of measure development, the meanings of subsequent
empirical findings are unclear (DeVellis & Thorpe, 2022).
Further, enhanced use of theory can reduce the likelihood of
construct proliferation and/or the creation of overlapping, ill-
defined constructs that harm our ability to systematically build
scientific knowledge within our discipline (Locke, 2003; Nete-
meyer et al., 2003). Finally, precisely defined constructs are a
critical part of good theory (Podsakoff et al., 2016).
As a second contribution, we identify the standards that
contemporary scholars apply to develop and validate emergent
scales by employing a “methods-in-use” review of articles
published in strong marketing journals since 2000 (we refer
to these articles that develop new scales as “emergent-scale
articles”). We chose this date as it captures most scale work
since the last major marketing work on scale development
(e.g., Bearden & Netemeyer, 1999; Netemeyer et al., 2003).
Our review reveals areas where established scale development
procedures are and are not well utilized. These insights inform
our refined process with a deeper role for theory.

A clearer role for theory in scale
development
Whereas scale-guidance articles note that theory plays an
important preliminary role in determining the nature of
the construct, discussions quickly turn to more mechani -
cal steps in the scale-development process. For example,
Churchill’s Fig. 1 (1979, p. 66) indicates the need to “spec-
ify domain of construct,” but his process then immediately
moves to generating potential measurement items. Hinkin
(1995) similarly mentions the need for construct definition
but then focuses his “Stage 1” discussion around item gen -
eration. Netemeyer et al. ( 2003) distinguish between con -
struct definition and item generation but lump them into a
single chapter. In contrast, we assert that these steps are
distinct (albeit interrelated) and equally critical elements in
designing a useful scale and that the role of theory should
not be downplayed. 3 MacKenzie et al. ( 2011) explicitly
distinguish between theoretical and empirical elements in
their framework (e.g., see their Fig. 1), but their guidelines
do not systematically carry through to other elements of
their model.
This tendency to recognize theory but not engage with it
deeply is evident in emergent-scale articles published since

Our framework
The crucial but under-appreciated role of theory leads us
to propose an expanded and refined scale-development
process model that comprises six stages, most with both
theory- and empirically-focused steps, as shown in Fig. 1:
(1) construct definition and specification of content domain,
(2) generation of items , (3) reduction of items , (4) assess-
ment of dimensions and items , (5) assessment of scale, and
(6) establishing norms and determining generalizability .
Before applying the framework to evaluate recent research
and more-fully describing these stages, we emphasize two
important points: our framework’s theory emphasis and the
non-linear nature of scale development.
Theory emphasis To emphasize theoretical rigor, we illus -
trate two regions in Fig. 1, with the shaded region contain -
ing theory-focused steps. As the figure shows, there are
potentially more steps to conduct for theory-focused (versus
empirically-focused) validation, despite the propensity of
prior scale-development efforts to be empirically focused.
Both types of validation are crucial, but to be truly rigorous,
the theory-focused components must be fully addressed.
For example, we explicitly separate “Construct Definition
and Specification of Content Domain” as Stage 1 from
“Item Generation” as Stage 2. Across stages, we separate
the validity assessment steps into two categories: theory-
focused and empirically-focused. Additionally, the steps
and stages in our model differ to varying degrees from prior
frameworks, adding nuance and clarity where it is needed.
The non-linear nature of scale development As later stages
reveal new insights, we anticipate that researchers will
return to prior stages in our framework to ensure that a scale
remains true to its theory foundations. Despite the allure
of speed in scale-development to facilitate article submis -
sion, crafting a scale that is theoretically sound may require
returning to prior stages; as the researcher’s knowledge
grows this is often necessary and beneficial. Thus, even
though we present framework stages in a linear manner
for clarity, scale development involves iterations versus a
one-way flow. Figure 1 emphasizes potential returns to ear-
lier stages for stages 1 through 4. Although returns may be
needed at stages 5 or 6, this should be rare if scholars follow
best practices in the earlier stages.
We recognize that our scale development framework fol-
lows a traditional “define-measure-refine” model. Scholars
are increasingly interested in alternative approaches that
begin with data and then use computational methods to
derive theory (e.g., Herderich et al., 2024; Newman et al.,
2016). We discuss implications of these newer approaches
later but retain our focus on augmenting the traditional scale
development model for two keys reasons. First, marketing
scholars still heavily use this model, but, as we describe
below, there is substantial evidence that they do not con -
sistently follow existing best practices. In line with MacK -
enzie, Podsakoff, and Podsakoff ( 2011, p. 294), we strive
to “provide an updated set of recommendations that can
be used” to yield stronger, more valid scales. Second, our
focus is on systematically dealing with theoretical concerns
throughout the scale-development process. The framework
we present explicitly identifies specific actions scholars
should consider at each stage.
Reviewing scale-development practices in
marketing (2000–2025)
We delve into each stage and step of our framework in the
following section, but first we examine scale-development
standards via a systematic review of emergent-scale arti -
cles published since 2000 in eight high-impact marketing
journals: International Journal of Research in Marketing ,
Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science , Journal of
Consumer Psychology , Journal of Consumer Research ,
Journal of Marketing , Journal of Marketing Research ,
Journal of Service Research , and Marketing Science . We
searched for articles that indicated scale development using
numerous varied terms (e.g., discriminant validity, item
generation, scale development, nomological), generating an
extensive list (n = 558) (Web Appendix 1).
From our review, we recognized three general types of
emergent-scale articles: those that develop (1) a generaliz -
able scale for a construct, (2) a scale as an ancillary tool, and
(3) an industry-specific scale for research. Given our goals,
we focused on category (1) articles, retaining articles from
categories (2) and (3) only if they purported to develop a
generalizable scale for a construct; we erred on the side of
inclusion if we had any doubt. Consequently, we did not

that AI tools have evolved to the point of being able to pro-

we created and collaborated with an AI assistant (via Ope -

our coding, and an evaluation of a separate set of 40 articles

the authors. From this information, we developed a rubric
with thorough explanations of each step in our framework
to train and, over multiple iterations, fine-tune the coding
scheme and the AI assistant. This assistant then graded all

raw agreement of 93.4% and Perreault and Leigh’s ( 1989)
IRR = .949, supporting the usefulness of the AI codings. Of
note, the differences revealed that the human was slightly
more critical than the AI, particularly on theory steps, sug -

Stage 1: Construct definition and specification of
content domain
Theory plays three fundamental roles in Stage 1. First, it
guides the precise definition of the focal construct. A clear
definition is the bedrock for any scale; a weak founda -
tion will hinder subsequent scale-development steps and
the scale’s usefulness in application (Locke, 2003). Sec -
ond, theory situates the focal construct in a nomological
net, linking it to both the constructs it predicts as well as
to those that predict it. Doing so establishes the construct’s
value in the context of its relationships with other important
constructs, thereby motivating the development of a scale
for it. Establishing the construct’s unique conceptual value
enhances the likelihood that scholars will apply the scale in
future research. It also minimizes the likelihood of “prolif -
eration of different terms for the same concept,” which is
“one of the main factors that … inhibits conceptual progress
in science” (Podsakoff et al., 2016, p. 166). Third, theory is
crucial in determining whether the focal construct is unidi -
mensional or multi-dimensional, revealing whether distinct
sub-domains exist and guiding subsequent item generation.

construct, specify its domain, and engage domain experts
to critically review your application of theory. The first two
steps ensure that the construct is precisely defined and spec-
ified within a nomological net (i.e., these steps collectively

Define construct (100.0% ( n = 123); 95.1% (n = 123)) Nete -
meyer et al., ( 2003, p. 90) argue that “clearly defining the
construct—its facets and domains—is an essential first step
that many consider the most difficult step in the scaling
process.” Despite the difficulty, for a new scale’s validity,
researchers must invest the effort to precisely define the
construct, based on an intimate knowledge of the extant lit-
erature. Although all articles in our review define their con-
struct, examples of doing so without necessary rigor include
not clarifying whether a construct is uni- or multi-dimen -
sional, or when a scholar does not reconcile their definition
with existing theory in the literature.7
Specify domain (100% ( n = 123); 95.1% ( n = 123)) Relat -
edly, it is essential to determine both what is included in
the domain of the construct and what is excluded, avoiding
domains (and definitions) that are too narrow or too broad.
Netemeyer et al., ( 2003, p. 90) see the “key issue for the
content aspect of construct validity [to be] the clear specifi-
cation of the boundaries of the domain of the construct to be
assessed.” If the domain is overly narrow, the construct may
be of limited practical use. On the other hand, if the domain
is too broadly defined, elements beyond the target construct
may be included in the definition, leading to construct-irrel-
evant variance and confounds between the target construct
and related variables. Researcher confusion (Singh, 1991)
or deficient and/or contaminated construct operationaliza -
tions can result (MacKenzie, 2003; Podsakoff et al., 2016).
In sum, an explicit delineation of the domain of the con -
struct will help researchers justify their intended construct
and guide the scale-development process. Although the
articles in our review typically mention their domains, some
authors imply, rather than explicitly identify, their construct
domains, leading to ambiguity. Gahler et al. (2023) provide
a useful example of clear domain specification in develop -
ing their omnichannel customer experience scale.

and weaknesses of those prior measures. These interviews
can reveal novel ideas for use in item generation (Stage 2),
offering insights for creating items that are neither found in
the existing literature nor previously salient to the research

Stage 2: Generation of items (domain sampling)
Existing works provide guidance to help researchers gen -
erate potential measurement items (e.g., Baumgartner &
Steenkamp, 2001; DeVellis & Thorpe, 2022). Still, our
review suggests that item generation is too often conducted
without theoretical grounding. This is unfortunate, as any
set of potential items generated for a scale must clearly link
back to the focal construct’s definition.
For item generation to link to theory, three elements are
required. First, each item must represent a part of the con -
struct’s domain. Second, for a multi-dimensional construct,
items must represent each theoretical dimension. Finally, the
full set of items must collectively reflect the entire domain
of the construct. Whereas scholars usually address the first
two elements, the third, ensuring the set of items captures
the construct’s entire domain, requires authors to articulate
the construct’s full nature. In most definitions we observed,
authors noted the core aspect(s) of a construct, but often did
not clearly define its conceptual boundaries, making it dif -
ficult to capture its full domain.
According to Hinkin ( 1995), two broad approaches can
be used to generate items that are grounded in theory: a
deductive approach (i.e., “classification from above”) or
an inductive approach (i.e., “classification from below”).
We discuss the usefulness of each for enabling a scholar to
develop items that, individually and collectively, capture a
construct’s theoretical nature.
Deductive item generation (99.2% ( n = 123); 73.0%
(n = 122)) The deductive approach relies on clearly stated
theory, wherein the scholar attempts to generate items that
reflect various aspects of the construct’s domain (defined in
Stage 1). Use of this “top down” approach, starting from
theory, increases the likelihood that all items are theoreti -
cally relevant; thus, we believe it is the most useful method
for ensuring theoretical rigor. If the scholar takes sufficient
care to define the construct and specify its full domain in
Stage 1, creation and/or collection of items for this Stage
2 step can be relatively straightforward. Depending on the
construct, literature can either provide specific items from
prior ad-hoc instruments or items can be generated from
theoretically relevant verbiage.
Deficiencies in this step usually occurred from a failure
to identify specific articles that the scholar relied upon or a
lack of clarity regarding how the literature informed each
item (e.g., including a general statement that “the literature”
was utilized to generate items). A full reporting of sources
and a clear indication of how these sources were used can
address such issues.
Inductive item generation (78.9% ( n = 123); 39.2%
(n = 97)) The inductive approach is typically implemented
by the researcher asking respondents to reflect on their feel-
ings or beliefs toward a topic (Hinkin, 1995). Researchers
then content analyze responses to identify potential items
that relate to the construct of interest. Because the process
is often implemented without clear guidance from theory,
careful screening of resulting items is needed to delete
“items that are … conceptually inconsistent” with the focal
construct (Hinkin, 1995, p. 970).
Although an inductive approach is a useful complement
to deductive item generation and is especially appropriate
when investigating novel domains, we advise scholars to be
cautious about using it alone. First, because theory does not
guide item creation, items generated via induction may con-
nect poorly to the focal construct and have to be discarded.
Second, with an inductive approach alone, it can be difficult
to know when a set of items captures the construct’s full
domain. In fact, the opposite problem, “domain creep,” can
emerge as the scholar retains items that extend the emerging
scale’s meaning beyond the construct’s theoretical boundar-
ies. Drifts in either direction (poor coverage and/or domain
creep) can be difficult to detect. Thus, this approach entails
risk.

Stage 3: Reduction of items (assessment of domain
relevance)
Completing Stages 1 and 2 with rigor should result in a
set of potential items that directly relate to the theoretical
domain of the latent construct. However, when put into
psychometric use, not all generated items will turn out to
be suitable. An important next stage in refining a scale is
to ensure that the remaining items adhere to the construct
definition and domain, to specify whether the nature of
the relationship between the constructs and its measures is
reflective or formative, and to eliminate inadequate, poorly
performing items (i.e., item reduction). Our review revealed
three steps that scholars should conduct within this stage:
clearly specifying whether the constructs are formative or
reflective, face validity assessment, and Exploratory Factor
Analysis (EFA).8
Formative vs. reflective measures (87.8% ( n = 123); 28.7%
(n = 108)) The measures proposed for a construct can be
either reflective or formative. This decision has important
consequences for how to appropriately assess construct
validity and reliability in testing and purifying multi-item
scales (Jarvis et al., 2003; MacKenzie et al., 2005). Reflec-
tive measures (indicators) are seen as the observable out -
comes of an underlying (latent) theoretical construct,
whereas formative measures are viewed as causally defining
characteristics of the construct (Hulland et al., 2018).
Based on a review of articles, Jarvis et al., (2003; p. 216)
stated that “as many as 28% of the latent constructs with
multiple indicators published in the top marketing journals
were incorrectly specified as reflective when they should
have been formative.” As MacKenzie et al. (2005) describe
in detail, formative (versus reflective) measures require dif-
ferent approaches to scale purification. Thus, incorrectly
specifying the relationship between a construct and its mea-
sures can have serious consequences for scale development.
For example, because formative measures represent an
empirical census of characteristics that define the construct,
they may have low internal consistency assessments (DeV-
ellis & Thorpe, 2022). Reviewers trained to only evaluate
scales using a reflective indicator lens (e.g., valuing internal
consistency) may reject a sound formative scale.
To overcome such concerns, scholars must first consider
a theoretical question: whether their scale should use forma-
tive or reflective indicators. Table 1 in Jarvis et al ( 2003)
provides guidelines for making this decision. Second,

Face validity assessment (75.6% ( n = 123); 26.9%
(n = 93)) Face validity assessment is conducted by asking
domain experts to evaluate whether items align with the
intended domain and dimension(s) of the scale (Hardesty
& Bearden, 2004). That is, the language utilized in each
item should be consistent with experts' understanding of the
underlying construct. Furthermore, these experts should be
convinced that future respondents will understand the items.
In this step, scholars recruit experts (different from
those used in Stage 1 to avoid carryover effects) to evalu -
ate the theoretical relevance of potential scale items, i.e.,
item evaluation. Typically, the scholar gives the expert the
construct definition that delineates its domain and notes any
subdimensions. The experts evaluate an item’s usefulness
for the construct (or sub-dimension), using a rating scale
(e.g., a 7-point Likert scale, from “extremely inappropriate”
to “extremely appropriate”). Items that fall below the scale
mean (e.g., 0 on a scale from −3 to 3) should be reworded
or dropped. The experts can also provide open-ended item-
improvement suggestions.
An alternative method for soliciting face-validity assess-
ments from domain experts is item sorting  (“Q-sort”
technique; McKeown & Thomas, 2013). Q-sort is a forced-
ranking task in which experts sort items into discrete scale
categories that range from “ best aligns with a definition of
the focal construct” (or subdimension) to “ least aligns with
the construct’s definition,” restricting the number of items
that can be placed the in the “best” category (e.g., McKeown
& Thomas, 2013). Scores are assigned to items based on the
category in which experts sort them. Whereas Likert-scale
approaches ask experts to rate items one at a time, possi -
bly creating halo biases and a failure to discriminate among
items, Q-sort forces more-nuanced judgments because not
all items can be assigned one rating. Experts can again pro-
vide open-ended suggestions for item improvement.
Exploratory factor analysis (96.7% ( n = 123); 95.8%
(n = 119)) Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) is a commonly
used technique that looks for underlying common dimen -
sions (factors) associated with a larger set of variables. The
factors are estimated using the between-variable correla -
tions, most typically via a principal components approach.
The use of EFA on data from a sample of respondents is an
important step in scale development to empirically identify
commonalities between individual items proposed in the
item-generation stage. Although these data are collected
only for item evaluation (not as a formal pre-test of the
scale), it is preferable to sample respondents similar to the
intended respondents for the future scale (e.g., managers,
experienced consumers).
Note that whereas EFA will reveal items that do not prop-
erly load (i.e., have little variation in common with the other
items), it will not reveal items that load properly by chance,
without theoretical relevance to the focal construct. Thus,
scholars should supplement EFAs with insights gained from
item evaluation and/or sorting by experts to assess the theo-
retical relevance of items that load properly. This can occur
before EFA (collecting data on only theoretically relevant
items) or after EFA (dropping items that experts judge to be
inadequate).
From the studies we graded, less-rigorous execution of
EFA primarily occurred when researchers retained items
with loadings that were below accepted levels (i.e., < .70),
perhaps because too few useful candidate items had been
generated in Stage 2. We also gave lower rigor grades
when authors did not describe their procedures or provide
full disclosure of all loadings and cross loadings. Finally,
we assigned lower grades to studies in which authors did
not describe the nature of their experts’ expertise (academic
or practice) or when one’s expertise was questionable. In
a strong example of face validity assessment, Wilson and
Bellezza (2022) recruited 11 academic judges (faculty and
Ph.D. students) to rate their items’ clarity and representa -
tiveness, using this input to remove items from their pool
prior to EFA. In an example of utilizing a different set of
expert types, Sample et al. (2024) recruited practice experts
(architects, graphic designers, industrial designers) after
EFA to formally evaluate item appropriateness, validating
their proposed item set.
In our review of articles, we also noted that some authors
use the same dataset to estimate both EFA and CFA models.
This is an invalid approach as it yields deceptively strong
model fit indices and parameter estimates due to overfit -
ting the data (e.g., Fokkema and Greiff (2017)). If both EFA
and CFA are used, they should be run on separate data sets,
typically with EFA done first. This aligns with advise by
(Churchill, 1979, p. 69), and endorsed here, that any scale
measure should be subjected to “additional testing for which
a new sample of data should be collected.”

In Stage 4, the adequacy of scale items should be evalu -

ability. Both steps are often discussed in scale development
articles and fall within the theory-focused (dimensionality
assessment) and empirically-focused (item reliability) por -

Surprisingly, whereas researchers typically conduct both, a
fair number fail to do so with high rigor.
Dimensionality assessment (91.9% ( n = 123); 88.5%
(n = 113)) Because of the extensive use of dimensionality
assessments by articles in our review, we provide only a
brief overview of this step, making suggestions regarding
reporting practices. To test dimensionality of proposed mea-
sures, researchers typically use confirmatory factor analysis
(CFA) implemented within a structural equation modeling
framework. CFA uses theory to guide the specification of
the model dimensionality and then seeks to confirm that the
theoretically proposed structure is sound.

emerged as a viable alternative to CFA. UFA tends to handle
cross-loadings better than CFA (Steenkamp & Maydeu-Oli-
vares, 2023). Whereas the merits of UFA versus CFA are
still being explored, one or the other should be used. The
primary reason underlying low rigor grades for how articles
in our set assessed dimensionality was that many research -
ers did not clearly report all individual item loadings from
their analysis, depriving the reader of important information
needed to assess the performance of items, or used the same
sample and data as they used in Stage 3 for EFA.

Stage 5: Assessment of scale (validity assessment)
Validity assessment is an aspect of measure development
that marketing scholars take seriously, yet applying our the-
ory-focused vs. empirically-focused lens reveals inconsis -
tent application of standards across the articles we graded.
Scholars in our review exhibited the most rigor when using
the empirically-focused approaches of assessing convergent
and discriminant validity, and, less often, test–retest reli -
ability. Yet, as Carmines and Zeller ( 1979, p. 23) note, “it
is impossible to ‘validate’ a measure of a concept … unless
there exists a theoretical network that surrounds [it]. For
without this network, it is impossible to generate theoretical
predictions which, in turn, lead directly to empirical tests
involving measures of the concept.” From our review, we
see less usage and less rigor applied for the theory-focused
assessments of validity: nomological, predictive, known-
groups discriminability, and experimental-control. Specifi -
cally, the percent of articles that conducted these four theory
steps and did so with a rigor grade of 3 averaged 70.6%
versus an average of 81.9% for the three empirical steps.
Nomological validity (85.4% ( n = 123); 62.9%
(n = 105)) Nomological validity represents “the degree to
which the measure of the construct relates to measures of
other constructs in a manner that is consistent with theory”
(Houston, 2004, p. 157). Cronbach and Meehl ( 1955) refer
to this as the extent of “lawful” fit between proposed items
for a focal construct and a network of related constructs.
Assessing nomological validity “involves investigating both
the theoretical relationships between different constructs
and the empirical relationships between measures of those
constructs” (Netemeyer et al., 2003, p. 82). If the proposed
scale is sound, it will exhibit strong empirical connections
with these other measures in a pattern predicted by theory.
Specifically, a researcher seeking to assess validity by
testing a scale’s interrelationships within a nomological
network needs to (1) identify a set of theoretically related
constructs (2) for which useful, validated measures exist.
Drawing from theory and prior extant findings in the litera-
ture, the researcher specifies the direction (positive, nega -
tive) and perhaps even the strength (absolute or relative;
Lambert & Newman, 2023) of the relationships. After col -
lecting data from a sample of respondents for all constructs
in the nomological network, finding significant paths (of
predicted sign and strength) between the focal and related
constructs provides empirical evidence that the proposed
items perform in a manner consistent with theory (Nunnally,
1978).

ist Consumer Scale by demonstrating significantly higher
minimalism from a Facebook group focused on minimalism
(known-group) as opposed to a Facebook group focused on
books (control group).
Experimental-control validity (25.2% ( n = 123); 77.4%
(n = 31)) The term “experimental-control validity” refers to
the ability of scholars to manipulate the focal construct to
demonstrate that the manipulation has the expected effect on
related outcomes. Such a test combines the ability to manip-
ulate the focal construct with one or more of the theoretical
relationships it has with other constructs in the nomologi -
cal net. Although this step was infrequently employed, most
studies that used it demonstrated high rigor. For example,
Nenkov et al. ( 2008) manipulate their EPO construct to be
either high or low in the short term and confirm that the
manipulations are successful using their proposed EPO
scale and then use theory to predict that high EPO individu-
als will contribute more to their retirement savings. Findings
supported their predictions, providing empirical evidence
that the underlying focal, measured construct can both be
manipulated and that its behavior conforms to its expected
relationships with other constructs.
Convergent validity (99.2% (n = 123); 95.9% (n = 122)) Turn -
ing to the empirically focused steps for assessing validity,
convergent validity (i.e., construct-level reliability for the
scale) is assessed using either Cronbach’s coefficient alpha
or composite reliability (CR). Both approaches estimate the
squared correlation between a construct and an unweighted
sum or average of its measures; coefficient alpha is based
on equal weighting of the items whereas CR allows for
non-equal loadings. For both, a rule of thumb is that values

1 3
372
Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science (2026) 54:359–380
of 0.7 or higher provide confidence in the scale’s conver -
gent validity (Hulland et al., 2018; Nunnally, 1978). More
recently, some scholars recommend the use of omega over
alpha; however, as Hayes and Coutts (2020, p. 20) note, “in
our experience, the two measures typically produce simi -
lar estimates of reliability when applied to real data.” Their
conclusion applies whenever all the loadings have similar
magnitudes. Convergent validity results are easily reported
in a summary table of items and should always be included.
In general, scholars appropriately assess convergent
validity. However, a few papers in our review used scales
that did not meet the discipline-standard convergent validity
threshold (i.e., ≥ .70) and/or failed to report all item loadings.
Discriminant validity (91.9% (n = 123); 65.5% (n = 113)) Dis -
criminant validity represents the extent to which measures of
a given construct behave differently from measures of other
constructs that are analyzed in the same model (Hulland et
al., 2018). V oorhees et al. (2016) conduct Monte Carlo sim-
ulations to examine the effectiveness (i.e., the likelihood of
drawing an incorrect conclusion) of various approaches for
assessing discriminant validity, finding that the easiest-to-
conduct and most-used empirical tests are the weakest per -
formers. One such test is whether the correlation between
two constructs is less than unity. Another commonly used
test is offered by Fornell and Larcker (1981) who argue that
the average variance extracted (A VE) independently for
any two constructs (X, Y) should be larger than their shared
variance (squared correlation between X and Y). A popular
assessment of discriminant validity for multi-dimensioned
constructs is conducted by comparing model results when
dimensions are combined versus when they are separated,
relying on Chi-square difference tests (Bagozzi et al., 1991;
Jöreskog, 1969). Fourth, Henseler et al. ( 2015) heterotrait-
monotrait (HTMT) ratio of correlations has shown some
promise compared to other methods, especially for multi-
dimensioned scales. Finally, Pieters et al. ( 2025) propose
a new two-step procedure for discriminant validation that
outperforms any of the other single approaches.
In reviewing the discriminant validity literature, Pieters,
Baumgartner, and Pieters identify three requirements that
a useful discriminant validation criterion should possess.
Their review of 79 scale development articles published
between 2000 and 2024 reveals that many of the existing
approaches fail on one or more of these requirements. In
particular, they demonstrate that the Fornell and Larcker
(1981) criterion “is inappropriate for discriminant valida -
tion of multi-item scales and should no longer be used.”
Instead, Pieters, Baumgartner, and Pieters propose a two-
step procedure for discriminant validity assessment that
combines two criteria, the existing Phi criterion and a new
congeneric reliability (CR) criterion; both meet all three of
the requirements specified by Pieters et al. They then pro -
pose a procedure that “offers a graded approach to discrimi-
nant validation [that] moves beyond binary “yes” or “no”
conclusions” and provide both R-code and an online Shiny
app to help researchers implement the approach.
Because no studies in our sample could have included the
just-introduced two-step approach of Pieters et al. ( 2025),
we assigned high rigor scores for the articles we graded
when researchers assessed discriminant validity using
HTMT or A VE or assessed discriminant validity through
more than one method. Sample et al. (2024) provide a good
overview of discriminant validity assessment, especially
when encountering high correlations, using both traditional
and newer assessments. Going forward, we recommend
using the new Pieters et al. (2025) two-step approach.
Test–retest (26.0% ( n = 123); 84.4% ( n = 32)) Test–retest
reliability assessments provide evidence of scale validity
by examining the temporal stability of results using the
proposed scale measures. Researchers administer scale
items to participants at one point in time and then, after a
predetermined period, administer them again to the same
participants. Researchers choose the time delay between
administrations to allow subjects to forget their initial
responses without risking fundamental changes in subjects’
underlying attitudes or behaviors. A delay of at least two
weeks is typically recommended (e.g., Peter, 1979). High
between-administration scale-response correlations dem -
onstrate strong test–retest reliability. Cronbach’s alpha,
McDonald’s omega (McDonald, 1999), or Pearson’s r are
all commonly used, depending on the data composition.
Test–retest is especially applicable when developing
a trait scale, as these consumer specific measures should
remain consistent over periods of time. Nenkov et al.
(2008) provide a good example of this type of assessment.
Although test–retest is seldom utilized by scale-develop -
ment researchers in the articles we reviewed, when it is used
we find that this rather-straightforward step is usually exe -
cuted with high rigor and explained thoroughly.
Stage 5 Conclusion  Stage 5 sets the stage for implement -
ing a scale. As noted previously, the empirical steps of con-
vergent and discriminant validity tend to be heavily utilized
by researchers, yet the theory steps of nomological, known
groups, and experimental-control are less-frequently con -
ducted (and done with less rigor) by researchers. Greater
adherence to the theoretical procedures should bolster the
usability of a scale, setting the course for generalizability.

Stage 5: Key recommendations for rigorous scale develop -
ment practice.
 ● Establishing nomological validity among a network of
related constructs is an essential task to increase the
likelihood of creating a scale that is theoretically rel -
evant and practically useful. Use only well-validated
scales for the related constructs.
 ● For convergent validity, we challenge scholars to
hold all scales to disciplinary standards for scale-level
reliability.
 ● Demonstrating discriminant validity is an essential em -
pirical step as it demonstrates that a scale measures only
its intended construct and not other constructs. The two-
step procedure described by Pieters, Baumgartner, and
Pieters (2025) provides the strongest evidence of dis -
criminant validity.
 ● When creating scales to measure enduring traits, test–
retest data provide useful insights into a scale’s validity.
 ● Thoroughly document the process followed and the evi-
dence compiled in Stage 5.
Stage 6: Establish norms and assess generalizability
Generalization (80.5% ( n = 123); 5.1% ( n = 99)) As
Churchill (1979, p. 72) observes, “the raw score on a mea -
suring instrument … is not very informative … because the
units in which the scale is expressed are unfamiliar.” Over
time, as empirical evidence from studies using the scale
accumulates, expectations and norms (e.g., overall means,
group differences) can be established. Norm development
requires an accumulation of studies before high confidence
in those norms can grow; thus, the role of norm develop -
ment for a scale will vary based on when that a scale is
developed during the life-stage of a construct (Bergkvist
& Eisend, 2021).
Even if population-level norms cannot be established
during development, researchers will ideally attempt to
provide at least initial evidence for their scale’s generaliz -
ability. Whereas the predictive and experimental steps from
Stage 5 can aid in this effort, conducting an additional study
or two toward this aim can greatly bolster a scale’s contribu-
tion. The gold standard in marketing for providing evidence
of generalizability (or, at least, external validity) is a field
study (Gneezy, 2017). Another method to provide initial
evidence of generalizability is to test the scale in different
cultures/languages or by conducting an in-article meta-anal-
ysis of the performance of the scale across multiple stud -
ies. No matter the method chosen, establishing the broader
usability of a scale can provide greater confidence to future
researchers.
Finally, although there are many pressures on research -
ers to shorten scale length (e.g., desire to reduce respondent
fatigue, lower survey administration costs, and minimize
missing data), we recommend that researchers resist this
pressure, at least in the short term. To fully assess the gen -
eralizability of an original full scale, it needs to be tested
across various groups of respondents and in different con -
texts. Only after these early applications are completed can
the scale be confidently reduced in length. From our graded
set, Botner et al. ( 2019) and Reich et al. ( 2018) are among
several that did a fine job in establishing generalizability.
Stage 6: Key recommendations for rigorous scale develop -
ment practice.
 ● Over time, it is useful to determine norms for scale re -
sults across studies.
 ● In the shorter term, future researchers’ confidence in a
scale is enhanced by efforts to provide evidence of gen-
eralizability by using the scale in different context and/
or via field studies.
 ● Provide robust evidence across studies for a scale before
attempting to reduce it too drastically in length.
 ● Document the process followed, and the evidence com-
piled in Stage 6.
GenAI Considerations for scale development
The explosive growth in the availability and use of GenAI
creates new opportunities for scale design that can poten -
tially augment, or even replace, traditional approaches.
(Blanchard et al. (2025) provide a useful overview of GenAI
and LLMs (Large Language Models).) GenAI’s capabilities
are rapidly changing, so specific suggestions for using it in
developing scales may quickly become obsolete. Nonethe -
less, as GenAI evolves, we foresee that its use (in appropri-
ate ways) will enhance both the efficiency and effectiveness
of scale development. To help guide thinking about how
GenAI can be applied to scale development, we suggest
below how it may play a role in each stage of our frame -
work. (Table 2 summarizes key elements of our thinking.)
Before doing so, it is worth noting several general
insights drawn by marketing scholars regarding the
strengths and weaknesses of GenAI application in the
research context. Most importantly, there is broad agree -
ment that GenAI is best viewed as a research collaborator,
completing tedious and repetitive tasks that allow human
partners to focus on more creative, less structured ele -
ments of research design and execution (e.g., Arora et al.,

to code the articles we review here is a good example of
this collaborative approach.)
For example, GenAI can efficiently collect and summa -
rize a broad array of relevant literature for a specific research
question, but the resulting summary will be shallow (Epp &
Humphreys, 2025). Tomaino et al. (2025) attribute this in sig-
nificant part to GenAI’s inability to scan hybrid open-access
and closed-access journals (often top publications in many
behavioral sciences fields), limiting its knowledge base and
thereby potentially skewing its findings. Furthermore, the
probabilistic nature of LLMs results in an emphasis on the
most common elements embedded in the knowledge base and
a de-emphasis on novel insights, leading Epp & Humphreys,
(2025) to conclude that with use of GenAI, deeper interpreta-
tions of phenomena will not spontaneously emerge. Finally,
GenAI is not wholly reliable (e.g., De Freitas et al., 2023) and
can generate seemingly plausible information that does not
match the inputs (i.e., hallucinations; Alkaissi & McFarlane,
2023) and should be monitored for veracity.
There is also agreement that GenAI can generate promising
study designs, both experimental and survey (e.g., Arora et al.,
2025; Blanchard et al., 2025), and that synthetic study partici-
pants can be useful for pilot testing initial materials (Tomaino
et al., 2025; Yoo et al., 2025). However, results obtained using
GenAI subjects do not consistently mirror those obtained
using traditional approaches. One key reason for this (although
there are others) is that the knowledge database used to derive
GenAI samples can be biased due to the inaccessibility of
some important studies (Yoo et al., 2025). Moreover, Park et
al. (2024) observe that outliers in the underlying population can
be under-sampled by LLMs, leading them to express doubts
regarding “the validity of using LLMs as a general replacement
for human subjects in the social sciences.”

Reconciling Stages 1 and 2  Traditionally, the process of
scale development begins with the conceptual identification
and subsequent definition of a construct, followed by the
generation of relevant measures. However, as noted earlier,
this linear approach is being challenged by new methodolo-
gies that allow data to inform construct identification and
definition. Chen et al. (2025, p. 185) suggest that the arrival
of the “big data era” combined with these new measurement
tools offers researchers an opportunity to study constructs
“using not only item-response data but also a much wider
range of daily behavioral data including those from social
media, facial expression and writing and speech.” Using
such data can inductively inform how constructs are con -
sidered and operationalized. As an early example, the NLP-
based computational method proposed by Herderich et al.
(2024) analyzes text data to reveal latent constructs.
These inductive approaches to construct definition show
promise, suggesting the need to potentially iterate more
fully between the theory steps in Stages 1 and 2 of our
framework. At the same time, researchers need to resist the
temptation to rely too heavily on observables. De Boeck et
al. (2023, p. 239) suggest that this trend “arises from dis -
satisfactions with the vagueness of constructs” and a “lack
of progress in accumulating knowledge” but advise that
over-reliance on a bottom-up (inductive) approach to con -
struct measurement will still require conceptual integration
if meaningful knowledge is to be gained. In our view, this
necessitates an ongoing engagement with both Stages 1 and
2 to simultaneously ensure both a proper construct defini -
tion and identification of appropriate empirical measures for
the construct.
Stage 3 (Item reduction)  For thoroughly studied domains,
GenAI can provide assessments of face validity. Provided
that procedures are executed with care (e.g., see Blanchard
et al. 2005 for guidelines), GenAI can be utilized as a
regular consumer (Lake & Baroni, 2023) or as a domain-
specific expert (e.g., De Freitas et al. 2023). In these roles,
GenAI will primarily supplement existing approaches to
judging item suitability. Arora et al. ( 2025) encourage use
of LLM-generated synthetic samples for early item assess -
ment (e.g. pilot study or pretest). In addition, Gonzalez
(2021) proposes use of a machine learning approach to
item elimination, although he finds that the effectiveness
of this approach depends heavily on context (e.g., item
complexity).
Stage 4 (Dimensionality; item reliability)  GenAI can auto -
mate calculations related to dimensionality and reliability
and may be of potential code writing assistance. However,
whereas GenAI may be well suited to conceptually deter -
mining the appropriate number of dimensions (i.e., Stages
1 and 2), the benefits of using GenAI in Stage 4 appear
limited.
Stage 5 (Scale validity assessment ) GenAI can play a role
for various steps in Stage 5. For example, it can be trained
or informed to behave like certain entities (De Freitas et al.,
2023) or serve as an alternative test group for known-groups
differences. Additionally, whereas research indicates that
GenAI broadly mirrors human behavior in response activi -
ties (e.g., Mei et al. 2024), researchers can use it sparingly
as test subjects for nomological, experimental, and predic -
tive validity, and even test/retest assessments. However, as
noted earlier, there is growing recognition that GenAI is best
used as a preliminary validation tool rather than as a defini-
tive test of validity. Scale developers need to remain vigilant
in overseeing implementation of GenAI in such situations,
being cautious when interpreting findings and taking care to
use clear, precise prompts.
The generative abilities of AIcan also facilitate the cre -
ation of novel or the modification of existing experimental
designs. This can be particularly powerful for replication
studies. However, researchers need to avoid the creation
of overly complex designs while ensuring a suitable match
between the proposed experiments and the focal domain.
Tomaino et al. ( 2025), Arora et al. ( 2025), and Yoo et al.
(2025) all recommend GenAI use for early study design and
stimuli generation combined with thoughtful human refine-
ment to eliminate concerns, such as design confounds or
inappropriate wording.

to be used in new research contexts. It might also be used to
find and summarize instances of related scale items in other
domains. However, AI’s most promising longer-term appli-
cation with respect to generalizability likely lies in its ability
to create shorter versions of existing scales as new empiri -
cal evidence is cumulated, without degrading their validity
(e.g., Götz et al., 2024). To date, the effectiveness of this
approach has been mixed (see Gonzalez, 2021), so further

Summary of GenAI usage for scale development  GenAI
(and related) tools show substantial promise as aids to tradi-
tional scale-development practices, supplementing various
steps of the scale development process. Used appropriately,
they can help provide clearer construct definitions, generate
and refine large numbers of suitable scale items, and pro -

need to be aware of both the strengths and limitations of
GenAI approaches. We agree with the growing sentiment
(e.g., Arora et al., 2025; Blanchard et al., 2025; Tomaino et
al., 2025) that well-managed human-AI collaborations have
the potential to be both more efficient and more effective
than traditional research approaches.
Discussion
The latent nature of many phenomena we study in market -
ing necessitates the use of multi-item scales to adequately
reflect construct richness. Churchill (1979) proposed guide-
lines for scale development that have been applied in many
studies over the past half-century. In writing this article, we
set out to reexamine the quality and performance of these
guidelines, with the aim of ensuring that our field adheres to
the highest standards for scale-based research.
We offer four key contributions. First, we propose a
refined scale-development process model that builds on
existing standards and corrects weaknesses in scale-devel -
opment practice. Our framework emphasizes the role of
theory for creating meaningful, valid, and novel scales.
Further, stronger use of theory reduces construct prolif -
eration and aids the systematic development of marketing
knowledge. Most crucially, because a scale developed to tap
an important, emerging construct may underlie the founda -
tional knowledge for an entire research domain (e.g., Kohli
et al. (1993) market orientation scale, MARKOR), the scale
must be theoretically sound, or subsequent research will rest
on faulty grounds.
Second, we use a “methods-in-use” approach to examine
the standards marketing scholars use to develop and validate
their scales. Doing so reveals aspects of scale-development
in which established procedures are appropriately used but
also aspects that are executed with insufficient rigor. The
stage-by-stage recommendations we offer can help future
scholars avoid some of the common pitfalls we observed in
reviewing and evaluating extant scale-development efforts.
Third, by drawing on our framework, our observations
of current scale-development practices, and our review of
recent scale-guidance articles, we delineate best practices
for creating high-quality scales. Table 3 summarizes these
practices and describes both common deficiencies associ -
ated with each stage (and step) of our framework as well as
recommendations.
Finally, we describe a potential role for GenAI in refining
each of the six stages embedded in our proposed scale devel-
opment process. There is a growing sentiment that GenAI
can be a valuable contributor to experimental and survey
design as well an engine for synthetic samples. Increasingly,
researchers see GenAI as a potential collaborator, complet -
ing mundane tasks to free human researchers to focus on
more challenging aspects. We discuss some opportunities in
the preceding section but acknowledge that AI-human col -
laborations need further study to reveal the most promising
applications. Over time, we believe that the linear approach
to scale development described here will be further trans -
formed by the availability of new methods that can use
data to inform construct identification and definition. While
this will not invalidate the framework we propose here, it
will require researchers to be flexible and adaptive in using
emerging AI tools.
Implications
Our goal with this paper is to encourage scholars who choose
to measure latent constructs to employ state-of-the-art rigor
so that findings are valid and useful. Data from self-report
survey-based scales have somewhat fallen out of favor in
marketing, replaced by econometric analyses of secondary
data measures that capture behavioral artifacts. We argue
that data from scales and secondary data are complements,
not adversaries. When a research question involves a latent
construct, it is often difficult to find secondary data mea -
sures that cleanly align with the full domain of the construct
(Houston, 2004). Theory-based multi-item scales can fill
this void. When sound scale-based evidence is combined
with behavioral data that shows that “something like” the
construct operates in the real world, scholars’ confidence in
their conclusions can grow.
Beyond that general implication, we include many spe -
cific and actionable tactical implications in the recommen -
dations we offer for each stage of the scale-development
process (summarized in Table 3). We conclude with three
further specific implications informed by our review and
evaluation of emergent-scale articles published in top mar -
keting journals.
First, our review reveals that scale-development report -
ing is increasingly relegated to online supplemental
materials. Whether due to the review process or authors’
preferences, we encourage both sides to view scale-develop-
ment efforts as a substantial contributing factor of an article.

Page constraints and complex subject matters force hard
choices regarding reporting scale development but should
not downgrade its importance. The scientific downside of
placing scale-development materials in a web appendix is
that reviewers and future readers may not take the time to
carefully study the supplemental materials. In turn, the scale
quality, confidently described by the author in the main text,
may not receive merited scrutiny. Thus, we believe that
authors should avoid placing key scale-development details
for crucial constructs in web appendices and that reviewers
and editors minimize the pressure to do so.
Second, scale-development papers are often sent for
review to domain experts who may not have specific
expertise in scale development. Our article can be use -
ful in reviewer-training workshops conducted by journal
editors. A published scale that is theoretically weak may
have unfortunate ripple effects across a scholarly domain
as future scholars employ the scale and reference its pub -
lication as evidence of scale quality. All researchers need
to better understand and value scale development so that
collective rigor increases for both development and use of
scales.
Finally, as doctoral programs in marketing train behav -
ioral and quantitative students, the amount of time and
coursework required to help these students develop the
toolkits to surmount the design, econometric, and cau -
sality demands of the review processes in top marketing
journals continues to increase. In turn, training in mea -
suring latent constructs, especially developing scales, has
become less of a priority. Scales are still important in our
field, however, because many of the phenomena we study
are attitudes, traits, and perceptions that cannot be directly
observed. Further, many experimental studies expose sub -
jects to manipulations and then capture responses via mea -
surement scales. This paper can be useful to help a doctoral
student (or junior professor) with little scale-development
training quickly gain a meaningful understanding of and
then apply a step-by-step process for developing a scien -
tifically useful scale.
