Introduction: The rapid emergence of Web 3.0 technologies presents a disruptive
force in global marketing, yet academic literature lacks a consolidated strategic
framework to guide practitioners in this transition. This study addresses this
critical research gap by systematically examining how decentralization,
blockchain, and tokenization fundamentally reconfigure advertising paradigms.
Methods: Employing a rigorous systematic integrative review methodology guided
by the PRISMA 2020 reporting standards, this research synthesizes 127 peer-
reviewed sources and industry publications from January 2020 to November
2025. Thematic analysis was conducted using NVivo software, and the Marketing
3.0 Framework was developed through an abductive reasoning process.
Results: The analysis reveals four foundational pillars that constitute the
proposed Marketing 3.0 Framework: (1) community-as-channel architecture,
(2) advertising-as-value-exchange mechanisms, (3) owned digital
environments, and (4) verifiable brand narratives. Findings demonstrate that
Web 3.0 enables a paradigm shift from extractive, platform-dependent
advertising to participatory, trust-based engagement models. The study
identifies significant implementation challenges including technological
scalability limitations, regulatory fragmentation, and user adoption barriers.
Discussion and Conclusion: Practical implications emphasize the need for
strategic literacy in tokenomics and community governance, while theoretical
contributions advance understanding of decentralization’s impact on
consumer-brand relationships. This research establishes a foundational
framework for future empirical investigation into decentralized marketing
ecosystems. Limitations include reliance on nascent literature, potential
publication bias, and lack of empirical testing of the proposed framework.

1 Introduction
The digital marketing ecosystem stands at an inflection point as Web 3.0 technologies
challenge the centralized architectures that have dominated the Web 2.0 era. Where previous
digital marketing paradigms relied on platform-controlled data aggregation and targeted
advertising algorithms, Web 3.0 introduces fundamentally different principles of
decentralization, user sovereignty, and cryptographic verification. This technological evolution
represents more than incremental improvement; it constitutes a paradigmatic shift in how
brands conceptualize, execute, and measure marketing communications in global contexts.
Despite growing academic interest in blockchain applications and tokenized economies,
marketing scholarship exhibits a significant theoretical gap. Current literature tends to

contributions. First, it develops the Marketing 3.0 Framework, a

analysis of implementation challenges and ethical considerations that
will shape the adoption of decentralized marketing practices. The
framework is analytical—categorizing the strategic shifts enabled by
Web 3.0—and prescriptive—offering actionable guidance for
marketers—while remaining open to empirical testing. The novelty of
this work lies in its integrative approach, moving beyond
technological description to develop a theoretically grounded
framework for strategic decision-making in Web 3.0 environments.
The subsequent sections detail the systematic methodology
employed, establish conceptual foundations, present the Marketing 3.0
Framework, analyze emerging practices through case studies, examine
implementation challenges, and outline future research directions.
2 Research methodology
2.1 Study design and guiding standards
This study employs a systematic integrative review methodology,
selected for its rigorous approach to synthesizing diverse literature and
developing theoretical frameworks in emerging research domains.
Systematic integrative reviews are particularly appropriate when
addressing complex, multidisciplinary topics where primary research
remains nascent, as they enable comprehensive analysis while
identifying conceptual gaps and theoretical inconsistencies (Snyder,
2019). While the synthesis method is integrative, the reporting of the
review process was guided by the PRISMA 2020 statement (Page
et al., 2021) to ensure transparency and reproducibility. A completed
PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in Supplementary File A, with
page references mapping each applicable item to the manuscript.
Where the integrative approach departs from standard systematic
review expectations (e.g., qualitative synthesis of diverse study types
rather than meta-analysis), these adaptations are noted.
2.2 Research question and scope
The review was structured around the following research question:
What strategic framework can guide global marketing practitioners in
navigating the transition from Web 2.0 to Web 3.0 advertising
paradigms? To specify the review’s boundaries, an adapted PICOS
framework was employed:
• Population: Global marketing and advertising practices;
• Intervention/Interest: Web 3.0 technologies (blockchain,
tokenization, semantic web, decentralized protocols);
• Context: Decentralized advertising ecosystems;
• Outcomes: Strategic shifts, engagement models,
implementation challenges, framework components;
• Study designs: Peer-reviewed journal articles, rigorously
reviewed conference proceedings, and seminal industry
whitepapers from established research institutions.
• This structured scope ensures that the review’s parameters
are transparent and reproducible.

2.7 Quality appraisal
Methodological quality of the included sources was appraised
using tools appropriate to each study type. Peer-reviewed journal
articles were assessed using the Critical Appraisal Skills

Conference proceedings were evaluated based on the
documented rigour of the review process. Industry whitepapers
were appraised using the Authority, Accuracy, Coverage,
Objectivity, Date, Significance (AACODS) checklist for grey
literature (Tyndall, 2010). Sources scoring low on
methodological transparency or relevance were excluded during
the eligibility stage. In the synthesis, claims grounded in peer-
reviewed evidence are explicitly distinguished from those based
on industry data or case reports by using qualifying language
such as “early industry analyses suggest” or “practitioner reports
indicate.”
2.8 Data extraction and thematic synthesis
Data from each included source were extracted into a
standardized template capturing: author(s), year, publication
type, study design (if applicable), Web 3.0 technologies
discussed, marketing domain, key findings, and emergent
themes. Thematic analysis followed established qualitative
research protocols using NVivo software [version (insert)]. Each
source underwent iterative coding employing constant
comparison techniques (Wieland and Durach, 2021). Initial
codes were refined through multiple analytical cycles, with the
coding framework emerging organically from the data. This
process identified seven primary thematic clusters: technological
infrastructure components, emerging advertising models,
consumer behavior implications, regulatory considerations,
ethical challenges, measurement approaches, and case study
analyses. Relationships, contradictions, and underexplored areas
within and between clusters were systematically examined.
2.9 Framework development
Building upon the thematic analysis, the Marketing 3.0
Framework was constructed through an abductive reasoning
process that integrated empirical observations with established
marketing theories (see Section 5 for theoretical grounding).
This approach enabled the development of a conceptually
robust model that both describes current practices and
provides prescriptive guidance for strategic implementatio n.
The framework’s validity was assessed through peer debriefing
with three marketing scholars specializing in digital
transformation, who reviewed the framework’s coherence,
completeness, and practical relevance. The framework was
further iterated through comparison with case study evidence
(Section 6.2). Feedback loops between the thematic analysis
and framework development phases (indicated in Figure 1)
ensured that the emergent model was firmly grounded in the
synthesized evidence.
3 Digital communication in global
marketing
3.1 The evolution from traditional to digital
advertising
Traditional advertising models, dominant throughout the
twentieth century, operated on broadcast principles
characterized by one-way communication, mass audience
targeting, and limited measurability. These models relied on
interruptive messaging through channels such as television,
print media, and outdoor advertising, where success metrics
primarily focused on reach and frequency rather than

engagement or conversion (Swacha and Kulpa, 2023). The
transition to digital advertising, accelerated by Web 2.0
technologies, introduced fundamental changes including precise
audience segmentation, real-time performance analytics, and
interactive engagement capabilities. However, this digital
transformation created new dependencies on centralized
platform intermediaries—notably Google, Meta, and Amazon—
whose algorithms and data policies increasingly mediate brand-
consumer relationships (Terranova, 2022).
The current digital advertising landscape exhibits significant
structural tensions between marketing efficiency and consumer
autonomy. While programmatic advertising enables
unprecedented targeting precision, it simultaneously raises
concerns about data privacy, algorithmic transparency, and
platform monopolization (Haleem et al., 2022). These tensions
have catalyzed regulatory responses including the European
Union’s General Data Protection Regulation and the California
Consumer Privacy Act, which constrain traditional data
collection practices while creating compliance complexities for
global marketing operations. Simultaneously, consumer
resistance manifests through ad-blocker adoption rates exceeding
40% in certain demographics and growing skepticism toward
personalized advertising (Dimoulas and Veglis, 2023). These
developments signal diminishing returns for traditional digital
advertising approaches and create strategic imperatives for
alternative engagement models.
3.2 Web 3.0’s transformative impact on
marketing strategies
Web 3.0 technologies introduce architectural changes that
fundamentally reconfigure marketing strategy foundations.
Blockchain technology enables transparent, verifiable transaction
records that address longstanding concerns about advertising fraud
and measurement inaccuracies. Industry analyses indicate that
digital advertising fraud currently drains substantial portions of
marketing budgets annually—one widely cited estimate suggests
figures in the tens of billions globally (Juniper Research, 2023, as
referenced in Fekri et al., 2023)—a problem that blockchain’s
immutable ledgers can significantly mitigate through verifiable
impression tracking and smart contract-based payment systems.
These claims, while largely derived from industry reports, are
increasingly supported by early empirical work demonstrating
blockchain’s capacity for transparent ad delivery verification.
Tokenization represents another transformative mechanism,
enabling microeconomic systems where consumer attention, data,
and engagement become directly compensatable assets. This shift
transforms marketing from an expenditure category to an
investment in community development and value co-creation
(Epstein, 2022). Brands can now design tokenized ecosystems
where participation rewards compound through network effects,
creating sustainable engagement loops that reduce dependence on
repetitive advertising expenditure. Practitioner reports from early

4 Conceptual foundations of Web 3.0
in marketing
Web 3.0 represents a paradigm shift in internet architecture
with profound implications for marketing theory and practice.
This research defines Web 3.0 as a user-centric digital ecosystem
enabled by decentralized protocols—particularly blockchain—
that redistributes data ownership and economic agency from
platform intermediaries to individual users, while incorporating
semantic technologies that enable machine-readable data
exchange and intelligent content discovery (Voshmgir, 2020).
This definition emphasizes three core dimensions: architectural
decentralization through distributed systems, economic
reconfiguration through tokenized value exchange, and
enhanced intelligence through semantic data structuring.
A critical conceptual clarification distinguishes Web 3.0 from
the semantic web, terms often conflated in marketing literature.
The semantic web, conceptualized by Berners-Lee et al. (2001),
focuses primarily on data standardization and machine-readable
content to improve information retrieval and automation. Web
3.0 incorporates semantic web principles but extends them with
decentralized governance and cryptographic economic layers
that enable peer-to-peer value transfer without centralized
intermediaries (Gan et al., 2023). This distinction matters
strategically because while semantic technologies improve
advertising relevance, Web 3.0’s economic layers transform
advertising’s fundamental value proposition and incentive
structures (see Table 1).

5 The marketing 3.0 framework:
strategies for decentralized
engagement
The Marketing 3.0 Framework represents a comprehensive
strategic model for leveraging Web 3.0 technologies in global
marketing contexts. Developed through systematic analysis of
emerging practices and theoretical synthesis, this framework
organizes decentralized marketing strategies into four
interdependent pillars that collectively address the architectural,
economic, and relational transformations enabled by Web 3.0
technologies. The framework is analytically descriptive—it
categorizes observed strategic shifts—and prescriptive—it offers
actionable guidance—while its propositions remain open to
empirical testing. Its theoretical grounding draws on established
marketing theories, which are elaborated below alongside each pillar.
5.1 Community as channel architecture
This pillar reimagines brand communities as primary
marketing channels rather than supplementary engagement
platforms. Unlike Web 2.0 communities hosted on centralized
social networks, Web 3.0 communities often organize as
decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs) with token-
based governance and shared resource pools. These
communities function as both engagement channels and co-
creation ecosystems, reducing dependence on paid media while
increasing advocacy authenticity (Mosch, 2022). Theoretically,
this pillar extends brand community theory (Muñiz and
O’Guinn, 2001) and service-dominant logic (Vargo and Lusch,
2004), which emphasize value co-creation and communal
identification. Web 3.0 technologies amplify these dynamics by
encoding governance rights and economic incentives directly

5.2 Advertising as value exchange
mechanisms
This pillar transforms advertising from interruptive messaging
to structured value exchange through smart contracts and token
incentives. Rather than purchasing attention through platform
intermediaries, brands design programmable value exchanges
where consumers receive direct compensation—in tokens, NFTs,
or other digital assets—for engagement activities including
attention, data sharing, content creation, or social amplification
(Hackl et al., 2022). This approach draws on value co-creation
theory (Prahalad and Ramaswamy, 2004) and transaction cost
economics (Williamson, 1985), positioning advertising costs as
direct investments in consumer relationships rather than
payments to intermediaries.
Implementation requires developing smart contract
templates for common engagement scenarios, designing token
economics that balance incentive effectiveness with
sustainability, and creating user interfaces that simplify
participation for non-technical audiences. Case evidence from
early value-exchange advertising pilots suggests engagement
rates several times higher than traditional digital advertising,
with improved brand perception metrics (James et al., 2023).
These results, while promising, derive from self-reported
industry data and small-scale pilots, necessitating caution
in extrapolation.
5.3 Owned digital environments
This pillar emphasizes brand-controlled digital spaces where
commerce, content, and community converge without platform
intermediation. These environments range from token-gated
websites and applications to immersive brand experiences in
metaverse platforms and virtual worlds. Unlike social media
brand pages that remain subject to platform algorithm changes
and policy shifts, owned digital environments provide brands
with complete control over user experience, data collection, and
monetization strategies (Mourtzis et al., 2022). The theoretical
foundation here lies in the disintermediation thesis and platform
capitalism critique (Srnicek, 2016), which highlight how
platform control over customer relationships extracts value from
both brands and consumers. Owned environments enable
brands to reclaim these relationships.
Implementation requires strategic decisions about technical
infrastructure—whether to build on existing blockchain platforms
or develop proprietary solutions—along with careful design of user
onboarding experiences that balance security with accessibility.
Brands investing in owned digital environments report reduced
customer acquisition costs and higher customer lifetime values
compared to platform-dependent approaches (Laato et al., 2023),
though these claims warrant independent verification.
5.4 Verifiable brand narratives
This pillar leverages blockchain’s transparency capabilities to
authenticate brand claims regarding sustainability, ethical
sourcing, product provenance, and social impact. By recording
verifiable credentials on immutable ledgers, brands transform
subjective marketing claims into auditable evidence, addressing
growing consumer skepticism toward corporate communications
(Potts, 2020). The pillar draws on signaling theory (Spence,
1973) and transparency literature (Rawlins, 2009), which posit
that credible signals reduce information asymmetry and build
trust. Blockchain provides a technological mechanism to make
signals verifiable rather than merely asserted.
Implementation involves developing credentialing systems for
supply chain verification, creating consumer-facing interfaces that
make verification accessible, and integrating these narratives
across marketing communications. Applications demonstrate
that verifiable sustainability claims increase consumer
willingness to pay compared to traditional certification systems,
while reducing greenwashing accusations and regulatory
compliance risks (Narayanasamy et al., 2022). Again, these
findings largely come from controlled experiments and pilot
programs; real-world generalizability is still being established.
5.5 Synergistic integration
The four pillars operate synergistically: community
architecture provides engagement foundations, value exchange
mechanisms drive participation, owned environments host
interactions, and verifiable narratives build trust. This integrated
approach addresses the fragmentation that often characterizes
early Web 3.0 marketing experiments, providing brands with a
comprehensive strategic roadmap for decentralized engagement.

6.1 Technological advancements reshaping
advertising infrastructure
Recent technological innovations are expanding Web 3.0’s
marketing applications beyond early experimentation phases.
Zero-knowledge proofs represent particularly significant
advancements, enabling privacy-preserving verification systems
where users can prove engagement or eligibility without
exposing personal data. These cryptographic techniques
reconcile blockchain’s transparency with privacy regulations,

allowing brands to verify audience characteristics for targeting
purposes while protecting individual identities (Chaccour et al.,
2024). Early implementation data suggests that zero-knowledge-
based advertising systems can reduce privacy compliance costs
while maintaining targeting effectiveness compared to traditional
data collection methods, though these figures currently come
from technology providers and require independent validation.
Layer-2 scaling solutions and alternative consensus
mechanisms address blockchain’s scalability limitations, enabling
real-time microtransactions for attention-based rewards at global
scale. Innovations including optimistic rollups, zk-rollups, and
proof-of-stake consensus reduce transaction costs by orders of
magnitude compared to base layer blockchains, making
microcompensation for advertising engagement economically
viable (Xu et al., 2024). These advancements facilitate new
advertising models where brands can compensate users for
attention in fractions of a cent, creating sustainable alternatives
to traditional advertising economics.
Artificial intelligence integration with semantic web
technologies enables context-aware advertising that responds to
real-time intent rather than historical behavior patterns. Natural
language processing advancements allow brands to engage
consumers through conversational interfaces that understand
nuanced context and cultural references, moving beyond
keyword matching to meaning-based engagement (Chen et al.,
2022). Practitioner reports indicate higher conversion rates for
semantic-aware advertising compared to traditional behavioral
targeting, with reduced consumer frustration metrics.
Decentralized identity protocols enable portable, self-
sovereign digital identities that users control across platforms
and applications. These identity solutions allow consumers to
manage reputation, preferences, and verification credentials
across brand interactions without repeatedly providing personal
information (Cao, 2022). Brands implementing decentralized
identity systems report reductions in customer onboarding
friction while improving data accuracy and compliance with
global privacy regulations, according to industry case studies.

Nike’s.SWOOSH platform represents a comprehensive
corporate implementation of Web 3.0 marketing principles,
moving beyond experimental NFT releases to develop an
integrated digital community ecosystem. The platform operates
on a private blockchain infrastructure that balances
transparency with brand control, offering virtual goods as
NFTs that function as both digital collectibles and community
membership credentials (James et al., 2023). Nike’s strategy
demonstrates sophisticated application of multiple Marketing
3.0 Framework pillars, particularly community-as-channel
architecture and advertising-as-value-e xchange mechanisms.
The platform’s community governance features allow NFT
holders to vote on product designs, community initiatives, and
charitable contributions, transforming passive consumers into
active brand stakeholders. Nike reports that voting
participation rates among NFT holders significantly exceed
typical brand survey response rates, and that secondary market
royalties from NFT trades flow partially to creators and
partially to community development funds, creating aligned
incentives (Mosch, 2022; Nike, 2023, as cited in corporate
communications). However, these performance figures derive
from company disclosures and should be interpreted as
indicative rather than independently verified benchmarks.
6.2.2 Starbucks odyssey
6.2.2.1 Blockchain-enhanced loyalty transformation
Starbucks Odyssey extends traditional loyalty mechanics
through blockchain integration, creating what the company
describes as a “third-place community” in digital form. The
programme rewards members with “Journey Stamp” NFTs for
completing brand engagements including educational modules,
interactive games, and purchase activities (Jiang and Murmann,
2022). These digital assets are tradable on an integrated
marketplace, introducing economic utility beyond traditional
points redemption. The programme’s technical implementation
utilizes a custodial wallet solution that lowers participation
barriers for mainstream consumers while maintaining blockchain’s
transparency benefits. This hybrid approach demonstrates how
established brands can implement Web 3.0 principles without
requiring technical sophistication from their customer base
(Albaom et al., 2022). Starbucks has publicly shared data
indicating that Odyssey members exhibit higher purchase
frequency and greater brand advocacy behaviors compared to
traditional rewards members (Starbucks Investor Relations, 2023).
As with the Nike case, these performance metrics are company-
reported and have not been independently validated.
6.2.3 Friends with benefits
6.2.3.1 Decentralized brand governance through DAO
structures
Friends With Benefits (FWB) operates as a cultural
community where membership requires ownership of the FWB
token, with all major decisions—including marketing initiatives,
partnership selections, and budget allocations—determined
through token-holder voting (Guerola-Navarro et al., 2024).
This model eliminates traditional marketing hierarchies, with
community members proposing, funding, and executing
marketing activities through transparent treasury management.
The FWB case illustrates the most complete implementation of
Web 3.0 marketing principles, with full decentralization across
all marketing functions. While this approach presents scalability
challenges for mass-market brands, it provides valuable insights
into community-led brand building and transparent resource
allocation. Data from community analytics suggests that
member-generated content achieves higher engagement rates per
dollar spent compared to traditional influencer marketing
campaigns (Fekri et al., 2023), though these comparisons rely on
community self-reporting and lack rigorous control conditions.
7 Challenges and ethical
considerations
7.1 Privacy and data security in
decentralized systems
Web 3.0 introduces novel privacy and security challenges that
marketers must address strategically. While blockchain
transparency provides auditability benefits, its immutable nature
creates compliance conflicts with data protection regulations
including the GDPR’s “right to be forgotten” provisions
(Dimoulas and Veglis, 2023). Technical solutions including
zero-knowledge proofs and selective disclosure mechanisms offer
promising approaches, but implementation remains complex for
marketing applications requiring both verification and
privacy protection.
Security vulnerabilities in smart contracts present significant
risks. Industry analyses have documented substantial financial
losses from smart contract exploits (Narayanasamy et al., 2022).
Marketing applications involving token distributions or
automated reward systems require rigorous security auditing and
failsafe mechanisms to protect both brand assets and consumer
funds. The irreversible nature of blockchain transactions
amplifies these risks, necessitating sophisticated key management
solutions and consumer education initiatives.
Consumer understanding of security practices remains a
substantial barrier, with surveys indicating that a minority of
mainstream consumers feel confident managing cryptographic
keys or identifying wallet security threats (Albaom et al., 2022).
Brands implementing Web 3.0 marketing initiatives must invest
in educational resources and user-friendly security interfaces to
prevent asset losses that could damage brand trust and
consumer adoption.

remains fragmented and rapidly evolving, creating compliance
challenges for international campaigns. Jurisdictional variations

Advertising standards and consumer protection regulations are

scalability advantages.
7.3 Ethical imperatives for Web 3.0
marketing practices
Web 3.0 marketing introduces ethical considerations
extending beyond regulatory compliance to encompass fairness,
inclusion, and social responsibility. Algorithmic transparency
represents a critical concern, with token distribution
mechanisms and community governance systems requiring
auditability to prevent hidden biases or exclusionary practices
(Haleem et al., 2022). Brands must implement transparent
algorithms with explainable decision-making processes,
particularly when automated systems determine reward
allocation or community participation rights.
Inclusive design principles must guide tokenomics and
community structures to prevent exacerbation of digital divides.
Early Web 3.0 implementations have demonstrated exclusionary
tendencies, with gas fees, technical complexity, and financial
requirements creating barriers for diverse participation (George,
2024). Ethical implementations require designing for accessibility
across technical proficiency levels, economic circumstances, and
geographic locations, potentially incorporating tiered participation
models or subsidized entry pathways.
Environmental sustainability concerns associated with energy-
intensive consensus mechanisms necessitate conscientious
technology selection. While proof-of-stake and other energy-
efficient alternatives reduce environmental impact, brands must
evaluate their technology choices through sustainability frameworks
and communicate these considerations transparently (Mourtzis
et al., 2022). Marketing initiatives should align with broader
corporate sustainability commitments, with environmental impact
assessments informing implementation decisions.
Consumer financial literacy and protection represent
additional ethical responsibilities, particularly given the
speculative nature of some tokenized assets. Brands must
provide clear risk disclosures, avoid overly complex financial
mechanisms, and implement safeguards against predatory
behaviors within their ecosystems (Epstein, 2022). These
protections balance innovation with consumer welfare, building
sustainable trust essential for long-term Web 3.0
marketing success.
8 Future trends and predictions
8.1 The evolving landscape of global
marketing
Global marketing will increasingly operate across
interconnected decentralized ecosystems, with competition
extending beyond traditional product and communication
dimensions to encompass the robustness and fairness of
tokenized brand communities. Success metrics will evolve to
include community health indicators such as token distribution
equality, governance participation rates, and stakeholder value
creation alongside traditional performance indicators (Mosco,
2023). This expanded measurement framework will encourage
more participatory relationships between brands and global
audiences, reducing dependence on monopolistic platform
intermediaries while increasing strategic emphasis on
community development and value alignment.
Cross-platform interoperability will emerge as a critical
capability, with portable reputation systems and asset
transferability enabling seamless consumer experiences across
brand ecosystems. Standardized protocols for identity, credentials,
and asset transfer will reduce friction in decentralized marketing
environments while maintaining user control (Cao, 2022). Brands
that pioneer interoperable systems will gain significant
competitive advantages through network effects and reduced
customer acquisition costs, potentially reshaping industry
structures toward more open, collaborative models.
Regulatory maturation will gradually reduce implementation
uncertainties while establishing clearer compliance frameworks
for global operations. Convergence toward international standards
for token classification, consumer protection, and tax treatment
will emerge through regulatory coordination and industry self-
regulation initiatives (Chaccour et al., 2024). This stabilization will
accelerate mainstream adoption while establishing guardrails that
protect consumers and maintain market integrity.
8.2 Strategic implications and research
directions
For marketing practitioners, successful navigation of Web 3.0
transitions requires developing organizational capabilities in
several emerging domains. Blockchain and token economics
literacy must become core competencies within marketing
departments, necessitating significant investment in education
and talent acquisition (Murray et al., 2023). Strategic
experimentation should progress through deliberate phases—
beginning with contained pilots in loyalty or community
initiatives before expanding to broader marketing applications.
This measured approach balances innovation with risk

User experience design represents another critical capability
area, requiring collaboration between marketing, technology,
and design functions to simplify complex Web 3.0 interactions

abstract technical complexity behind intuitive interfaces while

Future research should address several critical knowledge

priority, with current marketing measurement frameworks
requiring adaptation for decentralized environments. Developing
standardized metrics for community health, token circulation
analysis, and decentralized campaign attribution would advance
both academic understanding and practitioner effectiveness
(Wieland and Durach, 2021). These measurement frameworks
must balance transparency with privacy, providing actionable
insights while respecting user data sovereignty.
Interdisciplinary research addressing legal, ethical, and
technological intersections will prove particularly valuable as
Web 3.0 marketing matures. Studies examining regulatory
compliance frameworks, ethical design principles, and
technological risk mitigation would provide practical guidance
for responsible implementation (George, 2024). This integrative
research approach reflects Web 3.0’s inherent interdisciplinary
nature, requiring collaboration across traditional
academic boundaries.
9 Limitations
This study has several limitations that should be considered
when interpreting its findings. First, the review relies on a nascent
and rapidly evolving body of literature; many conclusions are
based on early evidence from pilot programmes and industry
reports that have not yet been validated through large-scale,
independent empirical research. As the field matures, some of the
performance claims and strategic prescriptions may require revision.
Second, possible publication bias exists. Sources that report
positive outcomes or successful implementations may be
overrepresented in both the academic and grey literature, while
failures and abandoned initiatives are less likely to be
documented. The inclusion of industry sources further
introduces potential optimism bias, as corporate
communications tend to emphasize successes.
Third, the Marketing 3.0 Framework has not been empirically
tested. It is presented as a theoretical synthesis grounded in the
available evidence, but its validity as a predictive or evaluative
tool requires future quantitative and case-based validation. The
framework should be treated as a starting point for empirical
research rather than a definitively proven model.
Fourth, Web 3.0 technologies and their regulatory environments
are evolving rapidly. Specific technological references (e.g., particular
consensus mechanisms, layer-2 scaling solutions) may become dated,
and regulatory frameworks are likely to change significantly in the
coming years. The framework’s general principles are designed to
remain applicable despite these changes, but its technological
specifics will require periodic updating.
Fifth, the reviewed literature exhibits a geographic bias toward
North American, European, and select Asian markets. The
framework’s applicability to marketing contexts in Africa, Latin
America, and other regions with different technological
infrastructures and regulatory environments remains underexplored.
Finally, space constraints prevented exhaustive citation of all 127
analyzed sources; 30 are directly cited, while the remainder are listed
in a Supplementary File. Future bibliometric or meta-analytic work
could provide a more granular mapping of the full evidence base.
10 Conclusion
This research establishes the Marketing 3.0 Framework as a
comprehensive strategic model for navigating the transition to
decentralized marketing ecosystems. Through systematic analysis
of technological capabilities, emerging practices, and
implementation challenges, this study provides both theoretical
foundations and practical guidance for marketing in Web 3.0
environments. The framework’s four pillars—community as
channel architecture, advertising as value exchange mechanisms,
owned digital environments, and verifiable brand narratives—
offer integrated strategies for leveraging decentralization,
tokenization, and transparency in global marketing contexts.
The study’s findings highlight both transformative potential and
significant implementation challenges. Web 3.0 technologies enable
more direct, transparent, and participatory relationships between
brands and consumers, potentially addressing longstanding
limitations of platform-dependent marketing models. However,
technological complexity, regulatory uncertainty, and adoption
barriers present substantial hurdles requiring strategic navigation.
Successful implementation will demand organizational capability
development, careful technology selection, and ethical
commitment to inclusive, transparent practices.
This research contributes to marketing literature by synthesizing
disparate Web 3.0 applications into a cohesive strategic framework,
advancing theoretical understanding of decentralization’s
marketing implications. For practitioners, it provides structured
guidance for strategic planning and implementation in rapidly
evolving technological environments. Future research should build
upon this foundation through empirical validation, measurement
development, and interdisciplinary investigation of Web 3.0’s
evolving marketing landscape.
As Web 3.0 technologies mature and adoption expands,
marketing’s fundamental principles will increasingly intersect
with decentralized architectures and tokenized economies.
Brands that develop strategic clarity, technical capabilities, and
ethical frameworks for this transition will establish sustainable
advantages in building trusted, engaged communities and
creating value in increasingly decentralized digital ecosystems.
