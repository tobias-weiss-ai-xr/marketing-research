Abstract
In recent years, the emergence and proliferation of startups have contributed to a
wide range of technological, economic, and social implications. Startups are orga -
nizations born in contexts of uncertainty, and they generally operate under resource
constraints and in the absence of business, management, marketing, and financial
knowledge. Accordingly, management accounting scholars have focused on un -
derstanding the management control (MC) phenomenon in startups. Over the past
two decades, literature on MC has employed diverse approaches to investigate MC
adoption. Although a few attempts have been made to integrate this literature, we
still do not have a clear understanding of the phenomenon. To provide a more con -
solidated perspective, we conducted a systematic literature review (SLR) of the MC
phenomenon in startups. Drawing on 37 articles, we structured our analysis around
two complementary approaches, namely, bibliometric analysis to map the develop -
ment and main trends of the literature and, more importantly, qualitative content
analysis to integrate and categorize the MC phenomenon in startups according to
its design, attributes of metric content and formality, intentions of use and formal -
ization, and its antecedents and consequences. Therefore, the main contribution of
this study is an integrated categorization of MC in startups that connects control
design, attributes and intentions with their antecedents and consequences, thereby
providing a more structured understanding of this fragmented literature. Building
on this integrated perspective, we identify avenues for future research in currently
underexplored areas.

1 Introduction
Startups are organizations that are involved in the development of new products and
services, mainly through disruptive and technology-driven business models (Blank
& Dorf, 2012). As startups are young ventures, their owners navigate high market
uncertainty to discover and implement business models that address underexplored
needs and opportunities (von Gelderen et al., 2000; Sommer et al., 2009). The ability
of startup owners to develop innovative products and services enables rapid growth
and the generation of competitive advantages (Fiorentino et al., 2021), driving tech-
nological advancements and fostering economic, social, and environmental develop-
ment (Ressin, 2022). For instance, in 2024, the Global Startup Ecosystem Report,
which is based on data from 4.5 million startups across 300 global ecosystems,
highlighted that the top 40 ecosystems alone had collectively generated 8.4 trillion
dollars in value (Startup Genome, 2024). Despite their vast potential for prosperity,
most startups face significant challenges in terms of management, commercializa -
tion, business operations, and resource availability, leading most to fail within a short
period of time (Franco-Leal and Diaz-Carrion ( 2020). Given both the potential of
startups and the challenges they face, both theory (Davila, 2005; Akroyd & Kober,
2020; Graña-Alvarez et al., 2024) and practice (Forbes, 2023, 2024) are increasingly
focused on understanding the management control (MC) phenomenon in startups.
Malmi and Brown (2008, p. 290) have argued that researchers should “start with
the managerial problem of directing employee behavior” and thus defined MC as
“those systems, rules, practices, values and other activities management put in place
in order to direct employee behavior.” MC is a mechanism used by managers to
achieve congruence between organizational and individual objectives (Akroyd &
Maguire, 2011; Kennedy & Widener, 2019), and throughout the last 50 years, it has
been a constant issue on the agendas of management accounting researchers (Truant
et al., 2025). Although research on MC has advanced significantly in recent years,
few recent studies have focused on MC in startups (Truant et al., 2025). This scarcity
of research may be due to several factors. First, the rise and widespread development
of startups are relatively recent, dating back to the 1990s and 2000s (Van Le & Suh,
2019; Haltiwanger, 2022). Second, for many years, the literature on MC assumed
that controls were less relevant for innovation, keeping the topic largely peripheral
(Barros & Ferreira, 2019). Third, scholars have highlighted that, unlike traditional
companies, startups must balance flexibility and efficiency—a challenge that requires
effective controls (Frare & Beuren, 2021). Owing to these factors, the early studies
examining adoption of MC in startups were published in the early 2000s (Davila,
2005; Davila & Foster, 2005, 2007). Nearly 20 years later, research on this topic
continues to grow (Gomez-Conde et al., 2023; Graña-Alvarez et al., 2024; Beuren
et al., 2026). In this study, a systematic literature review (SLR) was conducted to
understand the MC phenomenon1 in startups. This approach allowed us to verify bib-

instead classified and examined MC according to its design, attributes and inten -

the package perspective of Malmi and Brown ( 2008); MC attributes, that is, metric

2026) and formality (formal vs. informal) (e.g., Goebel & Weißenberger, 2017; Knels

should be considered in light of their internal and external drivers to provide more
specific insights, a categorization that we address in this study. Moreover, rather than
merely categorizing outcomes, we subcategorized them into dimensions related to
performance, innovation, and organizational issues, and analyzed these dimensions.
Additionally, we identified the need for more bibliometric details and a comprehen -
sive mapping of the theoretical background, data collection procedures, main data
analysis techniques, and the industries and countries from which the evidence is
drawn.

2 Theoretical background
2.1 Startups
Startups represent an initial business model with the potential to be scalable, repeat -
able, and highly innovative (Blank, 2010). Startups are newly established, innova -
tion-driven firms that differ from traditional companies in terms of their inherent
agility, which enables them to develop innovations and rapidly test them in the mar-
ket (Filippelli et al., 2025). Startups also differ from many other business models
in that they are driven by internally developed technology, which represents a key
factor in their performance and survival (Chattopadhyay et al., 2025). In contrast to
large organizations, which are constrained by sunk costs, established routines, and
bureaucratic structures, startups exhibit a greater capacity to adapt to dynamic, uncer-
tain and turbulent environments (Sreenivasan & Suresh, 2024). However, they often
face the challenge of balancing rapid growth and expansion with the need to secure
resources (Akroyd et al., 2019). Owing to their early-stage nature, many fail because
of a lack of resources and management skills (Franco-Leal & Diaz-Carrion, 2020).
Despite these challenges, startups’ innovative capacity and potential for rapid
growth attract a diverse number of stakeholders. For example, startups attract inves-
tors, including venture capital (VC) firms and angel investors who seek equity stakes
that can result in quick financial returns (Piazza et al., 2023; Weik et al., 2024). Large
corporations, which are often characterized by bureaucratic and rigid structures, form
partnerships with (and finance) startups to drive internal innovation more quickly and
efficiently (Frare & Akroyd, 2023). Governments also develop policies and programs
to foster innovative entrepreneurship and regional development (Lerner, 2002; Bau-
mol & Strom, 2007). The broader entrepreneurial ecosystem, including incubators,
accelerators, and hubs, provides infrastructure and management training that also
support startups in their early stages (Leitão et al., 2022; Filippelli et al., 2025). This
widespread attention stems from the role of startups in introducing new solutions;
driving employment; and contributing to economic, social, and environmental devel-
opment (Ressin, 2022).

2.2 Management control
MC was originally defined as “the process by which managers assure that resources
are obtained and used effectively and efficiently in the accomplishment of the orga -
nization’s objectives” (Anthony, 1965, p.17). In this definition, Anthony (1965) con-
ceptualized MC as the process that links strategic planning, understood as the setting
of long-term organizational objectives, with operational control, which concerns the
evaluation of specific tasks. More recently, Malmi and Brown (2008, p. 280) defined
MC as “those systems, rules, practices, values and other activities management put
in place in order to direct employee behavior.” This definition suggests that managers
use MC to achieve goal congruence and to correct or prevent deviations within orga-
nizations (Akroyd & Maguire, 2011; Kennedy & Widener, 2019). Therefore, MC is
essential for organizational growth, as it coordinates activities and routines to achieve
organizational goals (Flamholtz & Randle, 2000).
Although accounting and control scholars have generally examined well-estab -
lished firms in relatively stable environments (Davila et al., 2009), the intersection of
accounting, control, entrepreneurship, and innovation has recently attracted increased
attention (see, for example, Bedford, 2015; Akroyd et al., 2019; Gomez-Conde et
al., 2023). Historically, control was seen as a barrier to innovation, but a growing
consensus now recognizes that MC plays a crucial role in innovative environments
(Barros & Ferreira, 2019). Therefore, since many startups are focused on innovations
within a context of extreme environmental uncertainty (von Gelderen et al., 2000;
Sommer et al., 2009), the study of MC in startups has received increasing emphasis
from researchers and practitioners (see, for example, Davila & Foster, 2005, 2007;
Akroyd & Kober, 2020; Forbes, 2023, 2024; Graña-Alvarez et al., 2024). Although
early studies primarily aimed to understand why startups adopt MC (Davila, 2005;
Davila & Foster, 2005, 2007), more recent research focuses on their effects on factors
such as innovation (Santos et al., 2023), green innovation (Frare & Beuren, 2023),
open innovation (Frare & Akroyd, Frare & Akroyd, 2023), and financial leverage
(Graña-Alvarez et al., 2024). Indeed, it has been argued that MC plays a key role in
helping startups transition from entrepreneurial ventures to professionally managed
firms (Flamholtz & Randle, 2000), thereby enabling startups to remain competitive
in the face of organizational, societal and stakeholder demands (Davila et al., 2009).
Although the literature on MC includes well-established frameworks (Strauß &
Zecher, 2013), there is no traditional or dominant framework for startups, as they
are generally small firms that operate in highly uncertain environments and that are
characterized by a strong technological and innovative focus (Strauss et al., 2013;
Douglas et al., 2014; Schachel et al., 2021; Ebrahim et al., 2025). For this reason,
we developed a framework (Fig. 1) to examine and integrate MC approaches across
three analytical levels: MC design, MC attributes, and MC intentions.
At the first analytical level, we examined MC design based on the MC package
approach of Malmi and Brown ( 2008). The study of MC design has long been a
mainstream topic in accounting research and refers to the controls that a firm has at its
disposal and how it chooses and organizes these controls to function together (Har -
rison & McKinnon, 1999; Chenhall, 2003). According to Malmi and Brown (2008),
these include planning controls (long-range planning and action planning), cyber -

At the second analytical level, which we defined as “MC attributes”, we analyzed
the already designed MC based on two basic dimensions: metric content and formal-
ity. On the one hand, MC metric content refers to whether an MC is financial or nonfi-
nancial in nature (Gomez-Conde et al., 2023; Beuren et al., 2026). Financial MCs are
those involving monetary value, such as profit, revenue, and costs, whereas nonfinan-
cial MCs are those involving, for example, operational, behavioral, or strategic indi-
cators (Emmanuel et al., 1990). In addition, MC formality refers to whether an MC is
formal or informal (Goebel & Weißenberger, 2017; Knels et al., 2025). Formal MCs
are more mechanistic and involve, for example, detailed rules and standard operat -
ing procedures, whereas informal MCs are more organic and involve, for example,
selection and training indirect approaches, as well as the integration and constant
reinforcement of the vision, mission, and values within the organizational culture
(Ouchi, 1979; Abernethy & Chua, 1996; Flamholtz, 1996)3. In the literature, both
MC metric content and MC formality are widely considered as basic MC attributes.
At the final analytical level, we defined MC intentions, which refer to the use of
MC based on Simons’ LoC approach (Simons, 1995), including two styles of use
(diagnostic and interactive), and MC formalization based on the enabling and coer -
cive approach of Adler and Borys ( 1996) and Ahrens and Chapman ( 2004). Both
approaches are supported by Tessier and Otley (2012), who classified them as man -

3 Methodological procedures
3.1 Preparation of the data collection process
To achieve the outlined objective, an SLR was conducted. The structure of this
research was in accordance with Tranfield et al. ( 2003), who proposed three stages:
(i) planning the SLR, (ii) conducting the SLR, and (iii) reporting and disseminating
the results. The process used to conduct this research is shown in Fig. 2. This SLR
was conducted in different stages. In the first stage, the objective was developed
based on the research problems and the chosen theme, and this stage was called the
planning stage (see Fig. 2).
3.2 Data collection process

In the upper right quadrant, there are motor themes with high centrality and high
density, which include relevant and strongly developed themes, and these are referred
to as driving themes (Cobo et al., 2011). In this quadrant, our analysis revealed two
different clusters that interconnect different topics, such as “entrepreneurship”, “busi-
ness model innovation”, “incubator”, and “venture capital”. In the second cluster,
there are themes related to “management control systems”, “startups”, “fsQCA”,
“performance” and “product innovation”, which are well-established and central
themes in the literature. Notably, the fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis
(fsQCA) method stands out as a configurational technique that enables the identi -
fication of combinations of conditions that lead to a given outcome. The upper left
quadrant shows topics that are well developed but are less connected to other topics
and are considered niche topics, with topics such as “culture” and “organizational life
cycle”, which, despite being widely researched, have little connection with the focus
of this research. These are specialized fields, and opportunities for collaboration with
startups or for engagement with MC may foster interdisciplinary contributions.
The lower right quadrant contains the basic themes of the field that are still under-
developed. It can serve as a basis for further research but in different contexts. For
example, the theme of “innovation”, which, despite being connected to the central
theme of the research, requires further study or refinement when observed in specific
contexts (green innovation, open innovation, etc.). Finally, the lower left quadrant
shows emerging or declining themes, which are characterized by low centrality and
low density. This quadrant shows those themes that may be gaining attention or those
that are losing relevance in the current context of the topic (Yu & Muñoz-Justicia,
2020; Dai et al., 2022). In this quadrant, contingency theory is notable. Although it
is a well-established and influential framework in MC research (Chenhall, 2003), its
application in studies on startups is still emerging, reflecting its recent incorporation
into this field.

We mapped and classified the MC approaches examined in the studies included in the

With respect to MC design, most studies focused on cybernetic controls (72.97%),

the possibility of several MC packages in which different mechanisms are combined

studies adopted a formal view of MC (56.76%). Approximately one quarter (27.03%)

exploitation (Gomez-Conde et al., 2023). Finally, regarding MC intentions, less than
10% of the studies examined the use or formalization of MC. This finding further
highlights the predominance of studies focused on design.

4.2.3.1 Antecedents of MC adoption: internal reasons (link a) Historical and pre -
dicted performance: Predicted sales in the first year of operation (total expected sales,
revenues, or fees) are positively associated with the frequency of financial statement
preparation, including income statements, cash flow statements, and balance sheets,
as well as financial projections (Cassar, 2009). Time-to-revenue (the number of years
a company operates beyond the prerevenue stage) is positively associated with the
intensity of MC (Davila & Foster, 2007; Davila et al., 2015) and the adoption of both
differentiation and basic MC (Davila et al., 2015). However, recent evidence pres -
ents contrasting findings regarding the role of financial and nonfinancial performance
measures. On the one hand, nonfinancial performance measures, such as competitive-
ness, growth, and innovativeness, are positively associated with the adoption and use
of financial, human resources (HR), sales, and strategic MCs (Crespo et al., 2019).
On the other hand, historical financial performance measures, including return on
investment (ROI), return on equity (ROE), and return on sales (ROS), are negatively
associated with the adoption and use of these same MCs (Pavlatos, 2021). Although
not conclusive, this evidence suggests that historical and predicted performance play
distinct yet important roles in the future adoption of MC.
Internationalization: International sales are positively associated with the emer -
gence and intensity of various MC categories (Strehle et al., 2010). Beyond sales,
managing international operations further amplified MC intensity (Davila & Foster,
2007). International presence not only increased the number of adopted systems but
also accelerated the adoption of specific systems, such as operating budgets, product
profitability analysis, and customer acquisition cost tracking (Davila & Foster, 2005).

Organizational maturity and expansion : Studies indicated that firm age is posi -
tively associated with the emergence of MC (Davila, 2005; Davila et al., 2009; Strehle
et al., 2010). In contrast, the stage of product development in startups is negatively
related to the intention to prepare financial statements (Cassar, 2009). The reason is
that early-stage product development involves higher uncertainty regarding costs and
profitability, which increases the demand for accounting information. Interestingly,
investments in intangibles (such as patents and R&D) do not influence the frequency
of financial statement preparation. However, they are positively associated with sales
forecasts and financial projections (Cassar, 2009). Additionally, Davila and Foster
(2005) demonstrated that revenues in the year a company adopts an operational bud-
get—and in the following year—are significantly higher compared with revenues in
the year prior to adoption.
Recurring challenges: Fear of failure is a recurring factor that can drive the adop-
tion of controls to mitigate bankruptcy risks (Krishnan et al., 2022). Additionally, a
startup’s business model type (e.g., tech driven, market driven, exit driven, or port -
folio), which continuously evolves through innovation and change, also influences
the adoption and use of different cost systems, depending on the type’s specific needs
(Chammassian & Sabatier, 2020). Internal reactive factors, such as chaos and learn -
ing, further shape MC adoption. Crises, unforeseen problems, and misunderstand -
ings (chaos) can prompt the formalization of product development MC (e.g., project
milestones) as a response to unexpected challenges. However, this formalization may
come at the cost of project delays (Davila et al., 2009). In contrast, learning—through
emergent systems that codify best practices—does not necessarily lead to MC for -
malization, but it is positively associated with on-time project performance (Davila
et al., 2009). Beyond internal dynamics, startups face control challenges in interorga-
nizational relationships. Issues related to coordination, ownership, and cooperation
require balancing flexibility and adaptability, driving the adoption of controls for
managing external relationships (Rooney & Cuganesan, 2013). Thus, recurring chal-
lenges—including fear of failure, business model evolution, internal control issues,
chaos, learning, and interorganizational concerns—are key drivers of MC adoption.
Size: Firm size, which is typically measured by the number of employees and/
or revenues, is typically positively associated with the emergence of MC (Davila,
2005; Davila & Foster, 2005, 2007; Davila et al., 2009; Strehle et al., 2010). Addi-
tionally, a higher annual growth rate in terms of the number of employees correlates
with greater MC intensity, particularly in differentiation and basic MC but not with
cost leadership MC (Davila et al., 2015). The number of employees at year-end, just
before a valuation round, is also a key determinant of MC intensity, influencing dif -
ferentiation, cost leadership, and basic MC (Davila et al., 2015). This finding aligns
with the organizational literature, which suggests that larger firms typically exhibit
higher levels of management maturity.
Strategy: The literature on MC has long recognized strategy as an antecedent, as
MCs serve as the mechanisms through which strategy is implemented. Consequently,

HR, sales, or strategic MC. In contrast, Crespo et al. ( 2019) reported no association
between cost leadership and financial, HR, sales, or strategic MC, whereas Davila
(2005) reported no relationship between cost leadership and MC intensity. Samagaio
et al. (2018) suggested that, when combined with other internal or external factors,
cost leadership may lead to the adoption of planning and evaluation MC. Similarly,
Cassar (2009) argued that the emphasis on lower prices for competitiveness is not
associated with the frequency of financial statement preparation (income statements,

appear to be a key determinant of MC adoption or the use of accounting informa -

reported that a differentiation strategy is positively associated with HR, sales, and
strategic MC but not with financial MC. However, Crespo et al. ( 2019) reported no
significant relationships between differentiation and financial, HR, sales, or strategic
MC. Davila et al. (2015) also reported no significant relationship between differentia-
tion and overall MC intensity, but they noted that differentiation is positively associ-
ated with differentiation and basic MC but negatively associated with cost leadership
MC. Additionally, Samagaio et al. ( 2018) reported that high levels of planning and
evaluation MC adoption occur only when differentiation is present, although it must
be combined with other internal and external factors. Interestingly, compared with
startups that follow market strategies, startups that adopt technological or hybrid
(market-technological) leadership strategies tend to take longer to intensify their MC
use (Davila, 2005). Furthermore, an entrepreneurial orientation strategy (character -
ized by innovativeness, risk-taking, and proactivity) serves as an antecedent to MC
package adoption (Frare et al., 2022). Additionally, startups with international strate-
gies are positively associated with the adoption of financial, HR, sales, and strategic
MC (Crespo et al., 2019). Overall, the evidence suggests that strategy is not a primary
or direct driver of MC adoption in startups. Instead, its influence appears to be con -
tingent, configuration dependent, and more pronounced when it is associated with
growth-oriented or complexity-increasing strategic orientations.
Structure: There is substantial evidence of hierarchical structures in the context
of startups. Crespo et al. (2019) reported that structural decentralization is positively
associated with financial MC but is not associated with HR, strategic, or sales MC.
In contrast, Pavlatos ( 2021) reported positive relationships between decentraliza -
tion and financial, HR, sales, and strategic MC. Samagaio et al. ( 2018) suggested
that, when combined with other factors, decentralization may contribute to the adop-
tion of planning and evaluation MC. Additionally, Taylor et al. ( 2019) reported that
the establishment of a heterarchical (nonhierarchical) structure can also foster the
emergence of MC, initially through informal mechanisms (e.g., open communica -
tion and peer mentoring) and later through more formal controls (e.g., budgeting).
Furthermore, the legal classification of a new business as a limited liability entity
is not associated with the frequency of financial statement preparation but is posi -

evidence suggests that, regardless of the degree of hierarchy in the structure or legal

Technology: Investment in accounting information systems (implementation of
cloud-based accounting information systems) is positively (negatively) associated
with the extent of adoption of budget, cash and cost controls (Kubota & Okuda,
2023). Although investment in accounting information systems positively influences
external reporting (the extent of bookkeeping performance, financial statement prep-
aration and tax report preparation), the implementation of cloud-based accounting
information systems has no significant effect (Kubota & Okuda, 2023). Factors such
as the learning curve and organizational complexity may overshadow the effects of
implementing cloud-based accounting information systems. Additionally, low levels
of implementation may necessitate greater reliance on nonautomated controls.
Top management team : The imprinting of founders’ blueprints influences how
startups adopt controls, leaving traces and patterns in their MC (Akroyd & Kober,
2020). Factors such as managerial background and the need for focus drive the for -
malization of MC (Davila et al., 2009), whereas managers’ interest in accounting
information is a key determinant of the adoption of budget, cash, and cost controls,
as well as the quality of external reporting (Kubota & Okuda, 2023). Managers’ con-
trol preferences naturally shape the adoption of specific controls (Rooney & Cuga -
nesan, 2013), and CEOs’ planning beliefs influence the timing and number of MCs
adopted, including operating budgets, cash budgets, and variance analysis (Davila
& Foster, 2005). CEOs’ experience can drive the emergence of MC (Strehle et al.,
2010) and increase the number of systems adopted (Davila & Foster, 2005), although
it does not necessarily impact their intensity of use (Davila & Foster, 2007; Strehle
et al., 2010). Additionally, CEOs’ experience tends to shorten the adoption time of
key MCs, such as operating and cash budgets and variance analysis (Davila & Fos -
ter, 2005). However, prior experience in accounting and financial control does not
always determine the frequency of financial statement and projection preparation
(Cassar & Ittner, 2009). The effect of CEO change on MC adoption is ambiguous:
Although some studies suggest that it has little or no impact (Strehle et al., 2010),
others argue that a new CEO can reinforce MC adoption (Davila, 2005). Similarly,
CEO replacement is linked to MC intensity in some cases (Strehle et al., 2010), yet
founder replacement does not necessarily affect intensity (Davila & Foster, 2007).
Notably, founder replacement is positively associated with the adoption of product
profitability MC but not with key controls such as operating budgets, cash budgets,
or variance analysis (Davila & Foster, 2005). This evidence suggests that CEO tran-
sitions influence MC adoption in complex ways, potentially shaped by personal fac-
tors such as experience, background, and networking. In contrast, the introduction
of a CFO (financial manager) is consistently associated with both the emergence
and intensity of MC (Strehle et al., 2010) and accelerates the adoption of operating
budgets (Davila & Foster, 2005).

Environment: Evidence from Pavlatos ( 2021) suggests that perceived environ -
mental uncertainty is linked to the adoption of strategic MC but not to financial, HR,
or sales MCs. Startups respond to such uncertainty with a package of formal and
informal MCs (Eldridge et al., 2013). Environmental heterogeneity interacts with
strategy, structure, and investor profiles, potentially driving MC adoption for plan -
ning and evaluation (Samagaio et al., 2018). Competition positively influences MC
intensity (Davila et al., 2015) and the frequency of financial statement and sales fore-
cast preparation (Cassar, 2009). The industry type also plays a role: The software and
manufacturing sectors are positively associated with MC emergence (Davila, 2005;
Strehle et al., 2010), whereas semiconductors, telecommunications, medical biotech,
and IT have no significant relationship with MC emergence or intensity (Davila,
2005; Davila & Foster, 2005, 2007; Strehle et al., 2010). Thus, the external environ-
ment shapes MC adoption in startups.
Funding: Evidence suggests that VC funding is positively associated with the
emergence of MC (Davila, 2005; Strehle et al., 2010), the number of MCs adopted
(Davila & Foster, 2005), and MC intensity (Davila & Foster, 2007). However, some
studies have reported a nonsignificant relationship between VC and MC intensity
(Davila et al., 2015). Entrepreneurial investors (e.g., angel investors and VC firms)
and traditional investors (e.g., credit institutions and government agencies) consider
both external factors (such as environmental heterogeneity) and internal factors (such
as strategy and structure) to encourage MC adoption for planning and evaluation
(Samagaio et al., 2018). Indeed, entrepreneurial investors require specific and sophis-
ticated financial and nonfinancial information from startups (Bratfisch et al., 2023).
Additionally, outside funding is positively associated with the frequency of financial
statement preparation (Cassar, 2009). Thus, receiving funding creates both benefits
and obligations—related to trust and contractual commitments—which can drive
MC adoption in response to external financiers.
Legitimacy and contracts : Legitimacy and contractual obligations are external
factors that drive the adoption of MC, with both positively affecting the time needed
to adopt and formalize product development MC (Davila et al., 2009). Additionally,
external ownership is positively associated with the frequency of cash flow statement
preparation (Cassar & Ittner, 2009). These findings suggest that conveying a positive
image, fulfilling contracts, and having external owners are both formal and informal
factors that startups consider when they adopt MC.
National culture: Crespo et al. (2019) reported significant differences in the adop-
tion of strategic MC between startups in Portugal and those in Norway. More impor-
tantly, each group followed distinct paths, combining different internal and external
factors, to achieve a high rate of adoption of financial, HR, strategic, and sales man -

4.2.3.3 Consequences of MC adoption: performance-related outcomes (link
c) Financial leverage: Graña-Alvarez et al. ( 2024) reported that financial MCs are
negatively related to financial leverage, whereas nonfinancial MCs exhibit a positive
relationship. This finding suggests that financial MCs foster a risk-avoidance mind -
set, whereas nonfinancial MCs help identify opportunities that involve greater risk
and require greater leverage.
Firm performance: According to Davila and Foster (2005), adopting an operating
budget immediately influences performance in the current year and following year.
Gomez-Conde et al. (2023) reported that financial MCs aligned with an exploratory
innovation strategy and nonfinancial MCs aligned with an exploitative innovation
strategy enhance startup performance. Frare et al. (2022) demonstrated that MC pack-
ages that incorporate cultural controls, planning, cyber controls, rewards, compensa-
tion, and administrative controls positively affect startup performance. Additionally,
Frare and Akroyd ( 2023) reported that performance management practices have
positive indirect effects on both financial performance and nonfinancial performance.
Thus, adopting various MCs tends to improve performance directly and indirectly.
Firm value capture:  The adoption of cost MC positively influences value cap -
ture from business models by generating intangible assets (intellectual capital),
enabling monetization (annualized profit and loss (P&L), margin, and profitability),
and enhancing firm value for exit (intangibles, P&L, scalability, and partnerships)
(Chammassian & Sabatier, 2020). Therefore, costs serve as key enablers in value
capture for startup business models.
Organizational effectiveness: Frare & Akroyd ( 2023) reported that performance
management practices positively impact organizational effectiveness indirectly.
This finding suggests that these practices shape behaviors that drive agile decision-
making, innovation capacity, efficient talent and resource utilization, and continuous
adaptation to the environment, competitors, and stakeholders.
Valuation: MC intensity is positively associated with a company’s valuation, spe-
cifically its premoney valuation at each financing round (Davila et al., 2015). This
finding indicates that the number of systems implemented by the end of the year
before a financing round enhances company value.

ativity: Comprehensive performance measurement systems (PMSs), encompassing
financial, nonfinancial, and other metrics related to product, service, and process
innovation, are both directly and indirectly linked to the individual creativity of orga-
nizational actors (Frare & Beuren, 2021). Expanding this perspective to the environ-
mental context, informal controls, such as cultural and personnel controls, positively
influence green creativity (Frare & Beuren, 2023). Thus, well-designed and effec -

Innovativeness: The LoC use—interactive, diagnostic, beliefs, and boundaries—
and their combinations have varying effects on innovativeness among startups pur -

innovation (both PI and BMI) (Endenich et al., 2022). Additionally, MC is pivotal
in fostering innovation even within heterarchical organizations (Taylor et al., 2019).
Therefore, MC appears to play a crucial role in driving innovation across different
dimensions.
Open innovation:  Performance management practices are positively associated
with both inbound and outbound open innovation (OI) (Frare & Akroyd, 2023). This
finding suggests that MC plays a crucial role in aligning objectives in environments
in which startups engage in OI projects with other companies—contexts that are
often characterized by information asymmetry and contractual challenges that can
hinder startup success.
Technological innovation:  Nonfinancial performance indicators and informal
controls are positively associated with product innovation, whereas cost and bud -

to foster technological innovation, whereas more mechanistic controls are still per -

ing can support customer collaboration by facilitating technology commercialization
(Wouters & Kirchberger, 2015).
4.2.3.5 Consequences of MC adoption: organization-related outcomes (link
e) Accountability: Formalized management systems promote accountability and
facilitate control (Davila et al., 2010) by establishing structured procedures for exe -
cuting processes, enabling managers to monitor activities more efficiently and allo -
cate time and resources to exploring new opportunities. Additionally, MCs provide
standardized metrics and comparable benchmarks for evaluating the performance
and outcomes of specific actions, thereby enhancing accountability.
CEO rotation: Greater MC intensity is associated with the time to replace the
founder as CEO and the time to CEO replacement, confirming that a higher intensity
of MC correlates with increased CEO tenure (Davila & Foster, 2007). This finding
suggests that adopting a larger number of MCs facilitates the CEO’s transition to a
more managerial role, thereby reducing the likelihood of replacement.
Goal clarity: The formalization and use of management systems are also posi -
tively associated with making goals more explicit and stable (Davila et al., 2010).
Frare and Beuren (2021) highlight that comprehensive and formalized PMSs enhance
role clarity in terms of organizational processes and goals. The reason is that MCs are
designed to clearly define expectations for actions and results, facilitate monitoring,
generate relevant information, and serve as mechanisms to ensure that desired objec-
tives are implemented in a coordinated and sustained manner over time.

Knowledge and learning: Informal controls are positively associated with knowl-

processes and resources that are essential to the daily operations of startups, which
often require agility, speed, and flexibility.
Risk and conflict management:  Management accounting tools play a critical role
in failure prevention and risk management (Krishnan et al., 2022). Additionally,
enabling MC has both direct and indirect positive effects on conflict management in
interorganizational relationships (Luiz et al., 2024). Therefore, MCs serve as essen -

steering organizations away from paths that could lead to failure.

focused on “why” MC should be adopted, the discussion now explores “how MC
shapes and is shaped by contemporary issues”.

5 Conclusions
In this study, an SLR was conducted to understand the MC phenomenon in startups.
Based on a portfolio of 37 articles, we conducted a bibliometric analysis and a quali-
tative content analysis. In the bibliometric analysis, we examined the publications
over time and their authors, universities, journals, and keywords, and we developed
a thematic map of the articles. In the content analysis, we mapped and categorized
MC approaches according to MCs’ design (Malmi & Brown, 2008), their attributes
of metric content (financial × nonfinancial) (Gomez-Conde et al., 2023; Beuren et al.,
2026) and formality (formal × informal) (Goebel & Weißenberger, 2017; Knels et al.,
2025), and their intentions of use (diagnostic × interactive) (Simons, 1995) and for-
malization (coercive × enabling) (Adler & Borys, 1996; Ahrens & Chapman, 2004).
We also mapped and classified the antecedents of MC into internal and external rea-
sons and the consequences of MCs into those related to performance, innovation,
and organization. In addition, we provided an overview of the articles’ theoretical
backgrounds, data collection methods, main analytical techniques, industries, and
countries.
We highlighted the pioneering role of authors such as Antonio Davila and George
Foster, as well as the contribution of journals such as the Journal of Accounting
and Organizational Change , The Accounting Review , and the European Account-
ing Review in disseminating evidence on the topic. Our categorizations and analyses
revealed the presence of up to 12 distinct MC packages previously studied in start -
ups. Although cybernetic controls predominate in these studies, there are several MC
packages, indicating that startups do not follow a single MC design pattern. The attri-
butes of MC indicate that, regarding metric content, most follow a hybrid approach
that combines financial and nonfinancial measures and that most adopt a formal MC
approach rather than an informal or hybrid approach. Furthermore, studies examining
MC intentions, whether through use or formalization approaches, are rare. We identi-
fied nine main internal reasons and five main external reasons for the adoption of MC,
as well as five consequences of MC related to performance outcomes, four related
to innovation outcomes, and six related to general organizational outcomes. Finally,
we observed a predominance of theoretical approaches based on contingency theory
and agency theory, methodological designs involving mainly surveys via question -
naires and case studies, a wide variety of data analysis techniques, samples or cases

5.1 Theoretical implications
Several literature reviews have already been conducted in the MC context (Lueg
& Radlach, 2016; Helsen et al., 2017; Sageder & Feldbauer-Durstmüller, 2019;
Van der Kolk, 2019; Traxler et al., 2020; Mitter et al., 2024; Brenner et al., 2024),
including the interface with entrepreneurship and innovation (Davila et al., 2009)
(Lövstål & Jontoft, 2017; Lill et al., 2021; Fähndrich, 2023). Furthermore, several
literature reviews have been conducted on startups (Centobelli et al., 2017; Spender
et al., 2017; Ojaghi et al., 2019; Chaparro & Gomes, 2021; Berre & Pendeven, 2023;
Camilleri & Bresciani, 2024; González et al., 2024; Azeem & Khanna, 2024). How-
ever, only Ebrahim et al. (2025) conducted a literature review of MC in startups; they
categorized MCs according to Merchant and Van der Stede’s (Merchant & Van der
Stede, 2012) object of control framework and focused their analyses on contextual
factors and outcomes, both of which were treated as single dimensions.
Since our objective was to conduct an SLR to understand the MC phenomenon
in startups, we contribute theoretically in several ways. First, rather than centering
the discussion on a single MC approach, we developed and applied a theoretical
framework for studying MC in startups based on MC design, MC attributes, and MC
intentions. Thus, we demonstrated the logic behind the most investigated MC pack -
ages in startups based on the framework of Malmi and Brown (2008) and highlighted
the main attributes regarding metric content (Gomez-Conde et al., 2023; Beuren et
al., 2026) and formality (Goebel & Weißenberger, 2017; Knels et al., 2025). We also
highlighted the unexplored potential of MC intentions based on use (Simons, 1995)
and formalization (Adler & Borys, 1996; Ahrens & Chapman, 2004). This theoreti-
cal framework allowed for a holistic and detailed view of MC in startups, from their
design and attributes to their intentions.
Furthermore, using the notion of internal and external reasons for MC adoption
from Davila et al. ( 2009), we mapped, classified, and presented several elements
within these two levels that are antecedents of MC in startups. We specifically identi-
fied nine internal reasons (historical and predicted performance, internationalization,
organizational maturity and expansion, recurring challenges, size, strategy, structure,
technology, and top management team) and five external reasons (entrepreneurial eco-
systems, environment, funding, legitimacy and contracts, and national culture) based
on 67 latent constructs, variables, and phenomena presented in the studies examined.
In addition, based on 32 latent constructs, variables, and phenomena representing
the outcomes of MC identified in the studies examined, we proposed three clusters
of outcomes associated with MC in startups: innovation-related outcomes (four
topics: creativity, innovativeness, OI and technological innovation), organization-
related outcomes (six topics: accountability, CEO rotation, goal clarity, knowledge
and learning, process and resource coordination, and risk and conflict management),

and performance-related outcomes (five topics: financial leverage, firm performance,
firm value capture, organizational effectiveness, and valuation). Our categorization
and discussion of antecedents and consequences extend the framework proposed by
Ebrahim et al. ( 2025) by providing a more nuanced understanding of how internal
and external factors within and surrounding startups shape the MC phenomenon and
how MC, in turn, is associated with a broad range of performance, innovation, and
organizational outcomes. Finally, we highlighted the main bibliometric, theoretical,
methodological, and contextual patterns of the MC phenomenon in startups. In doing
so, we helped bring coherence to the existing body of research and contributed to the
maturation of the scientific field.
5.2 Managerial implications
Our categorization and examination of the design, attributes, and intentions of MC,
along with their antecedents and consequences, yielded several managerial implica -
tions for startups. First, startups in early stages or that face high uncertainty should
prioritize more flexible and informal controls to encourage creativity and improvisa-
tion, whereas growth in age, revenue, and number of employees demands a more
formal approach to MC, such as budgets and monitoring systems. Indeed, growth and
expansion require greater coordination, standardization, and monitoring, which can
be achieved through the design of additional MC, thereby expanding an MC package
that incorporates more hybrid attributes, combining formal and informal aspects as
well as nonfinancial and financial elements. Startups with a differentiation strategy
tend to benefit more from MCs focused on customers, people, and strategy, whereas
standalone financial MCs may be insufficient. Low-cost strategies, on the other hand,
exhibit a weaker association with the widespread adoption of MC. With respect to
internationalization strategies, startups that operate or intend to operate internation -
ally should proactively adopt MCs, such as product profitability analysis and cus -
tomer acquisition metrics, to handle greater operational complexity.
Both more decentralized and heterarchical structures can coexist with the devel -
opment of MC. Managers should adapt the design and attributes of MC to the type
of structure, starting with informal MC and evolving toward more formal MC as
needed. Furthermore, managers should be aware that different MCs generate distinct
effects. Financial MCs tend to reduce risk, whereas nonfinancial and informal MCs
are more closely associated with seizing opportunities. Managers should align MCs
with strategic objectives. For example, to foster creativity and innovation, startups
should prioritize informal and nonfinancial MCs, avoiding an excessive reliance on
rigid MCs such as static budgets. However, a combination of MCs with both formal
and informal attributes, as well as financial and nonfinancial attributes, can be rel -
evant in this context of innovation, provided that there is a balance between them.
Times of crisis, failures, or internal disorganization tend to trigger the adoption of
MCs. Managers can use these events as catalysts to establish more robust control sys-
tems, but they must be mindful of potential negative impacts, such as project delays.
The CEO’s experience and, above all, the presence of a CFO accelerate the adoption
of MC. Therefore, the professionalization of the members of the management team is
a key factor for startups that need to professionalize their management within shorter

their actors, as they provide guidance on the internal and external factors that influ -

This knowledge can support the design of more appropriate and effective incentives
and training programs aimed at the “professionalization” of startup management,
thereby fostering startup survival and prosperity in both the market and society.
5.3 Limitations and recommendations
Finally, this study is not without limitations. First, we acknowledge the limited num-
ber of articles included in our portfolio; however, we emphasize that the literature
is relatively recent (from 2005 onward) and that we prioritized articles published
in journals ranked at least above the median (i.e., quartiles 1 and 2 in SCImago).
Second, we relied on the Scopus and Web of Science databases, as well as some
documents retrieved through reference reviews in the selected studies and the related
literature. Although this is a common and accepted practice in related literature
reviews (e.g., Graña-Alvarez et al., 2024), future research could expand the database
coverage. A third limitation concerns the trade-off between conceptual specificity
and the coverage of articles in the portfolio. As highlighted in our comparison with
Ebrahim et al. ( 2025), our more focused approach to identifying studies explicitly
addressing MC may have excluded articles that examine MC-related practices with -
out using MC terminology. Thus, while this approach enhances the conceptual con -
sistency of our review, it may have resulted in narrower coverage of the broader
literature on management accounting practices in startups. Fourth, we recognize that
our classifications and categorizations involve a degree of subjectivity, particularly
in the classification of MC, their antecedents, and their consequences. Nevertheless,
to minimize bias and ensure balanced judgment, multiple discussions and consensus-
building exercises were conducted among the authors, and numerous (online) appen-
dices were structured and shared to support the classifications made. Fifth, our study
identified and examined the antecedents and consequences of the MC phenomenon
in startups. Although a few studies have also examined mediation and/or moderation
effects, these effects were not the focus of our analysis. Instead, consistent with our
research objective, we focused on identifying the antecedents and consequences of
MC adoption in startups according to their conceptual position. Accordingly, when a

consequences of MC, we did not include them in our categorization. Future research

its consequences.
