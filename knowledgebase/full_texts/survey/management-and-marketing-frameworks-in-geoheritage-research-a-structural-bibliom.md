Abstract
Research	on	geoheritage	has	experienced	rapid	expansion	in	recent	years.	However,	the	integration	of	management	and
marketing	perspectives	remains	conceptually	inconsistent.	This	study	employs	bibliometric	and	science-mapping	method-
ologies	to	characterise	the	indexed	knowledge	base	on	geoheritage	management	and	marketing	in	the	Web	of	Science	and
Scopus	(1981–2025).	Following	record-level	deduplication,	the	final	dataset	comprises	N	=	1344	documents.	The	analysis
examines	publication	growth,	citation	visibility,	country	productivity	and	collaboration	patterns,	source	and	author	concen-
tration,	as	well	as	the	thematic	structure	of	the	field	through	trend	topics,	co-occurrence	networks,	and	thematic	evolution
over	time.	The	findings	indicate	delayed	yet	robust	consolidation	since	2014	and	reveal	a	stable	conceptual	framework
centred	on	geoheritage,	geoconservation,	and	geotourism.	Furthermore,	geoparks	and	UNESCO-related	governance	serve
as	the	predominant	contexts	for	implementation.	Management	is	primarily	operationalised	through	inventorying,	assess-
ment,	and	decision-making	frameworks	geared	towards	conservation.	In	contrast,	explicit	marketing	constructs	exhibit
limited	structural	centrality	within	keyword	and	abstract-level	networks.	Themes	related	to	marketing	are	primarily	mani-
fest	through	discourses	on	tourism,	interpretation,	communication,	and	digital	engagement,	rather	than	through	clearly
articulated	marketing	theories	or	behavioural	mechanisms.	These	results,	therefore,	suggest	a	comparatively	weak	lexical
integration	of	formal	marketing	theory	within	the	indexed	geoheritage	corpus.	The	study	identifies	opportunities	for	more
precise	integration	within	behavioural	and	marketing	theory,	emphasises	a	more	systematic	evaluation	of	management	and
communication	interventions,	and	advocates	for	broader	empirical	coverage	beyond	geopark-centric	research	contexts.

While	existing	bibliometric	reviews	have	extensively
mapped	the	global	expansion	of	geoheritage,	geotourism,
and	geoparks,	these	analyses	have	primarily	focused	on
geoscientific	classification	and	conservation	frameworks.
To	date,	no	study	has	systematically	examined	the	manage-
ment–marketing	interface	through	structural	bibliometric
modelling,	resulting	in	a	theoretical	disconnect	between
site-governance	methodologies	and	strategic	visitor-engage-

opment	perspective,	is	critical.	This	study	addresses	that
critical	intersection,	utilising	co-word	and	thematic	network
analysis	to	bridge	the	gap	between	geomorphological	site
assessment	and	the	analytical	frameworks	of	marketing	and
behavioural	science.
Accordingly,	this	study	maps	the	indexed	literature	on
geoheritage	management	and	marketing	using	a	merged
WoS–Scopus	corpus	(1981–2025)	and	a	reproducible	bib-
liometric	workflow.	It	addresses	publication	growth	and
database	differences,	country-level	productivity	and	col-
laboration	patterns,	citation	visibility,	source	and	author
structures,	and	thematic	organisation	through	analyses	of
co-occurrence	and	thematic	evolution.	The	primary	goal	is
to	identify	where	the	knowledge	base	is	conceptually	con-
solidated	and	where	persistent	gaps	remain,	particularly
regarding	the	explicit	integration	of	marketing	and	behav-
ioural	mechanisms	into	geoheritage	management	research
and	practical	application.
Literature Review
The	concept	of	geoheritage	and	geoheritage	sites	is	increas-
ingly	framed	in	the	literature	as	complex	territorial	assets
rather	than	isolated	geological	objects,	because	their	values
are	perceived	from	various	perspectives,	including	protec-
tion,	education,	visitor	use,	and	place-based	development.
This	view	positions	geoheritage	management	as	inherently
interdisciplinary,	linking	geoscience	with	nature	conserva-
tion,	cultural	heritage,	destination	governance,	and	tourism
systems	(Brocx	and	Semeniuk	2007;	Gordon	2018;	Gray
2013;	Reynard	and	Brilha	2025).	In	parallel,	geoheritage
scholarship	has	expanded	towards	geocultural	and	heritage-
oriented	readings	that	emphasise	narrative,	identity,	and
experience	as	integral	to	how	geoheritage	is	interpreted
and	socially	valued	(Pijet-Migoń	and	Migoń	2022;	Scarlett
and	Riede	2019;	Guerini	et	al.	2023).	Within	this	broader
framing,	marketing	typically	enters	the	discourse	implic-
itly,	through	geotourism,	interpretation,	destination	image,
and	 experience,	 rather	 than	 through	 explicit	 marketing
constructs	and	theory	(e.g.,	Kotler	and	Keller	2013;	Park
2013),	which	already	suggests	a	structural	tension	between
practice-oriented	promotion	and	the	way	the	research	field
defines	and	studies	it.	Thus,	geotourism	studies	often	lack
a	rigorous,	bottom-line	marketing	foundation	and	instead
assume	or	focus	on	the	discourse	of	geoheritage-related
travel.
Bibliometric	and	science-mapping	studies	consistently
indicate	that	the	domain	has	grown	rapidly	while	remaining
thematically	concentrated.	The	most	visible	thematic	back-
bone	is	repeatedly	built	around	the	triad	geoheritage–geo-
conservation–geotourism,	complemented	by	geoparks	and
management-related	themes	(Herrera-Franco	et	al.	2022;
Hernández-Garrido	et	al.	2023;	Ólafsdóttir	and	Tverijona-
ite	2018).	Dedicated	bibliometric	work	on	geoparks	fur-
ther	reinforces	their	role	as	a	dominant	institutional	and
territorial	frame	through	which	geoheritage	is	researched,
governed,	and	communicated	(Herrera-Franco	et	al.	2021),
while	database-merging	approaches	and	R-based	pipelines
show	 the	 field’s	methodological	 maturation	 in	 mapping
and	interpreting	geopark-related	research	streams	(Nyulas
et	al.	2024).	Bibliometric	analyses	also	document	adjacent
specialisations,	such	as	geoheritage	and	tourism	(Grofelnik
2021),	ecotourism	in	geoheritage	contexts	(Sutiksno	et	al.
2024)	and	geotourism	trends	in	large	indexing	platforms
(Herrera-Franco	et	al.	2020),	and	place	geoheritage	within
broader	sustainability-oriented	heritage	debates	(Németh	et
al.	2021a;	Herrera-Franco	et	al.	2022).	At	the	same	time,
bibliometric	evidence	points	to	systematic	distortions	that
affect	the	interpretation	of	thematic	absences,	including
uneven	geographic	coverage,	language	and	database	filters,
and	the	uneven	visibility	of	regions	and	research	traditions
(Ibáñez	et	al.	2019).	These	facts	are	essential	for	drawing
conclusions	about	the	relative	marginality	of	marketing
vocabulary.	What	appears	marginal	may	partly	result	from
the	indexing	method	and	the	size	of	the	database,	and	not
necessarily	reflect	purely	conceptual	neglect	of	the	topic.

A	significant	portion	of	work	categorised	as	management
is,	in	practice,	founded	upon	a	methodological	framework
that	entails	inventorying,	assessing,	and	prioritising	geo-
heritage	resources.	Mapping	and	classification	techniques
facilitate	decision-making	across	diverse	spatial	scales	and
are	frequently	regarded	as	essential	prerequisites	for	both
conservation	and	tourism	utilisation	(Fuertes-Gutiérrez	and
Fernández-Martínez	2012;	Németh	et	al.	2021a,	b).	Frame-
works	for	geomorphosite	and	geosite	assessment,	developed
specifically	 for	 geotourism	 and	 management	 objectives,
provide	operational	metrics	concerning	site	value,	vulner-
ability,	and	utilisation	potential	(Brilha	2016;	Bruschi	et	al.
2011;	Kubalíková	2013;	Kubalíková	and	Kirchner	2016;
Reynard	et	al.	2007;	Serrano	et	al.	2009;	Suzuki	and	Takagi
2018;	Tomić	and	Božić	2014;	Vujičić	et	al.	2011).	More
contemporary	synthesis	works	explicitly	connect	assess-
ment	outcomes	to	geotourism	potential	and	development
trajectories,	 emphasising	 how	 evaluation	 results	 inform
planning	and	execution	decisions	(Štrba	et	al.	2023).	Case
studies	further	illustrate	the	integration	of	assessment	with
education,	tourism,	and	community	engagement	in	geopark
contexts	(Marescotti	et	al.	2022).	Methodological	advance-
ments	continue	toward	more	formalised	decision-support
tools,	including	quantitative	and	hybrid	multi-criteria	mod-
els	designed	to	enhance	transparency	and	robustness	in	geo-
heritage	evaluation	and	prioritisation	(Zorlu	et	al.	2024),	as
well	as	workflow-oriented	approaches	that	integrate	GIS
and	structured	assessment	procedures	to	bridge	evaluation
and	management	actions	(Pál	and	Albert	2023).	Collec-
tively,	this	body	of	research	underscores	that	the	primary
management	practices	in	the	field	are	driven	by	method-
ological	principles,	with	a	focus	on	resource	assessment,
classification,	and	conservation	planning.	Conversely,	tra-
ditional	marketing	approaches	(e.g.,	segmentation,	value
propositions,	 brand	 positioning,	 campaign	 evaluation)
remain	substantially	underdeveloped	and	consequently	are
less	frequently	operationalised.
Risk,	degradation,	and	climate-related	pressures	consti-
tute	another	prominent	stream	that	shapes	how	geoheritage
is	used	and	communicated.	Reviews	and	methodological
contributions	in	degradation	risk	assessment	underline	the
growing	emphasis	on	identifying	threats,	modelling	vulner-
ability,	and	designing	mitigation	strategies	(Vandelli	et	al.
2024).	Climate	change	is	increasingly	recognised	as	a	key
driver	of	geoheritage	vulnerability	and	as	a	factor	that	must
be	integrated	into	conservation	and	adaptive	management
frameworks	(Selmi	et	al.	2022).	Case	evidence	further	illus-
trates	how	acute	disturbances	(such	as	wildfire	impacts	in
geopark	territories)	lead	to	degradation	of	geoheritage	and
the	need	for	monitoring	and	recovery	planning	(Gonçalves
et	al.	2024).	This	discussion	of	geoheritage-specific	risks	is
reinforced	by	broader	heritage	risk	research	that	addresses
multi-hazard	 contexts	 and	 the	 challenge	 of	 managing
compound	threats	in	cultural	and	natural	heritage	settings
(Bosher	et	al.	2020),	as	well	as	by	work	on	monitoring,	miti-
gation,	and	preservation	practices	for	heritage	sites	affected
by	geohazards	(Valagussa	et	al.	2021).	Although	this	stream
is	not	“marketing”	in	a	narrow	sense,	it	defines	the	bound-
ary	conditions	of	any	visitor-facing	promise	of	value:	site
integrity,	safety,	and	conservation	credibility	directly	shape
reputation,	trust,	and	the	feasibility	of	sustainable	visitation,
yet	the	translation	of	risk-management	insights	into	market-
ing-language	constructs	remains	underdeveloped	in	much
of	the	geoheritage	literature.
Geoparks	and	governance-oriented	studies	represent	a
central	institutional	bridge	between	conservation	objectives
and	territorial	development	agendas.	Historically,	geopa-
rks	networks	have	been	conceptualised	as	mechanisms	that
integrate	the	protection	of	geological	heritage	with	local
development	 pathways	 (Zouros	 2004).	This	 governance
paradigm	 continues	 to	 influence	 contemporary	 research
trends.	Bibliometric	mapping	affirms	the	ongoing	signifi-
cance	of	geoparks	as	both	a	research	subject	and	a	gover-
nance	framework	(Herrera-Franco	et	al.	2021;	Nyulas	et
al.	2024).	Furthermore,	comparative	analyses	reveal	that
research	trajectories	concerning	geoparks	may	vary	across
different	regional	contexts	and	research	systems	(Göktaş
and	Boyraz	2025).	Studies	on	governance	and	implementa-
tion	are	increasingly	supplemented	by	research	incorporat-
ing	cultural	sites	and	cultural	heritage	assets	into	geopark
strategies,	thereby	broadening	the	interpretive	and	identity-
related	dimensions	of	territorial	development	(Guerini	et
al.	2023).	This	expansion	also	strengthens	the	connections
among	 education,	 tourism,	 and	 community	 engagement
(Marescotti	et	al.	2022).	Although	the	practical	strategies
often	align	with	marketing	practices,	such	as	place	identity,
narratives,	and	visitor	targeting,	the	research	focus	predom-
inantly	remains	on	governance	instruments	and	conserva-
tion	or	interpretation	practices,	with	limited	modelling	of
marketing	mechanisms	as	quantifiable	interventions.	An
exception	to	this	trend	is	the	explicit	incorporation	of	sus-
tainability-oriented	marketing	into	geoheritage	tourism	des-
tinations,	which	provides	a	direct	conceptual	foundation	for
integrating	conservation	concerns,	destination	management,
and	marketing	strategies	(Ferdowsi	2025).
The	most	direct	pathway	by	which	marketing-like	con-
cerns	surface	is	the	literature	on	communication,	digital
technologies,	 and	 online	 traces	 of	 visitors’	 experiences.
Reviews	of	geosite	communication	and	interpretive	prac-
tices	 emphasise	 that	 communicating	 geoheritage	 values
is	not	an	ancillary	activity	but	a	core	element	of	sustain-
able	use,	because	it	mediates	understanding,	behaviour,
and	acceptance	of	conservation	constraints	(Crofts	2024;
Pasquaré	 Mariotto	 et	 al.	 2023;	 Macadam	 2025;	 Migoń

and	to	inform	destination	management	decisions	(Sidor	et

combine	spatial	methods	with	sentiment	analysis	to	analyse
how	heritage	places	are	perceived	and	narrated	online	(Kun
et	al.	2024).	Earlier	work	on	user	experience	risk	assess-
ment	for	destination	websites	provides	a	transferable	logic
for	evaluating	digital	presentation	quality	and	user-facing
risks	in	the	context	of	destination	management	(Inversini
et	al.	2010).	Taken	together,	this	segment	of	the	literature
aligns	most	closely	with	an	explicit	marketing	vocabulary
(brand,	sentiment,	reviews,	experience),	yet	it	often	remains
positioned	as	an	auxiliary	stream	relative	to	the	assessment–
conservation	core.
Given	 that	 geotourism	 and	 interpretation	 frequently
act	as	proxies	for	marketing	in	geoheritage	publications,
adjacent	heritage	tourism	research	offers	a	broader	set	of
concepts	that	can	complement	geoheritage-focused	work.
A	long-standing	 meta-study	on	integrating	cultural	heri-
tage	and	tourism	highlights	the	complexity	of	balancing
conservation	and	use,	as	well	as	the	need	for	sustainable
integration	 principles	 (Loulanski	 and	 Loulanski	 2011).
Bibliometric	 mapping	of	heritage	tourism	over	multiple
decades	 documents	 the	 expansion	 of	 research	 themes
that	include	 destination	 management,	 visitor	 behaviour,
and	sustainability	transitions	(Orts-Cardador	et	al.	2024),
while	 bibliometric	 work	 focused	 on	 sustainable	 tour-
ism	in	heritage	sites	provides	further	evidence	of	grow-
ing	emphasis	on	sustainability-oriented	 management	and
policy	(Geçikli	et	al.	2024).	In	the	wider	sustainable	tour-
ism	 literature,	 resilience	 and	 innovation	 have	 become
prominent	managerial	themes,	supported	by	bibliometric
evidence	that	links	these	concepts	to	advanced	sustainable
tourism	 management	 (Della	 Corte	et	al.	2021).	Classic
heritage	tourism	management	research	further	frames	gov-
ernance,	stakeholder	coordination,	and	the	management	of
heritage	attractions	as	central	to	balancing	access	and	con-
servation	(Garrod	and	Fyall	2000).	These	adjacent	streams
contain	more	explicit	theorisation	 and	operational	 tools
for	marketing-related	mechanisms	than	are	typically	fore-
grounded	in	geoheritage	scholarship,	particularly	regard-
ing	branding,	segmentation,	intervention	evaluation,	and
demand-side	behaviour.
Across	the	literature,	the	knowledge	structure	can	be
summarised	as	an	interaction	of	three	dominant	pillars:
 ● Resource	inventorying	and	assessment	supporting	de-
cision-making	and	prioritisation	(Brilha	2016;	Bruschi
et	al.	2011;	Fuertes-Gutiérrez	and	Fernández-Martínez
2012;	 Kubalíková	 2013;	 Kubalíková	 and	 Kirchner
2016;	Reynard	et	al.	2007;	Serrano	et	al.	2009;	Štrba	et
al.	2023;	Pál	and	Albert	2023;	Suzuki	and	Takagi	2018;
Tomić	and	Božić	2014;	Vujičić	et	al.	2011);
 ● Geoconservation	framed	through	various	aspects	(Brocx
and	Semeniuk	2007;	Crisp	and	Ellison	2024;	Gonçalves
et	al.	2024;	Gordon	2018;	Henriques	et	al.	2011;	Németh
et	al.	2021a,	b;	Quesada-Valverde	and	Quesada-Román
2023;	Selmi	et	al.	2022;	Williams	et	al.	2020);
 ● Geotourism	and	geopark	governance	as	the	dominant
utilisation	 and	 institutionalisation	 pathways	 (Guerini
et	al.	2023;	Herrera-Franco	et	al.	2020,	2021,	2022;
Hernández-Garrido	et	al.	2023;	Marescotti	et	al.	2022;
Nyulas	et	al.	2024;	Ólafsdóttir	and	Tverijonaite	2018;
Zouros	2004).
Marketing-related	concepts	appear	most	clearly	in	commu-
nication	and	digital/online-analytics	strands	(Pasquaré	Mar-
iotto	et	al.	2023;	Hincapie	et	al.	2023;	Sidor	et	al.	2025)	and
in	sustainability-oriented	discussions	that	explicitly	link	des-
tination	management	and	marketing	(Ferdowsi	2025),	while
broader	heritage	tourism	scholarship	provides	additional
conceptual	resources	for	modelling	visitor	behaviour	and
resilience	(Della	Corte	et	al.	2021;	Garrod	and	Fyall	2000;
Geçikli	et	al.	2024;	Loulanski	and	Loulanski	2011;	Orts-
Cardador	et	al.	2024).	Within	this	structure,	the	recurrent
pattern	is	a	strong	methodological	and	conservation-centric

management’,	‘geological heritage marketing’ ,	and	‘geo-
heritage marketing’	with	the	search	conducted	across	publi-

Following	the	methodological	workflow	(Fig.	 1),	WoS
and	Scopus	counts	are	reported	separately	due	to	potential

without	the	implementation	of	a	specific	record-level	dedu-
plication	process	(such	as	DOI-based	matching	or	other
reliable	 identifiers)	 and	 without	 explicitly	 defining	 the
matching	criteria.
Building	on	the	distinction	between	WoS	and	Scopus,
international	collaboration	is	analysed	using	a	merged	and
record-level	deduplicated	corpus	of	search	results	from	both
databases	to	prevent	double-counting	and	artificial	inflation
of	tie	weights.	Deduplication	was	made	primarily	based	on
article	DOIs.	Where	DOI	was	missing,	matching	was	per-
formed	based	on	the	normalised	title,	publication	year,	and
first	author	name.	The	co-authorship	network	is	constructed
at	the	country	level	from	author	affiliations.	Within	each
publication,	the	rule	‘unique	countries	per	paper’	is	applied
(a	country	is	counted	at	most	once	per	paper,	even	when
authors	report	multiple	affiliations).	An	edge	between	two
countries	is	created	whenever	they	co-occur	in	the	same
publication.	 For	 multi-country	 publications,	 all	 unique
country	pairs	are	generated.	Edge	weight	equals	the	number
of	shared	publications	under	full	counting.	At	the	same	time,
the	main	patterns	are	verified	using	fractional	counting,	in
which	each	publication’s	contribution	to	dyads	is	down-
weighted	by	the	degree	of	team	internationalisation	to	limit
the	influence	of	large	consortia.	Because	raw	tie	weights	are
mechanically	coupled	to	countries’	publication	volumes,
collaboration	intensity	is	further	assessed	using	normalised
measures	(Salton/cosine	and	Jaccard)	(Leydesdorff	2008).
Countries’	structural	positions	are	assessed	via	weighted
strength	and	betweenness,	computed	on	a	weighted	net-
work	after	transforming	weights	into	distances	(typically
via	weight	inversion).	Community	structure	is	derived	via
modularity	 optimisation	 (Louvain/Leiden)	 (Traag	 et	 al.
2019),	with	the	algorithm	and	resolution	parameter	reported
explicitly.

basic	supervision	toward	high-quality	geo-interpretation,	specialised	guide	training,	and	empirical
monitoring	that	accounts	for	varying	environmental	sensitivities.	Ultimately,	success	depends	on
balancing	visitor	access	with	effective	legislation	and	a	clear	focus	on	geological	conservation.

 ● weak	anchoring	in	marketing	and	behavioural	theory

 ● insufficient	intervention	evaluation	based	on	replicable

 ● strong	 geographic–institutional	 clustering	 (Europe,

Discussion
The	results	indicate	that	research	on	geoheritage	manage-
ment	and	its	societal	utilisation	has	consolidated	relatively
recently,	with	publication	growth	accelerating	after	2014.
This	pattern	is	consistent	with	bibliometric	and	review	evi-
dence	showing	rapid	expansion	of	geoheritage/geotourism
scholarship	in	the	last	decade	and	the	increasing	institu-
tional	relevance	of	geoparks	and	UNESCO-related	framings
(Herrera-Franco	et	al.	2021,	2022;	Hernández-Garrido	et	al.
2023;	Ólafsdóttir	and	Tverijonaite	2018;	Zouros	2004).	At
the	same	time,	the	divergence	between	WoS	and	Scopus	tra-
jectories	in	recent	years	supports	prior	warnings	that	differ-
ences	in	database	coverage	and	indexing	can	distort	trend
interpretation	and	should	be	treated	as	a	validity	constraint
rather	than	a	substantive	shift	(Ibáñez	et	al.	2019;	Nyulas	et
al.	2024).
Across	 co-word	 structure,	 trend	 topics,	 and	 thematic
evolution,	the	corpus	reproduces	a	stable	conceptual	back-
bone	 centred	 on	 the	 geoheritage–geoconservation–geo-
tourism	triad,	with	geoparks	as	a	major	implementation
and	governance	context.	This	aligns	with	conceptual	and
methodological	literature	that	positions	geoheritage	as	an
integrating	 construct,	 geoconservation	 as	 the	 normative
protection	framework,	and	visitor	use/interpretation	as	the
main	pathway	for	societal	value	creation	(Brocx	and	Seme-
niuk	2007;	Gordon	2018;	Hose	2012;	Quesada-Valverde
and	Quesada-Román	2023).	The	strong	applied	orienta-
tion	identified	in	the	thematic	outputs—inventorying,	site
assessment,	 and	 place-anchored	 governance—directly
corresponds	 to	 the	 field’s	methodological	 ‘management
core’	based	on	mapping,	inventories,	and	assessment	mod-
els	(e.g.,	Brilha	2016;	Bruschi	et	al.	2011;	Fassoulas	et	al.
2012;	 Fuertes-Gutiérrez	 and	 Fernández-Martínez	 2012;
Kubalíková	2013;	Kubalíková	and	Kirchner	2016;	Reynard
et	al.	2016;	Reynard	and	Brilha	2025;	Suzuki	and	Takagi
2018;	Tomić	and	Božić	2014;	Vujičić	et	al.	2011),	including
later	syntheses	that	explicitly	connect	assessment	outcomes
with	development	pathways	(Štrba	et	al.	2015,	2018,	2023)
and	workflow-based	implementation	(Pál	and	Albert	2023).
A	key	similarity	between	the	findings	and	the	litera-
ture	review	is	that	management	mainly	focuses	on	assess-
ment	methods	and	decision-support	systems.	In	contrast,
marketing-related	concepts	are	less	central	in	the	networks
studied.	When	marketing	language	does	appear,	it	is	mostly
connected	to	tourism	development	and	interpretive	com-
munication,	especially	within	the	communication	and	digi-
talisation	streams,	such	as	geosite	interpretation	strategies,
immersive	technologies,	and	online	reputation	and	experi-
ence	analytics	(Cayla	2014;	Cayla	et	al.	2014;	Henriques	et
al.	2025;	Hincapie	et	al.	2023;	Sidor	et	al.	2025).	This	dif-
fers	from	more	explicitly	sustainability-focused	marketing
frameworks	found	in	some	geoheritage	destination	studies
(Ferdowsi	2025)	and	the	more	developed	marketing	theo-
ries	in	related	broader	heritage	tourism	research	(Carrillo-
Acosta	et	al.	2024;	Garrod	and	Fyall	2000;	Geçikli	et	al.
2024;	Loulanski	and	Loulanski	2011;	Orts-Cardador	et	al.
2024).
The	 analysis	 indicates	 that	 marketing	 is	 more	 often
practically	 embedded	 in	 geotourism	 and	 interpretation
discussions	than	explicitly	defined	through	behavioural	or
marketing	theory	models.	As	a	result,	concepts	like	trust,
persuasive	communication,	regulatory	compliance,	willing-
ness	to	pay,	and	visitor	choice	modelling	are	only	weakly
integrated	into	the	core	of	the	geoheritage	management-
marketing	literature	and	need	further	research	as	previously
suggested	by	Ólafsdóttir	and	Tverijonaite	(2018).
The	relatively	peripheral	role	of	explicit	marketing	con-
structs	should	be	understood	within	the	context	of	the	field’s
epistemic	trajectory.	Geoheritage	research	evolved	from
the	geosciences,	where	the	methodological	core	remains
centred	on	inventorying,	evaluation,	classification,	vulner-
ability	assessment,	and	conservation	planning	(see	Reynard

  248  Page 18 of 22
Geoheritage          (2026) 18:248
and	 Brilha	 2025).	 Within	this	 framework,	 management
is	 primarily	 operationalised	 through	 site	 evaluation	 and
governance	 design.	 Consequently,	 marketing	 concerns
typically	emerge	indirectly,	mediated	through	geotourism
development,	interpretation	strategies,	and	communication
practices,	rather	than	through	the	formal	adoption	of	mar-
keting-theoretical	frameworks.	This	structural	orientation
reflects	disciplinary	path	dependence	rather	than	a	concep-
tual	oversight.
The	relative	prominence	of	risk,	degradation,	and	cli-
mate-related	themes	in	both	the	literature	and	the	dataset
reinforces	that	conservation	constraints	shape	feasible	utili-
sation	pathways	(Kubalíková	and	Balková	2023;	Selmi	et
al.	2022;	Vandelli	et	al.	2024),	and	case	evidence	on	distur-
bance	impacts	in	geopark	settings	illustrates	the	need	for
adaptive	management	(Agastya	et	al.	2024;	Gonçalves	et
al.	2024).	These	patterns	align	with	broader	heritage	risk
research	on	multi-hazard	contexts,	monitoring,	and	mitiga-
tion	(Bosher	et	al.	2020;	Themistocleous	et	al.	2018;	Vala-
gussa	et	al.	2021).	Nevertheless,	the	thematic	framework
indicates	that	this	risk	management	scholarship	is	only
partially	linked	to	visitor-facing	behavioural	modelling	and
reputation	mechanisms	within	the	mapped	networks.	This
pattern	supports	the	interpretation	that	the	integration	of
behavioural	and	marketing	theory	remains	relatively	limited
in	domains,	such	as	risk	communication	and	visitor	com-
pliance,	where	visitor	responses	are	central	to	conservation
outcomes.	However,	this	structural	disconnect	suggests	a
functional	vulnerability	in	current	management	models,	as
conservation	effectiveness	in	high-sensitivity	sites	depends
not	only	on	technical	risk	mitigation	but	also	on	visitor
perception,	compliance	behaviour,	and	the	credibility	of
communication.
Finally,	the	observed	concentration	in	authorship,	out-
lets,	and	geographic	collaboration	indicates	a	field	shaped
by	 institutional	 clustering	 (Europe–UNESCO–geoparks)
and	by	a	small	productive	core,	consistent	with	known
geographic	 and	 database-driven	 biases	 (Herrera-Franco
et	al.	2021,	2022;	Ibáñez	et	al.	2019).	Such	concentration
increases	the	likelihood	of	thematic	endogeneity	and	ren-
ders	interpretation	of	impact	sensitive	to	author	disambigu-
ation	and	indexing	noise,	including	differences	arising	from
database	coverage	and	indexing	practices	(Slyvkanyč	et	al.
2024).
Overall,	comparing	the	results	with	the	literature	under-
scores	three	priorities	for	advancing	the	domain:
1.	A	more	robust	integration	into	marketing	and	behav-
ioural	theory,
2.	Evaluation	of	interventions	using	replicable	metrics	and
quasi-experimental	or	longitudinal	designs,
3.	Broader	 empirical	 coverage	 beyond	 geopark	 or
UNESCO-centred	contexts	while	acknowledging	persis-
tent	database	bias	constraints.
To	summarise,	geoheritage	management	is	widely	discussed
across	 diverse	 methodological	 and	 geographic	 contexts,
with	strong	consolidation	around	conservation	assessment,
governance	frameworks,	and	geotourism	implementation.
However,	within	the	indexed	dataset	analysed	in	this	paper,
explicit	integration	of	established	marketing	theory,	such	as
segmentation	logic,	value	proposition	design,	behavioural
modelling,	or	intervention	effectiveness	frameworks	(e.g.,
Kotler	and	Keller	2013),	appears	comparatively	limited	in
structural	prominence.	This	does	not	imply	the	absence	of
marketing	activities.	Instead,	it	suggests	that	marketing	is
usually	incorporated	into	conversations	about	tourism	and
interpretation,	rather	than	being	thoroughly	analysed	as	a
standalone	framework.	Consequently,	the	field’s	capacity	to
thoroughly	explain	how	management	and	communication
strategies	affect	visitor	preferences,	conservation	initiatives,
and	the	long-term	resilience	of	destinations	remains	less
developed	than	its	assessment	and	governance	methodolo-
gies.	Advancing	theoretical	integration	in	this	domain	pres-
ents	a	significant	opportunity	for	the	future	evolution	of	the
discipline.
Conclusion
The	bibliometric	mapping	presented	in	this	study	shows
that	research	on	geoheritage	management	and	its	societal
uses	has	moved	from	an	initial	phase	of	sparse	output	to	a
period	of	sustained	consolidation,	particularly	after	2014.
The	field	exhibits	a	stable	conceptual	base	focused	on	geo-
heritage,	geoconservation	and	geotourism,	with	geoparks
and	 UNESCO-related	 management	 frameworks	 serving
as	dominant	institutional	contexts.	Management	is	primar-
ily	operationalised	through	inventories,	assessment	meth-
odologies,	 priority	 schemes	 and	 conservation-oriented
governance	structures.	Within	this	consolidated	structure,
marketing	appears	primarily	as	an	applied	extension	of
tourism	and	interpretation,	rather	than	a	fully	articulated
theoretical	field.	Explicit	marketing	approaches	have	lim-
ited	structural	relevance	within	abstract-	and	keyword-level
networks.	While	communication,	digital	engagement,	and
visitor	 experiences	 are	 increasingly	 discussed,	 system-
atic	modelling	of	segmentation,	positioning,	behavioural
responses,	and	intervention	effectiveness	remains	marginal
or	absent.
These	 findings	 indicate	 a	 theoretical	 and	 strategic
gap:	 the	 field	 demonstrates	 strong	 capacity	 in	 resource

assessment	and	conservation	planning.	However,	it	shows
comparatively	less	integration	of	behavioural	and	marketing
theories	to	elucidate	how	management	and	communication
strategies	affect	visitor	decision-making	and	conservation
outcomes.
In	this	regard,	the	following	three	future	research	direc-
tions	emerge.
Firstly,	future	research	should	more	explicitly	integrate
geoheritage	management	with	behavioural	and	marketing
theories.	Mechanisms	such	as	trust	in	conservation	institu-
tions,	perceived	value	and	authenticity,	perception	of	risk
in	vulnerable	environments,	willingness	to	provide	financial
support	for	conservation	efforts,	and	compliance	with	con-
servation	regulations	should	be	modelled	within	the	context
of	geoheritage.	Such	integration	would	facilitate	a	transition
from	descriptive	mapping	to	comprehensive	explanatory
frameworks.
Secondly,	the	field	would	benefit	from	research	designs
that	assess	the	effectiveness	of	various	interventions.	Com-
parative	evaluation	of	interpretive	strategies,	digital	com-
munication	tools,	pricing	or	access	policies,	and	branding
initiatives,	utilising	longitudinal	monitoring,	quasi-exper-
imental	methods,	or	well-defined	performance	indicators,
would	facilitate	the	accumulation	of	knowledge	on	effec-
tive	practices	in	geoheritage	communication	and	destination
management.
Thirdly,	broadening	empirical	coverage	beyond	regions
predominantly	governed	by	geoparks	and	within	UNESCO-
focused	 contexts	 would	 enhance	 external	 validity.	The
existing	body	of	knowledge	predominantly	illustrates	insti-
tutional	clustering	within	high-level	governance	environ-
ments.	 Conducting	 comparative	 research	 across	 various
governance	 regimes	 and	 socioeconomic	 contexts	 would
facilitate	more	generalizable	insights	into	geoheritage	man-
agement	and	its	market-oriented	aspects.
Overall,	the	study	evaluates	geoheritage	management
research	as	methodologically	well	established	in	conserva-
tion	and	assessment,	with	institutional	consolidation	around
geopark	management;	however,	it	is	only	partially	inte-
grated	with	formal	marketing	theory.	Addressing	this	imbal-
ance	presents	a	significant	opportunity	for	the	next	phase	of
geoheritage	research.
