ABSTRACT Original Research Article
Despite the increasing emphasis on sustainability marketing and concerns regarding
greenwashing, relatively few studies have systematically examined the role of digitally
enabled transparency mechanisms in consumer -oriented sustainability communication.
Guided by a positivist research philosophy, this study employed a quantitative cross -
sectional survey design. Data were collected from 753 respondents using a quota sampling
technique. The findings indicate that, within the context of this study , green-proofing was
positively and significantly associated with consumer trust and consumer purchase
intention. These results suggest that consumers in the sampled population responded
favourably to transparent and digitally verifiable sustainability practices. Furthermore, the
relationships between green -proofing and the two consumer outcomes were not
significantly moderated by consumer digital literacy or the cultural –institutional
environment. This finding suggests that, in the present research context, the positive
associations observed were relatively consistent across respondents with different levels of
digital literacy and perceptions of institutional and cultural conditions. However, these
findings should be interpreted within the specific context of this study and should not be
regarded as evidence of universal effectiveness or causal influence. The study contributes
to the sustainability marketing literature by providing empirical evidenc e that green -
proofing is positively associated with consumer trust and purchase intention and by
conceptualizing green -proofing as a strategic sustainability signaling capability that
integrates transparency, verification, and authenticated environmental i nformation.
Additionally, the non -significant moderating effects refine existing theoretical
understanding by suggesting that the influence of consumer digital literacy and cultural –
institutional environments may depend on contextual conditions, thereby id entifying
important avenues for future research. From a practical perspective, the findings suggest
that organizations may benefit from improving the visibility, transparency, and verifiability
of their sustainability initiatives through practices such as eco-labeling, sustainability
certifications, digital traceability, and transparent environmental communication. While
these practices were positively associated with consumer trust and purchase intention
among the respondents surveyed, future studies employing longitudinal, experimental, and
cross-country research designs are needed to determine whether these relationships are
generalizable across different industries, markets, and institutional contexts.
Keywords: Green-Proofing, Customer Trust, Consumer Purchase Intention, Consumer
Digital Literacy, Cultural and Institutional Environments.

1.1 INTRODUCTION
Sustainability has evolved from a peripheral
corporate responsibility initiative into a strategic
imperative that shapes consumer preferences,
competitive positioning, and long -term organizational
success. Across industries, firms increasingly integrate
environmental sustainability into their branding and
marketing strategies in response to growing consumer
environmental awareness, stricter regulatory
requirements, investor expectations, and global
commitments to sustainable development (Lim, 2023;
Kumar et al., 2024; United Nations, 2023). However, the
rapid proliferation of sustainability claims has generated
widespread skepticism regarding their authenticity.

Consumers increasingly question whether firms
genuinely implement environmentally responsible
practices or merely employ sustainability rhetoric to
enhance corporate image without corresponding
operational commitment. This credibility deficit has
elevated greenwashing into one of the most significant
challenges confronting contemporary sustainability
marketing (de Freitas Netto et al ., 2020; Szabo &
Webster, 2021). The growing prevalence of
greenwashing has exposed a fundamental limitation in
conventional sustainability communication. Traditional
approaches—including eco -labels, corporate
sustainability reports, and environmental adve rtising—
often rely on symbolic representations that consumers
cannot independently verify. Consequently, information
asymmetry between firms and consumers persists,
reducing confidence in sustainability claims and
weakening consumers' willingness to suppor t genuinely
sustainable firms through their purchasing decisions
(Nuseir et al., 2024; Testa et al., 2021). As sustainability
markets mature, consumers increasingly demand
credible sustainability signals that provide transparent,
verifiable, and evidence -based environmental
information rather than persuasive promotional
messages alone. Consequently, fi rms are under
increasing pressure not only to communicate their
environmental commitments but also to substantiate
them through authenticated sustainability evidence
capable of strengthening consumer confidence and
reducing uncertainty surrounding environmental claims.

This shift has coincided with growing
organizational investment in digital transparency
mechanisms capable of generating credible sustainability
signals throughout product life cycles. Digital
technologies increasingly enable firms to verify
environmental performance, authenticate sustainability
practices, and communicate trustworthy environmental
evidence directly to consumers. Rather than functioning
merely as operational innovations, these technologies
provide organizations with the capability to transfo rm
sustainability information into credible market signals
that reduce information asymmetry, mitigate perceptions
of greenwashing, and strengthen confidence in
environmental claims (George et al., 2024; Wamba et al.,
2023). Nevertheless, prior studies have predominantly
examined these technologies from supply chain
management, logistics, or sustainability reporting
perspectives, leaving their strategic role in shaping
consumer trust and purchase intention insufficie ntly
understood. Moreover, the effectiveness of digitally
enabled sustainability communication is unlikely to be
universal. Consumers differ considerably in their ability
to understand and interpret digital transparency
information. Individuals possessing higher consumer
digital literacy are generally better equipped to evaluate
digitally verified sustainability evidence, distinguish
authentic environmental commitments from misleading
claims, and more effectively assess the credibility of
sustainability com munications (Van Laar et al ., 2020;
UNESCO, 2023). Likewise, broader institutional
environments, including regulatory quality and
environmental governance, shape the legitimacy of
sustainability information by influencing organizational
accountability and enforcement mechanisms (Scott,
2014; Li et al., 2023). Similarly, cultural environments,
reflected in societal values toward environmental
responsibility, uncertainty avoidance, and long -term
orientation, influence how consumers perceive and
respond to sustainability signals (Nguyen et al ., 2023;
Hofstede, 2001). These contextual factors suggest that
digital transparency alone may not guarantee positive
consumer responses; rather, its effectiveness depends on
consumers' interpretive capabilities and the institutional
and cultural contexts within which sustainability
communication occurs.

Against this background, this study
conceptualizes green -proofing as a credible
sustainability signal that emerges from a firm's strategic
capability to integrate digitally verifiable environmental
evidence into consumer -facing communication. Green -
proofing enables organizations to authenticate
sustainability claims proactively, reduce perceptions of
greenwashing, and enhance the credibility of
environmental communication. Unlike sustainable
supply chain transparency, blockchain traceability,
digital sustai nability disclosure, or technology
adoption—which primarily describe technological
systems or organizational capabilities —green-proofing
explains how firms convert verified sustainability
evidence into credible sustainability signals that shape
consumer ev aluations and behavioural responses.
Consequently, green -proofing extends beyond
technological implementation by positioning digital
verification as a strategic signalling mechanism capable
of strengthening consumer trust, stimulating purchase
intention, and creating su stainable competitive
advantage.

The theoretical foundation of this study
integrates Signaling Theory and Institutional Theory to
explain both the direct and contextual mechanisms
through which green -proofing influences consumer
behaviour. Signaling Theory provides the primary
explanatory lens by suggesting that green -proofing
functions as a credible sustainability signal, whereby
digitally verified environmental evidence reduces
information asymmetry, authenticates sustainability
claims, and enhances consumers' confidence in firms'
environmental commitments (Connelly et al ., 2011).
Institutional Theory complements this perspective by
explaining that the effectiveness of such sustainability
signals depends on consumers' digital literacy and the
broader cultural and institutional environments in which
sustainability informati on is interpreted (Scott, 2014).
Together, these theories explain not only why green -
proofing influences consumer trust and purchase
intention but also why these relationships vary across
different market contexts.

Second, although previous studies have
extensively examined sustainability transparency,
blockchain verification, traceability systems, and digital

been devoted to understanding green -proofing as a
credible sustainability signal that simultaneously drives
consumer trust and purchase intention. Existing studies
predominantly conceptualize digital verification as an
operational or technological capability while
overlooking its strategic role in signalling authentic
environmental commitment to consumers. Furthermore,
prior research largely assumes that consumers interpret
digitally verified sustainability information uniformly,
despite considerable differences in consumer digital
literacy and broa der cultural –institutional contexts that
may shape the effectiveness of sustainability signals .
Consequently, limited empirical evidence exists
regarding whether consumer digital literacy strengthens
the influence of green -proofing on consumer trust and
purchase intention. Likewise, although institutional
quality and cultural norms have independent ly been
associated with sustainable consumer behaviour, their
moderating influence on the relationships between
green-proofing and these consumer outcomes remains
theoretically fragmented and empirically underexplored.

Third, sustainability marketing literature lacks
an integrated theoretical framework explaining how
credible sustainability signals interact with consumer
capabilities and contextual environments to shape
consumer behaviour . Existing studies provide only
fragmented explanations of why digitally verified
sustainability communication strengthens consumer trust
and purchase intention in some contexts but produces
weaker effects in others. Addressing this limitation is
particularly important because the effectiveness of
credible sustainability signals depends not only on firms'
ability to authenticate environmental claims but also on
consumers' capacity to interpret those signals and the
legitimacy provided by cultural and instit utional
environments.

Accordingly, this study advances sustainability
marketing literature in three important ways. First, it
conceptualizes green -proofing as a novel credible
sustainability signal, extending existing notions of
sustainability transparency, digital disclosure,
blockchain traceability, and technology -enabled
sustainability communication. Second, it develops and
empirically tests an integrated framework explaining
how green-proofing drives consumer trust and purchase

consumer digital literacy and cultural –institutional
environments. Third, by integrating Signaling Theory
with Institutional Theory, the study explains  not only
how credible sustainability signals influence consumer
behaviour but also the contextual conditions under which
these signals become more or less effective.

2. LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1 Signaling Theory
Signaling theory provides a compelling lens
through which to understand the interplay between
consumer digital literacy, green -proofing, consumer
trust, and purchase intention. Signaling theory posits that
in markets characterized by information asymmetry,
firms use observable cues —signals—to communicate
unobservable attributes such as quality, credibility, or
environmental responsibility (Spence, 1973). Green -
proofing, through the use of traceability tools,
blockchain verification, IoT -enabled monitoring, and
digital product passports, functions as a credibility -
enhancing signal designed to demonstrate authentic
sustainability performance through blockchain -enabled
traceability, digital product passports, and supply chain
transparency, thereby reducing info rmation asymmetry
(Kshetri, 2018; Saberi et al ., 2019). These digital
transparency tools reduce information asymmetry by
offering verifiable evidence that a firm’s environmental
claims are genuine, thereby strengthening consumer trust
when properly interpreted. However, the effectiveness of
these sign als depends heavily on consumers’ ability to
decode them, which is where digital literacy becomes
critical. Consumers with high digital literacy are better
able to understand how blockchain immutability, real -
time monitoring, and digital tracking systems w ork,
enabling them to interpret green -proofing signals as
strong indicators of authenticity (Connelly et al., 2011).
This interpretive ability enhances trust, as consumers
perceive verified sustainability disclosures as reliable
and difficult to manipulate. Higher trust, in turn,
increases purchase intention, as consumers feel more
confident that environmentall y responsible products
genuinely align with their expectations and values.

Conversely, consumers with low digital literacy
may struggle to understand or evaluate digital
transparency signals, weakening their diagnostic value.
Signaling theory emphasizes that for a signal to be
effective, it must be observable and interpretable; w hen
interpretability is low, even strong signals may fail to
influence trust or purchasing behavior (Spence, 1973).
Thus, digital literacy acts as a critical boundary condition
that determines whether green -proofing successfully
reduces uncertainty and influences consumer decision -
making. By shaping the extent to which green -proofing
signals are understood and trusted, consumer digital
literacy fundamentally affects how transpare ncy
initiatives translate into heightened trust and stronger
purchase intention (Connelly et al., 2011).

2.2 Institutional Theory
Institutional theory provides a valuable
framework for understanding how cultural and
institutional environments influence the effectiveness of
green-proofing on consumer trust and purchase
intention. According to institutional theory,
organizations and in dividuals operate within broader
institutional fields defined by regulative, normative, and
cognitive structures, which establish what is considered
legitimate, appropriate, and credible behavior (Scott,
2014). In the context of sustainability, cultural no rms,
regulatory frameworks, and societal expectations shape
how consumers perceive environmental claims and
digital transparency initiatives. Green -proofing, which
leverages technologies such as blockchain, IoT -enabled
monitoring, digital product passports , and end -to-end
supply chain transparency, serves as a mechanism to
align organizational practices with institutional
expectations by enhancing transparency, accountability,
and legitimacy (Busse et al., 2017; Kshetri, 2018; Saberi
et al ., 2019). Consumers in environments with strong
institutional support for sustainability—such as rigorous
environmental regulations, prevalent green norms, or
highly sustainability -oriented cultures—are more likely
to interpret green -proofing signals as cr edible and
trustworthy.

This alignment increases consumer trust
because green -proofing demonstrates compliance with
widely accepted norms and standards (DiMaggio &
Powell, 1983; Suchman, 1995). Conversely, in
environments with weak regulatory enforcement, limited
sustainability a wareness, or cultural indifference to
environmental issues, the perceived legitimacy of green-
proofing may be diminished, weakening its effect on
trust and subsequent purchase intention. Cultural
dimensions, such as long-term orientation or uncertainty
avoidance, further influence how consumers process and
value transparency signals, moderating the degree to
which trust translates into purchase decisions (Hofstede,
2001). Thus, institutional theory explains that the success
of green-proofing in building consumer trust and driving
purchase intention is contingent upon the cultural and
institutional context. Firms’ digital sustainability
disclosures are interpreted through the lens of societal
norms, regulatory pressures, and cultural values, which
either ampl ify or attenuate their impact on consumer
behavior (Scott, 2014; DiMaggio & Powell, 1983).

2.2.1 Theoretical Distinction of Green-Proofing
Although green -proofing shares conceptual
similarities with sustainability transparency, green

certification, blockchain traceability, and sustainability
disclosure, it represents a theoretically distinct construct
rather than a rebranding of these existing concepts. The
distinction lies in its level of analysis, strategic purpose,
and theoretical f unction. Sustainability transparency
primarily concerns the availability and openness of
environmental information that organizations provide to
stakeholders. Its objective is to reduce information
asymmetry by making sustainability practices visible
(Mol, 2015). Similarly, sustainability disclosure focuses
on communicating environmental, social, and
governance (ESG) information through reports and other
formal channels to satisfy regulatory requirements and
stakeholder expectations (Global Reporting Initia tive,
2021). While transparency and disclosure improve
information accessibility, they do not necessarily ensure
that stakeholders perceive such information as credible
or strategically integrated into organizational decision -
making.

Green certification represents another related
but narrower concept. Certifications such as ISO 14001,
FSC, or LEED serve as external verification mechanisms
confirming compliance with predetermined
environmental standards. Their role is largely
institutional, providing third -party assurance regarding
environmental performance. However, certification
evaluates compliance at a particular point in time and
does not explain how organizations strategically leverage
verified environmental evidence to influence stakeholder
perceptions or create competitive advantage. Likewise,
blockchain traceability emphasizes the technological
infrastructure that records and verifies product or supply
chain information through decentralized digital systems
(Saberi et al ., 2019). Blockchain enhances traceability
and data integrity but remains fundamentally an
operational technology. It explains how environmental
information is verified rather than why such verified
information becomes persuasive in shaping consumer
evaluations. Green-proofing differs from these concepts
because it operates as a higher -order strategic capability
that integrates multiple sustainability mechanisms —
including transparency, disclosure, certification, and
digital verification —into a coherent syst em of credible
sustainability signalling. Rather than focusing on any
single environmental practice or technology, green -
proofing concerns the firm's ability to proactively
convert authenticated environmental evidence into
trustworthy market signals that reduce perceived
greenwashing, strengthen stakeholder confidence, and
enhance organizational resilience under evolving
environmental regulations and market expectations.
From the perspective of Signaling Theory (Connelly et
al., 2011), transparency, certification, blockchain, and
disclosure function as individual signalling instruments.
Green-proofing represents the broader organizational
capability responsible for orchestrating these instruments
into credible, consistent, and strategically meaningful
sustainability signals. Consequently, the construct shifts
theoretical attention from the existence of environmental
information to the strategic management of information
credibility. Institutional Theory (Scott, 2014) further
reinforces this distinction by explaining that
organizations face increasing coercive, normative, and
mimetic pressures to demonstrate authentic
environmental responsibility. Green-proofing reflects an
adaptive organizational capability that enables firms not
only to comply with institutional expectations but also to
anticipate future regulatory changes, evolving
stakeholder demands, and technologi cal developments.
This future -oriented perspective differentiates green -
proofing from existing constructs that largely emphasize
current reporting or compliance activities.

Accordingly, green-proofing can be defined as:
Green-proofing is a higher-order organizational
capability through which firms strategically integrate
verified environmental evidence, digital verification
mechanisms, governance systems, and transparent
communication to produce credible sustainability signals
that enhance stakeholder trust, reduce information
asymmetry, strengthen resilience against future
environmental uncertainties, and generate sustainable
competitive advantage. This conceptualization positions
green-proofing as a strategic capability rather than an
environmental practice, reporting mechanism,
certification system, or digital technology. It therefore
extends existing sustainability literature by integrating
these previously fragmented concepts into a unified
theoretical framework explai ning how organizations
transform authenticated environmental evidence into
credible market signals that influence consumer trust and
purchase intention. This clarification strengthens the
manuscript's theoretical contribution by establishing
green-proofing as a novel construct situated at the
intersection of strategic management, sustainability
marketing, and organizational signalling.

2.3 Green-Proofing
Green-proofing has emerged as a response to
growing concerns regarding the credibility of corporate
sustainability communication. Consumers, regulators,
and investors increasingly demand verifiable evidence
that firms' environmental claims reflect actual
operational practices rather than symbolic marketing
statements (de Freitas Netto et al ., 2020; Szabo &
Webster, 2021). Recent sustainability marketing
research indicates that traditional mechanisms such as
eco-labels, sustainability reports, and corporate
environmental advertising often fail to eliminate
information asymmetry because consum ers cannot
independently verify the underlying environmental
performance of firms (Testa et al., 2021; Kumar et al.,
2024). This limitation has intensified scholarly interest in
digital transparency systems capable of providing
traceable and auditable sustainability information.

Existing studies have examined a range of
digital technologies that support sustainability
verification, including blockchain -based traceability,

digital product passports, QR -code-enabled disclosures,
and IoT -enabled environmental monitoring (Casino et
al., 2021; Kouhizadeh et al., 2021; Wamba et al., 2023).
Empirical evidence consistently demonstrates that these
technologies enhance transparency by creating secure,
traceable, and verifiable records of sourcing, production,
and environmental performance throughout product life
cycles, thereby improvi ng supply chain visibility and
strengthening stakeholder confidence in sustainability
claims (Saberi et al., 2019; Casino et al., 2021; Wamba
et al ., 2023). Likewise, the introduction of Digital
Product Passports under the European Union's
Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation enables
consumers and regulators to access standardized
product-level sustainability information across the entire
product lifecycle (European Commission, 2024).
Nevertheless, most existing studies primarily examine
these technologies from the perspectives of supply chain
management, operational efficiency, regulatory
compliance, or corporate governance, with
comparatively limited attention devoted to their role in
consumer-oriented sustainability communication.

A second stream of research argues that digital
transparency alone does not automatically generate
credibility. Consumers may remain skeptical of
sustainability disclosures when environmental
information is highly technical, difficult to interpret, or
perceived as being entirely controlled by firms (Xu &
Pratt, 2023; Nuseir et al., 2024). Other studies suggest
that the effectiveness of digital transparency depends on
contextual factors, including institutional quality,
consumer digital literacy, and cultural attitudes toward
environmental sustainability (Li et al., 2023; Nguyen et
al., 2023). These findings indicate that transparency
technologies alone are insufficient to generate consumer
trust unless they are embedded within broader
credibility-building strategies that facilitate
understanding, verification, and confidence.

Against this background, this study
conceptualizes green -proofing as a distinct strategic
marketing capability rather than merely a technological
application. Unlike sustainable supply chain
transparency, which emphasizes operational visibility, or
blockchain traceability, which focuses on recording and
verifying transactions, green -proofing refers to the
deliberate integration of digitally verifiable sustainability
evidence into consumer -facing marketing
communication to authenticate environmental claims,
reduce perceived greenwashing, and strengthen
stakeholder trust. Thus, green -proofing extends beyond
disclosure by transforming verified environmental data
into credible market signals capable of influencing
consumer perceptions and purchase intentions. Th is
distinction is theoretically important because previous
studies have largely treated digital transparency
technologies as operational or governance mechanisms,
while relatively little research has examined how firms
strategically leverage verified susta inability evidence to
build consumer trust, market legitimacy, and competitive
advantage. Furthermore, existing research rarely
integrates digital transparency, consumer trust, purchase
intention, consumer digital literacy, institutional
environment, and cultural environment wi thin a unified
theoretical framework. Consequently, green -proofing
provides a novel conceptual lens for explaining how
digitally verified sustainability evidence functions as a
strategic marketing capability for enhancing credibility
and fostering sustaina ble consumer behaviour in
increasingly sustainability-conscious markets.

2.3.1 Green-Proofing and Consumer Trust
Consumer trust has become one of the most
important determinants of sustainable consumption
because consumers increasingly question the credibility
of firms' environmental claims amid the widespread
prevalence of greenwashing (de Freitas Netto et al .,
2020; Szabo & Webster, 2021). Recent sustainability
marketing research suggests that trust is no longer
established solely through persuasive environmental
messaging but increasingly depends on firms' ability to
provide transparent, verifiable, and evid ence-based
sustainability information (Testa et al., 2021; Kumar et
al., 2024). Consequently, digital transparency
mechanisms—including blockchain -enabled
traceability, digital product passports, and real -time
environmental monitoring —have received considerable
attention as mechanisms for reducing information
asymmetry and s trengthening the credibility of
sustainability communication (Casino et al ., 2021;
Kouhizadeh et al., 2021; Wamba et al., 2023). From the
perspective of Signaling Theory, green -proofing
functions as a credible strategic signal through which
firms communicate verifiable environmental
performance to consumers. Signaling Theory argues that
credible, difficult -to-imitate signa ls reduce information
asymmetry and enable stakeholders to distinguish
genuinely sustainable firms from those engaging in
symbolic environmental claims (Connelly et al., 2011).
By embedding digitally verifiable sustainability
evidence within consumer -facing communication,
green-proofing strengthens perceptions of honesty,
accountability, and authenticity, thereby fostering
consumer trust. Empirical studies support this argument,
showing that blockchain verification, traceability
systems, and digital sustainability disclosures improve
trust by providing transparent and immutable
sustainability records (Casino et al., 2021; Saberi et al.,
2019; Sun et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024).

However, an alternative perspective challenges
the assumption that digital transparency automatically
translates into consumer trust. Some scholars argue that
technological transparency may have limited influence
when consumers perceive sustainability info rmation as
overly technical, experience information overload, or
remain sceptical about firms' motives (Xu & Pratt, 2023;
Nuseir et al ., 2024). Others suggest that transparency
technologies alone cannot eliminate concerns about

opportunistic corporate behaviour unless consumers
believe that sustainability claims are supported by
credible regulatory oversight and independent
verification (Li et al., 2023). These competing findings
imply that the effectiveness of green -proofing depends
not only on the quality of sustainability signals but also
on the broader institutional context within which those
signals are interpreted. This perspective is reinfo rced by
Institutional Theory, which argues that organizational
signals derive legitimacy from the regulatory, normative,
and cultural environments in which firms operate (Scott,
2014). Strong institutional environments characterised
by effective environmental regulations, enforcement
mechanisms, and transparent governance increase
confidence in sustainability claims by constraining
opportunistic behaviour and reinforcing organizational
accountability (Li et al., 2023). Likewise, societies with
stronger environmental norms and higher levels of
digital and environmental literacy are more likely to
recognise and value digitally verified sustainability
information. Conversely, weak institutional
environments, limited environmental awareness, and low
digital literacy may reduce consumers' ability to
distinguish credible sustainability evidence from
greenwashing, thereby weakening the trust -building
effect of green -proofing (UNESCO, 2023; Van Laar et
al., 2020).

The complementary relationship between
Signaling Theory and Institutional Theory therefore
provides a more comprehensive explanation of consumer

effectiveness of green -proofing is expected to be
stronger in environments characterised  by supportive
institutional frameworks, pro -environmental cultural
values, higher consumer digital literacy, stronger
environmental literacy, and favourable perceptions of
environmental policy effectiveness. These contextual
factors enhance consumers' abi lity to interpret

Accordingly, green -proofing is conceptualised  as a

2.3.2 Green -Proofing and Consumer Purchase
Intention
Purchase intention represents one of the most
widely investigated behavioural outcomes in
sustainability marketing because it reflects consumers'
willingness to support environmentally responsible firms
through actual purchasing decisions (Ajzen, 1991;
Kumar et al ., 2024). While environmental concern
remains an important antecedent of sustainable
purchasing behaviour, recent research increasingly
suggests that consumers are unlikely to translate pro -
environmental attitudes into purchasing decisions unless
they perceive firms' sustainability claims as credible and
authentic (Testa et al ., 2021; Sun et al ., 2023).
Consequently, scholars have increasingly focused on
digital transparency mechanisms capable of enhancing
the credibility of sustainability communication and
reducing consumer uncertainty regarding firms'
environmental performance. Drawing on Sign aling
Theory, green -proofing serves as a strategic market
signal through which firms communicate credible and
verifiable sustainability performance. Signaling Theory
posits that high -quality, difficult -to-imitate signals
reduce information asymmetry, incre ase perceived
product authenticity, and lower the perceived risks
associated with environmentally responsible purchases
(Connelly et al ., 2011). By embedding digitally
verifiable sustainability evidence into marketing
communications, green -proofing enables consumers to
evaluate environmental claims more objectively, thereby
increasing confidence in sustainable products and
strengthening p urchase intentions. Empirical evidence
supports this theoretical perspective, demonstrating that
blockchain-enabled traceability, digital product
passports, QR -code disclosures, and real -time
sustainability verification improve consumers'
willingness to pu rchase environmentally responsible
products by providing objective information regarding
sourcing practices, carbon emissions, and product life -
cycle impacts (Casino et al., 2021; Saberi et al., 2019;
Wamba et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024).

Nevertheless, alternative theoretical arguments
suggest that the relationship between digital transparency
and purchase intention is neither automatic nor
universally positive. Behavioural  decision-making
research argues that consumers frequently prioritize
functional product attributes such as price, quality,
convenience, and brand familiarity over sustainability
information, particularly when sustainable alternatives
involve higher perceived costs or greater cognitive effort
(Nguyen et al., 2023; Xu & Pratt, 2023). Similarly, some
scholars contend that digitally verified sustainability
information influences purchasing behaviour  indirectly
through intermediary mechanisms such as consumer
trust, perceived authenticity, and brand credibility rather
than exerting an independent direct effect (Ameen et al.,
2023; Nuseir et al., 2024). These competing perspectives
indicate that credible sustainability signals alone may be
insufficient to influence purchasing decisions if

information as overly complex. From the perspective of
Institutional Theory, consumers interpret sustainability
signals within broader institutional, regulatory, and
cultural environments that shape the legitimacy of firms'

Furthermore, the effectiveness of green -

either theory alone. While Signaling Theory explains

purchasing decisions. Accordingly, green -proofing is
expected to have the strongest positive effect on purchase

2.3.3 Moderating Effect of Consumer Digital Literacy
The increasing digitalization of sustainability
communication has transformed how consumers access,
interpret, and evaluate firms' environmental claims.
Consumer digital literacy refers to individuals'
knowledge, skills, and confidence in locating,
understanding, evaluating, and applying digital
information to make informed decisions (Van Laar et al.,
2020; UNESCO, 2023). As organizations increasingly
rely on blockchain -enabled traceability, digital product
passports, QR -code verification, and interactive
sustainability dashboards, consumers differ considerably
in their ability to interpret digitally verified sustainability
information and distinguish authentic environmental
commitments from symbolic green marketing.From  the
perspective of Signaling Theory, the effectiveness of
sustainability signals depends not only on the credibility
of the signal itself but also on the receiver's ability to
decode and interpret that signal correctly (Connelly et
al., 2011). Green -proofing provides credible and
verifiable environmental evidence; however, its
effectiveness is contingent upon consumers possessing
sufficient digital competence to understand digital
verification mechanisms and assess their credibility.
Empirical studies consistently demonstrate that digitally
literate consumers are more capable of evaluating
traceability systems, interpreting sustainability
certifications, identifying misleading environmental
claims, and recognising authentic sustainability practices
(Ng, 2012; Siddiq et al ., 2022; Van Laar et al ., 2020;
Ameen et al ., 2023). Consequently, consumers with
higher digital literacy are expected to derive greater
value from green-proofing, resulting in stronger trust and
more favourable purchasing decisions.

Nevertheless, competing perspectives suggest
that digital literacy alone may not be sufficient to
strengthen sustainability communication. Some scholars
argue that highly digitally literate consumers are often
more sceptical of corporate sustainability claims because
greater access to online information also increases
awareness of greenwashing practices and corporate
opportunism (Szabo & Webster, 2021; Xu & Pratt,
2023). Similarly, information overload generated by
complex digital sustainability platforms may reduce
rather than enhance consumer confidence, particularly
when digital information lacks clarity or standardisation
(Nuseir et al ., 2024). These alternative arguments
suggest that digital literacy may both strengthen
consumers' ability to verify sustainability claims and
increase their critical scrutiny of firms' environmental
communication. Institutional Theory provides an
important complementary explanation for these
contrasting perspectives. While Signaling Theory
explains how consumers interpret digitally verified
sustainability signals, Institutional Theory argues that the
effectiveness of those signals depends on the legitimacy
of the institutional context within which they are
received (Scott, 2014). Consumers operating within
environments characterised by effective digital

governance, robust environmental regulations,
widespread digital adoption, and supportive
sustainability policies are more likely to trust digitally
verified environmental information because institutional
mechanisms reinforce organisational accountability .
Conversely, weak digital ecosystems and ineffective
regulatory institutions may reduce the value of digital
literacy by limiting confidence in the credibility of
sustainability verification systems (Li et al., 2023).

Furthermore, consumer environmental literacy
and perceived policy effectiveness operate as
complementary buffering mechanisms. Environmentally
literate consumers are better able to interpret
environmental performance indicators and evaluate the
substantive implications of sustainability claims, while
favourable perceptions of environmental policy
implementation increase confidence that organisations
are held accountable for their environmental
commitments. Together with digital literacy, these
contextual capabilities strengthen consumers' ability to
distinguish credible green -proofing initiatives from
symbolic environmental communication. The integration
of Signaling Theory and Institutional Theory therefore
suggests that consumer digital literacy does not operate
independently but enhances the effectiveness of green -
proofing when supported by favourable  institutional
conditions, strong environmental awareness, and
credible sustainability governance. Accordingly, the
positive influence of green -proofing on consumer trust
and the subsequent influence of consumer trust on
purchase intention are expected to become stronger
among consumers possessing higher levels of digital
literacy.

2.3.4 Moderating Effects of Cultural and Institutional
Environments
The effectiveness of sustainability
communication extends beyond organisational
capabilities and consumer characteristics to encompass
the broader institutional and cultural environments
within which sustainability information is interpreted.
While organisations may invest substantially in digitally
verified sustainability communication, consumers
evaluate these signals through the lens of societal norms,
regulatory quality, cultural values, and institutional
legitimacy. Consequently, identical green -proofing
initiatives may generate markedly different consumer
responses across countries and institutional contexts.
Institutional Theory provides the primary theoretical
foundation for understanding these contextual
differences. According to Institutional Theory,
organisational legitimacy is shaped by formal
regulations, normative expectations, and shared societal
beliefs that influence how stakeholders evaluate
organisational behaviour (Scott, 2014). Strong
institutional environments characterised by effective
environmental regulations, transparent governance
systems, credible certification mechanisms, and
consistent policy enforcement enhance the legitimacy of
sustainability claims by constraining opportunistic
behaviour and reducing greenwashing (Li et al., 2023;
Khan et al., 2023). Under such conditions, consumers are
more likely to trust green -proofing because institutional
safeguards reinforce the credibility of digitally verified
sustainability information.

However, alternative perspectives suggest that
institutional support alone does not necessarily guarantee
consumer trust. Some scholars argue that excessive
regulatory intervention may create compliance -oriented
sustainability practices that satisfy legal requirements
without fostering genuine consumer confidence (Delmas
& Burbano, 2011). Others contend that institutional
credibility varies substantially across countries, meaning
that consumers may continue to question sustainability
claims despite formal r egulatory systems if public trust
in institutions remains weak (Nuseir et al., 2024). These
competing perspectives indicate that institutional quality
represents an important but not exclusive determinant of
green-proofing effectiveness. Signaling Theory
complements this institutional perspective by explaining
that even highly cre dible sustainability signals require
favourable interpretive environments to achieve their
intended effects. Institutional mechanisms enhance the
credibility of organisational  signals by providing
external validation and reducing uncertainty surrounding
sustainability claims. Consequently, institutional
legitimacy strengthens the signalling value of green -
proofing, whereas weak institutional environments
reduce consumers' confi dence in digitally verified
environmental information despite the presence of
technological verification mechanisms.

Cultural values similarly shape consumers'
interpretation of sustainability communication. Societies
characterised by stronger environmental norms, long -
term orientation, collective responsibility, and higher
uncertainty avoidance generally place greater value on
transparent and verifiable sustainability information than
societies where environmental responsibility re ceives
lower social priority (Hofstede, 2001; Nguyen et al .,
2023). Nevertheless, cultural influences remain complex
because consumers from highly environmentally
conscious societies may simultaneously exhibit greater
scepticism toward corporate sustainability
communication, demanding stronger evidence before
developing trust. Thus, cultural environments may both
strengthen appreciation for green -proofing and increase
scrutiny of environmental claims. Moreover, perceived
environmental policy effectiveness, digital culture, and
environmental literacy function as complementary
buffering mechanisms that reinforce institutional
legitimacy. Consumers who perceive environmental

policies to be effectively implemented are more likely to
believe that organisations face meaningful accountability
for sustainability performance. Likewise, societies
characterised by advanced digital cultures facilitate
greater acceptance of digital verification technologies,
while higher environmental literacy enables consumers
to evaluate sustainability information more accurately.
Collectively, these contextual mechanisms streng then
the credibility and effectiveness of green -proofing by
improving both the institutional legitimacy of
sustainability claims and consumers' capacity to interpret
them.

By integrating Signaling Theory with
Institutional Theory, this study argues that institutional
and cultural environments do not independently
determine sustainable consumer behaviour ; rather, they
strengthen the effectiveness of green -proofing by
enhancing the credibility, legitimacy, and interpretability
of digitally verified sustainability signals. Therefore, the
positive influence of green -proofing on consumer trust
and the subsequent effect of consumer trust on purchase
intention are expected to become stronger under
favourable institutional and cultural conditions.

2.5 Conceptualization and Operationalisation of
Green-Proofing
Although sustainability communication and
green marketing have received extensive scholarly
attention, the concept of green -proofing represents an
emerging extension of sustainability practice that focuses
on how firms demonstrate, substantiate, and authenticate
their environmental commitments. Traditional green
marketing often emphasizes communicating
environmentally responsible attributes; however,
increasing concerns regarding greenwashing have
created demand for mechanisms that allow consumers to
evaluate the credibility and authenticity of environmental
claims. Green -proofing addresses this challenge by
emphasizing the provision of verifiable evidence,
transparent sustainability information, and credible
signals that demonstrate alignment between firms’
environmental claims and actual practices. Drawing
from Signaling Theory, green -proofing can be
understood as a strategic signaling mechanism through
which firms reduce information asymmetry between
themselves and consumers. Because consumers typically
possess limited ability to directly observe firms’
environmental performance, sustainability claims
function as signals whose credibility depends on the
availability of reliable evidence, transparency, and
verification mechanisms. Accordingly, green -proofing
represents firms’ ability to provide credible
environmental signals that enhance consumer confidence
and reduce perceived uncertainty regarding
sustainability claims. Furthermore, from a stakeholder
and transparency perspective, green-proofing reflects the
extent to which organizations adopt practices that allow
stakeholders to access, evaluate, and verify
sustainability-related information. This includes digitally
enabled mechanisms such as QR -code product
disclosures, blockchain -based traceability systems,
digital product passports, and online sustainability
reporting platforms. These technologies strengthen
environmental accountability by enabling con sumers to
move beyond passive exposure to green claims toward
active verification of sustainability information.
Therefore, this study conceptualizes green -proofing as a
consumer-perceived sustainability verification
capability, defined as the extent to wh ich firms provide
credible, transparent, and verifiable evidence that
supports their environmental claims and demonstrates
authentic commitment to sustainable practices.

2.5.1 Operationalization of Green-Proofing
Given the multidimensional nature of green -
proofing, the construct was operationalized using
indicators capturing four key dimensions:

Environmental Claim Credibility: This dimension
captures whether consumers perceive firms’
sustainability claims as believable, authentic, and
supported by reliable evidence. Credible environmental
claims reduce scepticism and enhance confidence in
sustainability communication.
Sustainability Transparency : This dimension reflects
the accessibility and openness of environmental
information provided by firms. Transparent disclosure
enables consumers to evaluate sustainability practices
and assess whether environmental commitments are
genuine.
Verification and Traceability Capability : This
dimension captures the extent to which consumers can
verify sustainability claims through digital tools and
traceability mechanisms. Digital verification reduces
information asymmetry and strengthens accountability.
Consistency Between Sustainability Claims and
Actions: This dimension reflects consumers’
perceptions that firms’ environmental statements
correspond with actual operational practices. Alignment
between claims and actions is essential for overcoming
greenwashing concerns.  Together, these indicators
capture th e core domain of green -proofing by
representing the mechanisms through which firms
demonstrate environmental authenticity, improve
sustainability credibility, and strengthen consumer
confidence.

3. METHODOLOGY
3.1 Research Design and Research Approach
This study adopted a quantitative, cross -
sectional research design to examine the associations
among green -proofing, consumer trust, and consumer
purchase intention, while assessing the conditional
influence of consumer digital literacy and cultural and
institutional environments. A quantitative design was
considered appropriate because the study seeks to
operationalize theoretically derived constructs, test
hypothesized relationships, and evaluate complex
interactions among latent variables using statistical
modelling techniques (Creswell & Creswell, 2018).
Specifically, the use of standardized measurement
instruments enabled the objective assessment of
consumers’ perceptions of digitally enabled
sustainability communication and their behavioural
intentions. The study was informed by a positivist
research philosophy, which guided the emphasis on
objective measurement, hypothesis -driven inquiry, and
empirical validation of theoretical relationships. Rather
than relying on subjective interpretations  of consumer

IOASD Journal of Business and Management Studies | Published by IOASD Publisher | India | 248

experiences, the positivist orientation informed the use
of structured questionnaires, validated measurement
scales, and statistical techniques to assess the extent to
which observed patterns in consumer responses support
the proposed theoretical relations hips (Saunders et al .,
2019). This philosophical positioning therefore shaped
the selection of a deductive research approach, where
hypotheses derived from existing theories were
empirically examined through quantitative analysis.

A cross -sectional survey approach was
employed because it allows the collection of data from a
relatively large sample of respondents within a defined
period, making it suitable for investigating consumer
perceptions and behavioural intentions in rapidly
evolving digital sustainability contexts (Saunders et al.,
2019). However, the authors acknowledge that the cross-
sectional nature of the data limits the ability to establish
temporal precedence and firm causal relationships
among the study variables. Consequently, the findings
should be interpreted as demonstr ating empirical
associations and conditional relationships rather than
definitive causal effects. Although moderation analysis
provides insights into how contextual factors may
strengthen or weaken observed relationships,
longitudinal or experimental research would be required
to confirm causal mechanisms and changes in consumer
behaviour over time.

To ensure methodological rigor, the study
employed a two -stage analytical strategy. First,
Covariance-Based Structural Equation Modelling (CB -
SEM) was conducted using IBM SPSS AMOS 29 to
evaluate the measurement model through Confirmatory
Factor Analysis ( CFA). This stage assessed construct
reliability, convergent validity, discriminant validity,
and overall model adequacy before testing the structural
relationships. Second, hypothesis testing was conducted
using Hayes’ PROCESS Macro Version 4.2 within IBM
SPSS Statistics 29 to examine moderation effects
through interaction -based regression analysis with
bootstrapped confidence intervals (Hayes, 2022).

The combination of CB -SEM and PROCESS
Macro was appropriate because the study required both
rigorous validation of latent constructs and examination
of conditional relationships among variables. While CB-
SEM provides a comprehensive assessment of
measurement quality and theoretical model
specification, PROCESS Macro offers a robust approach
for estimating moderation effects through conditional
regression analysis (Hayes, 2022; Kline, 2023). This
integrated analytical approach has been widely applied
in behavioural, sustainability, and marketing research
where researchers seek to examine complex relationships
involving latent psychological constructs and contextual
boundary conditions.

residing in the United Kingdom who had previously
purchased environmentally friendly products or had
experience accessing digitally enabled sustainability

excessive representation from particular demographic
segments and enhanced the comparability of responses

analysis from the pilot study demonstrated satisfactory
internal consistency across all constructs, with

3.5 Common Method Bias Assessment
Because the data for all constructs were
collected from the same respondents using a single
survey instrument, the possibility of common method
bias (CMB) was carefully considered. Following
Podsakoff et al. (2003), both procedural and statistical
remedies were implemented to minimize and assess
potential common method variance. From a procedural
perspective, several measures were adopted during
questionnaire design and administration. First,
respondents wer e assured of anonymity and
confidentiality to reduce evaluation apprehension and
encourage honest responses. Second, the questionnaire
was structured to psychologically separate predictor and
criterion variables by presenting construct items in
different sections and using neutral i nstructions. Third,
measurement items were carefully reviewed to avoid
ambiguous wording, leading questions, and socially
desirable phrasing. These procedural approaches reduce
the likelihood that respondents would systematically
answer items based on perceived expectations rather than
their actual evaluations.

Statistical assessments were subsequently
conducted to evaluate potential common method
variance. Initially, Harman’s single -factor test was

IOASD Journal of Business and Management Studies | Published by IOASD Publisher | India | 252

performed as a preliminary diagnostic. The results
indicated that the first factor accounted for 31.6%  of the
total variance, which is below the commonly referenced
threshold of 50%. However, given the recognized
limitations of this approach, Harman’s test was
interpreted only as an initial indication rather than
conclusive evidence regarding common method bias.
Further assessment was conducted using full collinearity
variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis following Kock
(2015). The full collinearity VIF values ranged from 1.42
to 2.76 , remaining below the conservative threshold of
3.3. These results suggest that excessive common
method variance and multicollinearity were unlikely to
substantially influence the structural relationships.

To provide a stronger assessment, a common
latent factor (CLF) analysis was conducted within the
confirmatory factor analysis framework. A common
latent method factor was introduced by allowing all
measurement indicators to load simultaneously on their
respective theoretical constructs and the common
method factor. The comparison between the baseline
measurement model and the model incorporating the
common latent factor showed negligible differences in
model fit and standardized factor loadings. The
standardized factor loading differences remained below
the recommended threshold of 0.20, indicating that
common method variance did not substantially explain
the covariance among the constructs. Taken together, the
combination of procedural controls, Harman’s si ngle-
factor assessment, full collinearity VIF analysis, and
CFA-based common latent factor assessment provides
reasonable evidence that common method bias is
unlikely to materially affect the interpretation of the
findings. Nevertheless, consistent with be st
methodological practice, the possibility of residual
common method variance cannot be completely
eliminated in cross -sectional self -reported survey
research.

3.6 Measurement and Structural Models
The study followed the two -step procedure
recommended by Anderson and Gerbing (1988). The
measurement model was first evaluated through
Confirmatory Factor Analysis using AMOS to establish
construct reliability and validity. Subsequently, the
structural mo del was estimated to examine the direct
relationships among green-proofing, consumer trust, and
purchase intention. Moderation hypotheses were then
examined using Hayes' PROCESS Macro Version 4.2
(Models 1 and 14, depending on the hypothesized
relationship) with 5,000 bootstrap samples and 95% bias-
corrected confidence intervals. PROCESS was selected
because it provides robust estimation of interaction
effects and conditional relationships while generating
bootstrap confidence intervals that do not rely on
normality assumptions (Hayes, 2022).

4.1.2.1 Measurement Development of Green-Proofing
Given that Green -Proofing is introduced as a
novel construct within sustainability marketing, a
rigorous measurement development process was
undertaken to establish its conceptual clarity, content
validity, and psychometric soundness. In this study,
Green-Proofing is defined as a strategic marketing
capability through which firms integrate digitally
verifiable sustainability evidence into consumer -facing
communication to authenticate environmental claims,
reduce perceived greenwashing, and enhance the
credibility of sustainability information. Unlike
sustainability transparency, environmental disclosure,
blockchain traceability, or digital verification, which
primarily focus on operational visibility, reporting
practices, or technological infrastructure, Gre en-
Proofing captures the strategic integration of verified
sustainability evidence into marketing communications
to influence consumer perceptions and purchasing
behaviour. Because no established measurement scale
for Green -Proofing currently exists, the construct was
operationalized by adapting and integrating
measurement items from conceptually related literature
on sustainability transparency (Mol, 2015; Testa et al.,
2021), green claim credibility (Chen & Chang, 2013),
environmental disclosure (Michelon et al., 2015), digital
traceability and blockchain-enabled transparency (Saberi
et al ., 2019; Wamba et al ., 2023), and digital
sustainability communication (George et al ., 2024).
Existing items were carefully reviewed and reworded to
reflect the conceptual domain of Green -Proofing while
preserving consistency with the underlying theoretical
foundations of Signaling Theory and Institutional
Theory. The adaptation process ensured that the final
scale measured the strategic use of digitally verifiable
sustainability information rather than technological
adoption or disclosure practices alone.

To establish content validity, the preliminary
measurement instrument was evaluated by a panel of five
experts comprising two professors specializing in
sustainability marketing, one expert in digital
transformation and information systems, one consumer
behaviour researcher, and one quantitative methodology
specialist. The panel assessed each item with respect to
conceptual relevance, representativeness, clarity,
readability, and alignment with the proposed definition
of Green -Proofing. Based on their recommendati ons,
several items were refined to improve wording precision,
eliminate conceptual overlap with related constructs, and
ensure that each indicator adequately represented the
theoretical domain of Green -Proofing. The expert
evaluation confirmed that the ret ained items possessed
satisfactory face and content validity. Following expert
review, a pilot study was conducted among 50 UK
consumers who satisfied the same screening criteria as
the respondents included in the main survey. The pilot
test assessed quest ionnaire clarity, item
comprehensibility, completion time, and preliminary
reliability. Participants reported that the questionnaire
was easy to understand and that the Green -Proofing
items adequately reflected digitally verified
sustainability communicati on encountered during
purchasing decisions. Minor wording revisions were
subsequently incorporated to improve readability and
contextual relevance. Reliability analysis from the pilot
study indicated satisfactory internal consistency, with
Cronbach's alpha  values exceeding the recommended
threshold of 0.70 (Hair et al ., 2022), supporting
progression to the main survey.

The final Green -Proofing construct was
operationalized using seven reflective measurement
items, each measured on a seven -point Likert scale

4.1 DISCUSSION OF RESULTS
The findings of this study provide empirical
support for the relevance of Signaling Theory in
explaining the relationship between green -proofing and
consumer trust within the context of digitally enabled
sustainability communication. Specifically, the resu lts
indicate that green -proofing was positively and
significantly associated with consumer trust  among the
respondents surveyed. This finding suggests that
digitally verifiable sustainability practices may serve as
credible market signals that reduce perceived
information asymmetry and enhance consumers'
confidence in firms' environmental claims, cons istent
with the propositions of Signaling Theory (Connelly et
al., 2011). Rather than relying solely on symbolic
sustainability narratives, green -proofing integrates
digital verification mechanisms —including traceability
systems, transparent environmental disclosures, and
third-party verification—to strengthen the cred ibility of
sustainability information. Consequently, consumers
who perceive stronger green-proofing practices are more
likely to develop greater trust in a firm's environmental
commitments.

This finding is consistent with previous studies
reporting that credible sustainability information
enhances consumers' trust in organizations by reducing
uncertainty surrounding environmental claims (Szabo &
Webster, 2021; Testa et al., 2020). However, the present
study extends this literature by conceptualizing green -
proofing not merely as an information disclosure
mechanism but as a strategic signaling capability that
integrates transparency, verification, and authenticated
environmental evidence into a coherent credibility -
building process. In doing so, the study advances
Signaling Theory by suggesting that the credibility of
sustainability communication depends not only on the
availability of environmental information but also on the
firm's ability to provide verifiable evidence supporting
its claims.

The findings further suggest that digital
verification mechanisms may complement consumers'
evaluation processes by making sustainability
information more accessible and credible. Rather than
implying that consumers no longer interpret
sustainability signa ls, the results indicate that verified
sustainability information may facilitate consumer
evaluations by reducing uncertainty regarding the
authenticity of environmental claims. This supports the
notion of system -supported signaling, whereby digital
verification enhances the perceived credibility of
sustainability signals while consumers remain the
ultimate evaluators of those signals. Importantly, these
findings should be interpreted within the context of the
present study. Given the cross-sectional research design,
the results demonstrate a significant positive association
between green-proofing and consumer trust rather than
establishing a causal relationship. Future longitudinal
and experimental studies are therefore needed to
examine whether improvements in green -proofing lead
to sustained increases in consumer trust across different
industries, countries, and institutional environments.
From a theoretical perspective, this finding contributes to
the sustainability marketing litera ture by positioning
green-proofing as a distinct strategic signaling capability
that extends existing concepts of sustainability
transparency, environmental disclosure, and digital
verification. Rather than representing another form of
sustainability communication, green-proofing integrates
these mechanisms into a higher -order capability that
strengthens the credibility of environmental information
and is positively associated with consumer trust.
Accordingly, the study provides empirical support for
applying Signaling Theory to digitally enabled
sustainability communication while offering a novel
conceptual perspective on how organizations can build

consumer trust through authenticated sustainability
practices.

Drawing on Signaling Theory , we argue that
green-proofing may operate as a sufficiently credible
sustainability signal because it combines digital
verification, traceability, authenticated environmental
evidence, and transparent communication into an
integrated signaling capability. Such credibility may
reduce consumers' reliance on individual characteristics,
such as digital literacy, when evaluating sustainability
claims. Rather than implying that digital literacy is
universally unimportant, our findings suggest that
standardized an d easily interpretable verification
mechanisms may lessen the extent to which consumers
require advanced digital competencies to perceive
sustainability information as trustworthy. Similarly, the
non-significant moderating effect of the cultural –
institutional environment is now interpreted as evidence
that the credibility associated with digitally verified
sustainability information may remain relatively
consistent within the study context, irrespective of
respondents' perceptions of institutional or cultur al
conditions. We emphasize, however, that this
interpretation is context -specific and should not be
generalized across countries or industries. Instead, it
suggests that the effectiveness of green -proofing may
depend more strongly on the perceived credibi lity of the
sustainability signal than on contextual institutional
differences in the present sample.

Importantly, we have avoided portraying these
findings as evidence that consumer characteristics or
institutional environments are universally irrelevant.
Instead, we position them as theoretically meaningful
because they refine the boundary conditions of Signaling
Theory and Institutional Theory , indicating that the
influence of these contextual factors may be weaker in
digitally enabled sustainability communication than
previously assumed. We further recommend that future
research examine these relationships using cross -
country, longitudinal, and industry-comparative designs
to determine whether the absence of moderation is
specific to the current research setting or reflects a
broader pattern in digitally verified sustainability
communication.

5. Managerial Implications
The findings offer clear guidance for managers
seeking to leverage sustainability as a competitive
advantage. First, firms should prioritize the development
of credible and verifiable green -proofing systems,
including third -party certifications, traceable
sustainability claims, and transparent reporting
mechanisms. The strength of green -proofing lies not in
promotional messaging but in its perceived authenticity
and verifiability. Second, managers should recognize
that green -proofing is not limited by consu mer
heterogeneity in digital literacy. This reduces the need
for segmentation strategies based on digital capability
and allows firms to adopt standardized sustainability
communication strategies across markets. Third, firms
should avoid overcomplicating sustainability messaging.
Since the study shows that green -proofing operates
effectively across cultural and instituti onal contexts,
managers can deploy globally consistent sustainability
signals rather than heavily localized variations. This
improves efficiency in branding and communication
strategies while maintaining effectiveness.

5.1 Practical Implications
Practically, the study demonstrates that firms
can enhance consumer trust and purchase intention
through simple, visible, and verifiable sustainability
cues. Eco -labels, QR -coded environmental disclosures,
and standardized certifications are particularly e ffective
because they reduce ambiguity and facilitate instant
recognition. In addition, the findings highlight that
green-proofing functions simultaneously as a trust -
building and conversion -enhancing mechanism,
meaning it should be integrated not only int o corporate
social responsibility reporting but also into core
marketing and product positioning strategies. Firms that
integrate green-proofing into packaging, advertising, and
digital platforms are more likely to convert sustainability
perceptions into actual purchase behavior.

5.2 Theoretical Implications
The study contributes to signaling theory by
demonstrating that green signals can exert strong
behavioral effects even in the absence of moderating
cognitive or contextual conditions. This challenges the
traditional assumption that signal interpretation de pends
heavily on receiver characteristics such as digital
literacy. Furthermore, the findings extend institutional
theory by suggesting that sustainability signals may be
increasingly decoupled from local institutional
environments, reflecting the emergenc e of globally
standardized environmental legitimacy criteria.
Importantly, the study foregrounds a key theoretical
insight: digital literacy and institutional environments are
not necessary boundary conditions for green -proofing
effectiveness. This represe nts a meaningful departure
from much of the prior literature and suggests that
sustainability signaling may be entering a maturity stage
characterized by universality and interpretive
convergence. Future research should examine alternative
boundary conditi ons such as environmental
involvement, perceived greenwashing risk, or brand
credibility to further refine the theoretical model.

5.3 CONCLUSION
This study provides strong evidence that green-
proofing is a powerful driver of consumer trust and
purchase intention. Beyond confirming its direct
effectiveness, the findings reveal that its influence
remains stable across differences in consumer digital
literacy and across varying cultural and institutional
environments. This indicates that the effectiveness of
sustainability signaling is less dependent on consumer

capabilities or contextual conditions than previously
assumed. A key contribution of this research is the
identification of green-proofing as a broadly transferable
mechanism of trust formation in consumer markets. The
findings suggest that when sustainabi lity claims are
transparent, verifiable, and consistently communicated,
they can generate behavioral responses across diverse
consumer groups without requiring extensive adaptation.

At a broader level, the study highlights an
important shift in consumer behavior research:
sustainability signals are increasingly functioning as
standardized market cues rather than context -dependent
messages. This has implications for both theory and
practice, as it suggests a move toward convergence in
how consumers interpret environmental responsibility
across markets. Finally, while the study confirms the
robustness of green -proofing, it also opens avenues for
further research into more nuanced psychol ogical and
contextual moderators that may shape its effectiveness in
specific competitive or high-skepticism environments.

5.4 Limitations and Suggestions for Future
Despite the theoretical and practical
contributions of this study, several limitations should be
acknowledged to contextualize the findings and guide
future research.

First, although the study collected data from UK
consumers who had previously purchased sustainable
products and had been exposed to digital sustainability
information, the sample was obtained through an online
survey using predefined screening criteria.
Consequently, the findings may not fully represent the
broader population of UK consumers, particularly
individuals with limited internet access, lower levels of
digital engagement, or little exposure to digitally enabled
sustainability information. Future studies could employ
probability-based sampling techniques or more diverse
consumer panels to enhance the representativeness and
generalizability of the findings.

Second, the study is geographically limited to a
single national context, which constrains the
generalizability of the findings. Consumer perceptions of
Green-Proofing may vary across countries due to
differences in regulatory strength, environmental
awareness, market maturity, and cultural orientation
toward sustainability. As such, the findings may not fully
capture cross -country variations in sustainability
signaling effectiveness. Future studies should therefore
replicate the proposed model across multiple countries or
adopt cross -cultural comparative designs to enhance
external validity and provide broader insights into
contextual differences.

Third, the study relies on a cross -sectional and
self-reported survey design, which presents inherent
methodological limitations. Cross -sectional data restrict
the ability to establish causal relationships among Green-
Proofing, Consumer Trust, and Consumer Purchase
Intention. Furthermore, self -reported measures may be
subject to common method bias and social desirability
bias, particularly in sustainability-related research where
respondents may overstate environmentall y responsible
attitudes. Future research should employ longitudinal,
experimental, or field -based research designs and
incorporate objective behavioural measures to strengthen
causal inference and reduce potential response bias.

Finally, this study examined only two
contextual moderators—Consumer Digital Literacy and
Cultural and Institutional Environment. Other potentially
relevant boundary conditions, including environmental
concern, perceived greenwashing, brand loyalty, prior
sustainability experience, product involvement, and
socioeconomic characteristics, were beyond the scope of
the present research. Future studies should investigate
these additional contextual factors to provide a more
comprehensive understanding of the con ditions under
which Green -Proofing most effectively influences
consumer trust and purchase intention.

Future research should incorporate these
constructs to provide a more nuanced understanding of
boundary conditions influencing the effectiveness of
green-proofing. In addition, this study conceptualized
green-proofing as a single overarching construct.
However, sustainability practices are multidimensional
and may include environmental initiatives (e.g., carbon
reduction), social responsibility (e.g., labor practices),
ethical sourcing, and governance transparency. These
dimensions may exert differential ef fects on consumer
trust and purchase intention. Future studies should
therefore decompose green -proofing into its constituent
dimensions to determine which aspects are most
influential in shaping consumer behavior. Finally, future
research may benefit from  mixed-method approaches,
such as combining surveys with interviews or focus
groups. Qualitative insights could help explain the
psychological mechanisms underlying consumer
responses to green -proofing, particularly how trust is
formed and how sustainabili ty cues are interpreted in
different contexts.
