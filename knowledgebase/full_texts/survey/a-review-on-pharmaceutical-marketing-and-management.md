Abstract: Pharmaceutical marketing and management occupy a distinct position among commercial disciplines beca use they
operate at the intersection of profit-driven business objectives and public health responsibility. This review examines the strategic,
operational, and ethical dimensions of pharma marketing and management, drawing on recent literature covering promotional
practices, digital transformation, supply chain operations, and regulatory oversight. The review finds that traditional
promotional tools such as medical representative (MR) visits, free drug samples, and continuing medi cal education (CME)
sponsorships remain influential in shaping physician prescribing behaviour, even as digital channels, artificial intelligence (AI),
and data analytics increasingly reshape how companies  engage healthcare professionals (HCPs) and patients. On the
management side, the Indian pharmaceutical supply chain continues to face structural challenges including fragm entation,
import dependence on active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), cold chain limitations, and counterfeit products, even as digital
tools and regulatory reforms attempt to  close these gaps. Ethical concerns persist around inducements, exaggerated promotional
claims, and inconsistent enforcement of codes such as the Uniform Code for Pharmaceutical  Marketing Practices (UCPMP),
which became legally binding in 2024. The review concludes that a balanced, ethically anchored, and technologically integrated
approach to marketing and supply chain management is essential for the pharmaceutical sector to serve both com mercial and
public health goals sustainably.
Keywords: Pharmaceutical marketing, pharma management, promotional strategies, supply chain  management, medical

I. INTRODUCTION
The pharmaceutical industry is unlike most other commercial sectors because the "customer"  for a medicine is rarely the same
person who chooses it. Physicians typically decide what gets prescribed, pharmacists influence what gets dispensed, and patients are
often the last link in this decision chain. This three -way structure makes pharmaceutical marketing a fundamentally di fferent
exercise from consumer product marketing, one that has to inform, persuade, and comply with regulation all at once.
The World Health Organization  defines pharmaceutical marketing broadly as  any activity undertaken  by a manufacturer or
distributor to promote the prescription, supply, purchase, or use of medicinal products, including advertising, sponsorship, and other
promotional formats directed at either healthcare professionals or the general public. Pharmaceutica l management, meanwhile,
extends beyond promotion into the operational backbone of the industry — procurement, manufacturing, distribution, inventory
control, and logistics — all of which determine whether a medicine actually reaches the patient who needs it, in usable condition, at
the right time.
India occupies a particularly interesting position in this global picture. It is often called the "pharmacy of the world" due to its role as
the largest supplier of generic medicines by volume, yet its domestic supply chain still struggles w ith fragmentation, infrastructure
gaps, and heavy reliance on imported raw materials. At the same time, Indian regulators have tighten ed the ethical framework
governing promotional conduct, most notably through the 2024 revision of the UCPMP, which converted a previously voluntary
code into an enforceable regulation.
This review paper aims to consolidate current literature on three interconnected themes:
(1) the strategies and tools used in pharmaceutical marketing, (2) the operational and logistical  challenges in pharmaceutical
management, particularly within the Indian context, and (3) the ethical and regulatory tensions that arise when commercial incentives
meet patient  welfare obligations. The objective is to provide pharmacy students and early-career resear chers with a structured,
evidence-based understanding of how marketing and management functions shape access to medicines and influenc e the credibility
of the pharmaceutical profession.

II. MEANING AND SCOPE
Pharmaceutical marketing identifies patient and healthcare needs, develops a product’s value proposition, communicates scientific
product information, and ensures the medicine reaches appropriate users through lawful channels.
Pharmaceutical management covers the internal systems that make this possible: strategy, finance, operations, quality, supply chain,
people management, regulatory affairs, and performance monitoring.
For a herbal or OTC product, marketing may include consumer education and retail visibility; for prescription drugs, communication
is primarily directed toward qualified healthcare professionals and must be evidence-based.iris.who+1

A. Core Objectives
 Improve patient access to safe, effective, quality-assured medicines.
 Achieve sustainable sales, market share, profitability, and brand equity.
 Communicate accurate benefits, risks, dosage, and appropriate-use information.
 Build trust with healthcare professionals, distributors, pharmacies, hospitals, and patients.
 Maintain compliance with drug-approval conditions, promotional rules, and pharmacovigilance requirements.iris.who+1

C. STP framework Segmentation
The market can be segmented using:
 Therapy area: Diabetes, dermatology, cardiology, gastrointestinal disorders, etc.
 Customer type: General physicians, specialists, hospitals, pharmacies, wholesalers, patients.
 Geography: National, state, district, urban, rural, or territory level.
 Behavior: High-, medium-, and low-potential prescribers; formulary versus non-formulary hospitals.
 Patient characteristics: Age, disease severity, affordability, adherence needs, and comorbidities.

D. Targeting
Targeting means selecting segments where the product has a clear clinical and commercial  fit. A new antidiabetic product, for
example, may prioritize diabetologists, endocrinologists, high-volume general physicians, and hospitals with diabetes clinics.

In pharmaceutical practice, a fifth practical element is often added: People—medical representatives, product managers, key-account
managers, regulatory personnel, pharmacists, and customer-support teams.

G. Product Management
A product manager or brand manager is responsible for the product’s commercial and strategic  lifecycle. Their role connects
medical, regulatory, sales, manufacturing, supply chain, market research, and finance functions.
Key responsibilities include:
 Preparing the annual brand plan and sales forecast.
 Developing scientific promotional material with medical and regulatory review.
 Studying competitors, market trends, and prescriber feedback.
 Designing training material for medical representatives.
 Monitoring availability, stock-outs, product complaints, and sales performance.
 Managing lifecycle actions such as line extensions, new pack sizes, relaunches, or new indications.

H. Sales-force management
Medical representatives (MRs) are a major communication channel in prescription-drug marketing. They provide approved scientific
information to healthcare professionals, collect market feedback, support lawful sample distribution, and help ensure availability
through the trade channel.
A sales manager typically handles:
 Territory design and doctor-list classification.
 Recruitment, induction, product training, and field coaching.
 Call planning and coverage frequency.
 Sales-target setting and incentive administration.
 Monitoring field activity, secondary sales, and distributor stock.
 Ensuring ethical conduct and compliance by representatives.
In India, companies are accountable for the actions of their MRs and other employees under UCPMP 2024.pib.gov+1

I. Distribution and supply chain
Pharmaceutical distribution aims to provide the right medicine, in the right quantity and condition, at the right location and time.
Main channel flow:
Manufacturer → Carrying & Forwarding agent → Stockist/wholesaler → Retail pharmacy or hospital → Patient
Important management areas include:
 Demand forecasting and production planning.
 Inventory control, expiry-date management, and batch traceability.
 Cold-chain maintenance for temperature-sensitive products.
 Prevention of stock-outs and overstocking.
 Product returns, recalls, complaint handling, and reverse logistics.
 GDP and quality-system compliance.

III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
A. Traditional Promotional Strategies Remain Influential
Across multiple studies, medical representative (MR) visits consistently emerge as the  single most influential promotional tool
affecting physician prescribing behaviour. One study quantified MR-led "doctor detailing" as accounting for roughly 33 percent of
promotional significance, followed by CME programs and health camps (18 percent), product sample dis tribution (16 percent), and
gifts to doctors, chemists, and stockists  (14 percent). Other reviews similarly report that personal engagement by MRs, free drug
samples, sponsored travel, and continuing medical education funding significantly shape doctors' pre scription choices, alongs ide
supporting materials like visual aids, leaflets, and audio-video resources.

This pattern indicates that despite the rise of digital tools, in -person and relationship-based promotion has not been displaced — it
continues to function as the backbone of pharmaceutical detailing, particularly in markets like India where physician -patient trust
dynamics remain strong.

B. Digital Transformation and the Shift Toward Data-Driven Marketing
A clear trend across recent literature is the accelerating shift toward digital and AI-enabled marketing. Industry surveys indicate that
over 85 percent of pharmaceutical companies are becoming data -driven, using predictive analytics and automation to personalize
patient engagement and adjust campaigns in real tim e. In a 2025 industry survey, AI and machine learning were selected by 72
percent of marketing executives as the digital trend that excites them most, followed by data -driven targeting and personalization
(52 percent) and interactive content formats such as quizzes and infographics (30 percent). Digital advertising spend in the healthcare
and pharma sector reflects this shift, with 2025 estimates placing digital ad spend at approximately 24.8 billion US dollars, up 13.3
percent year-over-year and now accounting for more than three -quarters of total industry advertising  expenditure. Companies are
increasingly deploying omnichannel strategies that blend  traditional MR detailing with search engine marketing, content
marketing, email campaigns, mobile health applications, and social media outreach on platforms such as  LinkedIn and Instagram
to reach both HCPs and patients.  However, this digital expansion has not gone unchecked. Regulatory scrutiny of direct -to-
consumer (DTC) advertising has intensified, with U.S. authorities directing stricter enforcement on misleading drug advertisements
across television and social media channels in 2025, and proposals to require fuller side -effect disclosures in advertising formats.
This tension between marketing innovation and regulatory tightening is a recurring theme across the literature and suggests that future
promotional strategy will need to be "compliance-by-design" rather than compliance-as-an-afterthought.

C. Supply Chain and Logistics Management Challenges in India
On the management side, the Indian pharmaceutical supply chain reveals a set of persistent structura l weaknesses. Studies describe
the distribution network as highly fragmented, involving more than 20,000 to 60,000 registered manufacturing and distribution units
and over 550,000 retail pharmacies, which complicates inventory visibility, cold chain integrity, and quality control across the chain.
Indian pharmaceutical companies reportedly spend roughly one-third of their revenue on supply chain m anagement activities, with
SCM costs amounting to an estimated 13 percent of India's GDP contribution from the sector — figures substantially higher than
global averages, largely attributable to inadequate transportation infrastructure.  A recurring set of operational issues appears
consistently across the literature: degradation of temperature -sensitive medicines during transit, shipping of expired products,
complex network design with large numbers of stock -keeping units (SKUs), outdated hospital information systems with poor inter-
organizational connectivity, and difficulty forecasting demand accurately. One recent analysis further quantifies the cost architecture
of pharmaceutical SCM, attributing 25 percent of costs to manufacturing, 20 percent each to p rocurement, inventory management,
and distribution, and the remaining 15 percent to transportation.

D. Ethical and Regulatory Dimensions
Ethical tension is arguably the most sensitive theme running through pharmaceutical  marketing literature. Studies focused on the
Indian context report that while 85  percent of marketing executives claim awareness of ethical codes, only about 60 percent had
received formal ethics training in the preceding year, and incentive-driven sales targets correlated significantly with higher
frequencies of ethically questionable practices (correlation  coefficient of 0.64). Physicians surveyed in the same research reported
that 72 percent of promotional claims were perceived as sometimes exaggerated, and 48 percent had experienced direct prescription
pressure from promotional activity.
The Uniform Code for Pharmaceutical Marketing Practices (UCPMP), historically a voluntary guideline, was revised in March 2024
into a legally enforceable code addressing transparency around free samples, CME event sponsorship, brand reminders, and an
outright ban on inducements to doctors. Despite this stronger framework, enforcement g aps remain; the literature cites a case in
which the Department of Pharmaceuticals reprimanded a multinational company for sponsoring foreign t rips for doctors worth
approximately 1.91 crore rupees, in violation of UCPMP provisions. Other studies  similarly find that while some Indian companies
demonstrate reasonable adherence to ethical guidelines, others continue unethical promotional conduct, with the primary dri vers
being weak regulatory enforcement, intense market competition, and profit-oriented incentive structures for sales personnel.
Taken together, these findings suggest that codification alone is insufficient without parallel investment in ethics training, incentive-
structure reform, and consistent regulatory enforcement across states — a conclusion echoed across nearly all the Indian -focused
studies reviewed here. This aligns with the broader observation that pharmaceutical marketing must b alance commercial
competitiveness with its underlying public health mandate,  a tension that shapes nearly every strategic and operational decision
examined in this review.

E. Integrating Marketing and Management for Sustainable Outcomes
The literature increasingly frames marketing and supply chain management not as separate functions but as interdependent systems.
When promotional strategy and distribution logistics are  well integrated, companies are better positioned to serve patients reliably,
remain competitive, and meet compliance  requirements simultaneously,  whereas disjointed marketing and operations tend to
amplify problems such as stock-outs, counterfeit exposure, and reputational damage from unmet promotional claims.

Emerging opportunities — including AI-based demand forecasting, blockchain-enabled transparency in both promotion disclosure

IV. CONCLUSION
Pharmaceutical marketing and management sit at a genuinely unique crossroads of commerce and public health obligation. The
evidence reviewed here shows that traditional relationship-based promotion, especially through medical representatives, continues to
dominate prescribing influence even as digital an d AI -driven marketing rapidly expands, particularly in patient engagement and
data-driven targeting. On the operational side, India's pharmaceutical supply chain remains constrained b y fragmentation, API
import dependence, cold chain gaps, and counterfeit risk, though digital tools and stronger sourcing  diversification are gradually
addressing these weaknesses. Ethical and regulatory tightening, exemplified by the legally binding UCPMP 2024, signals a broader
industry shift toward accountability, though enf orcement consistency remains an open challenge. Future  research and practice
should prioritize the integration of ethical marketing conduct with resilient, technology-enabled supply chain management to ensure
that pharmaceutical companies can meet both commercial objectives and their fundamental responsibility to patient welfare.
