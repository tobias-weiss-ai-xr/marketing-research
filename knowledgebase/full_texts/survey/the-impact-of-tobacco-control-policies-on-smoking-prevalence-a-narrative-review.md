Abstract
Background While the tragedy’s scope is numerically enormous causing more than
8 million fatality annually, Tobacco use remains a leading cause of preventable death
worldwide. In response, the World Health Organization Framework Convention on
Tobacco Control (WHO FCTC) has galvanized global efforts to implement evidence-
based tobacco control policies. This review synthesizes recent evidence on the impact
of key FCTC-recommended policies, taxation and price increases, smoke-free laws,
warning labels, advertising bans, and cessation support, on smoking prevalence
from a global perspective. The $1.4 trillion annual economic burden is not merely a
byproduct of addition but as a result of deliberate industry efforts to exploit regulatory
weaknesses in emerging economies.
Methods A narrative review of scientific literature published between 2015 and
2024 was conducted, synthesizing evidence from 58 primary sources, drawing on
longitudinal studies, cross-country comparisons, and reports from international
organizations like the WHO and the World Bank. A narrative approach was selected
over a systematic review to allow for a broader qualitative synthesis of the complex
interplay for a broader qualitative synthesis of the complex interplay between legal
frameworks, regional sociopolitical factors and evolving industry tactics which strictly
quantitatively meta-analysis.
Results The evidence consistently demonstrates that comprehensive tobacco control
policies are effective in reducing smoking prevalence. MPOWER policy packages, when
implemented at a high level of achievement, are associated with significant declines
in smoking rates. However, the pace and comprehensiveness of policy adoption
vary significantly by region and country income level, leading to disparities in their
effectiveness. Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), which face intense tobacco
industry interference, often lag in implementation despite bearing a growing burden
of tobacco-related disease.
Conclusion The global fight against tobacco is yielding positive results, but progress
is uneven. Accelerating the implementation of the WHO FCTC’s MPOWER measures,
particularly in LMICs, is critical to further reducing global smoking prevalence and
achieving associated public health gains. Future challenges include addressing novel
tobacco products and combating persistent tobacco industry tactics, and overcoming
significant funding deficits and the lack of robust national tobacco control systems.

1 Introduction
One of the biggest and most persistent public health issues of our day is tobacco usage.
It is one of the main causes of the worldwide pandemic of non-communicable diseases
(NCDs), which includes diabetes, cancer, heart disease, and chronic respiratory condi -
tions. The tragedy’s scope is enormous: tobacco use causes more than 8 million fatali -
ties globally each year, including the roughly 1.3 million deaths of nonsmokers who are
exposed to secondhand smoke [ 1]. In addition to the significant human costs, tobacco
smoking has a startling financial impact on societies; it is estimated that healthcare costs
and lost productivity amount to US$1.4 trillion annually, or 1.8% of global gross domes -
tic product [ 2]. A strong, evidence-based, and internationally coordinated response is
required to this complex situation.
Over the past 50 years, there has been a significant change in the epidemiology of
tobacco use. The majority of the smoking epidemic in the 20th century occurred in high-
income countries (HICs), where prevalence rates peaked before steadily declining as
a result of the public health campaigns that followed the landmark 1964 U.S. Surgeon
General’s report [ 3]. However, the tobacco industry stepped up its efforts in low- and
middle-income countries (LMICs) as tobacco markets in HICs became saturated and
faced more regulation. As a result, the tobacco epidemic’s burden has transferred dis -
proportionately. Over 80% of the 1.3 billion tobacco smokers worldwide currently reside
in LMICs, where health services are ill-prepared to manage the ensuing wave of illness
and impairment [4].
….Being the first treaty negotiated under the WHO’s auspices, the World Health Orga-
nization Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (WHO FCTC) was the interna -
tional community’s most comprehensive response to this global threat at the time of
its adoption. Adopted by the World Health Assembly in 2003 and coming into effect
in 2005, the FCTC remains a paradigm shift in global health, it is now recognized as
a foundational platform that can be further strengthened through its own instruments
and the integration of “Tobacco Endgame” strategies, embodying a recognition that the
transnational nature of the tobacco epidemic, fueled by a globalised industry, requires
transnational solutions based on evidence and collective action [ 5].The treaty provides
countries with a legal framework to implement a comprehensive set of measures to
reduce the supply and demand for tobacco products.
In 2008, The MPOWER package was designed by the World Health Organization
(WHO) to support the implementation of the most effective tobacco control measures,
as outlined in the Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC), by all countries,
especially FCTC States Parties, globally. This allows WHO to reach countries that are
not FCTC States Parties [6]. This program is a useful road map that closely complies with
the FCTC’s main demand-reduction clauses. These are the six MPOWER measures:
  • Monitor tobacco use and prevention policies.
  • Protect people from tobacco smoke.
  • Offer help to quit tobacco use.
  • Warn about the dangers of tobacco.

Each MPOWER measure is grounded in a robust body of evidence demonstrating its
individual effectiveness. However, the greatest impact is observed when these policies
are implemented not in isolation, but as a comprehensive, mutually reinforcing pack -
age that denormalizes tobacco use, reduces its affordability, and supports population-
level behavioral change.Significant increases in tobacco taxes, for example, are generally
accepted as the most successful single intervention for lowering consumption, especially
among young people and those with low incomes [7]. In a similar vein, complete smoke-
free legislation helpsdenormalize smoking behavior, which in turn encourages stop
efforts, in addition to shielding nonsmokers from the negative effects of secondhand
smoke [8].
The synergistic effect of the MPOWER package’s simultaneous and complete adop -
tion, however, is where its real strength resides. Smoke-free legislation provides cir -
cumstances that encourage quitting, tax hikes provide a strong economic incentive
to maintain abstinence, and graphic health warnings raise the urge to quit while eas -
ily accessible cessation programs provide the means [ 9].While the efficacy of individual
tobacco control policies is well-documented in specific national or regional contexts,
there is a need for a synthesized, up-to-date analysis from a truly global perspective.
This paper aims to fill that gap by providing a comprehensive review of the impact of
FCTC/MPOWER policies on smoking prevalence worldwide. It will critically examine
the evidence base for each policy pillar, with a particular focus on literature from the
last decade. Furthermore, it will analyze the significant disparities in policy implementa -
tion and effectiveness across different WHO regions and country income levels. Finally,
the review will address contemporary challenges, including the rise of novel tobacco
and nicotine products and persistent tobacco industry interference, which threaten to
undermine global progress and by synthesizing recent evidence and highlighting both
successes and persistent challenges, this paper seeks to reinforce the critical importance
of accelerated and equitable implementation of the WHO FCTC as a cornerstone of
global public health strategy.
Between 2015 and 2024, the increase in ENDS and HTPs has challenged the WHO
FCTC framework. Although originally designed for combustible tobacco, its principles
are being adapted with varying degrees of success. The Digital Loophole in Advertis -
ing Bans (TAPS) often allows ENDS marketing to slip through, especially on social
media and through influencers. Digital marketing’s borderless nature bypasses laws and
exposes youth to lifestyle branding that traditional combustible tobacco can’t use. The
warning label issue involves adapting Article 11 to these new products amid regulatory
pushback. Countries require warnings about nicotine addiction, but there’s no interna -
tional consensus on graphic health warnings like those for cigarettes. The industry pro -
motes ‘harm reduction’ to support lighter warnings, but public health experts argue this
can create false perceptions of safety and hinder nicotine denormalization.
Applying Article 6 to ENDS challenges fiscal policy. Lower taxes aim to encourage
adult smokers to switch, but evidence shows low prices lead to youth initiation and dual
use instead of quitting. This shows a failure to adapt fiscally, weakening incentives to quit
all nicotine products. Many Latin American countries have banned ENDS, citing the
FCTC’s “precautionary principle’ that without long-term safety data and given harms,

2 Methods
A narrative review was conducted to synthesize evidence regarding the impact of
tobacco control policies on global smoking prevalence. This methodological approach
was selected to provide a comprehensive qualitative overview of the intersection
between health policy, international law and regional implementation challenges.
Search strategy and databases  A comprehensive search of electronic databases was
performed between January and March 2024. Databases include PubMed/MEDLINE,
Scopus, Science direct and Google Scholar. Additionally, grey literature was sourced
from official repositories of the World Health Organisation (WHO) and the World.
Search terms  The following keywords and Boolean operators were used (“Tobacco
Control, WHO FCTC, MPOWER) And (Smoking prevalence or Tobacco use trends).
Studies were included if they provided longitudinal data, cross-country comparison,
or systematic evaluations of at least one FCTC/MPOWER measure published between
2015 and 2024.
Priority was given to peer-reviewed articles and official international health reports.
Studies focusing solely on clinical drug trials without population-level prevalence data
were excluded. And the language was restricted to English-language publications.
2.1 The impact of key tobacco control policies
The efficacy of the WHO FCTC and its MPOWER package is not theoretical; it is estab -
lished in decades of scientific research and real-world implementation. Through a vari -
ety of interrelated mechanisms, the policies support quitting and denormalize tobacco
use while lowering the accessibility, affordability, and appeal of tobacco products. This
section offers a thorough, fact-based examination of each key policy domain’s effects.
2.2 Taxation and price increases (raising taxes)
Tobacco taxation is the cornerstone of tobacco control, consistently identified as the
most effective single measure for reducing consumption, particularly among price-sensi-
tive populations such as youth and low-income groups.
Mechanism of action  The law of demand is fundamental: as the price of a product
increases, consumption decreases. Tobacco taxes, specifically excise taxes, are passed on
to consumers as higher retail prices. This price increase has a dual effect: it motivates
current smokers to quit or reduce consumption, and it prevents potential new smokers
from initiating the habit. The price elasticity of demand for cigarettes, the percentage
change in consumption resulting from a 1% price increase, is typically between − 0.2 and
− 0.5 in high-income countries, meaning a 10% price increase leads to a 2–5% reduction
in consumption. This elasticity is often higher (around − 0.5 to -0.7) in LMICs, indicat -
ing an even greater impact [10].
Global evidence and impact  The evidence base is robust and spans the globe. A semi -
nal review by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) concluded that
tobacco tax increases are effective in all income settings [ 11]. More recent studies rein -
force this. An analysis of 36 European countries from 2004 to 2018 found that a 10%

increase in cigarette prices was associated with a 2.7% reduction in overall smoking
prevalence and a 3.4% reduction in heavy smoking prevalence [ 12]. The impact is par -

demonstrated that higher cigarette prices were strongly associated with lower smoking
prevalence among 15- and 16-year-olds [13].
Challenges and Industry tactics The effectiveness of taxation is frequently undermined
by the tobacco industry, which employs strategies to dampen the price signal. These
include:
Price smoothing Offering a wide range of price tiers, so when taxes increase on pre -
mium brands, the industry can maintain a market for cheaper alternatives, mitigating
the quit incentive for budget-conscious smokers.
Illicit trade The industry often exaggerates the threat of illicit trade to argue against tax
increases. While a genuine problem, robust track-and-trace systems and international
cooperation, such as the FCTC Protocol to Eliminate Illicit Trade, are the solution, not
forgoing tax policies [14].
Tax structure Specific excise taxes (a fixed amount per cigarette) are more effective
than ad-valorem taxes (a percentage of price) because they reduce price differentials
between brands, making it harder for consumers to down-trade to cheaper products
(Table 1).
2.3 Comprehensive smoke-free laws (protecting people)
Smoke-free laws that prohibit smoking in all indoor public places, workplaces, and pub -
lic transport are a critical public health intervention with immediate and long-term
benefits.
Mechanism of Action: These laws work on multiple levels. Primarily, they provide uni-
versal protection from the harmful effects of secondhand smoke, which contains over
7,000 chemicals, hundreds of which are toxic and about 70 that can cause cancer [ 9].
Secondly, they denormalize smoking by making it less visible and less socially accept -
able, thereby reducing its appeal to youth. Thirdly, they create environments that sup -
port quitting by eliminating smoking triggers in social and work settings, leading to an
increase in quit attempts and successful cessation.
Global Evidence and Impact: The health impact of smoke-free laws is rapid and dra -
matic. Studies from Scotland, Ireland, and the United States have documented signifi -
cant reductions in hospital admissions for acute coronary syndromes and childhood
asthma attacks within months of implementation [10]. A global meta-analysisconcluded
that the implementation of comprehensive smoke-free legislation was associated with a
significant acceleration in the decline of smoking prevalence, with a pooled estimated
reduction of 1.5 to 2.0% points more than in regions without such laws [11]. The laws are
also economically beneficial, with no negative impactor a positive impact on the hospi -
tality industry [12].

2.4 Health warnings and plain packaging (warning people)
Packaging is a primary marketing tool for the tobacco industry. Health warning policies
aim to turn this tool against the industry by using the pack to communicate risk.
Mechanism of Action: Large, pictorial health warnings (PHWs) are far more effective
than text-only warnings because they are more noticeable, evoke stronger emotional
responses (e.g., fear, disgust), and are better understood by populations with low literacy.
They increase knowledge about health risks, stimulate quit attempts, and can reduce the
appeal of tobacco products. Plain packaging (standardized packaging) standardizes the
appearance of tobacco packs by removing all brandinglogos, colors, and fontsmandating
a uniform drab color (e.g., olive green), and enlargingthe PHWs. This eliminates the pack
as a form of advertising, reduces its appeal, and makes the warnings more salient [13].
Global Evidence and Impact: The succession of countries implementing best-practice
PHWs (covering ≥ 50% of the pack) and plain packaging has created a powerful natu -
ral experiment. Australia, the first country to implement plain packaging in 2012, saw
increased calls to quitlines, a higher rate of pack concealment among smokers, and a
significant increase in the perceived effectiveness of health warnings following imple -
mentation [14]. A multi-country study (the ITC Project) found that the effectiveness of
warnings is “dose-dependent”: larger, graphic warnings are consistently associated with
higher levels of effectiveness across diverse cultural contexts [15].
2.5 Comprehensive bans on advertising, promotion, and sponsorship (enforcing bans)
Tobacco advertising, promotion, and sponsorship (TAPS) are designed to create a posi -
tive image of smoking, attract new users, and foster brand loyalty. Comprehensive bans
are essential to counter this marketing onslaught.
Mechanism of Action: A comprehensive TAPS ban removes tobacco marketing from
all media channels (TV, radio, print, internet), point-of-sale displays, and corporate
sponsorship of events (e.g., sports, concerts). This reduces the visibility and glamoriza -
tion of tobacco use, prevents the industry from targeting new generations, and rein -
forces the denormalization of the product.
Global Evidence and Impact: Evidence shows that comprehensive marketing bans can
reduce tobacco consumption. A systematic review found that comprehensive bans led
to an average reduction in tobacco consumption of approximately 7%, while partial bans
had little to no effect, as the industry simply reallocates resources to permitted chan -
nels [16]. The tobacco industry has rapidly shifted to digital and social media market -
ing, often using subtle tactics like influencers and sponsored content, which necessitates
constant updates to legislation to ensure bans remain truly comprehensive [17].
2.6 Population-wide cessation support (offering help)
While policies like taxes and smoke-free laws create a population-level impetus to quit,
access to cessation support is critical to converting quit attempts into long-term success.
Mechanism of Action: Cessation support encompasses a range of interventions, from
brief advice from a healthcare provider to intensive behavioral counseling and cost-
covered pharmacotherapy (e.g., nicotine replacement therapy, varenicline, bupropion).
When these services are easily accessible, widely promoted, and affordable or free, they
significantly increase the likelihood of a smoker successfully quitting.

Global Evidence and Impact: Population-level studies confirm the effectiveness of
integrating cessation support into tobacco control programs. The 2019 WHO Report on
the Global Tobacco Epidemic highlighted that of an estimated 1.1 billion smokers world-
wide who want to quit, only 30% have access to tools that can help them [ 18]. A longitu-
dinal study of 18 countries found that implementing comprehensive cessation support
policiessuch as national quitlines and full cost coverage of treatmentwas associated with
a 2.5 to 4.0% point increase in the rate of successful quit attempts at a population level
[19]. The MPOWER measure “Offer help” remains one of the least implemented policies
globally, representing a major missed opportunity for public health gain.
3 Global and regional variations in policy impact
The theoretical effectiveness of tobacco control policies, as established in Sect.  2, is not
uniformly translated into real-world outcomes across the globe. The impact of these pol-
icies is profoundly shaped by a complex interplay of economic development, political
will, governance capacity, and the relentless counter-efforts of the tobacco industry. This
results in a highly heterogeneous landscape of policy implementation and, consequently,
smoking prevalence trends. Analyzing these variations by WHO region and country
income level reveals critical disparities that define the current frontlines of the global
tobacco epidemic.
3.1 The disparity between high-income and low- and middle-income countries
The most significant divide in tobacco control progress is between High-Income Coun -
tries (HICs) and Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs). HICs, having faced the
devastating health consequences of tobacco for longer, have generally been earlier and
more comprehensive adopters of MPOWER policies. In contrast, many LMICs are in
the early or intermediate stages of the tobacco epidemic and face systemic barriers to
implementing strong controls.
The implementation gap:  The WHO’s MPOWER reports consistently show a strong
positive correlation between a country’s income level and the likelihood of having at
least one MPOWER measure at the highest level of achievement. HICs are more likely
to have comprehensive smoke-free laws, large pictorial warnings, and strong tax poli -
cies. LMICs, particularly low-income countries, are disproportionately represented
among those with no MPOWER measures at the best-practice level [ 13]. This gap is the
primary driver of the shifting burden of tobacco-related disease.
Tobacco industry interference: The tobacco industry has identified LMICs as its growth
market. Consequently, it deploys aggressive tactics in these regions to delay, dilute, and
defeat public health legislation. These tactics include direct lobbying of governments,
funding front groups to oppose tax increases, litigating against health regulations, and
promoting corporate social responsibility activities to gain political influence [ 20]. The
political and economic fragility of many LMICs makes them particularly vulnerable to
this interference.
Capacity and resource constraints:  LMICs often lack the institutional capacity, fund -
ing, and technical expertise required to establish robust monitoring systems, enforce
advertising bans, or manage complex tax administrations. Competing health priorities,
such as infectious disease control, can also divert attention and resources away from

regional and global statistics, while the challenges in China (WPRO) and Indonesia
(SEARO) present massive obstacles to global tobacco control targets. Success in these
countries is arguably the single most important factor for future global progress.

4 Discussion and future challenges
The evidence presented in this review leaves little room for doubt: the implementation of
the WHO FCTC and the MPOWER package has been a transformative force. The cor -
relation between strong, comprehensive tobacco control policies and declining smoking
prevalence is robust, consistent across diverse geographical and cultural contexts, and
supported by a formidable body of scientific literature. The global perspective, however,
reveals that this success story is incomplete and precarious. Progress is not a guaran -
teed linear trajectory; it is a hard-won achievement that faces persistent and evolving
challenges. This discussion synthesizes the key findings and outlines the critical frontiers
that will define the next phase of the global fight against tobacco.
4.1 Synthesis of key findings: the power of synergy and the peril of inequity
The most compelling conclusion from the global data is the synergistic effect of inte -
grated policies. While individual measures like taxation are powerful, their impact is
magnified when combined with smoke-free laws, graphic warnings, advertising bans,
and cessation support [ 24]. For instance, a tax increase creates a powerful economic
incentive to quit; when coupled with easily accessible cessation services (Offer help) and
environments free of tobacco smoke (Protect people), the likelihood of successful, long-
term cessation increases substantially. Similarly, graphic health warnings (Warn people)
are more effective when not counteracted by pervasive tobacco advertising (Enforce
bans). This synergy underscores the importance of the MPOWER package as a cohesive
strategy rather than a menu of disjointed options.
However, the second overarching finding is the profound inequity in policy impact.
The dramatic decline in smoking prevalence in the Americas and parts of Europe stands
in stark contrast to the slow progress in Africa, the Eastern Mediterranean, and East -
ern Europe. This disparity is not due to a difference in policy effectiveness but rather a
difference in implementation. The barriers in lagging regionsincluding intense tobacco
industry interference, limited financial and technical capacity, and competing health pri-
oritieshave created a world where the burden of tobacco-related disease is increasingly
concentrated in the most vulnerable populations. This constitutes a major global health
injustice.

4.2.1 Combating tobacco industry interference
The tobacco industry remains the most significant obstacle to progress. Its tactics are
adaptive and relentless. Beyond traditional lobbying and litigation, the industry now
employs sophisticated strategies, including Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) fund -
ing of social programs to build political goodwill and present itself as a partner in devel -
opment, particularly in LMICs [ 25].Front Groups and Third-Party Lobbying, Creating
and funding astroturf organizations to oppose public health policies while hiding indus -
try involvement.Exploiting International Trade and Investment Agreements, using these
agreements to challenge tobacco control laws as barriers to trade [ 20].the implementa-
tion of the WHO FCTC and the MPOWER package has been a transformative force,
which mandates that parties protect their public health policies from commercial and
other vested interests of the tobacco industry, is paramount. This requires transparency
in interactions with the industry, rejecting partnerships, and preventing conflicts of
interest.
4.2.2 Regulating novel tobacco and nicotine products
The rapid emergence of e-cigarettes, heated tobacco products (HTPs), and nicotine
pouches presents a formidable regulatory dilemma. The public health community is
divided between two approaches: Proponents argue that these products, while not risk-
free, are significantly less harmful than combustible cigarettes and should be promoted
as alternatives for adult smokers who cannot or will not quit.Advocates highlight the risk
of renormalising smoking behaviour, creating dual use (instead of complete switching),
and hooking a new generation of youth on nicotine.Recent systematic reviews and popu-
lation-based studies indicate that these products cause significant harm, contrary to the
industry’s claims of harm reduction [ 26]. A middle path is emerging, focusing on strict
regulation or prohibition to maximize public health protections. While some advocate
harm reduction, the decisions and guidance from the Conference of the Parties (COP)
to the WHO FCTC emphasise a more cautious approach to minimize population-level
harms. This includes:
1. Banning all flavors that appeal to youth.
2. Enforcing strict marketing and advertising bans.
3. Implementing nicotine concentration limits and safety standards.
4. Applying excise taxes to prevent cheap access.
The goal must be to prevent a new epidemic of nicotine addiction, not to facilitate the
parallel commercial interests of the tobacco industry.
4.2.3 Addressing the illicit tobacco trade
The illicit trade in tobacco products undermines tax policies, reduces government rev -
enue, and makes cheap cigarettes readily available. The industry often exaggerates the
scale of illicit trade to argue against tax increases.The primary tool to combat this is
the FCTC Protocol to Eliminate Illicit Trade in Tobacco Products, which requires the
establishment of a global track-and-trace system and supply chain control. Ratifying and
implementing this protocol is essential to secure tax policies and deprive the industry of
one of its main arguments against fiscal measures [13].

4.2.4 Bridging the implementation gap in LMICs
The concentration of the tobacco epidemic in LMICs is the single greatest challenge.
Closing the implementation gap requires a concerted global effort focused on, sup -
porting LMICs in securing domestic funding for tobacco control programs and explor -
ing innovative international financing mechanisms, strengthening technical expertise
in areas like tax administration, law enforcement, and surveillance. Facilitating the
exchange of knowledge and best practices between countries that have successfully
implemented policies and those that are beginning their journey.
5 Conclusion
This review confirms that tobacco control policies, like those in the WHO FCTC and
MPOWER, effectively reduce smoking by making tobacco less affordable, less appealing,
harder to access, and more stigmatized, leading to public health improvements. While
regions like the Americas and Europe have made significant progress, many low- and
middle-income countries lag behind due to industry interference, resource limits, and
other challenges. The future depends on accelerating efforts in these areas. Emerging
tobacco and nicotine products, illicit trade, and industry tactics pose new threats that
require strict, evidence-based regulation and global cooperation, especially around
FCTC Article 5.3. The tools and knowledge exist to end the tobacco epidemic; success
now depends on political will, solidarity, and equitable action worldwide. Addressing
these issues can lead to a sustained decline in smoking and a healthier, tobacco-free
future.
