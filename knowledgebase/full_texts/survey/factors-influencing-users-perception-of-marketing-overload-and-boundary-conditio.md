Abstract. This study investigates how marketing intensity, ad intrusiveness, and linguistic
pressure shape consumers' perception of marketing overload on the Douyin platform and
identifies the boundary conditions under which an attitude-behavior split occurs. In other
words, it is when consumers dislike a brand's marketing yet continue to purchase its
products. Data were drawn from a main survey of 91 active Douyin users aged 18-30, a
pilot study of 19 respondents, and 320 valid user comments collected from a Douyin
advertisement video through web crawling. The study finds that marketing density is the
strongest predictor of overload perception (r = 0.52), followed by ad intrusiveness (r = 0.44)
and linguistic pressure (r = 0.38), by using SPSS for descriptive statistics, Pearson
correlations, t-tests, one-way ANOVA, and factor analysis, along with sentiment and
thematic analysis of the social media comments. The results show that although perceived
overload does not significantly reduce purchase intention overall, three boundary conditions
emerge that weaken the effect: urgent need (r = 0.53), product uniqueness (r = 0.43), and
brand trust (r = 0.41). Analysis of Douyin comments corroborates these survey findings,
showing that consumers frequently continue purchasing despite disliking excessive
advertising when products are trusted or needed urgently. These findings extend advertising
avoidance and psychological reactance theories by clarifying when negative attitudes
toward marketing overload fail to translate into avoidance behavior, offering practical
guidance for moderating marketing frequency on digital platforms.
Keywords: Douyin platform, marketing overload perception, attitude‑behavior split,
boundary conditions, purchase intention
1. Introduction
Brand promotion through the Douyin platform frequently adopts strategies such as high-frequency
repetition, forced irrelevant placement, and sensationalism. Such strategies contribute to the feeling
of "marketing overload," meaning that the users become subject to an adverse emotional and
cognitive experience triggered by information exceeding their psychological capacity [1]. Such
excessive marketing creates the effect of negative externalities and consumer fatigue [2]. According
to the principle of advertising avoidance, when consumers perceive an obstacle to their goal

achievement, they tend to avoid advertisements [3]. Similarly, according to psychological reactance
theory, consumers resist ads due to excessive persuasion, which leads them to skip or block the
advertisements [4].
While advertising avoidance theory supposes that consumers will disengage from excessive
marketing, during the research process, many users, although not appreciating such marketing, still
buy these products. The attitude-behavior split in this regard contradicts conventional theories.
Therefore, the current research poses two related questions in succession. First, how do marketing
density, ad intrusiveness, and linguistic pressure affect users' perceptions regarding marketing
overload? Second, under what boundary conditions will marketing overload not have an impact on
the reduction of purchase intention? This paper attempts to provide empirical findings through
questionnaires and social media analysis to address the above research questions.
2. Methods
2.1. Study design and sample
In Study One, data collection took place between April 9 and 10, 2026, via the WJX platform. The
participants were active Douyin users aged between 18 and 30 years, using the platform for at least
five days each week. A total of 111 respondents were gathered, of which those aged 18-25 (n = 79)
and 26-30 (n = 12) did not differ significantly on market overload through a test for an independent
samples t-test (p=0.13). Thus, the 18-25 and 26-30 groups were combined to make up the 18-30
group consisting of 91 participants, and the rest were excluded because they belonged to the 31+
category. The Study One survey contained 18 questions on demographics, usage behavior,
marketing density perceptions (Q6, 1-5), intrusive ads (Q9, 1-5), linguistic pressure (Q12, 1-5), total
feeling of being overloaded (Q15, 1-7), purchase intention (Q16, decreased/unchanged/increased),
and purchase behavior. For purchase behavior, participants were asked to select the statement that
best described their usual response to overload marketing: "The more I dislike marketing, the less I
buy." (avoidance), "I still buy occasionally, e.g., due to discounts or actual need." (occasional buy),
or "I will buy it if the product is good enough." (product quality).
Data collection for the pilot study occurred on May 17, 2026, where 19 responses were recorded.
The participants were 94.74% women and 89.47% ages 18-25. Given the small sample size and low
reliability, the study is used solely for exploring trends and hypotheses. The survey included
questions about the frequency of being overloaded, brand trust, kind of product, price level, urgency,
substitutability, discounting, price sensitivity, and the dependent variable, "probability of buying
even if overloaded," which was rated on a 1-5 scale. The overload marketing strategy was compared
to regular marketing techniques.
This research also applies a crawler to obtain the emotional trends of the Douyin users. Social
media comment data was obtained from an advertisement video posted on Douyin by GrainRain
Radiance Repair Essence Water, where it had very visible screen-filling marketing practices. The
initial number of comments collected through the Houyi web crawler is 500, while only 320 remain
after removing duplicate, irrelevant, and blank comments.

3. Results
3.1. Study one main sample
In the case of 91 participants who ranged from ages 18 to 30 years old, 82.14% were females, and
82.14% were students. Usage patterns revealed that 72.32% used Douyin 5-7 days weekly, 50%
spent 30 minutes to 2 hours using Douyin each day, while 27.68% were using the app for over 2
hours a day. The mean value of Q6 (annoyed by three times per day) is 4.09 (SD = 1.02). The mean
value of Q9 (interrupted by unrelated ad) is 4.41 (SD = 0.85). The mean of Q12 (urging phrases
make me not want to order) was 3.47 (SD = 1.18). The mean of Q15 (overload rating) was 6.01 (SD
= 1.08), with 68.75% of participants scoring 6 or 7, corresponding to high or very high perceived
overload. Whereas purchase intention change was distributed as: "decrease" in 21.6%, "no change"
in 60.4%, and "increase" in 18.0%.
Factor analysis revealed that Q12 had a low communality of 0.172, indicating it does not align
well with the other items. Pearson correlations showed that marketing density was positively
correlated with overload rating (r = 0.52, p < 0.001). Ad intrusiveness also correlated positively (r =
0.44, p < 0.001). Correlation with linguistic pressure was positive (r = 0.38, p < 0.001). Correlation
between overload rating and purchase intention change was r = –0.18 (p = 0.06) and statistically
insignificant.
As per the buying pattern, there were three categories. The basic avoidance category (the more I
dislike, the less I buy) was 21.43%. The occasional buy category (still buy occasionally, for
example, due to discounts or actual requirement) was 60.71%. The group of those who disliked the
product but whose purchases were not affected because it was good enough was 16.96%. There was
a significant difference between the three groups in terms of overload ratings, using one-way
ANOVA F(2,88) = 4.12, p = 0.02. least significant difference (LSD) post-hoc analysis demonstrated
that the avoidance group had a significantly higher overload score (M = 6.33) when compared to the
occasional buyer group (M = 5.88, p = 0.01), whereas there was no significant difference with
regard to the product-good-enough group (M = 6.11, p = 0.38). Regarding impulsive shopping that
has been influenced by screen filling marketing (multiple choices allowed), 46.43% have buyer's
remorse, 31.25% were satisfied, 33.93% have not purchased anything, while 15.18% cancelled their
purchases.

47.37% occasionally encountered it. The outcome variable was the likelihood of still buying when a
brand's marketing is perceived as overload (1‑5). Pearson correlations with potential boundary

3.3. Social media comment validation
Among the 320 valid comments on the GrainRain Radiance Repair Essence Water video, 22.5%
were positive, 48.8% were negative, and 28.7% were neutral or mixed. In the negative remarks, the
most common problems cited include too much advertisement or advertising at frequent intervals
(43.6%), misleading or overstated claims (26.9%), allergies/skin irritation (19.9%), and
price/volume-related complaints (9.6%). This distribution corresponds to the questionnaire results
from Study One: correlation coefficient r=0.52 for marketing frequency, which suggests that
marketing density is a major influential factor in market overload. Notably, 34.0% of the negative
comments (16.6% of all comments) expressed ambivalence because even if they were annoyed by
too many advertisements, they ended up buying the product anyway. Common responses were:
"Lots of GrainRain ads, but it works"; "Another GrainRain… but it is a good one"; and "Sick of
seeing this one around, but I trust its formulation and therefore buy." These responses highlight how
product effectiveness and brand trust can drive purchasing habits regardless of ad fatigue. This
figure is smaller compared to 60.4% of occasional purchasers in the questionnaire, but the direction
is in agreement, indicating the presence of an attitude-behavior split in actual usage situations.
4. Discussions
4.1. Core factors influencing marketing overload perception
The highest correlation occurred between marketing density and overload perception (r = 0.52), thus
confirming that the mere exposure effect is conditional in its nature. It was found that a mere
exposure to a stimulus leads to an increased level of liking [5], however, subsequent research
showed that the positive effects of mere exposure diminish under negative conditions of exposure
[6], which on Douyin may result from ads being viewed as disturbing the viewing experience or
being unrelated to it. The advertising avoidance theory suggests that the perception of goal impeding
is the most significant predictor for advertising avoidance [7]. This conclusion correlates highly with
ours. Regarding ad intrusiveness, the correlation was at r = 0.44, which represented the users'
sensitivity towards being interrupted while watching. For linguistic pressure, the correlation was
lower at 0.38, and the communality for the urging-phrases scale was extremely low. This indicates
large individual variations in response to expressions such as "limited time." The marketing
language of Douyin also employs rhetorical questions and exclamations to create an aura of urgency
[8].
4.2. Boundary conditions of the attitude‑behavior split
There was no significant correlation between the perception of overload and the changes in the
purchase intention (r = –0.18, p = 0.06), with 60.4% of consumers continuing to make purchases
occasionally. Analysis of the pilot study provides the indications required for replication. Need for

urgency was highly correlated with purchase intention (r= 0.53). With time pressure involved, the
decision process becomes one that entails reasoning rather than emotional judgment, disregarding
negative emotions, as explained in dual systems theory. Uniqueness of product (r = 0.43) decreases
the accessibility of other options, leading to increased tolerance among consumers towards overload;
this could possibly be partially attributed to the psychological effort associated with the search
process when there is nothing available as an alternative option. Brand trust (r = 0.41, marginally
significant) also acts as a potential condition against the negative effects of overload. Also,
according to relationship marketing theory, trust diminishes uncertainty and fosters the
establishment of relationships [9]. That is, previous trust in the brand may lead to a different
understanding of the high frequency of the advertisements. Instead of seeing the ads as being too
much, the trusted brand loyalists will see them as a manifestation of the brand's belief in their
products, hence minimizing any negative impact on advertising overload.
In other words, where there is high substitutability, a lack of urgency, and low trust, overload
causes avoidance, as evidenced by 21.43% of the respondents who selected "the more I dislike, the
less I buy." The Information Overload Theory asserts that where information input exceeds
information-processing capacity, an individual faces cognitive strain and avoidance [10].
However, special needs can momentarily trump the above pattern for consumers. It is uncertain
whether this can be done on a consistent basis; post-purchase assessment through regrets (46.43%)
or satisfaction (31.25%) may have a role to play in determining brand attitudes in the future.
4.3. Practical implications and limitations
This research makes three primary contributions. First, it empirically classifies the construct of
marketing overload on Douyin platforms into three distinct perceptual aspects: density,
intrusiveness, and linguistic pressure. And demonstrates their differential associations with overload
perception. Second, it claims an attitude-behavior split in the context of digital marketing overload,
which challenges the straightforward predictions of advertising avoidance and psychological
reactance theories. Third, it identifies boundary conditions: urgent need, product uniqueness, and
brand trust, which help to explain when and why consumers override their negative attitudes and
continue to purchase.
Practically, these findings suggest that single-brand exposure must be minimized by allowing no
more than two such advertisements in one day and once every hour. The use of soft placements of
ads must be ensured, and any sort of sudden logo insertion must be avoided. Use "Limited time
offer" instead of "Today only." Moderate intensity of marketing is acceptable for necessities, unique,
valuable, or trustworthy. In contrast, strict control over the level of intensity is needed when
marketing products that are highly substitutable or lack strong trust. Notably, price level was not
considered as a boundary condition in this study.
Also, it should not be ignored that this research is limited by a number of factors. First, the
sample was mostly represented by young women, and thus, generalization of the results is unlikely
since women are more sensitive to excessive marketing. Second, the boundary conditions analysis
was based on an extremely small sample (n = 19) and low reliability, making the findings
speculative only. Third, the social media comments were drawn from only one brand's video, which
may not be indicative of all categories of products.

5. Conclusions
Marketing density, intrusiveness of advertisements, and linguistic pressure all have significant
influences on the perception of marketing overload in Douyin. Among all three constructs,
Marketing Density is found to have the most substantial influence, having a correlation of 0.52.
Overload perception does not have a significant correlation with Purchase Intention Change.
Nevertheless, an overwhelming majority of people (60.4%) continue making their purchases despite
the excessiveness of the marketing process. The boundary conditions that help reduce the negative
influence of overload are urgent need, product uniqueness, and high brand trust. In contrast, where
the product is highly substitutable, non-urgent, and lacking trust, dislike would likely lead to
disengagement and user churn.
Therefore, in order to improve the effectiveness of enterprise marketing, brands need to
concentrate more on frequency moderation and content relevance rather than relying solely on
marketing intensity, as the effect is often counterproductive.
