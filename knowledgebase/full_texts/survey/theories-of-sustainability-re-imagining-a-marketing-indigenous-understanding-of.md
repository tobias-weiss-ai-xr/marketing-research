Abstract
Sustainability demands system-level transformation, yet marketing theory remains largely grounded in firm-centric, trans -
actional, and equilibrium-based assumptions that are ill-suited to address planetary-scale challenges. This article responds
to the need for more conceptually ambitious marketing scholarship capable of grappling with sustainability as a dynamic,
contested, and future-oriented phenomenon. We identify key conceptual and methodological barriers that have constrained
sustainability theorizing in marketing and examine how foundational perspectives within the discipline can be re-imagined
and extended to meet these demands. Through a review of conceptual contributions in AMS Review, we trace how sus -
tainability-related thinking has evolved within the field and assess the potential of three marketing-indigenous theories—
Service-Dominant Logic, Resource-Advantage Theory, and Market Shaping—to inform the development of sustainability
theory. Building on this foundation, we propose a future research agenda structured around four interlinked themes: (1)
advancing system-level theorizing, (2) reimagining stakeholder responsibility to include nature and future generations,
(3) conceptualizing sustainability as a dynamic resource domain, and (4) enabling prospective theorizing to support the
shaping of more sustainable market futures.

Challenges in developing theories of
sustainability
Developing theory for grand challenges, such as sustain -
ability, requires addressing intricate conceptual and meth -
odological obstacles extending beyond the typical scope of
single-discipline research (McDonagh & Prothero, 2014;
White et al., 2019). As sustainability cuts across social, eco-
logical, and economic domains, the theoretical approaches
to understanding and advancing it must incorporate a wide
array of perspectives, blurring definitions, and disciplinary
insights (Dahlmann, 2024). While factors like complexity,
stakeholder heterogeneity, and institutional rigidities shape
the content of emerging theories, scholars face significant
difficulties in constructing robust theoretical frameworks
for multifaceted phenomena like sustainability.
One of the most fundamental difficulties in theories
on sustainability is the term’s conceptual ambiguity and
multiple definitions (Baden & Harwood, 2013). Differ -
ent sectors, disciplines, and stakeholder groups interpret
“sustainability” differently—from resource efficiency and
ecological stability to fair social systems (Kotler, 2011).
This plurality of definitions, while reflecting the concept’s
broad relevance, can delay the creation of unified theoretical
frameworks. Scholars must cope with reconciling and map-
ping divergent interpretations to avoid fragmentation and
ensure coherence in sustainability research. Each discipline
brings its own paradigms, methods, and evaluative crite -
ria, making alignment inherently difficult. Interdisciplinary
research often requires the creation of shared languages and
epistemological bridges—a slow and sometimes conflict-
ridden process. The complexity is compounded by the inter-
connected nature of sustainability issues themselves, which
span climate change, biodiversity loss, pollution, water
scarcity, resource depletion, social inequality, labor condi -
tions, food security, and economic resilience. Theorizing
sustainability thus demands conceptual frameworks capable
of capturing interdependencies, feedback loops, and cross-
scale dynamics.
Further complicating theory development is the dynamic
and multilevel nature of sustainability (Keller et al., 2022).
It unfolds across micro, meso, and macro levels.
For instance, the growing consumer demand for sustain -
able products impacts the organization of supply chains
responsible for delivering these goods. This, in turn, can
prompt the emergence of new supply chain structures,
potentially necessitating changes to the laws and regula -
tions governing integrated supply chain actors—changes
that may challenge existing antitrust laws. The layered and
nested nature of complex business environments adds to
this challenge, as illustrated by the interplay between sus -
tainable products, supply chains, and legal systems (Möller
et al., 2020). These environments are also characterized
by multimodality, intricately intertwined with social, eco -
nomic, political, and legal dimensions. Furthermore, change
is a constant factor, as actors’ behavior influences each ana-
lytical level of the system, frequently entering and exiting,
gaining or losing influence over time.
Addressing the complexity of sustainability requires
tackling dynamics and nonlinearity, particularly the feed -
back loops, emergent properties, and adaptive processes
that define this domain. Traditional static or linear mod -
els often fail to capture the fluid and transitional nature
of grand challenges like sustainability. To overcome these
limitations, researchers must adopt novel ontological and
epistemological perspectives and advanced methodologi -
cal approaches that accommodate such dynamism (Polese
et al., 2021; Vargo et al., 2023). This attempt demands

Actors involved in sustainability theory-building often
exhibit heterogeneous and sometimes conflicting quali -
ties. For instance, a company producing electric vehicles to
promote sustainability for consumers and society—such as
Tesla—may simultaneously face criticism for greenhouse
gas emissions or river pollution resulting from its manu -
facturing processes (Pulliam et al., 2024). Similarly, public
actors can exhibit contradictory approaches to sustainabil -
ity. One government agency may facilitate sustainable busi-
ness practices by providing subsidies for renewable energy
deployment, while another imposes constraints through reg-
ulations and reporting requirements. Consumers also play
varied roles as agents of sustainability as they may actively
engage in collaborating initiatives (Arora, 2024), recycling
(Claudy & Peterson, 2022), or supporting sustainable busi -
nesses (Peterson et al., 2021, 2023). Instead, researchers
have described certain consumers as vulnerable, with lim -
ited capacity—or inclination—to participate actively along-
side other actors (Hill & Sharma, 2020; Gallan & Helkkula,
2022). Similarly, companies exhibit diverse stances toward
sustainability goals. While some act as proactive agents
advancing sustainability objectives (Sheth & Parvatiyar,
2021), others may remain passive or even resist actions to
achieve these goals (Gaski, 2023).
Sustainability often involves ethical considerations and
collective priorities, creating tension among actors and rec-
ognizing that pursuits like ecological integrity and social
justice are rooted in explicit value judgments. This tension
necessitates reflexivity, prompting scholars to critically
examine how their normative orientations shape theoretical
constructs and research questions (Bulten et al., 2021; Susur
& Karakaya, 2021). By articulating their values and assess-
ing how these influence their frameworks, researchers can
strive for transparency and rigor. Balancing these norma -
tive dimensions remains an ongoing negotiation, especially
given the potential for theory-building and derivative policy
recommendations to significantly affect societal outcomes
(Shepherd & Suddaby, 2017). Besides, researchers must
also reconcile the tension between the need for contextual
depth and the goal of generalizability (Levitt, 2021). Sus-
tainability initiatives unfold within distinct local contexts,
shaped by historically embedded social norms, ecological
realities, and political structures. While local specificities
provide valuable insights, they can limit the applicability of
theories across different regions or research communities.
Bridging the scholarly–practical divide highlights the
urgency of sustainability issues (Bansal et al., 2012). Poli-
cymakers, community leaders, and practitioners demand
timely, actionable insights, whereas the process of rigorous
theory-building often progresses at a more deliberate pace.
Aligning these divergent rhythms and objectives requires
collaborative research designs, the cocreation of knowl -
edge, and sustained actor engagement to ensure that theoret-
ical advances are credible and responsive to societal needs.
When academic rigor is paired with practical relevance,
theoretical insights can be transformed into impactful strat-
egies that address pressing sustainability challenges.
Temporality plays a critical role in developing theories on
sustainability (George & Jones, 2000) because it introduces
a set of interrelated complexities that undermine straight -
forward causal explanations and predictions. A key question
is: at what pace do actors work toward sustainability, and
with what intensity do their efforts persist over time? This
issue is particularly salient in sustainability, where the ten -
sion between short-term and long-term perspectives often
comes to the forefront. Some actors may adopt a focused
and sustained approach to achieving sustainability, while
others may exhibit less consistent efforts, with priorities
shifting rapidly over time. For example, five years after
the 2015 Paris Agreement, researchers observed that while
targets for achieving Net Zero—defined as balancing CO 2
emissions with CO 2 removal by 2050—had become wide -
spread globally, robust operationalization of these targets
accounted for only 5% of the global economy (Hale et al.,
2022). In this context, the 2050 Net Zero target provides a
critical temporal benchmark for global sustainability efforts,
particularly in the drive to limit climate change to a 1.5 °C
increase (Fankhouser et al., 2022).

AMS review’s conceptual contributions to
sustainability
AMS Review  has a rich history of publishing research on
developing conceptual frameworks and innovative theories
(Vargo, 2019). Because of its focus on conceptual articles,
AMS Review has become a key outlet for disciplinary dia -
logue about marketing’s value proposition (Vargo, 2020)
and, importantly, marketing’s contributions to global chal -
lenges, such as sustainability (Fehrer, 2020). Given this
positioning, it is both timely and relevant to examine how
sustainability has been conceptualized within the journal
and what theoretical groundwork has been established. In
the following section, we present a focused review of AMS
Review articles that address sustainability and sustainable
marketing, with the aim of identifying key contributions,
conceptual trends, and opportunities for future theorizing.
Lunde ( 2018) offered a systematic review of sustain -
ability in marketing from 1997 to 2016. This review of 228
articles identified a lack of conceptual and theoretical clar -
ity about sustainability. Lunde proposed that the fragmented
nature of marketing scholarship led to numerous definitions
of sustainability and its relationship with other variables.
Others have cited this fragmentation as an impediment to
the advancement of marketing theory (Hunt et al., 2022).
In order to unify marketing scholars’ efforts in sustainabil -
ity, Lunde (2018) offered a revised definition of sustainable
marketing as promoting offerings that lower harm to the
environment while ethically increasing the quality of life
(QOL) of consumers and other global stakeholders now, as
well as for future generations. Further, in response to the
call for TBL innovation (Elkington, 2018), Lunde offered a
five-dimensional GREEN framework for sustainable mar -
keting. This GREEN framework is grounded upon the 13
theoretical lenses Lunde identified from the 228 articles
reviewed, as well as the numerous definitions of sustainabil-
ity Lunde found (categorized as environmentally-focused or
holistically-focused).

In 2019, the Journal of Marketing published a conceptual
article addressing this question of how to move consumers
to adopt sustainable consumption (White et al., 2019). Here,
White et al. reviewed and categorized the behavioral science
literature, and uncovered five broad psychological routes to
encouraging sustainable consumer behavior change: Social
influence, Habit formation, the Individual self, Feelings and
cognition, and Tangibility. These five comprised the SHIFT
framework for “shifting” consumers to more sustainable
consumption. This article adopted a firm focus for pursu -
ing sustainability as the SHIFT dimensions would serve as
a guideline for firms intending to influence consumers more
favorably to sustainability initiatives of theirs.
Chandy et al. ( 2021) introduced “Better Marketing for
a Better World” in the Journal of Marketing’ s special issue
on this BMBW topic. Notably, this overview article treats
sustainability as just one of the challenges which marketers
could address, such as “sustainability and climate concerns,
economic and social empowerment, health and well-being”
(Chandy et al., p. 6). In short, BMBW (like the Kotler and
SHIFT efforts) narrows sustainability in focus to the natu -
ral environment, rather than the three dimensions of people,
planet and profit that comprise more widely accepted views
of sustainability.
The Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science  has
published more conceptual articles related to sustainability
this century than the Journal of Marketing. JAMS published
a landmark special issue on sustainability in February 2011
edited by G. Tomas M. Hult that included 11 articles—seven
of which were conceptual articles that included a tripartite
view of sustainability featuring people, planet and profit.
The overview editorial for the special issue (Hult, 2011)
proposed market-focused sustainability, which is important
in making a distinction for sustainability efforts that include
firms (rather than excluding them as enemies of sustainabil-
ity which non-business fields tended to do). This overview
article placed customers in the middle of firms’ marketing
strategy to pursue market-focused sustainability. Two other
articles in the special issue developed a focus on consump -
tion in sustainability (Sheth et al., 2011; Huang & Rust,
2011).

What should be the primary focus for firms?
AMS Review  authors have considered multiple aspects of
this question. Three viewpoints emerge proposing either (1)
the primacy of profits, (2) the primacy of the common good,
or (3) the primacy of stakeholders and marketing strategy.
In general, opposing views differ on (1) who is responsible
for remediating environmental degradation, (2) marketing
as hero, villain, or redeemed villain, and (3) an emphasis on
short-term value creation versus long-term value creation.

As Ferrell (2022) notes, Peter Drucker asserted that all firms
and institutions have two responsibilities—performance and
the community. In this way, Drucker takes a more “middle
of the road” perspective as compared to those taking either
the primacy of profits view or the primacy of the common
good view. In Hult et al.’s ( 2011) depiction of stakeholder
marketing, there are only stakeholders who are human and
are currently alive. Notably, the natural environment and
future generations are not included as in other conceptual -
izations of primary and secondary stakeholders (Peterson,
2021). In this way, a firm’s market orientation takes cen -
ter stage in market-focused sustainability (Hult & Ketchen,
2017), as sustainability efforts can be explained as part of a
firm’s marketing strategy to satisfy customers. Hult terms
this “marketing orientation plus!.” Here, an organization
would achieve market-based sustainability to the extent it
aligns itself with customer preferences and the interests of
multiple stakeholders concerned about social responsibil -
ity issues involving economic, environmental, and social
dimensions.
Later, Hult and Morgeson (2020) assert that marketing’s
value propositions center on satisfying customers with the
required efforts to effectively handle customer complaints
while maintaining loyal customers. These authors propose
the “satisfaction-exit-voice-loyalty (SEVL) theory to pro -
vide more about how market processes of dyadic exchange
between firms and consumers explain support or lack of
support for the sustainability efforts of firms. In line with
Hult’s thinking about market-focused sustainability, Tajdini
and Ramirez (2019) propose that firm authenticity is impor-
tant in winning customers in markets. Here, signaling theory
would explain a firm’s sustainability efforts as intended to
boost its presentation of itself as genuine, truthful, and con-
sistent so that consumers would regard the firm as authentic
(and would choose to purchase its brands).

strong and active presence of the government as a regula -

is understated in both virtue ethics and stewardship ethics.

Categorizing firms according to their level of sustainable
business practice raises a more pointed and urgent question:
What compels firm leaders to transcend profit-maximizing
logic and commit to deeper forms of sustainability? Is it
a genuine moral commitment to intergenerational justice
and planetary boundaries, or merely a calculated response

Resource-Advantage (R-A) theory
Resource-Advantage (R-A) theory offers a dynamic, evolu-
tionary, and interdisciplinary framework for understanding
competition, positioning marketing and entrepreneurship
at the core of firm strategy and market performance (Hunt,
1995; Hunt & Morgan, 1996, 1997). Developed in oppo -
sition to the static assumptions of neoclassical economics,
R-A theory challenges premises such as perfect competition,
costless and complete information, homogeneous consumer
demand, and universal profit maximization under equilib -
rium conditions. Instead, it views markets as characterized
by heterogeneity, information asymmetries, and continuous
change, where firms compete for superior financial perfor-
mance, not absolute profit maximization (Hunt, 2017).
This shift in focus from equilibrium to disequilibrium
redefines the role of firms as adaptive, resource-integrating,
learning organizations embedded in evolving institutional
environments. R-A theory asserts that markets are “never
still,” and that marketing and entrepreneurial behavior are
the primary engines of change and innovation (Hunt &
Lambe, 2000). Firms engage in the acquisition, develop -
ment, and leveraging of heterogeneous resources—both
tangible (e.g., technologies, supply chains) and intangible
(e.g., brand equity, market knowledge, organizational cul -
ture)—to achieve competitive advantage. These resources
vary in terms of mobility, imitability, and relevance to mar-
ket context, and their value is shaped by institutional norms
and stakeholder expectations.
A distinguishing feature of R-A theory is its treatment
of operant resources—intangible, knowledge-based assets
that act on other resources to create value—as central to
sustained advantage. Madhavaram and Hunt ( 2008) offer
a hierarchical model of operant resources, showing how
higher-order capabilities such as dynamic learning, stake -
holder relationships, and ethical commitments enable firms
to outperform rivals in complex environments. This recog -
nition of non-financial resources as strategic assets under -
pins the theory’s compatibility with broader sustainability
concerns.
In his 2017 AMS Review article, Hunt provides a detailed
account of how R-A theory can accommodate the TBL. He
proposes that the TBL should be treated as a literal meta-
phor rather than a strict measurement system. That is, while
financial performance remains primary for for-profit firms,
environmental and social outcomes may become legitimate
strategic objectives when they contribute to long-term stra -
tegic advantage, stakeholder satisfaction, or risk mitigation.
This flexibility allows R-A theory to explain how sustain -
ability enters into competitive strategy without assuming it
must be prioritized.

Conflicting goals, such as short-term profitability versus

of value—economic, social, and ecological (Vargo & Lusch,

reconfiguring consumer behavior, and renegotiating regu -

ecosystem-level shifts coordinated through shared institu -

Importantly, S-D logic does not assume that value cocre-
ation is automatically beneficial (Vargo & Lusch, 2008). It
recognizes that value can be contested, unevenly distrib -
uted, or even harmful—such as when firms externalize envi-
ronmental costs or when consumers reinforce unsustainable
consumption norms. Therefore, value cocreation must be
viewed critically, especially when assessing the systemic
outcomes of marketing practices. The question becomes
not just what value is created and for whom, but also how
that value creation process affects the resilience, equity, and
integrity of the service ecosystem (Fehrer & Wieland, 2021).
In this respect, S-D logic offers an integrative platform for
sustainability scholarship in marketing—one that connects
micro-level interactions with macro-level outcomes. It has
the potential to move the marketing discipline toward a
more participatory, ethical, and systems-aware orientation,
better suited to address the environmental and social disrup-
tions of our time.

Market Shaping
The Market Shaping framework offers a forward-looking,
processual view of how firms and other actors do not merely
adapt to existing market structures but actively participate
in forming and transforming them (Nenonen et al., 2019;
Baker & Nenonen, 2020). It departs from equilibrium-based
views of markets as naturally occurring outcomes of supply-
demand dynamics and instead emphasizes that markets are
performative arrangements—socially constructed through
the coordinated activities of firms, consumers, regulators,
and other stakeholders (Callon, 1998; Kjellberg & Helges -
son, 2007). As such, Market Shaping aligns with a growing
body of research that views markets as malleable, contested,
and institutionally embedded fields of action (e.g., Jaworski
et al., 2020; Möller et al., 2020; Nenonen et al., 2019, Baker
et al., 2019; Giesler & Fischer, 2017).
In contrast to reactive or opportunity-seeking models
of strategy, Market Shaping emphasizes intentionality and
agency. Firms are not just price takers but can be price mak-
ers, category definers, rule setters, and infrastructure build -
ers (Nenonen & Storbacka, 2020). This reframing opens
space for firms to envision and enact future-oriented market
configurations—whether through changing industry norms,
setting new standards, influencing policy (Kaartemo et al.,
2020), or fostering new ecosystems of collaboration (Kullak
et al., 2022). Particularly in contexts of sustainability, where
existing market arrangements often sustain unsustainable
production and consumption patterns, Market Shaping pro-
vides a valuable lens for theorizing systemic transitions
(Fehrer, 2020; Madhavaram & Nirjar, 2025; Storbacka et
al., 2022).
A useful way to understand Market Shaping is through
an analytical scaffolding of five interdependent layers: (1)
business purpose, (2) exchange practices, (3) networks, (4)
market representations, and (5) market norms (Nenonen &
Storbacka, 2018; Kjellberg & Helgesson, 2007). These lay-
ers are not discrete or sequential but mutually constitutive,
offering complementary vantage points on how markets are
From this perspective, sustainability is not framed as an
external constraint or moral imperative but rather as a strate-
gic resource domain—one that firms can leverage to create
value and develop differentiation. For example, improving
environmental performance may reduce costs through effi -
ciency gains, build brand reputation, or preempt regulatory
pressures. Similarly, social engagement can generate trust,
attract talent, and deepen customer loyalty. These outcomes
enhance firms’ positions in dynamic markets and contribute
to sustainable strategic advantage.
However, R-A theory does not prescribe why firms pur-
sue sustainability. Instead, it provides a diagnostic lens to
explain variation in sustainability performance across firms
and industries. Hunt ( 2017) identifies seven explanatory
factors: (1) the evaluators of TBL performance (e.g., share-
holders, regulators, NGOs); (2) differences in competitive
position and resource endowment; (3) the influence of for -
mal institutions such as laws and standards; (4) informal
institutions including cultural norms and expectations; (5)
the moral codes and values of individual leaders; (6) the
perceived value of sustainability by consumers; and (7)
differences in firms’ marketing strategies and capabilities.
These interrelated variables help explain why some firms
proactively integrate sustainability, while others lag despite
similar environmental or societal challenges.
Crittenden et al. (2011) extend R-A theory by developing
a market-oriented sustainability framework, where sustain-
ability is not peripheral but integrated into the firm’s core
ideology, capabilities, and societal engagement—what they
term a firm’s “DNA.” Their model highlights the importance
of stakeholder involvement and performance management
in aligning sustainability with competitive advantage. The
idea is that firms can embed sustainability in ways that rein-
force strategic alignment, innovation, and value creation.
Empirical support for this theoretical integration is provided
by Doherty (2011), who explores how fair trade enterprises
compete through social resources —ethical credibility,
strong partner networks, and behavioral consistency. These
resources, although not traditionally financial, enhance mar-
ket legitimacy and customer loyalty, and thus function as
strategic differentiators. That is, R-A theory supports sus -
tainability without diluting its strategic focus. Hunt ( 2015)
argues that strategic marketing’s foundational principles—
such as creating superior value and aligning firm capabili -
ties with market demands—can be grounded in R-A theory.
When extended to sustainability, this foundation accommo-
dates the need for adaptive, market-responsive strategies
that reflect environmental and social considerations.

The preceding sections provide a rich foundation for articu-

through the lens of marketing-indigenous theory. If market-

understanding of what motivates firms to act sustainably.

advancing system-level theorizing grounded in marketing’s
unique ontology; (2) reimagining stakeholder responsibil -
ity to include nature and future generations; (3) sustain -
ability as a resource domain to develop strategic advantage,
where measurement matters in theory development; and (4)
enabling prospective theorizing that supports the shaping of

ity. Thermodynamic theory (Georgescu-Roegen, 1971)
brings attention to entropy and irreversibility, reframing
sustainability as a problem of energy degradation. Social
metabolism (Fischer-Kowalski & Haberl, 2007) analyzes
material and energy throughput between human societies
and ecosystems, highlighting structural coupling between
economy and ecology. Finally, quantum theory—used met-
aphorically in the social sciences (Bohr, 1935; Heisenberg,
1983; Murphy, 2021)—opens new conceptual ground for
theorizing interconnectedness, entanglement, and emergent
dynamics in complex market systems.
Advancing system-level theorizing in marketing
Future research should advance a coherent, system-
level sustainability theory that is indigenous to market -
ing. Service-Dominant (S-D) logic provides a compelling
foundation through its emphasis on service ecosystems,
institutional arrangements, and co-created value. However,
its full potential remains underutilized in studies examining
how sustainability transitions unfold across complex, nested
systems.
Marketing scholars should engage more fully with how
service ecosystems—comprising firms, customers, families,
society, and nature—facilitate or hinder sustainable out -
comes across local, national, and global levels. For instance,
local initiatives in regenerative agriculture or renewable
energy often interact with national regulations and trans -
national value chains, creating feedback loops that either
stabilize or disrupt broader sustainability goals. Saviano et
al. (2025) propose a conceptual framework that integrates
complex adaptive systems theory with S-D logic to con -
ceptualize sustainability as an emergent, co-created service.
Such dynamics call for more research focused on interde -
pendencies, emergent patterns, and adaptive capabilities
across system layers (Vargo et al., 2023).
S-D logic’s framework allows for a multi-level per -
spective, as resource integrators operate across the micro
(individual actors), meso (organizational networks, supply
chains), and macro (market institutions, societal norms)
levels. Its compatibility with complexity science and insti -
tutional theory makes it well-suited to explore how sustain-
ability can emerge as a system property. Moreover, future
research can deepen this theorizing by drawing on allied

An essential component of advancing such theorizing
is the development of robust sustainability measurement
systems. Measurement matters in developing meaningful
theories of sustainability. Because of the tripartite nature of
the TBL, sustainability is multidimensional. How can actors
be evaluated across multiple dimensions of sustainability?
Do researchers naively expect actors to give equal weight
to their efforts across the three dimensions of sustainability?
Patrick Thomas, the retired CEO of Denmark’s Novo
Nordisk—a firm recognized for its sustainability achieve -
ments—asserted that the proper use of the TBL would be
that a firm would, at minimum, progress on two dimen -
sions while the third remains unaffected (Elkington, 2018).
Accordingly, progressing on one dimension while declining
on the second and third would be problematic for a firm com-
mitted to sustainability. Different actors in societies have
varying capabilities to initiate new actions across the three
domains of the TBL, deliver these new actions with force,
and sustain or increase these actions over time. Because of
a competitive marketplace, firms would likely experience
variability in their abilities to make progress across the three
domains of the TBL year in and year out.
Measurement also matters in the realm of sustainability
research and theory development as researchers have vary -
ing abilities to gauge results in the studies they initiate.
Because of the relevance of sustainability to all research
disciplines, a variety of research methods will be brought to
the study of sustainability phenomena. These would include
quantitative, qualitative, mixed methods, and case studies.
Accordingly, the development of sustainability theories and
the empirical evaluation of models related to these theories
would require not only different methods, but also differ -
ent time frames for delivering research results. For exam -
ple, researchers have more control in fielding surveys and
in conducting experiments. However, at the meso-level
of markets or marketing systems (such as supply chains),
researchers have less control. At the macro-level of soci -
eties, researchers must rely on aggregated measurements
(done by NGOs or government entities; Pollesch & Dale,
2015), or clever measurements of a certain type of business
activity (such as the success or failure of new product intro-
ductions as reported by professional research agencies; van
Doorn et al., 2021).
Reimagining stakeholder responsibility to include
nature and future generations
Without the explicit inclusion of the natural environment
and future generations as stakeholders of businesses, it
is likely that pursuing sustainability will be positioned as
optional for business leaders. Hult et al.’s ( 2011) work on
market-focused sustainability omits the natural environ -
ment and future generations as stakeholders. This short -
coming leads researchers to regard consumers’ interest in
sustainability as a hobby or current fashion trend, rather
than as an essential and enduring part of many consumers’
calculus for value sought in market exchanges. In this way,
researchers are likely to lapse into viewing weak sustain -
ability for firms as practical, while viewing strong sustain -
ability as suitable only for a few exceptional cases. On the
contrary, evidence suggests that developing a reputation for
sustainability boosts a firm’s efforts in recruiting, winning,
and retaining customers (Bhattacharya et al., 2022), as well
as in launching sustainability-oriented products and services
(van Doorn et al., 2021).
To reframe the nature and role of stakeholders in market-
ing theory in more inclusive terms, Indigenous frameworks
can offer a powerful interface. For instance, the Māori prin-
ciple of kaitiakitanga (guardianship) embeds environmental
responsibility within intergenerational ethics, highlighting
the duty to care for the land and future descendants (Spiller
et al., 2011). Similarly, the Andean concept of buen vivir
(“living well”) positions harmony with nature and collec -
tive well-being as central societal goals, rejecting extractive
market logic (Gudynas, 2011). These relational ontologies,
grounded in reciprocity and non-anthropocentric ethics,
suggest that nature and unborn generations are not external
stakeholders but intrinsic participants in systems of value
creation. Theoretical frameworks from intergenerational
justice reinforce this view, emphasizing the moral and
political duties present generations owe to those who cannot
speak for themselves, including future people and the natu-
ral world (Thompson, 2016).

strategic advantage

petitive advantage stems from the possession of superior,
rare, and imperfectly mobile resources. In this context, sus-
tainability capabilities—such as regenerative design and
circular economy models (e.g., Geissdoerfer et al., 2017;
Conduit et al., 2023)—can be understood as strategic assets

Traditionally, theorizing in marketing has followed a
retrospective orientation. Whether inductive or deductive,
most theories have been developed by analyzing past pat -

but with normative visions of alternative futures.
Prospective theorizing anchors itself in aspiration and

Future research should further explore how marketing schol-

technological disruption, and societal polarization, the role
of theory must evolve beyond retrospective explanation.
Market Shaping provides a valuable anchor in this regard—
it foregrounds the capacity of actors to intentionally inter -

if marketing is to meaningfully influence sustainability tran-

Lessons for practitioners and policymakers from our
integrative review of theories of sustainability include the
importance of scope when considering sustainability. First,
sustainability efforts should include the tripartite definition
of sustainability (people, planet, profit). Without the profit
dimension, firms and marketers will not be part of sustain -
ability solutions. Second, the planet dimension of sustain -
ability should include a broad set of stakeholders, including
the natural environment and future generations. Without
this important stakeholder group, the planet dimension can
readily drop to an afterthought or a footnote in firm plan -
ning. In sum, practitioners and policymakers would do well
to include a broad scope when viewing sustainability and
not succumb to a narrowing of scope, which might arise
without intentionally choosing to retain a broad view on
sustainability.

Conclusion
The development of sustainability theory in marketing pres-
ents a timely opportunity to advance the discipline and con-
tribute meaningfully to global challenges. Drawing on the
Brundtland Commission’s definition, sustainability brings
the needs of future generations and the natural environment
into focus—yet these are often overlooked in firm-centered
stakeholder models. Addressing this gap may offer deeper
insight into what motivates firms to engage with sustain -
ability. Conceptual contributions in AMS Review , such as
Lunde’s (2018) GREEN framework, illustrate the value of
greater definitional clarity and theory development. Indig -
enous marketing theories—including Service-Dominant
logic, Resource-Advantage theory, and Market Shaping—
provide promising foundations to explore sustainability
across micro, meso, and macro levels. Each offers distinct
yet complementary perspectives that can help illuminate the
dynamics of systemic change, resource strategies, and sus -
tainable market emergence. Collectively, these frameworks
create space for marketing scholarship to engage more fully
with sustainability—broadening its ethical imagination and
