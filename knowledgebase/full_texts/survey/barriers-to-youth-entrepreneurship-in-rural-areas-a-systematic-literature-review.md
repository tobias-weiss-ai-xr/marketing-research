Abstract:
Rural youth entrepreneurship supports economic development and limits migration, but
significant barriers remain.To identify and analyze these barriers, we performed a Systematic
Literature Review of peer -reviewed articles from the Web of Science Core Collection database,
published between 2014 and 2025 (final sample - 57 articles). The bibliometric analysis using
VOSviewer, including co -occurrence, co -authorship, citation analysis, was complemented by a
qualitative content analysis to synthesize the identified barriers. The study reveals that research on
this topic is geographically concentrated, with a notable focus on African contexts. The bibliometric
analysis reveals a fragmented research network with limited worldwide collaboration. We identified
six main rural youth entrepreneurship barriers: financial (lack of start -up capital, low income),
infrastructural (inadequate roads, limited digital connectivity), educational (skills mismatch, limited
entrepreneurship education), social/cultural (traditional gender roles, risk aversion), institutional (lack
of government support, bureaucratic hurdles, corruption), and market -related (limited access,
marketing challenges). The study points out that the barriers are extremely interdependent and
therefore require a multi -dimensional, holistic intervention approach and not isolated solutions. The
review also highlights key research gaps, particularly the need for more comparative, region -specific
studies and a more integrated approach to gender.

Key words: youth entrepreneurship; systematic literature review; rural youth; VOSviewer

1. Introduction

Entrepreneurs serve as fundamental catalysts for economic development across
global markets, driving innovation and growth through their central role in economic
systems. The substantial contributions of successful entrepreneurial ventures to national
and international economies have been extensively documented in the literature (Acs,
2006; Guerrero, et al., 2021; Neumann, 2020; Pato & Teixeira, 2016; De Guzman et al.,
2020). Entrepreneurship functions as a major mechanism for economic expansion by
generating employment opportunities, addressing poverty reduction initiatives, providing
essential resilience during periods of economic instability (Alvarez & Barney, 2014;

Morozova et al., 2019; Si, et al., 2019; Sutter et al., 2019; Humberg & Braun, 2014),
promoting sustainable rural development (Bawa et al., 2007).
Entrepreneurial activity plays a particularly significant role in developing economies,
where micro, small, and medium -sized enterprises account for more than fifty percent of
total employment and exceed ninety percent of value -added business activities across all
firm categories (OECD, 2023). Furthermore, entrepreneurship represents a strategic
pathway for maximizing the economic potential of youth populations, offering sustainable
employment alternatives and promoting economic self -sufficiency and independence
(Awogbenle & Iwuamadi, 2010; Baskaran & Mehta, 2016; Igwe et al., 2020). Youth
entrepreneurship is increasingly recognized as a key driver of economic diversification,
innovation and employment in rural areas (De Guzman et al., 2020; Chakravarty et al.,
2021; Ningrum, 2018), contributing to livelihood security and sustainable development
(Deng et al., 2020).
However, despite these well -established benefits, young entrepreneurs across
numerous global regions continue to face significant structural and systemic barriers that
restrain their entrepreneurial endeavors (Baskaran & Mehta, 2016). These include limited
access to entrepreneurial education and training, which reduces the readiness of youth to
start ventures or effectively navigate business challenges (Nurbekovna et al., 2025;
Matlhake & Kalitanyi, 2025), and cultural constraints that influence entrepreneurial
motivation differently in rural contexts compared to urban ones (Escandón -Barbosa et al.,
2019). Beyond the lack of technical skills, cultural attitudes and insufficient encouragement
from families and educational institutions can also discourage young people from
entrepreneurial pursuits (Davids et al., 2021).
Rural entrepreneurship, in particular, contributes to building a more inclusive and
sustainable future (Mishra et al., 2020) and has proven especially important for improving
rural livelihoods, including those of women in less developed economies (Akinbami et al.,
2019). Yet rural regions are experiencing increasing pressure to revitalize their local
economies as they confront demographic decline, limited job opportunities and persistent
outmigration (De Guzman, et al., 2020). Within th ese settings, young entrepreneurs
represent a major resource, not only as job creators but also as agents of change capable
of introducing new business models and revitalizing traditional sectors. Their engagement
in entrepreneurial activity holds the potential to address structural imbalances, foster
community resilience and stimulate inclusive rural development.
However, despite growing policy attention to rural entrepreneurship, empirical
evidence consistently highlights the fact that young people face a distinct set of challenges
when attempting to start and grow businesses in rural environments (De Guzman, et al.,
2020; Li et al., 2019; Paniagua, 2013). These challenges are often exacerbated by sparse
institutional presence and underdeveloped infrastructure (Yeung et al., 2015; Baumgartner
et al., 2013), as well as by lack of resources and poverty in rural areas (Tabares et al.,
2022). Barriers include inadequate access to finance and infrastructure (Bouichou et al.,
2021; Robb, 2021; Yeung et al., 2015), mismatches between academic training and
market needs (Bullock et al., 2025; Ferreira et al., 2021), institutional voids (Audretsch et
al., 2022), the digital divide and insufficient ICT infrastructure (Warren, 2007; Galloway &
Mochrie, 2005), restricted access to markets and business networks (Ashby et al., 2009;

Shu et al., 2018), gender -specific barriers particularly affecting women entrepreneurs
(Kitole & Genda, 2024; Semkunde et al., 2022; Naegels et al., 2018; Samandar & Wardak,
2025; Bannor et al., 2020; Aggarwal & Johal, 2021), and limited access to quality
education and entrepreneurial training programs (Baskaran & Mehta, 2016).
Yet, much of the literature on entrepreneurship either treats youth as a
homogeneous group or focuses predominantly on urban entrepreneurial dynamics,
thereby overlooking the multiple barriers present at the intersection of youth and rurality.
Existing literature often treats the barriers faced by rural youth entrepreneurs as
separate and isolated constraints. Studies typically focus on specific obstacles such as
limited access to finance, infrastructural deficiencies, or socio -cultural norms, without fully
examining how these barriers interact or reinforce one another over time.
Entrepreneurship research has applied institutional theory, including Scott’s (2014)
regulative, normative, and cognitive pillars in various contexts (Álvarez et al., 2025; Lopez
et al., 2025), however most studies continue to analyze these dimensions independently.
This fragmented approach limits understanding of why barriers persist and often results in
policy responses that address individual symptoms rather than underlying institutional
constraints.
This limitation is especially evident in research on rural youth entrepreneurship.
Although institutional theory has been commonly applied to entrepreneurship generally, its
use in examining rural youth remains limited, particularly when integrating all three pillars
of Scott's framework. Studies that do apply institutional perspectives tend to focus on
village-owned enterprises (Way, 2025), cooperatives (Hidayat et al., 2024), or urban
settings rather than on rural youth entrepreneurship. This represents an important gap
because rural contexts present distinct institutional configurations. They are influenced by
particular combinations of formal and informal institutions that differ from urban
environments, while young entrepreneurs face additional age -specific constraints including
limited experience, restricted networks, and perceptions that question their entrepreneurial
capability.
To address this gap systematically, our study adopts institutional theory as an
analytical framework, building on North’s (1990) foundational work and Scott’s (2014)
multidimensional approach. We view entrepreneurial behavior as the outcome of
interactions between regulative rules, normative expectations, and the cognitive
perceptions that guide individual decision -making. This framework is especially suited to
rural youth entrepreneurship, as young people in rural areas face institutional
disadvantages across all three dimensions. These include formal exclusion from credit
markets and support programs, normative pressures toward migration and traditional
employment, and cognitive perceptions that devalue youth capabilities and rural
entrepreneurial opportunities. Consequently, this study is guided by the following research
question: What barriers do young entrepreneurs face in rural contexts and how can these
be understood institutionally?
To answer th is question, the paper conducts a systematic literature review (SLR)
combining bibliometric analysis with qualitative content synthesis to identify, categorize,
and interpret barriers faced by young entrepreneurs in rural areas over the last decade.

2. Methodology

Research design. This study employs a systematic literature review methodology to
identify, analyze and synthesize existing research on barriers to youth entrepreneurship in
rural areas. SLRs are recognized as an effective method for rigorously analyzing and
evaluating a specific body of research (Tranfield, et al., 2003). Following established
systematic review protocols, we have combined bibliometric analysis with qualitative
content analysis. This approach will provide both quantitative insights into research
patterns and a qualitative understanding of identified barriers.
Search strategy and database selection.  The literature search was conducted using
the Web of Science (WoS) Core Collection.  The WoS Core Collection was selected to
ensure metadata homogeneity, an important requirement for bibliometric analysis.
Integrating records from multiple databases can introduce inconsistencies in citation
counts, keyword standardization, and document classification, potentially affecting the
accuracy of network visualizations produced in VOSviewer. In addition, the stringent
selection criteria of the WoS Core Collection provide a consistent quality threshold, in line
with common practices in bibliometric research. Although this choice limits overall
coverage, it allows for a more consistent and methodologically reliable bibliometric
analysis.
We used a systematic combination of keywords targeting three core conceptual
domains: youth entrepreneurship, rural contexts and barriers or challenges. This
comprehensive search strategy was designed to capture the entrepreneurship barriers
while maintaining focus on experiences specific for youth in rural contexts. The keywords
were connected using Boolean operators (“OR”, “AND”) to refine and expand the search
results. The example of the search strategy and search query that was applied to titles and
abstracts is provided in (Figure 1).
Inclusion and exclusion criteria. The following inclusion criteria were established to
ensure the relevance and quality of selected studies:
• Period: Publications from 2014 -2025 to capture contemporary barriers and recent
developments in rural youth entrepreneurship.
• Geographic scope : Global perspective with no regional restrictions to ensure
comprehensive understanding of barriers across different contexts.
• Document types: Peer-reviewed articles and review articles to maintain academic
rigor.
• Research areas : Studies within Business Economics, Development Studies,
Geography, Social Sciences, Social Issues and Agriculture disciplines.
• Content focus : Studies addressing youth entrepreneurship in rural contexts with
specific attention to barriers, challenges, constraints.
Studies were excluded based on the following reason:
• General young business: Research on “young social businesses” without specific
focus on individual youth entrepreneurs.
• Migration studies: Research primarily examining youth mobility or migration for
employment opportunities rather than local entrepreneurship development.

• Insufficient rural focus: Studies mentioning rural areas without addressing unique
rural entrepreneurship challenges.
• Peripheral mention: Research where youth entrepreneurship appears as tangential
rather than central to the analysis.
• Non-entrepreneurial focus: Studies primarily addressing other phenomena (e.g.,
environmental adaptation) where entrepreneurship barriers are not systematically
examined.

Selection process. The systematic selection process followed PRISMA (Preferred
Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta -Analyses) guidelines to ensure
transparency and reproducibility in the documentation of study selection procedures. A
PRISMA-style flow diagram was created to illustrate the screening and selection process
(Moher, et al., 2009).
The initial search yielded 96 potentially relevant publications. Following systematic
abstract screening, 39 studies were excluded for failing to meet inclusion criteria. The
remaining 57 studies formed the final corpus for our study.
Analytical approach . Data analysis and visualization were conducted using
VOSviewer software, a specialized bibliometric tool designed for mapping and analyzing
scientific literature networks ( Van Eck and Waltman, 2010 ). Three analytical techniques
were applied: co -occurrence analysis to identify frequently associated keywords and
thematic patterns; co -authorship analysis to examine collaboration structures; and citation
analysis to assess patterns of research influence within the field.
For the bibliometric mapping, a minimum threshold of two keyword occurrences
and two documents per author was applied. We have selected t his relatively low threshold
due to the limited dataset size (N = 57), as higher thresholds risk excluding emerging
themes and smaller collaborative links relevant to rural youth entrepreneurship. At the
same time, we acknowledge the sensitivity of cluster structures to parameter selection,
with different thresholds yielding varying levels of network consolidation and detail.
Also a qualitative content analysis was employed to systematically examine the
findings of included studies. This process involved b arrier categorization  where
entrepreneurship barriers reported across studies were identified.

Citation analysis. The citation analysis of selected papers shows distinct patterns
in the scholarly development of research on youth entrepreneurs barriers in rural areas.
Several papers have emerged as highly influential contributors to the field. De Guzman et
al. (2020) achieved 38 citations for their exploration of rural communities as
entrepreneurial contexts, focusing specifically on youth perceptions and business owner
experiences in the United States. This work has been influen tial in establishing the
conceptual framework for understanding how rural contexts themselves create unique
barriers and benefits for young entrepreneurs.
The African context has produced particularly impactful study, with Mwaura’s
(2017) work of educated youth in Kenya accumulating 37 citations. This research
showcased the complex relationship between educational attainment and agricultural

livelihoods under neoliberal economic structures, highlighting how educational -employment
misalignment creates significant barriers for rural youth entrepreneurship. Similarly, Ripoll
et al. (2017) advanced the discourse on youth and agrifood systems with a widely cited
study (36 citations).  In his study, he analysed how structural rural transformation shapes
young people’s economic agency in cereal -based economies across Africa. The authors
proposed an analytical framework to assess youth engagement in agricultural value
chains, emphasizing context -specific constraints and opportunities within rural African
areas.
Afreh et al. (2019) advanced research on informal entrepreneurship through their
study of migrant youth in rural Ghana, which has been cited 34 times. The study is
particularly significant for showing how migration and informal economies create both
opportunities and barriers for young entrepreneurs in Sub-Saharan Africa.
Emerging themes and recent developments . The analysed papers show a clear
evolution toward more sophisticated analytical approaches and specialized focus areas.
Financial access barriers have gained prominence through Senou and Manda's (2022)
study of rural youth entrepreneurship in Benin, which has already accumulated 23 citations
despite its relative recency. Their research employed advanced econometric techniques
including propensity score matching to examine gender gaps in financial access,
highlighting that a significant gender gap in rural entrepreneurship among youths exists.
The authors showed that age, education, poverty status, experience, working in the
agricultural sector and the existence of a bank branch are important determinants of rural
youths' access to finance.
The Chinese context has emerged as a particularly rich area for recent high -
impact research. Chen and Barcus’s (2024) study on women returning home to start
businesses, already cited 22 times, highlights gender -specific barriers and empowerment
mechanisms in rural entrepreneurship.

The prominence of agriculture -related clusters (Clusters 1, 5, and 6) points to a
strong alignment between rural entrepreneurship research and agriculture. While this partly
reflects the empirical reality that agriculture dominates many rural economies, this
concentration may limit scholarly attention to non -agricultural entrepreneurial opportunities

The separation of gender research into distinct clusters (2 and 7), instead of being
integrated across thematic areas, is particularly notable. This pattern suggests that gender
barriers are treated as a specialized subfield, not as a cross -cutting dimension affecting
rural youth entrepreneurship more broadly. Such fragmentation may limit understanding of
how gender norms interact with other institutional barriers to create compounded
disadvantages for young women entrepreneurs.
The strong concentration of studies in African contexts reflects important empirical
realities but also raises questions about the institutional transferability of existing findings.
Barriers shaped by specific regulative environments (land tenure arrangements, access to
credit), as well as by normative factors (e.g., gender roles, youth status) may operate
differently in other rural regions. The limited number of comparative or cross -regional
studies therefore constrains our ability to distinguish between institutional barriers that are
context-specific and those that may be more largely applicable across rural contexts.

Geographic distribution and research intensity . The network analysis reveals
several important patterns in global research distribution (Table 3):
There is a dominance of African countries . The researched topic is represented
significantly in the literature by Ghana, Nigeria, Kenya, Benin, Cameroon, Madagascar and
Morocco. This concentration may reflect the challenges the African continent encounter s
regarding youth unemployment and the policy relevance of rural entrepreneurship. Also,
this concentration means empirical evidence is densely context-specific, potentially limiting
the applicability of findings to rural settings in other regions.
Asian countries are underrepresented . Although Asia has a large rural population,
only China and Malaysia appear in the collaboration network , and China’s collaboration
links are limited (link strength: 1). This may point to potential gaps in regional coverage and
limited cross-regional knowledge exchange.
Research isolation and knowledge silos. Several countries (Greece, India,
Morocco, Romania, Spain) exhibit zero total link strength, suggesting that some research
has been conducted largely without international collaboration . This fragmentation creates
knowledge silos in the field, where researchers may be working on similar issues without
relying on each other’s findings. Such research isolation can limit opportunities for
comparative analysis, slow cumulative theory development, and reduce the transferability
of relevant policy lessons across different rural contexts.

4. Discussions

Analysis of main barriers to youth entrepreneurship in rural areas. The
systematic literature review reveals several significant and persistent barriers to youth
entrepreneurship within rural contexts. We have categorized these impediments into
financial, infrastructure, educational/skills, social/cultural, institutional/policy, and market -
related challenges.
These barriers are not isolated obstacles but reflect deeper institutional
deficits across regulative, normative, and cognitive dimensions  (Scott, 2014). Young
people in rural areas face disadvantages across all three dimensions. Formal systems
hinder their access to credit and support programs, social norms pressure them toward
migration and traditional employment, and prevailing assumptions devalue both youth
capabilities and rural entrepreneurial opportunities. Rather than addressing these

challenges separately, we examine how they function as components of a broader
institutional environment that systematically marginalizes rural youth entrepreneurs.
Financial barriers as regulative exclusion.  Access to finance emerges as the
most frequently cited barrier. Young rural entrepreneurs face more than capital scarcity -
they are systematically excluded from formal financial systems. Formal financial institutions
use eligibility criteria that disadvantage young people. Requirements for collateral, credit
history, and stable income create an inherent disadvantage for young people who have not
yet accumulated assets or formal employment records (Arthur -Holmes & Yeboah, 2025;
Iwara, 2025; Pervez et al., 2024).  This issue is intensified by “inadequate startup capital”
and a “shortage of credit access” (Leul et al., 2023), “low savings rates” among young
people (Pervez et al., 2024), and “low income” levels that prevent entrepreneurial initiation
(Dragin et al., 2024).
In the agricultural sectors, “limited access to land" remains a critical regulative
barrier, as existing funding programs inadequately support youth land acquisition and
capital needs (Makris et al., 2025). Funding limitations further constrain agricultural service
providers' sustainability (Kadzamira et al., 2024), while the informal and seasonal nature of
rural markets creates “inconsistent income” streams (Ratompoarison et al., 2023). These
conditions reflect structural biases rather than simple market failures; regulative
frameworks favor established, asset-holding entrepreneurs and thereby constrain the entry
of younger participants.
Infrastructure deficits as regulative underinvestment.  Infrastructure
inadequacy reflects systematic regulative underinvestment in rural areas . “Inadequate
roads” and the deficiency of transportation systems impede market access for perishable
products (Leul et al., 2023; Pervez, et al., 2024; Ratompoarison et al., 2023), while the lack
of storage and processing facilities prevents year -round product availability
(Ratompoarison et al., 2023). “Limited utilities” including power and water, absence of
paved roads and electricity (Leul et al., 2023; Ratompoarison et al., 2023), and critically,
“limited digital infrastructure” (Zhang et al., 2024) represent not mere development gaps
but regulative priorities that systematically disadvantage rural economies . Infrastructure
deficits and financial exclusion create a reinforcing cycle. Without basic infrastructure, such
as roads, utilities, and storage , youth-led ventures often cannot meet the requirements for
formal credit, limiting their ability to grow. So far, regulatory efforts have been insufficient to
address this persistent barrier.
Institutional/policy barriers as governance deficiency.  Direct governance
deficiencies further exacerbate  these structural problems. General “lack of government
support” manifests through “poor political governance and corruption”, “institutional
weakness in employment planning” (Iwara, 2025), and failure “to generate formal job
opportunities” (Arthur -Holmes & Yeboah, 2025). Limited information access due to
“ineffective labor market information systems” further disadvantages young entrepreneurs
(Iwara, 2025). Formal institutions often exhibit “inefficient property rights protection”, “slow
judicial systems”, “excessive bureaucratic requirements”, and “high transfer taxes” that
impede land mobility for young farmers (Makris et al., 2025). “Complex licensing systems”
and criminalization of certain informal activities increase suspicion and make formalization
more difficult  (Arthur-Holmes & Yeboah, 2025), while “political instability” creates

environmental fragility (Iwara, 2025).  These challenges are compounded by the relative
ineffectiveness of current government interventions in mitigating youth underemployment
(Tadzong Mouafo et al., 2024). In this context, regulative institutions often act as barriers,
as poorly aligned policies and weak enforcement undermine entrepreneurial initiative.
Social and cultural norms as entrepreneurial deterrents.  Beyond formal
regulations, normative institutions play a decisive role in legitimizing or devaluing specific
entrepreneurial paths. The "socialization of Good Jobs" establishes a rigid cognitive
hierarchy. Within this framework, formal salaried positions are viewed as the only
“respectable” occupations, a perception that actively discourages educated youth from
pursuing entrepreneurship, particularly in the informal sector (Arthur -Holmes & Yeboah,
2025). This deficit of social recognition drives youth migration; when local ventures are
devalued, urban centers offer a dual promise of both economic opportunity and higher
social status. Such trends contribute to a rural “brain drain” that systematically depletes
potential entrepreneurial talent (Dragin et al., 2024; Iwara, 2025; Pervez et al., 2024).
These pressures are further reinforced within the domestic sphere. “Family
attitudes/tradition” often dictate labor divisions and entrepreneurial roles, including
gendered expectations around domestic work (Ratompoarison et al., 2023). Furthermore,
a prevalent culture of risk aversion, deeply rooted in values that favor security over
independence further discourages entrepreneurial endeavor (Pervez et al., 2024;
Ratompoarison et al., 2023). From an institutional perspective, these barriers reinforce one
another. Migration reduces the presence of local role models, which strengthens the view
of rural entrepreneurship as a “fallback” option instead of a viable career. Over time, this
perception sustains outward migration and deepens the resulting talent gap.
Gender as a complex normative barrier.  In addition to general youth -related
constraints, gender functions as a distinct and often insufficiently examined institutional
dimension. This is reflected in our bibliometric analysis, where gender research is
concentrated in separate clusters instead of being integrated across broader
entrepreneurial themes. Gender norms create additional normative barriers for young
women entrepreneurs, who experience both youth -based and gender -based institutional
exclusion. Traditional gender roles limit young women’s participation in certain sectors,
while family and community expectations constrain their entrepreneurial autonomy. This
dual marginalization suggests that institutional barriers are multiplicative, not merely
additive, resulting in a higher level of exclusion for young women compared to young men
or older women entrepreneurs
Educational systems as a source of cognitive institutional deficiency.  A
persistent mismatch between education and labor market demands (Arthur -Holmes &
Yeboah, 2025; Iwara, 2025) indicates a cognitive institutional misalignment . Many
educational systems remain "chiefly theory -oriented rather than aligned with a knowledge
economy," producing “irrelevant qualifications” that are often decoupled from market needs
(Iwara, 2025). This disconnect generates “inadequate graduate developmental skills”,
“mismatched skills”, and a “lack of practical experience” (Arthur -Holmes & Yeboah, 2025;
Iwara, 2025). The scarcity of business and management skills, “poor startup expertise”
(Iwara, 2025; Pervez et al., 2024), and “lack of tailored support programs” and mentorship
opportunities (Dragin et al., 2024; Iwara, 2025) may suggest that cognitive institutions have

- 149 -

not yet recognized entrepreneurship as a legitimate educational outcome. Inadequate
technical training in areas like postharvest processing (Arthur -Holmes & Yeboah, 2025;
Ratompoarison et al., 2023), absence of comprehensive knowledge regarding agricultural
business practices, and “insufficient extension services” (Leul et al., 2023) highlight
specific knowledge gaps. Furthermore, in a digital economy, deficiencies in digital literacy
and resource mobilization (Iwara, 2025; Zhang et al., 2024) create additional cognitive
barriers. These gaps do not merely represent a lack of tools; they restrict an individual’s
capacity to recognize and exploit diverse digital entrepreneurial opportunities.
Institutionally, this misalignment reproduces itself over time. Educational systems
that prioritize formal employment as the primary “legitimate” outcome continue to
emphasize traditional career preparation. This emphasis reinforces social perceptions that
devalue entrepreneurship, allowing these cognitive biases to persist.
Market access as structural institutional weakness.  Market-related barriers
illustrate how institutional deficits across all dimensions converge to isolate rural youth
from economic opportunities. The “limited market access” for rural enterprise products
(Iwara, 2025; Leul et al., 2023; Ratompoarison et al., 2023) stems not only from
geographic distance, but also from weak institutional infrastructure. Specifically, the
absence of standardized quality controls, “organized marketing channels” and value chain
coordination prevents rural producers from reaching markets beyond the local level
(Ratompoarison et al., 2023). This structural isolation is further intensified by persistent
employment pressures. As shrinking economies push youth toward alternative informal
livelihoods (Arthur -Holmes & Yeboah, 2025), and rural depopulation renders many local
businesses unsustainable (Dragin et al., 2024), the consumer base for new ventures
becomes increasingly precarious. These supply chain disruptions and “marketing
challenges” (Ratompoarison et al., 2023) reflect institutional voids. In the absence of
coordinated support structures, whether formal or informal, youth -led ventures struggle to
secure stable market access necessary for long-term viability.
Institutional interdependencies and systemic exclusion. These barriers do not
operate independently but reinforce one another . Regulative exclusion from finance
prevents youth from overcoming infrastructure deficits; normative devaluation of
entrepreneurship reduces demand for entrepreneurship education; cognitive deficiencies
within educational systems leave graduates unprepared for available opportunities ; and
market barriers make entrepreneurship appear unviable, reinforcing normative pressures
toward migration. This creates an institutional poverty trap where each barrier strengthens
others, systematically limiting rural youth's access to entrepreneurial pathways.

5. Conclusions

This systematic literature review explores a central question: what barriers do
young entrepreneurs encounter in rural environments? Our synthesis of 57 studies
published over the past decade reveals that these barriers relate mostly to financial
access, infrastructure deficits, educational misalignment, social and cultural norms,
governance deficit, and market constraints. These barriers are not separate issues; they
interact and reinforce each other.

The bibliometric analysis reveals several structural imbalances in the field. Most
notably, research is densely concentrated in Africa. This focus is understandable given the
region’s urgent policy challenges, but it leaves us with a thin evidence base for Asia, Latin
America, and Eastern Europe. Such geographic narrowness makes it difficult to know if
current findings apply globally. Furthermore, research networks show significant
fragmentation, with several countries conducting isolated studies without international
collaboration. These "knowledge silos" make comparative learning difficult. Without more
collaboration, it remains unclear which barriers are unique to specific locations and which
are universal across the rural settings.
Implications for policy and practice.  Our findings show that tackling barriers one by
one is rarely effective. A financial assistance program, for instance, is unlikely to succeed if
it ignores the crumbling infrastructure around it. Similarly, training initiatives often fall short
if they fail to address the social norms that discourage youth from starting businesses in
the first place. Effective policy, therefore, needs to move away from “one -size-fits-all”
solutions. Instead, it should prioritize integrated, place -based strategies that account for
how different obstacles mutually reinforce each other within a specific rural context.
For practitioners and development organizations, support should intersect with the
lived realities of rural youth.  Mentorship and training work best when they move away from
“top-down”, standardized models. To be truly effective, these programs need to address
the specific cultural expectations, gender roles, and community structures that define what
“success” looks like in a particular village or region.
Limitations. As with all systematic reviews, this study faces certain methodological
constraints. The reliance on the Web of Science Core Collection and the exclusion of grey
literature might have narrowed the practical perspective. Also, our ten -year timeframe,
though justified by the need for contemporary relevance, means we have not fully engaged
with earlier foundational works that continue to shape theoretical debates. The dominance
of studies from certain geographic regions also restricts the generalizability of the findings.
Gender appears frequently in the literature, but it is often treated as a peripheral theme
instead of being integrated across analyses, which limits understanding of how it interacts
with other barriers.
Directions for future research.  Future studies would benefit from broader
geographic coverage, particularly studies that allow comparison across different rural
contexts. Such work could help clarify which barriers are common across settings and
which are influenced by specific institutional and economic environments. There is also
scope for more integrated analytical approaches that examine how barriers interact and
reinforce one another, rather than considering them in isolation . Finally, longitudinal
research could add much -needed depth by tracing how young entrepreneurs adapt and
persist over time, revealing the dynamics of resilience within rural entrepreneurial
ecosystems.
