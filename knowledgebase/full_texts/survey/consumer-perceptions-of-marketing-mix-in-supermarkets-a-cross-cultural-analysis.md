The primary objective of this study was to examine whether significant
differences exist in the perception of supermarket marketing mix elements
between consumers in Croatia and Tanzania. The analysis focused on five key
dimensions (product, price, distribution, promotion, and personnel) with the
aim of identifying the factors contributing to consumer satisfaction or
dissatisfaction within distinct market contexts. The research employed a
quantitative methodology, gathering data from 991 respondents (400 from
Croatia and 591 from Tanzania) through a structured survey based on a five-
point Likert scale. The findings revealed statistically significant differences
across all five marketing mix dimensions. Tanzanian respondents consistently
expressed more favorable perceptions, particularly in relation to promotional
activities and personnel interactions. Exploratory factor analysis suggested
three broader dimensions, whereas confirmatory factor analysis provided
stronger support for the theoretically specified five-factor structure. Multi-
group CFA further supported metric and scalar measurement invariance
across the Croatian and Tanzanian samples. The findings highlight the
importance of adapting retail marketing strategies, particularly
communication, service delivery, and promotional activities, to the specific
characteristics of different national market contexts.

1 Introduction
Consumer perceptions of marketing activities play a critical role in shaping
satisfaction, loyalty, and purchasing behavior, particularly within the retail sector,
which is increasingly influenced by local economic conditions and cultural norms.
Supermarkets, as a dominant retail format, represent a point of convergence between
global marketing practices and local consumer expectations. The way consumers
interpret and respond to the elements of the marketing mix (product, price,
distribution, promotion, and personnel) can vary considerably across markets.
Understanding these cross-cultural differences is essential for developing marketing
strategies that are not only effective but contextually relevant. While numerous studies
have examined the marketing mix, comparative research that investigates consumer
perceptions across culturally and economically distinct regions remains limited. This is

the need to explore how consumers from different backgrounds
perceive and prioritize various marketing factors within the
supermarket context.
Consumers in Tanzania, much like those in many sub-Saharan
African markets, tend to operate in environments characterized by
lower average income, intense retail competition, and a
heightened emphasis on personal interaction with personnel
(Kupeni and Magali, 2022; Nyangarika, 2016). In contrast,
Croatian consumers navigate a more established retail landscape,
where brand familiarity and service standardization support a
more analytical approach to price and product evaluation
(Pavlić et al., 2020; Vretenar et al., 2025). These differences
suggest that perceptions of the marketing mix are likely to
diverge significantly between the two groups, influencing overall
satisfaction with the shopping experience.
Cross-cultural theories offer a useful lens for understanding
why consumers in different countries interpret elements of the
marketing mix differently. According to Hofstede’s cultural
dimensions, Croatia scores higher on individualism and
uncertainty avoidance, while Tanzania displays stronger
collectivist tendencies and higher power distance (Hofstede, 2001;
Hofstede et al., 2010). These differences imply that Croatian
consumers may evaluate product attributes, pricing strategies and
promotions more analytically, whereas Tanzanian consumers may
assign greater importance to interpersonal interaction, trust and
social value. Similarly, Schwartz’s value theory suggests that
societies with stronger embeddedness and hierarchy (as in many
East African contexts) place greater emphasis on social
relationships and normative expectations, which can heighten the
importance of personnel behavior and promotional engagement
(Schwartz, 1992; Schwartz, 2006). In contrast, Croatian
consumers, shaped by values of autonomy and egalitarianism,
may respond more strongly to objective cues of product quality,
price fairness and assortment variety. Integrating these
frameworks helps explain why each of the 5Ps (product, price,
place, promotion and people) may be perceived differently across
the two markets. Although Hofstede’s dataset does not provide
direct scores for Tanzania, studies on East African and Sub-
Saharan cultural patterns indicate generally higher collectivism,
stronger power distance, and a greater emphasis on interpersonal
relationships and community orientation (Minkov, 2018). These
theoretical perspectives provide a contextual framework for
interpreting potential cross-national differences in consumer
perceptions. However, cultural dimensions were not directly
measured in the present study; therefore, cultural explanations are
treated as theoretically informed interpretations rather than
empirically tested mechanisms.
Marketing theory traditionally conceptualizes the marketing
mix as a set of controllable instruments that organizations use
to influence consumer behavior and market outcomes. The
concept was originally introduced by E. Jerome McCarthy
through the well-known 4P framework consisting of product,
price, place, and promotion (McCarthy, 1960). Over time, this
framework has become one of the most widely used conceptual
models for analyzing marketing strategies in retail and service
industries (Kotler and Keller, 2016).
As retail environments increasingly evolved toward service-
oriented interactions, scholars such as Bernard H. Booms and
Mary J. Bitner expanded the traditional model into the extended
marketing mix framework, introducing additional elements such
as people, process, and physical evidence (Booms and Bitner,
1981). Within retail environments, particularly supermarkets,
the “people” dimension is especially important because direct
interaction between employees and customers strongly
influences perceptions of service quality and the overall
shopping experience (Zeithaml et al., 2018).
From the perspective of consumer behavior theory, marketing
stimuli affect consumers through cognitive and emotional
evaluation processes. According to Philip Kotler, consumer
responses to marketing activities depend not only on objective
product characteristics but also on perceived value,
psychological interpretation, and situational factors present
during the shopping experience (Kotler and Keller, 2016). In
retail settings, these evaluations often occur simultaneously
across multiple marketing dimensions, including product
assortment, pricing strategy, promotional communication, and
interpersonal service interaction.
Consumer satisfaction itself is commonly explained through
the expectation–disconfirmation theory developed by Richard
L. Oliver. This theoretical framework suggests that satisfaction
emerges when perceived performance meets or exceeds prior
consumer expectations (Oliver, 1980; Oliver, 1997). In the
context of supermarket retailing, expectations are shaped by
previous shopping experiences, economic conditions, cultural
norms, and the competitive structure of the local retail market.
Consequently, perceptions of marketing mix elements may
differ significantly across countries and cultural contexts.
Differences in economic development, consumer purchasing
power, and cultural norms influence how consumers interpret
pricing fairness, promotional incentives, service interaction, and
product quality (De Mooij, 2019).
This study aims to examine how consumers in Croatia and
Tanzania perceive key marketing mix elements in supermarkets
and to what extent these perceptions contribute to satisfaction
or dissatisfaction. Using a structured quantitative approach
incorporating exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis,
multi-group measurement invariance testing, MANOVA,
ANOVA, and independent-samples t-tests, this research provides
empirical insights into similarities and differences between the
two national samples. The findings contribute to the existing
body of knowledge by clarifying how supermarket marketing
mix perceptions differ across distinct national market contexts,
while also showing how advanced quantitative techniques can be
used to examine the underlying measurement structure of
consumer perceptions. By integrating marketing mix theory,
consumer satisfaction theory, and cross-cultural perspectives, the
study provides a broader explanation of how supermarket
marketing strategies are interpreted by consumers in different
socio-economic environments.

These factors collectively determine perceived value and

The transformation of supermarkets into customer-

the importance of marketing strategies that integrate multiple
dimensions of consumer value. Studies show that customer
satisfaction is not determined by a single marketing variable but
rather by the combined influence of product availability, price
fairness, promotional communication, store layout, and service
interaction. As a result, understanding the interaction between
marketing mix elements has become a central research topic in
contemporary retail and consumer behavior studies (Grewal
et al., 2017; Verhoef et al., 2015).
Although numerous studies investigate individual determinants
of retail satisfaction, recent research increasingly suggests that
marketing mix elements should be analyzed as an integrated
system rather than isolated variables. In supermarket settings,
product quality, price competitiveness, store accessibility,
promotional strategies, and employee interaction operate
simultaneously and jointly influence consumers’ perceptions of
value and satisfaction (Hübner et al., 2016; Roggeveen et al., 2021).
At the same time, global retail markets are experiencing rapid
structural changes driven by digitalization, sustainability
initiatives, and increasing competition among retail chains.
These developments have intensified the need for retailers to
understand how different marketing mix components contribute
to customer satisfaction in diverse market environments
(Pantano and Vannucci, 2019; Shankar et al., 2021).

Product, price, promotion, and location have a positive and
significant impact on consumer loyalty in supermarkets,
explaining as much as 87.9% of the influence on consumer
loyalty (Haryadi et al. 2021). The study by Wahab et al. (2024)
shows that key factors influencing consumer choice in
supermarkets include discounts, price, and location, with
customers expecting a variety of high-quality products and
improved services such as spacious parking. Product, place, and
promotion variables significantly affect customer satisfaction,
while price does not (Risal and Aqsa, 2021). Functional value
(price/value for money) has a stronger positive influence than
social value (Slack et al., 2020). Attitudes, price, and quality
have significant direct effects on satisfaction and indirect effects
on purchase intention in South Korea, but not in Tanzania,
indicating the need for market-specific strategies (Marius et al.,
2020). Mushtaq and Salar (2022) concluded that personnel,
promotion, and price are the most influential factors affecting
consumer behavior when shopping in supermarkets. On the
other hand, the study by Waithaka and Wamalwa (2020) found
that price, followed by location and product, represents the most
influential marketing mix strategies for mini-supermarket
customers in Nairobi County. In his research, McCollough
(2025) explains that promotion and product form is a powerful
combination in marketing, as they jointly create a sense of
positive dissatisfaction in consumers, encouraging repeat
purchases and a desire for something new.
Almost 55% of consumer loyalty in supermarkets is
determined by brand perception, value creation, brand image,
trust, price, and location (Rachmad et al., 2023). Products and
promotions have less influence on purchasing decisions in
Islamic retail, while price and store atmosphere have a
significant impact (Monoarfa et al., 2023). The study by Larson
and Batterson (2024) highlights that promotion and the
psychological aspects of the product are the most influential
elements of the marketing mix, as they have the greatest impact
on shaping consumer perception and decision-making. The
study by Fajrin and Aprianingsih (2024) reveals that price,
people, process, and physical evidence significantly influence
customer satisfaction, while product, place, and promotion have
a nuanced impact. Customers are motivated to visit
supermarkets due to price, quality, location, hygiene, and
personal experience (Sanapala et al., 2023). Also, product, price,
promotion, and retail service influence supermarket customers’
purchasing decisions (Sugito et al., 2022). Customers expect
variety, quality, spacious parking, and eco-friendly products,
which supermarkets must address to achieve satisfaction
(Varalakshmi et al., 2024). Product, place, promotion, and
personnel significantly affect customer satisfaction (Mondir
et al., 2023). Research by Gustama (2021) shows that product
and price variables have a positive and significant influence, as
does promotion, while location does not. Customer satisfaction
is significantly affected by product, price, promotion, and
personnel, while location has a minimal impact (Devina and
Yanuar, 2023). Good product quality, affordable prices aligned
with the purchasing power of the target audience, easily
accessible locations, and effective promotions influence
consumers to buy products and remain loyal (Dimelda et al.,
2024). Brand image, service quality, promotion, and price
influence customer satisfaction (Murtini, 2025). Price
perception, promotion, and service quality have a positive and
statistically significant influence on consumer loyalty (Hakim
et al., 2023). Product, price, location, and employees
significantly affect customer satisfaction (Solihiyah et al., 2024).
The study by Htwe (2020) shows that product offerings, price,
and presentation have a significant positive effect on customer
satisfaction, while place, promotions and events, and personnel
do not. Price, place, and product have a positive and significant
effect on loyalty, while promotion does not (Sutianingsih and
Samodra, 2021). Marketing mix elements (product, price,
promotion) have a direct and significant positive effect on
consumer loyalty, while location has an insignificant effect
(Malelak et al., 2021). Product, promotion, place, physical
evidence, and process variables significantly affect customer
loyalty, while price and employee variables have an insignificant
impact (Anwar and Saleh, 2022).

Recent literature confirms that marketing mix elements
remain fundamental determinants of customer satisfaction in
supermarket environments. However, studies increasingly
demonstrate that the relative importance of individual marketing
dimensions varies across markets and consumer segments.
While product assortment and price competitiveness are
frequently identified as primary drivers of supermarket choice,
promotional incentives, store accessibility, and employee
interaction may become more influential depending on local
economic conditions and cultural expectations (Pantano and
Vannucci, 2019; Roggeveen et al., 2021). Despite the growing
body of research on retail marketing and consumer satisfaction,
several important gaps remain. First, most empirical studies
focus on single-country contexts, limiting the understanding of
how marketing mix elements are perceived across culturally and
economically diverse markets. Second, many studies analyze
individual components of the marketing mix rather than
examining the combined influence of all five dimensions within
supermarket environments. Third, comparative studies between
European retail markets and emerging African economies
remain relatively scarce. Addressing these gaps is important
because consumer responses to marketing strategies are shaped
not only by marketing activities themselves but also by broader
socio-economic and cultural conditions. Therefore, this study
addresses these limitations by providing a cross-national
comparative analysis of consumer perceptions of all five
marketing mix elements within supermarket environments,
using an integrated analytical framework. In doing so, the study
contributes to the marketing literature by extending
understanding of perception-based satisfaction across culturally
and economically distinct retail contexts. It also contributes to
the analytics literature by demonstrating how exploratory and
confirmatory factor analysis, measurement invariance testing,
and multivariate group comparisons can be integrated within
cross-national retail research.
3 Methodology of research
3.1 Hypothesis development
Based on the reviewed literature, the key elements of the
marketing mix (product, price, place, promotion, and people)
emerge as central drivers of consumer satisfaction in the
supermarket and retail context. Although individual studies
emphasize different components, the collective evidence
highlights consistent cross-contextual patterns. These insights
provide a theoretical foundation for the present study and guide
the development of the research hypotheses, which aim to
examine whether these marketing mix elements are perceived
differently across the Croatian and Tanzanian markets. This
study contributes to the existing literature by integrating
marketing mix theory, cross-cultural consumer behavior
frameworks, and perception-based satisfaction models within a
single comparative empirical design. By examining all five
marketing mix dimensions simultaneously in two culturally and
economically distinct markets, the research provides a more
comprehensive understanding of how retail marketing strategies
are interpreted by consumers operating in different socio-
economic environments. More specifically, the study makes
three distinct contributions. It contributes to marketing
literature by providing a rare cross-cultural comparison of
supermarket marketing mix perception between a European and
an African market. Also, it extends existing retail research by
analyzing all five marketing mix dimensions simultaneously
rather than focusing on individual elements in isolation. It
contributes to analytics-oriented research by showing how a
multi-method quantitative design can integrate measurement
validation with multivariate and univariate tests of cross-
national differences within the same dataset.
3.1.1 Perception of marketing mix elements in
supermarkets: a comparison between African and
European consumers
Cultural values, such as individualism vs. collectivism and
power distance, strongly shape how consumers perceive product
quality, pricing strategies, promotion, and distribution across
different markets. These values differ significantly between
regions such as Europe and Africa, making it clear that
marketing strategies cannot be generalized but must be tailored
to each cultural context (Asamoah and Chovancová, 2016).
Roffman (2023) compared consumer responses from multiple
countries to identical marketing messages and product formats,
concluding that notable perceptual differences exist across
markets, reinforcing the need for localized strategies in both
African and European contexts. Li (2024) further emphasizes
that given the profound cultural disparities across global
markets, standard marketing campaigns are unlikely to resonate

H1: There are statistically significant differences in the

3.1.2 Perceived product quality and variety among
African and European supermarket consumers
The study by Truong (2025) non-directly confirmed that
African consumers place more importance on emotional and
symbolic product attributes, while European consumers tend to
adopt a more rational evaluation approach. Furthermore, Rajavi
et al. (2024) reported that product variety substantially enhances
brand perception in developing markets (such as those in
Africa) more than in mature European markets. According to a
Euromonitor International (2025), Tanzanian consumers
demonstrate higher loyalty towards supermarkets offering
broader product assortments, while European shoppers
prioritize specialized and high-quality product lines.
H2: Consumers in Tanzania perceive product quality and
variety more positively than consumers in Croatia.
3.1.3 Perceptions of price accessibility and
promotional pricing among African and European
consumers
In developing markets such as those in Africa, emotional
responses tend to amplify price sensitivity and trigger more
impulse purchases (Zafar et al., 2025). Zhang et al. (2024)
conducted a global study which found that African consumers
exhibit greater variability in price sensitivity compared to
European consumers, particularly during early shopping hours.
Similarly, Candeias and Alonso (2024) applied pricing sensitivity
models and demonstrated that African consumers react more
strongly to price changes than their European counterparts.

In South Africa, store proximity and interior design are among
the strongest drivers of store visitation, far more so than in many
European markets (Musasa and Tlapana, 2023). Beckers and

accessibility plays a critical role in shaping customer value

3.1.5 Perceptions of promotional activities in
supermarkets: Africa vs. Europe
Giveaways and loyalty programs are dominant purchase
motivators among African consumers, often more impactful
than conventional advertising (Edel et al., 2018). Sudhaker et al.
(2020) found that promotional campaigns strongly influence
brand perception and purchase behavior in countries like
Ghana, whereas European consumers exhibit higher expectations
and lower emotional responsiveness to such incentives. Eze
(2024) also demonstrated that African consumers show stronger
emotional reactions to promotional stimuli than their European
counterparts.
H5: Promotional activities (discounts, flyers, sweepstakes) are

than by Croatian consumers.
3.1.6 Perceptions of personnel professionalism
and friendliness in supermarkets

employees as a central driver of consumer loyalty, an approach

communication, service behavior, and employee friendliness are
among the strongest predictors of customer satisfaction and
loyalty, according to Noel and Govender (2024). Rezki et al.

interaction significantly enhances customer loyalty—especially
when supported by trust and a positive corporate image.
H6: The professionalism and friendliness of supermarket
personnel are perceived significantly more positively by
Tanzanian consumers than by Croatian consumers.

3.2 Data collection
The research was conducted in 2025 in two countries: Croatia
and Tanzania. The dataset used in this study consists of primary
survey data collected specifically for the purpose of analyzing
consumer perceptions of supermarket marketing mix elements.
The data were collected through a structured questionnaire,
available in both physical and online formats (Google Forms),
designed to capture consumer attitudes toward five key
dimensions: product, price, distribution, promotion, and
personnel. A quantitative research design was employed to
analyze differences in perceptions of the supermarket marketing
mix among consumers from two culturally and economically
diverse environments. Respondents were recruited using non-
probability convenience sampling. According to institutional
guidelines and Article 26 of the Code of Ethics of Josip Juraj
Strossmayer University of Osijek, this study did not require
formal ethical approval, as it involved anonymous survey data
and did not include sensitive personal information. The research
was conducted in accordance with national and international
ethical standards. Participation was voluntary, and informed
consent was obtained from all respondents prior to data
collection. For online surveys, consent was provided
electronically before completing the questionnaire, while for
paper-based surveys, consent was implied through voluntary
completion of the questionnaire.
Table 3 includes the demographic characteristics of
respondents in Tanzania and Croatia. A total of 991 valid
responses were collected, with 400 from Croatia and 591 from
Tanzania. The dataset therefore represents a cross-national
sample that enables direct comparison between two distinct
retail environments. The relatively large sample size enhances
the robustness of the statistical analysis and supports the
application of multivariate techniques. The inclusion criteria
required respondents to be adults and regular supermarket
shoppers (at least once a month). Demographic data included
gender, age, employment status, and monthly income, to allow
for additional control of variables and insight into contextual
differences. In addition to demographic variables, the dataset
includes multiple observed variables measuring consumer
perceptions across the five marketing mix dimensions. Each
dimension is operationalized through several survey items,
resulting in a multidimensional dataset suitable for factor
analysis and cross-national comparison. This structure enables
both variable-level analysis (e.g., mean comparisons) and
examination of the underlying measurement structure through
factor analysis. The dataset is cross-sectional in nature and
captures consumer perceptions at a single point in time. While
this design enables comparison between groups, it also reflects
real-world consumer evaluations under current market
conditions, which enhances the practical relevance of
the findings.

3.3 Measurement of the constructs
The measurement model was designed to ensure consistency
across both national samples, allowing for direct comparability
of responses. All items were standardized and adapted to
maintain conceptual equivalence across cultural contexts. For
the purposes of the research, a survey instrument was developed
to measure the perception of marketing mix elements,
structured into five dimensions (product, price, distribution,
promotion, and personnel), using a 5-point Likert scale
(1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree).
Table 4 presents the dimensions that encompass a series of
statements reflecting specific aspects of consumer behavior and
their preferences when choosing a supermarket. The statements
are designed to examine the influence of individual elements of
the marketing mix on consumer choice, as well as their
willingness to change shopping habits in the presence of more
favorable alternatives. The product dimension includes
perceptions of quality and assortment variety, while the price
dimension captures attitudes toward pricing, preferences for
store brands, and the importance of promotional benefits such
as discounts. Distribution is operationalized through the
geographic and spatial context, specifically, the proximity and
organization of the retail space. The promotion dimension
addresses the influence of advertising and sales incentives,
while the personnel dimension explores the importance of
personnel expertise and friendliness in shaping the perceived
value associated with a particular supermarket. The statements
were structured to capture the cognitive, affective, and
behavioral aspects of consumer perception. The scales were
designed to be universally understandable in both cultural
contexts (Croatia and Tanzania), and prior to the final
distribution of the survey, a comprehension and cultural
adaptation test was conducted.
3.4 Methodology and statistical analysis
Since all data were collected through self-reported responses at
a single point in time using the same structured questionnaire,
there is a potential risk of common method bias (CMB), which
can compromise the validity of the results. To mitigate this risk,
several measures were implemented:
• The questionnaire was structured into clearly separated
sections, each preceded by a brief explanation of the
corresponding dimension, reducing the likelihood of
automatic or patterned responses.
• The order of items was randomized to prevent respondents
from giving similar answers due to response patterns.
• Anonymity and confidentiality were guaranteed,
encouraging honest responses and minimizing the risk of
socially desirable answering.
• Additionally, a statistical test was conducted to assess the
presence of common method bias. Harman’s single-factor
test was used as part of the exploratory factor analysis to
determine whether a single factor accounted for most of
the variance in the data—an indicator that would suggest
a potential bias.
• These procedural and statistical controls enhanced the
credibility of the findings and supported the validity of
the constructs used in the study.

Statistical analyses were conducted using JASP, IBM SPSS
Statistics, and Python. JASP and IBM SPSS Statistics were used
for descriptive statistics, exploratory factor analysis, MANOVA,
ANOVA, and independent-samples comparisons, whereas
confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), multi-group CFA,
measurement invariance testing, and the calculation of
composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE)
were performed in Python using maximum-likelihood
estimation and numerical optimization routines. The data
analysis was carried out in several stages, with the overarching
goal of examining differences in the perception of marketing
mix elements between Croatian and Tanzanian consumers.
The initial phase involved descriptive statistical analysis. Key
measures such as mean, standard deviation, standard error, and
coefficient of variation were calculated separately for each
country. This provided a preliminary overview of the data
distribution and allowed for an initial assessment of group-level
differences. To assess the validity of the constructs, exploratory
factor analysis was conducted using Promax rotation. Prior to
running the analysis, the suitability of the dataset was evaluated
through the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure and Bartlett’s test of
sphericity. This phase of the analysis aimed to identify the
underlying structure of the variables associated with the
marketing mix components.
Given the cross-national nature of the study, confirmatory
factor analysis (CFA) and multi-group CFA were additionally
conducted using maximum-likelihood (ML) estimation to
evaluate the measurement structure and measurement invariance
across the Croatian and Tanzanian samples. First, the
theoretically specified five-factor measurement model,
comprising product, price, distribution, promotion, and people,
was evaluated and compared with the three-factor structure
identified through EFA. Model fit was assessed using the chi-
square statistic (χ2), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis
Index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation
(RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR).
Subsequently, measurement invariance across countries was
examined sequentially at the configural, metric, and scalar levels.
Configural invariance assessed whether the same factor structure
applied across the two groups; metric invariance constrained
factor loadings to equality across groups; and scalar invariance
additionally constrained item intercepts. Because chi-square
difference tests are sensitive to large sample sizes, invariance
decisions were primarily based on changes in approximate fit
indices. Following Chen (2007), changes of no more than.010 in
CFI and.015 in RMSEA, together with small changes in SRMR,
were taken as evidence that increasingly restrictive invariance
constraints did not substantially deteriorate model fit.
To explore whether the independent variable (country of
residence) had a combined effect on the set of dependent
variables (product, price, distribution, promotion, and
personnel), a multivariate analysis of variance was performed.
Pillai’s Trace was selected as the test statistic, given its
robustness in the presence of potential violations of the
assumptions related to homogeneity of variance-covariance
matrices. For a more detailed examination of each individual
dimension of the marketing mix, a series of univariate analyses
of variance were conducted. This step provided further insight
into whether significant differences existed between groups for
each specific marketing element. Additionally, independent
samples t-tests were applied to compare the mean values
between the Croatian and Tanzanian respondents across all key
dimensions. These tests served to statistically confirm differences
between the groups, with effect sizes estimated using Cohen’s d
to assess the magnitude of those differences. Collectively, these
analytical steps contributed to a comprehensive understanding
of differences in consumer perceptions of supermarket
marketing strategies across the Croatian and Tanzanian samples.

To further assess the appropriateness of applying factor

4.2 Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)
The core findings of the factor analysis are presented in the
factor loading table, which illustrates how variables cluster
together based on shared variance, revealing the underlying
latent factors (Table 7). Using Promax rotation, which allows for
correlations among factors, three distinct factors were extracted.
The first groups variables related to personnel, store appearance,
and promotional activities; the second includes items connected
to pricing; and the third captures those measuring product
quality and assortment. A loading threshold of 0.40 was applied
for interpretative clarity, and all variables loaded cleanly onto
one of the three factors. These exploratory results suggest three
broader groupings among the observed items: (1) service and
ambient impression, combining personnel, promotion, and
aspects of distribution; (2) price; and (3) product. The EFA
therefore indicates that some of the theoretically distinct
marketing mix dimensions may overlap empirically. However,
because EFA is exploratory in nature, this three-factor solution
was subsequently evaluated against the theoretically specified
five-factor structure using CFA before selecting the
measurement structure for the cross-national comparisons.
Although the measurement instrument was designed
according to the five dimensions of the marketing mix, the
factor analysis extracted only three latent factors. This
exploratory pattern suggests potential empirical overlap among
promotion, personnel, and aspects of the store environment,

them as a single construct. Such integration may be particularly
relevant in emerging-market retail contexts, where interpersonal

benchmarks for retail performance differ between emerging and
more mature European markets (Kadigi et al., 2024).
The relationship between the exploratory and confirmatory
factor analyses provides an important methodological insight.
Classical marketing frameworks conceptualize product, price,
place, promotion, and people as theoretically distinct marketing
instruments (Booms and Bitner, 1981; McCarthy, 1960). In the
present study, the EFA identified three broader dimensions,
suggesting empirical overlap among several marketing mix
elements, particularly personnel, promotion, and aspects of the
store environment. However, the subsequent CFA showed that
the theoretically specified five-factor model provided a
substantially better representation of the data than the
alternative three-factor model. Accordingly, the EFA findings
should not be interpreted as evidence that the traditional five-
dimensional structure should be replaced. Rather, they suggest
that some theoretically distinct dimensions may overlap in
consumers’ evaluations while remaining distinguishable
constructs within the confirmatory measurement model. The
study therefore does not reject the traditional marketing mix
framework but provides evidence that its theoretically distinct
components may exhibit some empirical overlap in consumer
perceptions. This distinction supports the use of the original five
marketing mix dimensions in the subsequent cross-national
analyses and illustrates the complementary roles of exploratory
and confirmatory factor analysis.
The observed cross-national patterns can be considered in
relation to previous research, while recognizing that the
explanatory mechanisms were not directly measured in the
present study. Prior studies have reported differences in the role
of interpersonal service, promotional activities, and functional
product attributes across different retail environments. Marius
et al. (2020) identified differences in consumer satisfaction and
purchase intentions across national contexts, while Sirajuddin
et al. (2023) and Mondir et al. (2023) emphasized the relevance
of promotional and marketing mix elements in shaping
consumer evaluations. Research from Tanzania has also
highlighted the importance of relationship quality and
interpersonal trust in consumer evaluations (Tegambwage and
Kasoga, 2022). In European retail contexts, previous research
has emphasized product quality, price, brand-related attributes,
and consumer segmentation as relevant aspects of consumer
evaluation (Pavlić et al., 2020; Swoboda and Batton, 2020).
Furthermore, previous research has demonstrated the relevance
of promotional stimuli for consumer purchase intentions and
decisions (Suyanto and Dewi, 2023). Taken together, these
studies provide useful contextual perspectives for interpreting
the differences observed between the Croatian and Tanzanian
samples. However, the present study extends this literature
primarily by demonstrating statistically significant cross-national
differences in perceptions of the marketing mix rather than by
identifying their cultural causes. Because cultural values,
interpersonal orientation, economic conditions, and related
contextual mechanisms were not directly measured, the present
findings cannot establish that these factors caused the observed
differences. They should therefore be regarded as theoretically
informed contextual explanations that require direct empirical
examination in future research.

Truong (2025) provide a relevant theoretical context, the higher
personnel evaluations observed among Tanzanian respondents
cannot be empirically attributed to cultural differences on the
basis of the present data.
Perceptions of products and pricing also differed significantly
between the two national samples, although the effect sizes were
smaller than for promotion and personnel. Tanzanian
respondents reported more favorable evaluations of both
dimensions. Previous international research has shown that
evaluations of product attributes and prices may vary across
markets with different economic and competitive characteristics
(Davvetas et al., 2023). Nevertheless, the present study did not
directly examine market competition, purchasing power, price
expectations, or product availability as explanatory variables.
These factors therefore represent possible contextual
interpretations rather than empirically established explanations
for the observed country differences.
A significant cross-national difference was also observed for
distribution, encompassing supermarket location, accessibility,
appearance, and organization. Previous research indicates that
accessibility and retail service characteristics can influence
consumer evaluations and repeat-purchase intentions (Sirican
and Wangbenmad, 2025). However, the present study did not
measure urban structure, transportation availability, or other
infrastructural conditions. Therefore, these factors cannot be
identified as direct explanations for the observed difference
between Croatian and Tanzanian respondents.
The combined EFA and CFA findings provide a more nuanced
picture of the measurement structure. While the exploratory
analysis suggested three broader groupings and therefore
indicated overlap among several marketing mix elements, the
confirmatory analysis showed that the theoretically specified
five-factor model represented the data substantially better than
the three-factor alternative. The five dimensions were therefore
retained for the cross-national hypothesis tests. The exploratory
overlap remains informative, but it should not be interpreted as
evidence that the traditional five-dimensional measurement
structure was rejected.
Supermarkets aiming to enhance customer satisfaction and
loyalty should carefully consider market-specific consumer
perceptions and adapt their strategies accordingly, particularly in
areas such as promotion and employee interaction. Beyond the
retail sector, these findings have broader implications for
international marketing by demonstrating that the same
marketing mix dimensions may be evaluated differently across
national market contexts. The findings therefore support a
context-sensitive approach to international retail strategy.
Cultural values, economic conditions, and other contextual
characteristics may contribute to these differences, but their
specific explanatory roles require direct empirical examination in
future research.
5.1 Theoretical implication
This study contributes to the theoretical understanding of
marketing mix perception in supermarket retailing by
providing evidence from two distinct national market contexts,
Croatia and Tanzania. The results demonstrate significant
cross-national differences across all five examined marketing
mix dimensions. Importantly, the combination of exploratory
and confirmatory factor analysis provides a nuanced
contribution to the measurement of the marketing mix.
Although the EFA suggested empirical overlap among several
dimensions, the CFA showed that the theoretically specified
five-factor model provided a substantially better representation
of the data than the alternative three-factor model. The
findings therefore support retaining product, price, distribution,
promotion, and personnel as theoretically distinct constructs
while recognizing that some dimensions may overlap in
consumers’ evaluations. Furthermore, the establishment of
metric and scalar measurement invariance provides evidence
that the five-factor structure operates with sufficient
comparability across the Croatian and Tanzanian samples to
support cross-national comparisons. The study therefore
extends the application of the marketing mix framework to
two different national retail environments. Cultural and socio-
economic theories may offer possible explanations for the
observed differences, but because these mechanisms were not
directly measured, the present study does not establish culture
as their causal source. Future research can extend these
findings by incorporating direct measures of cultural values
and other contextual variables.
5.2 Practical implication
The results provide practical guidance for supermarket
retailers operating in different national markets. Tanzanian
respondents reported more favorable perceptions across all five
marketing mix dimensions, with the largest differences observed

5.3 Limitation and direction for future
research
Although this study provides valuable insights, several
limitations should be acknowledged.
The sampling procedure relied on non-probability
convenience sampling through social media platforms, meaning
that respondents self-selected into the study. As a result, the
sample may not fully represent the broader population of
supermarket consumers, especially individuals who are less
active online or who do not use social media. Therefore, caution
is required when generalizing the findings beyond the digitally
engaged segment of the population. While the research
encompasses various types of retail centers, it does not
distinguish between them (e.g., hypermarkets, discount stores,
shopping malls). Future research could focus on different retail
formats to explore whether perception patterns vary across retail
types. Even though a two-country comparison was conducted,
the data were collected through self-reported responses at a
single point in time, which may introduce response bias.
Longitudinal research designs could provide deeper insights into
how perceptions evolve over time. The study is based solely on
quantitative methods. An additional limitation is that cultural
dimensions were not directly measured at the individual level.
Consequently, the observed differences should be interpreted as
cross-national differences between the Croatian and Tanzanian
samples rather than as direct effects of culture. The cultural
frameworks discussed in the study provide theoretically
informed contextual interpretations but were not empirically
tested as explanatory mechanisms. Future studies should
incorporate validated measures of cultural values and relevant
economic and market characteristics to determine which factors
account for the observed cross-national differences. Qualitative
approaches, including focus groups and in-depth interviews,
could further contribute to understanding the mechanisms
underlying these differences. Future research could expand the
analysis by including additional variables such as the impact of
digital marketing, sustainability initiatives, or brand trust, to
provide a more comprehensive view of the factors influencing
consumer satisfaction in diverse retail environments.
6 Conclusion
This study provides evidence of significant cross-national
differences in consumer perceptions of supermarket marketing
mix elements between Croatia and Tanzania. Differences were
identified across product, price, distribution, promotion, and
personnel, with the largest disparities observed for promotion
and personnel. The measurement analyses further showed that,
although the exploratory factor analysis suggested overlap
among several marketing dimensions, the theoretically
specified five-factor model provided a substantially better fit
than the alternative three-factor model in the confirmatory
analysis. Moreover, the establishment of metric and scalar
measurement invariance supported the comparability of the five
marketing mix dimensions across the two national samples and
provided a methodological basis for the subsequent cross-
national comparisons.
The findings emphasize that supermarket marketing strategies
should be responsive to differences in consumer perceptions
across national markets. However, the study does not establish
that the observed differences are caused by cultural factors, as
cultural dimensions were not directly measured. Cultural and
socio-economic frameworks can therefore be used only as
possible contextual explanations for the observed patterns.
Future research should directly measure cultural values,
economic conditions, and other contextual characteristics to
determine the mechanisms underlying cross-national differences
in supermarket consumer perceptions. Overall, the study
contributes to international retail marketing research by
demonstrating both the relevance of the five-dimensional
marketing mix framework and the importance of establishing
measurement comparability before interpreting differences
between national consumer groups.
