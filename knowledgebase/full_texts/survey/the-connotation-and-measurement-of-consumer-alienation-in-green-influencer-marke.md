  Abstract
Situated in the emerging context of green influencer marketing (GIM), this study
examines the connotation and dimensional structure of consumer alienation and de-
velops theoretically grounded meas urement content for subsequent empirical re-
search. Drawing on a constructivist grounded theory approach, the study integrates
interview materials, online textual data, and relevant literature to identify the core
manifestations, underlying categories, and process logic of consumer alienation in
GIM contexts. The findings indicate that consumer alienation in GIM is a multidi-
mensional construct comprising five core dimensions—negative image perception,
passive interaction, perceived marketing normlessness, demand frustration, and in-
trinsic deficiency—together encompassing 15 main categories and 43 subcatego-
ries. On this basis, the study further formulates a preliminary item pool intended to
support future scale development and quantitative testing. By clarifying the conno-
tation of consumer alienation and explicating its grounded theoretical framework,
this research enriches the literature on consumer alienation in digital green market-
ing and offers practical implications for enterprises, green influencers, and consum-
ers.

1. Introduction
In an era in which environmental sustainability has become a salient business value, green marketing has moved from
peripheral positioning to a mainstream strategic concern. Within this broader transformation, green influencer mar-
keting (GIM) has emerged as a prominent mode of digital persuasion, relying on social media influencers to com-
municate environmental values, narrate sustainable lifestyles, and shape consumption choices (Hudders & Lou, 2023;
Kapoor et al., 2023). Through interactive content, symbolic storytelling, and perceived interpersonal closeness, green
influencers can reduce information-processing costs and make sustainability claims appear more vivid, relatable, and
actionable.
At the same time, however, the symbolic and value -laden nature of GIM also makes it especially vulnerable to
consumer estrangement. Once environmental claims are overperformed, excessively aestheticized, or weakly aligned
with actual products and organizational conduct, consumers may begin to question not only message credibility but
also the moral legitimacy of the actors involved (Parguel et al., 2011; Testa et al., 2018). In such circumstances, green
symbols no longer function as reassuring signals; instead, they may trigger suspicion, discomfort, disappointment,
and resistance. Compared with ordinary influencer marketing, GIM embeds stronger ethical expectations and stronger
evaluative sensitivity, which means that discrepancies between symbolic claims and lived consumption experience

For firms, this issue is not merely attitudinal but strategic. Alienated consumers are more likely to engage in com-
plaint behavior, avoidance, negative word- of-mouth, and other confrontational marketplace responses, thereby in-
creasing the reputational and relational costs of green communication (Lambert, 1981; Gruber et al., 2020). Yet
consumer alienation should not be viewed only as a negative outcome. When examined carefully, it also reveals
where value communication has broken down, where consumers perceive ethical inconsistency, and where digital
marketing relationships fail to sustain trust. From this perspective, identifying the connotation and internal structure
of consumer alienation in GIM is theoretically important and managerially urgent. Moreover, the strategic relevance
of the topic extends beyond immediate transaction outcomes. In digitally networked green markets, consumers’ ad-
verse reactions can quickly become collectively amplified through comments, reposts, reviews, and platform-based
discussion threads. Alienation may therefore travel from an individual feeling to a wider re putational dynamic that
affects how sustainability claims are socially interpreted. A grounded understanding of alienation can thus help firms
identify not only isolated service failures but also broader fractures in moral communication and stakeholder trust.
Despite this importance, the existing literature remains insufficient in at least three respects. First, classic consumer
alienation research has mainly been developed in general marketplace settings and usually emphasizes broad dimen-
sions such as powerlessness, meaninglessness, normlessness, social isolation, and self-estrangement (Seeman, 1959;
Allison, 1978; Johnson, 1996). These foundational constructs provide an important starting point, but they do not
fully capture the symbolic, relational, and e thically charged features of GIM. Second, although recent studies on
green influencer marketing have examined credibility, persuasion effectiveness, sustainability symbolism, and green-
washing concerns, they have rarely conceptualized consumer alienation as a distinct and multidimensional phenom-
enon within this context (Kılıç & Gürlek, 2024; König & Maier, 2024). Third, extant work remains largely frag-
mented, often offering descriptive observations or broad conceptual discussions without clarifying the connotative
dimensions, category relationships, and process logic through which alienation is formed and expressed in GIM set-
tings.
To address these gaps, this study draws on the cognition–emotion–behavior framework and adopts constructivist
grounded theory as its primary methodological orientation. Rather than statistically testing causal relationships, the
study aims to inductively clarify what consumer alienation means in the context of GIM, how its dimensions are
structured, and how its associated cognitive, emotional, and behavioral elements can be theoretically connected. In
addition, based on the qualitative findings, the study organizes preliminary measurement content that may inform
subsequent quantitative research. In doing so, the paper contributes a context-sensitive conceptualization of consumer
alienation in green influencer marketing and provides a grounded foundation for future hypothesis development and
scale validation.
2. Theoretical Basis and Literature Review
2.1 Cognitive-Emotional-Behavioral Theoretical Framework
Green influencer marketing (GIM), the focal context of this study, combines green marketing with influencer-based
communication by mobilizing influencers to interpret, endorse, and circulate the environmental attributes of products
and services (Hudders & Lou, 2023; Li et al., 2024). Alienation, by contrast, has a longer theoretical lineage. Seeman
(1959) famously identified powerlessness, meaninglessness, normlessness, social isolation, and self-estrangement as
major variants of alienation. In marketing research, consumer alienation generally refers to the sense of detachment,
powerlessness, normlessness, and self-dislocation that consumers experience in relation to the marketplace, products,
or firms (Allison, 1978). Building on the cognition–emotion–behavior perspective, the present study treats consumer
alienation in GIM as an emotionally charged state rooted in consumers’ value -related cognitions and manifested in
subsequent marketplace responses. This framework does not presuppose a statistically verified causal model; rather,
it serves as a sensitizing lens for organizing grounded qualitative analysis.
2.2 Green Influencer Marketing and Consumer Alienation
As an emerging marketing form, GIM reshapes the relationship among consumers, firms, influencers, and products.
Existing studies suggest that green communication on social media can foster sustainable consumption intentions,
but it may also provoke skepticism when consumers perceive symboli c inconsistency, excessive performance, or
greenwashing (AI-Sharouf & Naesae, 2022; Ktisti et al., 2022; Kapoor et al., 2023). At the same time, the influencer’s

3. Identification of Connotative Dimensions of Consumer Alienation
3.1 Research Methodology
Constructivist grounded theory is particularly suitable for theory building in underexplored contexts where existing
constructs do not adequately capture lived experience or context-specific meaning structures. Rather than imposing
a predetermined dimensional framework, this approach allows categories to be generated through iterative engage-
ment with empirical materials while acknowledging the interpretive role of the researcher (Jia & Heng, 2020; Glaser
& Strauss, 2017). Given that the connotation of consumer alienation in GIM has not yet been systematically clarified,
constructivist grounded theory provides an appropriate methodological path for identifying dimensions, relating cat-
egories, and developing an explanatory framework grounded in textual and experiential data.
3.2 Data Collection
To enhance contextual richness and analytic depth, this study collected qualitative materials from three complemen-
tary sources: interviews, online textual materials, and relevant literature. This design reflects a triangulation logic
and, in particular, incorporates a netnographic orientation insofar as  a substantial portion of the data derives from
digitally mediated interactions, public platform discourse, and user-generated textual traces surrounding green influ-
encer marketing. Rather than treating social media texts as secondary background information, the study regards
them as an important empirical site through which alienation is expressed, negotiated, and circulated in naturally
occurring digital environments. The combination of these sources also reduces the risk that the construct is defined
only through post hoc academic interpretation or only through one-off self-reports. Interviews capture reflective ac-
counts, online texts capture spontaneous and publicly situated expressions, and literature provides a comparative
theoretical backdrop. Together, they allow the study to move between lived experience, naturally occurring discourse,
and conceptual refinement, which is particularly important for a phenomenon such as alienation that may be simul-
taneously emotional, relational, and structurally conditioned.
3.2.1 Interviews
Semi-structured interviews lasting 40 –60 minutes were conducted with consumers who had prior experience with
green influencer marketing (see Appendix A). Participants were initially recruited through major social media plat-
forms and screened according to their purchasing experience, degree of engagement with green influencers, and
familiarity with green marketing content. To ensure that respondents were capable of reflecting on their experiences
in a focused way, only individuals scoring between 5 and 7 on two pre-screening items concerning engagement with
GIM and awareness of their own emotional reactions were retained. The interviews centered on consumers’ percep-
tions of green influencers, platform -mediated interactions, evaluations of products and services, and emotional re-
sponses to discrepant or unsatisfactory experiences.
Following this procedure, 25 consumers aged 18–40 were selected, which is broadly consistent with the dominant
user profile reported in social media influencer marketing research (Vrontis et al., 2021). The interviews generated
approximately 275,000 Chinese words. These materials offered detailed insight into how consumers narrate distrust,
disappointment, resistance, emotional discomfort, and self-related tension in relation to green influencer marketing,
thereby providing a rich basis for subsequent coding and category refinement.
3.2.2 Network Data
This study also employed web crawler technology to collect publicly available textual materials from major social
media platforms, including YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, REDnote, and MicroBlog, using keywords such as

3.2.3 Literature
In addition, the study reviewed literature retrieved from major academic databases, including CNKI, Wiley, Springer,
and Web of Science. After screening for relevance to consumer alienation, green influencer marketing, and related
issues of sustainability communication and marketplace response, 96 articles were retained. These texts were not
treated as equivalent to interview or platform discourse, but as theoretically informative materials that helped sensi-
tize category development, support comparative interpretation, and situate the emerging framework within prior
scholarship.
An additional methodological consideration concerns researcher reflexivity. Because constructivist grounded the-
ory recognizes that categories are co-constructed through the interaction between researchers and materials, the study
did not assume that coding could be enti rely neutral or mechanical. Instead, the coding process involved repeated
movement between raw expressions, emerging interpretations, and theoretical comparison. This reflexive stance was
especially important when dealing with emotionally charged or cultur ally specific expressions found in interviews
and online discourse, as these could not always be understood adequately through literal translation alone. The iter-
ative discussions with knowledgeable scholars therefore functioned not as quantitative reliabi lity checks, but as a
way to refine interpretive sensitivity and reduce avoidable distortions in category construction.
Guided by constructivist grounded theory, the analysis proceeded through four stages of coding: initial coding,
focused coding, axial coding, and theoretical coding. Throughout the process, data collection and analysis were con-
ducted iteratively rather than as strictly separate stages. The coding logic emphasized constant comparison across
sources, repeated revisiting of category boundaries, and progressive abstraction from descriptive expressions to more
stable conceptual groupings.
3.3 Data Coding
3.3.1 Initial Coding
At the initial coding stage, all interview materials, two-thirds of the online texts (1,433 items), and 64 articles were
coded, while the remaining materials were reserved for later saturation assessment. The raw data were first cleaned
and standardized where necessary, after which the textual materials were coded line by line and sentence by sentence.
The purpose of this stage was to remain as open as possible to participants’ and users’ expressions without prema-
turely imposing a rigid conceptual scheme. To improve interpretive stability, the preliminary coding results were
repeatedly discussed with three scholars familiar with influencer marketing research, and the coding categories were
revised through several rounds of feedback and comparison until a relatively stable understanding was reached.
During data processing, entries that were clearly irrelevant to t he topic of consumer alienation, extremely low in
interpretive value, or semantically ambiguous were removed. This yielded 116 candidate initial codes. After further
comparison, discussion, and consolidation, 109 initial codes were retained as analytically meaningful representations
of alienation-related expressions in the GIM context.
3.3.2 Focused Coding
At the focused coding stage, the initial codes were grouped according to conceptual similarity and interpretive rele-
vance in order to identify more analytically significant patterns. The study then again drew on expert discussion to
assess the consistency and plausibility of the emerging categories. After entries with weak representativeness or low
relevance were eliminated, 43 focused coding entries, labe led C1 to C43, were retained (see Table 1). This stage
moved the analysis beyond fragmented descriptive expressions and toward more coherent subcategories of consumer
alienation.

3.3.3 Axial Coding
Axial coding was then used to explore the relationships among the focused codes and to integrate them into higher-
order main categories. Through repeated comparison and semantic clustering, 43 subcategories were ultimately or-
ganized into 15 main categories, labeled B1 to B15 (see Table 1). This stage was especially important for clarifying
the internal structure of alienation in GIM, because it showed that seemingly dispersed expressions—such as dissat-
isfaction with after-sales service, discomfort with communication style, suspicion toward promotional standards, and
feelings of self-powerlessness—could be meaningfully related within broader conceptual clusters.
3.3.4 Theoretical Coding
Theoretical coding further integrated the 15 main categories into five core categories, labeled A1 to A5, thereby
forming the basic dimensional framework of consumer alienation in the GIM context. Specifically, “questioning
influencer persona,” “questioning corporate ethics,” and “questioning influencer appearance” were grouped under
negative image perception; categories such as resentment toward expression, negative emotional interaction, incom-
prehension arising from cultural differences, dis satisfaction with after-sales service, and discomfort with delivered
content were grouped under passive interaction; negative perceptions of marketing publicity and disapproval of mar-
keting standards were grouped under perceived marketing normlessness; product quality problems, goods not match-
ing promotional claims, and damaged expectation judgment were grouped under demand frustration; and negative
conceptions of marketing information together with powerlessness were grouped under intrinsic deficiency. The se
five dimensions capture how alienation in GIM simultaneously involves evaluations of external actors, interactional
experience, marketing norms, consumption outcomes, and self-related psychological reactions.
To strengthen the credibility of this categorization, the study used feedback-based validation in a qualitative sense
rather than statistical validation. First, three graduate students with research experience in influencer marketing were
invited to conduct reverse categorization of the 15 main category entries after they had familiarized themselves with
the meanings of the five core categories. Their categorizations showed a high degree of consistency. Second, five
interviewees were asked to review whether their earlier experiences could be appropriately located within the emer-
gent category structure, and no substantial mismatches were identified. These procedures do not constitute quantita-
tive hypothesis testing; rather, they serve to enhance interpretive coherence and category plausibility within a
grounded theory design.
3.3.5 Saturation Testing
For theoretical saturation testing, one -third of the online textual materials (716 items), 32 articles, and additional
interview materials from five participants were reserved and coded after the preliminary framework had been estab-
lished. No substantively new categories emerged during this stage. Taken together with the reverse -categorization
and interview-retrospection procedures, this finding indicates that the five-dimensional framework had reached the-
oretical saturation for the purposes of the present study.
3.4 The Emotional Correlation Mechanism of Consumer Alienation
Based on the cognition–emotion –behavior framework and the grounded coding results, this study develops a
grounded theoretical framework of consumer alienation in the context of green influencer marketing (see Fig. 1). It
is important to note that this framework is intended as a qualitative, theory-building representation of category rela-
tionships rather than a statist ically tested causal model. In this sense, Fig. 1 summarizes how cognitive appraisals,
alienation-related emotional states, and marketplace responses can be conceptually connected through the evidence
generated in the coding process.
The coding results suggest that consumer alienation in GIM is often preceded by distorted or unstable value-related
cognitions. More specifically, the analysis identified three cognitive tendencies —low-value cognition, ambiguous
cognition of value, and value cognition bias —as recurrent antecedent conditions. These cognitive tendencies were
grounded in six main categories: product quality problems, service quality problems, non-standard marketing, fuzzy
value anchors, platform mechanism distortion, and long- term value reduction ( see Table 2). In substantive terms,
consumers may begin to experience alienation when they perceive exaggerated product claims, emotionally coercive
narratives, inconsistent standards, weak complaint channels, or platform -induced distortions in how value i s dis-
played and interpreted. In line with prior work emphasizing the interdependence of cognition and emotion (Pessoa,
2008), the qualitative materials also suggest a recursive dynamic: negative cognitions generate alienating emotions,
and sustained alienation further intensifies negative perceptions of influencers, firms, and marketing messages.

4. Development of Measurement Content and Future Quantitative Research
4.1 Development of Preliminary Measurement Content
Although the present study is qualitative in methodological orientation, grounded theory is particularly valuable for
developing context-specific concepts that can later guide rigorous quantitative inquiry. Accordingly, after identifying
the five-dimensional framework of consumer alienation in GIM, this study organized preliminary measurement con-
tent based on the focused and axial coding results. The purpose at this stage is not to claim that a finalized scale has
already been statistically validated, but to translate the qualitative categories into a structured item pool that may
support subsequent measurement development.
Specifically, drawing on the dimensional framework derived from coding, the wording patterns found in the em-
pirical materials, and relevant scale -development literature, the study repeatedly refined candidate items through
discussion with scholars familiar with grounded t heory and influencer marketing. In addition, several respondents
were invited to review the clarity and contextual appropriateness of the item expressions. Through this iterative pro-
cess, 43 preliminary items were retained and organized under the five core dimensions (see Table 4). These items
should be understood as conceptually grounded measurement content rather than a finalized and psychometrically
verified scale.
4.2 Analytical Value of the Preliminary Item Pool
The preliminary item pool serves at least three analytical purposes. First, it provides a more operational representation
of the connotation of consumer alienation in GIM by translating abstract dimensions into observable expressions.
This is useful because grounded theory often produces concept ually rich categories, yet subsequent empirical re-
searchers still need clearer indicators when moving toward survey-based or experimental designs. The present item
pool therefore functions as a bridge between qualitative conceptualization and future quantitative operationalization.
Second, the item pool helps preserve contextual specificity. A recurring problem in consumer alienation research
is that general marketplace constructs may overlook the symbolic, ethical, and interactional features that characterize
GIM. By grounding item expressions in interview materials and online discourse, the present study retains nuances
such as distrust toward influencer persona, resistance to emotionally coercive communication, dissatisfaction with
shifting promotional standards, and feelings of self-powerlessness in the face of platform-mediated green marketing.
These nuances are central to understanding why alienation in GIM cannot be fully reduced to older generic notions
of marketplace detachment.
Third, the preliminary item pool clarifies the internal boundaries among the five dimensions. Negative image per-
ception mainly concerns distrust directed toward the influencer or firm as social actors; passive interaction captures
discomfort and negativity emerging within inte raction processes; perceived marketing normlessness refers to per-
ceived disorder, manipulation, or ethical inconsistency in promotional standards; demand frustration concerns failed
expectations in relation to products and services; and intrinsic deficiency reflects self-related feelings such as anger,
inferiority, distance, and powerlessness. Distinguishing these dimensions at the item-development stage can facilitate
more precise model specification in future studies.

Following the reviewer’s concern regarding methodological consistency, this study does not conduct statistical vali-
dation within the present paper. Instead, it argues that the qualitative framework and item pool established here should

be treated as the groundwork for subsequent quantitative research. Future studies may draw on the 43 preliminary
items to conduct item purification, exploratory factor analysis, confirmatory factor analysis, and external validation
in independent samples, the reby testing whether the five -dimensional structure identified here is psychometrically
stable across platforms, populations, and product categories.
Future quantitative work may also examine the propositions implied by Fig. 1. For example, researchers may test
whether low-value cognition, ambiguous cognition of value, and value cognition bias significantly predict different
dimensions of consumer alienation; whether alienation dimensions differentially relate to complaint behavior, avoid-
ance, and negative word-of-mouth; and whether consumer attributes or platform characteristics moderate these rela-
tionships. In this way, the present study should be understood as offering a grounded conceptual and measurement
foundation upon which later hypothesis-driven research can build.
From the perspective of content interpretation, the dimension of negative image perception captures alienation
directed primarily at the social and moral image of influencers and firms. The corresponding items do not merely
reflect dislike in a general sense; rather, they indicate consumers’ suspicion that the influencer persona is excessively
packaged, strategically performed, morally inconsistent, or instrumentally constructed for commercial gain. In GIM
contexts, such perceptions are particularly consequential because environmental advocacy invites heightened expec-
tations of authenticity and ethical coherence. Once consumers perceive hypocrisy, double standards, or manipulative
image construction, alienation is likely to be attached not only to the message but also to the actor who communicates
it. Future quantitative work should therefore be attentive to whether this dimension is empirically separable from
broader notions such as source distrust or perceived hypocrisy.
Passive interaction represents a different layer of alienation. Here, the focal issue is not simply whether consumers
believe the influencer or the firm, but whether the interaction process itself becomes emotionally uncomfortable,
exhausting, or distancing. The relevant subcategories include resentment toward communication style, negative affect
arising in interaction, unease related to cultural difference, dissatisfaction with after -sales service, and discomfort
with content delivery. This suggests that alienation in GIM is not reducible to abstract value disagreement; it is also
lived through concrete communicative encounters. Consumers may feel ignored, preached at, emotionally mirrored
in a negative way, or unable to identify with the discourse style being used. Such interactional negativity is important
because it helps explain why alienation can emerge even before a concrete product failure occurs.
The dimension of perceived marketing normlessness highlights another distinctive feature of GIM. Consumers in
the qualitative materials repeatedly expressed a sense that green promotional activities were governed by unstable,
inconsistent, or morally questionable standards. They reported feeling incited, controlled, deceived, or confused by
marketing practices whose normative b oundaries seemed to shift across contexts. This finding extends classic dis-
cussions of normlessness by showing how, in digital green marketing, normative instability is often experienced
through the interplay of platform mechanics, promotional rhetoric, and symbolic environmental claims. For future
measurement development, it will be important to preserve this context -specific meaning rather than translating it
too quickly into generic items about manipulation or dissatisfaction.
Demand frustration concerns alienation grounded in failed consumption outcomes. Product deterioration, discrep-
ancies between promotional content and received goods, expectation failure, and perceived infringement of consumer
rights all contributed to this dimension. In conventional consumer research, such experiences are often studied under
dissatisfaction, service failure, or expectation -disconfirmation frameworks. The present study does not deny those
overlaps, but it suggests that in GIM contexts these failures can acquire an alie nation-like quality because they are
interpreted against a backdrop of ethical symbolism and relational persuasion. In other words, disappointment is
intensified when consumers believe that a sustainability-oriented promise has been used to legitimize outcomes that
ultimately feel ordinary, misleading, or harmful.
Intrinsic deficiency reflects the most inwardly oriented dimension of alienation. The relevant items and categories
point to feelings of anger, inferiority, distance, and powerlessness triggered by repeated exposure to green influencer
marketing information. This dimension is analytically significant because it demonstrates that alienation in GIM is
not solely outward- facing. Consumers may come to feel estranged from their own judgment, capacities,  or social
position when confronted with idealized sustainable lifestyles, strong symbolic contrasts, and perceived inability to
affect marketing practices. Future studies should examine whether this dimension overlaps with constructs such as
self-discrepancy or relative deprivation, or whether it retains distinctive explanatory value within the alienation
framework proposed here.

treats the wording of candidate items as theoretically meaningful. Many items retain explicit references to influencer
conduct, marketing scenarios, and emotional reactions because these elements are central to how alienation is actually
experienced in GIM rather than incidental surface features.

expressions may be semantically overlapping, too colloquial, or too context -bound for direct survey deployment
across different samples. Item purification in later work should therefore aim to preserve conceptual nuance while

The present study also has implications for the sequencing of mixed-stage research. In some emerging areas, re-
searchers are tempted to move immediately from exploratory observation to quantitative testing without sufficiently
clarifying the construct itself. The reviewer’s concern in the present case usefully highlights why that move can be
premature. When a construct has not yet been adequately specified for a novel context, statistical testing may create
an appearance of rigor while resting on unstable conceptual foundations. By contrast, a grounded theory phase can
first clarify connotation, dimensionality, and process relationships, after which quantitative researchers can more
responsibly formulate hypotheses, identify rival models, and evaluate boundary conditions.
Accordingly, the contribution of this paper lies not in claiming that the dimensional structure has already been
confirmed beyond doubt, but in offering a theoretically disciplined basis for the next stage of inquiry. Future re-
searchers may use Table 4 as an initial pool from  which to select, revise, merge, or eliminate items. They may also
compare whether the five-dimensional structure identified here is invariant across platform types, categories of green
products, or cultural settings. Such work would allow scholars to dete rmine whether the connotation of consumer
alienation in GIM is broadly generalizable or whether some dimensions are especially salient only under particular
communicative or institutional conditions.
Beyond scale construction, the present framework also supports proposition development. A first grounded prop-
osition is that value-related cognitive instability—manifested as low-value cognition, ambiguous cognition of value,
and value cognition bias—constitutes an important precondition for consumer alienation in GIM. A second proposi-
tion is that alienation is likely to manifest through multiple dimensions simultaneously rather than as a unitary feeling.
A third proposition is that these dimensions are differentially expressed in antagonistic and exclusionary behaviors,
including complaint, reporting, avoidance, and emotional venting. A fourth proposition is that consumer attributes
and the broader characteristics of digitally mediated consumption spaces intensify these processes.
Such propositions offer a more appropriate bridge to future empirical testing than direct statistical claims embedded
within a qualitative article. Researchers can use them to design models that examine mediation, moderation, dimen-
sional differentiation, or cross -platform comparison while remaining faithful to the construct as grounded in this
study. In addition, proposition-based follow-up research may help avoid an overly mechanical reading of Fig. 1. The
figure should not be interpreted as a rigid linear chain but as a theoretically informed representation of how cognition,
emotion-laden alienation, and behavior are intertwined in GIM environments marked by virtuality, symbolic excess,
and uncertain standards of judgment.

5. Discussion and Prospects
This study investigates consumer alienation in the context of green influencer marketing and yields three main find-
ings. First, consumer alienation in GIM is not a single diffuse feeling but a multidimensional construct comprising
five dimensions: negative image perception, passive interaction, perceived marketing normlessness, demand frustra-
tion, and intrinsic deficiency. Second, the study clarifies a grounded theoretical framework in which alienation is
associated with distorted value-related cognition, conditioned by both consumer attributes and the digital consump-
tion space, and expressed through antagonistic and exclusionary behavioral tendencies. Third, the study develops
preliminary measurement content that can support future quantitative operationalization without conflating the pre-
sent qualitative design with statistical validation. Importantly, these findings suggest that alienation in GIM should
be treated not merely as a downstream emotional reaction but as a substantive lens for understanding how sustaina-
bility communication succeeds or fails under conditions of commercialization, mediation, and symbolic performance.
This study makes several contributions. First, it extends consumer alienation research into the emerging domain
of green influencer marketing, where environmental symbolism, influencer authenticity, and platform mediation in-
tersect in ways that intensify consumers’ evaluative and emotional sensitivity. Second, it refines the conceptual
boundaries of consumer alienation by showing that, in GIM contexts, alienation is not reducible to classic market-
place powerlessness or meaninglessness alone; rather, it also includes distrust toward symbolic personas, interactional
discomfort, perceived disorder in green promotional norms, and self -related psychological deficiency triggered by
digitally mediated value conflicts. Third, the study demonstrates the usefulness of constructivist grounded theory for
generating a context-sensitive conceptual framework and preliminary measurement content in an area where existing
constructs remain insufficiently specified. In this respect, the study also contributes to the literature on green market-
ing communication by showing that negative consumer reactions cannot be fully understood through persuasion ef-
fectiveness or credibility measures alone; the deeper issue may be the emer gence of a more pervasive sense of es-
trangement from the actors, norms, and promises that structure the marketplace encounter.
The findings also offer managerial implications. For enterprises, reducing consumer alienation requires more than
increasing the volume of green communication; it requires ensuring coherence among environmental narratives,
product performance, service quality, and complaint handling. Firms should therefore strengthen verifiable green
commitments, improve transparency in promotional claims, and avoid symbolic overstatement that can easily be
interpreted as greenwashing. For green influencers, the results highlight the importance of maintaining credible per-
sonas, using restrained and culturally intelligible communication styles, and avoiding emotionally coercive or exces-
sively dramatized persuasion. For platforms and firms jointly, attention should be paid to after -sales support, com-
plaint accessibility, and the standardization of green promotional practices so that consumers are less likely to expe-
rience ambiguity, disappointment, and helplessness during digital interactions. For consumers, the findings suggest
the importance of strengthening critical literacy in relation to green symbolic appeals, sponsored content, and plat-
form-curated consensus. Alienation is often intensified when consumers have limited ability to evaluate environmen-
tal claims, distinguish persuasive performance from substantive commitment, or access effective channels of redress
after disappointment occurs. Educational initiatives, clearer disclosure practices, and more accessible complaint sys-
tems may therefore help reduce the conditions under which alienation becomes entrenched.
At the same time, the study indicates that consumer alienation can function as a diagnostic signal rather than merely
a negative endpoint. Feelings of distrust, resistance, and estrangement reveal where value communication becomes
misaligned with consumer expectations and where ethical narratives fail to remain credible in practice. For this reason,
organizations should treat alienation-related expressions in online environments as important feedback for revising
communication strategies, service systems, and relationship-management mechanisms.
Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the study followed rigorous grounded theory proce-
dures, category construction inevitably involved interpretive judgment; future research may further strengthen trans-
parency by combining team coding with software-assisted qualitative analysis. Second, although the study adopted a

netnographic orientation by drawing on multiple major platforms, the platform scope remains limited and cannot
exhaust the diversity of digital green marketing environments. Third, the preliminary measurement content proposed
here has not yet been statistically validated within this paper. This limitation is deliberate rather than accidental: in

are most salient and deserve closer attention in follow-up studies.
Future research may therefore proceed in two directions. One direction is theory testing: scholars can trans form

other direction is measurement development: researchers may refine the preliminary item pool, evaluate its psycho-

platform types. Through such cumulative work, the concept of consumer alienation in green influencer marketing
can be advanced from qualitative theorization to robust mixed-stage empirical development.
