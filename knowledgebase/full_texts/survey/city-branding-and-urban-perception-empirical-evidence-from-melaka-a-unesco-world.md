1. Introduction

Global urbanization and economic transformation have reshaped the way cities position themselves in competitive
regional and global environments. Cities are no longer viewed only as administrative centers but also as strategic entities t hat
must attract in vestment, talent, businesses, residents, and visitors. In this context, city branding has become an important
approach for communicating urban identity, strengthening public perception, and supporting long-term sustainability (Akturan
& Kuter, 2024).
In Malaysia, urban development strategies increasingly integrate branding, cultural identity, tourism performance, and
economic competitiveness. Public perception of a city is shaped not only by its physical environment but also by education,
culture, social welfare, and economic opportunity, all of which influence how stakeholders evaluate livability, attractiveness,
and urban quality (Kamaluddin et al., 2023; Katahenggam & Wee, 2024).
Melaka represents an important example within Malaysia's national heritage landscape. Situated on the western coast
of Peninsular Malaysia, the city is widely recognized for its historical significance, cultural diversity, and architectural legacy.
Along with George Town in Penang, Melaka was jointly inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage City on 7 July 2008, in recognition
of its outstanding cultural and historical value (Law et al., 2024; Jumbri et al., 2025) . Since then, Melaka has held a central
position in Malaysia's tourism and cultural development, serving as one of the country's most prominent heritage destinations.
Abstract Urban competitiveness has increased the need for strategic place marketing that strengthens economic
performance, cultural distinctiveness, and social well -being. Despite growing interest in city branding, limited empirical
research has examined how multip le urban development sectors collectively shape public perception in Southeast Asian
UNESCO heritage cities. This study examines how five urban development sectors, namely the economy, education, tourism,
culture, and social welfare, influence public perceptions of Melaka city, Malaysia. As a UNESCO World Heritage City, Melaka
provides a valuable context for understanding how heritage preservation and contemporary development jointly shape city
image, competitiveness, and long -term sustainability. The study employed a quanti tative descriptive-correlational design
using survey data collected from 384 respondents, including residents, students, workers, and tourists. A structured
questionnaire was used to measure perceptions of the five sectors and the overall  perception of Melaka city. Data were
analyzed using descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation, and multiple regression analysis through SPSS. The results show
that all five sectors are positively and significantly associated with public perception of Melaka city. Together, the five
predictors explain 46.9% of the variance in city perception. Tourism and culture emerged as the strongest predictors,
followed by the economy, education, and social welfare. These findings confirm that Melaka’s city image is shaped not only
by its heritage and tourism appeal but also by broader functional dimensions such as economic opportunity, educational
development, and social well -being. The study contributes to city branding literature by proposing a multidimensional
framework that integrates symbolic and functional urban attributes in a heritage-city context. Practically, the findings offer
guidance for policymakers, urban planners, tourism authorities, and heritage managers in developing integrated branding
strategies that balance conservation, inclusive growth, community well-being, and sustainable urban competitiveness.

2. Literature Review

2.1. City Branding Theory

City branding theory  provides a foundational perspective for understanding how cities construct, communicate, and
nurture their identities within an increasingly competitive global environment. Early contributions by Anholt (2007) introduced
the concept of competitive identity, which posits that the combined influence of governance, culture, tourism, the investment
climate, population characteristics, and international presence shapes a city ’s reputation. These elements collectively
determine how a city is perceived, suggesting that effective branding arises not only from promotional activities but also fr om
the consistent performance and credibility of the city across multiple domains. This t heoretical shift has moved the study of
place marketing toward a more strategic and holistic understanding of urban identity formation.
Building on this foundation, the city brand communication framework , which explains the formation of urban identity
through three interconnected forms of communication , was introduced in 2004 . Primary communication involves the city ’s
physical environment, architectural character, infrastructure, and urban design. Secondary communication refers to the
policies, initiatives, and public services implemented by city authorities. Tertiary communication emerges from the narratives,
opinions, and shared experiences of residents, visitors, media outlets, and online communities. These three forms demonstrate
that a city’s brand is generated through the interaction of physical structures, institutional practices, and social interpretations
rather than through planned promotions alone (Kavaratzis, 2004).
Contemporary scholars emphasize that city branding is a cocreative process shaped by continuous interaction among
various stakeholder groups, such as residents, tourists, investors, and policymakers (Ma et al., 2025) . Their collective
experiences and evaluations combine functional attributes, including economic opportunities, quality of education, public
services, and social well-being, with symbolic attributes such as heritage significance, cultural identity, and emotional meaning.
This perspective highlights that residents are not passive receivers of branding initiatives but rather active contributors whose
everyday experiences validate or contest the city ’s intended identity. This cocreation strengthens the authent icity and
acceptance of the city brand.

City branding has emerged as a crucial dimension of contemporary urban governance, as cities compete for investment,
talent, tourism, and global recognition. Traditionally rooted in territorial and place marketing, the concept has evolved int o a
comprehensive strategy that integrates promotional activities, spatial planning, and socioeconomic development to enhance
a city’s attractiveness and competitiveness (Papanikolaou et al., 2022) . Modern city branding transcends simple promotional
campaigns by positioning the city as a dynamic product, comprising physical qualities, symbolic meanings, cultural narratives ,

Effective city branding requires a synthesis of economic strategy, governance performance, urban design, cultural

In Malaysia, city branding has gained traction as part of national strategies aimed at strengthening urban
competitiveness and enhancing tourism performance. Heritage cities, such as Melaka, Georgetown, and Kuching, are
increasingly adopting city-branding frameworks to enhance visibility, diversify economic activities, and preserve their cultural
identity (Kamaluddin et al., 2023) . These cities face growing pressures to balance heritage conservation with modernization,
requiring branding approaches that highlight authenticity while supporting contemporary development. As tourism and urban
development intensify, city branding plays a pivotal role in aligning stakeholder interests, managing public perceptions, and
reinforcing the distinctiveness of Malaysian cities within regional and global contexts. Melaka, in particular, presents a unique
case study due to its rich cultural heritag e, UNESCO status, and ongoing efforts to improve its livability and economic
competitiveness.

2.3. City Branding and the Formation of Urban Images

City branding has become a central pillar of urban competitiveness, representing the symbolic and communicative
dimension of city branding. Unlike traditional branding, which focuses on promoting products or services, city branding involves
shaping the meaning and identity of a place by crafting narratives that resonate with diverse audiences (Vale & Vale, 2025). It
encompasses the development of a coherent brand essence that communicates what makes a city distinctive, valuable, and
emotionally appealing. Scholars argue that city branding influences how stakeholders interpret and experience urban
environments, shaping impressions of livability, cultural richness, investment potential, and tourism appeal (Zhao & Seokhyun,
2024). In this sense, a strong city brand becomes an intangible strategic asset that enhances visibility and strengthens a city ’s
position in competitive regional and global contexts.
Central to city branding is the concept of the urban image, which refers to an individual's cognitive and affective
representations of a city, shaped through memory, perception, and experience. Lynch (1960) introduced a foundational theory
that highlights the role of physical elements, such as paths, landmarks, and spatial organization, in structuring the urban image.
However, contemporary research expands this understanding by demonstrating that urban image is also shaped by cultural
narratives, social interactions, and symbolic meaning (Bradley & Hall, 2025). For heritage cities such as Melaka, whose identity
is deeply rooted in multicultural history and architectural heritage, the urban image is fundamentally intertwined with
authenticity, tradition, and historical continuity. Stakeholders, including residents, tourists, and investors, evaluate heritage
cities not only through their physical appearance but also through the meanings attached to past events, cultural memory, and
lived experiences.
City branding becomes increasingly challenging when cities face pressures related to modernization, mass tourism, and
urban development. Heritage cities are particularly vulnerable to issues such as overcommercialization, loss of authenticity,
spatial crowding, and cultural dilution, all of which diminish brand credibility and weaken emotional connections among
stakeholders (Rangkuti et al., 2025) . In cities such as Melaka, rapid tourism growth has created tensions between economic
development and heritage preservation, underscoring the importance of strategic branding frameworks that balance cultural
identity with necessary improvements. Misaligned development decis ions or inconsistent narratives can lead to fragmented
perceptions, reducing the coherence and effectiveness of a city’s brand.
Similar challenges have been observed in international heritage cities such as Barcelona, Prague, and Cusco, where
tourism growth has strengthened global visibility but also created pressure on authenticity, local communities, public space,
and cultural co ntinuity (Cocola-Gant, 2023; González -Reverté, 2022; Walas et al., 2024) . In these cities, overtourism,
touristification, and commercialization have generated tensions between heritage preservation and resident well -being,
forcing local authorities to reconsider conventional tourism -centered branding strategies. These cases de monstrate that
heritage-city branding must extend beyond tourism promotion by integrating community participation, cultural governance,

2.4. Urban Perception: Foundations and Emerging Perspectives

Urban perception encompasses the cognitive and emotional processes through which individuals evaluate and interpret
the characteristics of a city. It involves the integration of environmental cues, social experiences, symbolic meanings, and
emotional react ions that construct a mental representation of a place. Environmental psychology highlights how people
develop cognitive maps that guide their spatial understanding and shape their sense of comfort, attachment, and identity.
Elements such as streetscapes, architectural forms, greenery, cleanliness, and spatial organization influence how residents and
visitors perceive and experience urban environments (Ito et al., 2024). These perceptual frameworks directly affect satisfaction,
place attachment, and overall urban image.
Beyond physical surroundings, urban perception is shaped by sociocultural interactions and the symbolic meaning of
place. Cities convey cultural narratives, historical memories, and collective identities through their built environments,
traditions, and community practices (Hilmy et al., 2025). These symbolic dimensions enrich perceptions by offering emotional
depth and cultural resonance. In heritage cities such as Melaka, the presence of multicultural traditions, historical landmarks,
and preserved architectural styles plays a crucial role in signaling authenticity and cultural continuity. Stakeholders often
interpret these attributes as reflections of a city's character, identity, and historical significance, which contribute to d eeper
emotional engagement with the city’s place.
Emerging perspectives highlight the influence of digital technologies in reshaping how people form perceptions of cities.
Digital platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, Google Maps, and travel review sites create “virtual perceptions” that shape
expectations long before any physical interaction occurs (Qu et al., 2024). Algorithm-curated imagery, user-generated content,
and online storytelling collectively construct digital versions of urban experiences that may amplify or distort real -world
conditions. For heritage destinations, visually appealing images of monuments, cultural festivals, and historical architecture
have become powerful tools for shaping perceptions of places globally. However, digital exposure can also intensify the
pressure on cities to maintain highly aesthetic or idealized versions of themselves, esp ecially in tourism-driven economies.
Thus, urban  perception is multidimensional and influenced by functional attributes such as economic opportunities,
educational quality, tourism experiences, and social welfare conditions, as well as symbolic attributes such as heritage valu e,
cultural richness, and emotional resonance. In the context o f Melaka, perceptions extend far beyond its UNESCO heritage
designation. Stakeholders evaluate a city through its economic dynamism, cultural diversity, tourism quality, environmental
conditions, governance practices, an d community well -being. These diverse dimensions collectively shape how Melaka is
perceived as a place to live, study, invest in, and visit. Understanding the interplay of these perceptual factors is crucial  for
developing holistic and sustainable city branding strategies that reflect both heritage identity and contemporary aspirations.

2.5. Sectoral Determinants of City Perception and Hypothesis Development

2.5.1. Economic Sector

Economic vitality is widely recognized as one of the most significant determinants of a city ’s attractiveness, influencing
how residents, visitors, and investors perceive a city ’s stability and long -term prospects. Cities that offer diverse industries,
employment opportunities, strong infrastructure, and a supportive investment climate are often viewed as modern, forward -
looking, and competitive. Economic performance influences not only material conditions but also the symbolic value associated
with urban development, generating perceptions of growth, progress, and opportunity (Zhao & Seokhyun, 2024).
Furthermore, scholars argue that economic vibrancy strengthens a city ’s capacity to invest in public amenities, cultural
facilities, education, and social welfare, all of which contribute to a positive urban experience (Hourie et al., 2015; Răducan et
al., 2025). Economic stability also supports innovation ecosystems, enabling cities to attract entrepreneurs, technology firms,
and creative industries that enhance competitiveness. For medium -sized heritage cities such as Melaka, strategic economic
diversification beyond tourism is essential, as overreliance on a single sector makes cities vulnerable to economic shocks,
seasonal fluctuations, and global crises such as pandemics.
In the Malaysian context, economic development is closely linked to urban image and branding. Cities that demonstrate
economic resilience are perceived as more livable and attractive for investment, particularly when they combine heritage

2.5.2. Education Sector

Education is a critical driver of urban competitiveness and city image, particularly in knowledge-driven economies where
universities strengthen human capital, research capacity, and innovation performance. Evidence indicates that stronger higher
education systems can stimulate urban innovation and entrepreneurship and contribute to broader economic growth, which
reinforces a city ’s reputation as a place with long -term potential (Marlen & Miklós, 2025; Peng & Xu, 2024) . In city branding
terms, education assets also signal a commitment to capability building and future readiness, which can enhance perceptions
of credibility and progress (Ghafar et al., 2022).
Education further enhances a city ’s attractiveness by attracting students, academics, and skilled workers who expand
knowledge networks and increase a city ’s visibility in competitive talent markets. Malaysian research indicates that
international postgraduate students associate their study experience with opportunities, societal contributions, and the quality
of their surrounding environment, all of which influence how places are evaluated by mobile populations (Singh & Jamil, 2021).
This finding supports the view that education ecosystems influence external perceptions that matter for both reputation and
investment-related narratives (Zhao et al., 2025).
In addition, education contributes to urban perception through cultural and social vibrancy associated with student
communities and campus-related activities. Universities can help advance culture and creativity-led development in cities and
regions, although they also face institutional pressures , as they balance their academic missions with regional expectations
(Cinar & Coenen, 2023). Research on community engagement with international students suggests that social connections and
local integration have a significant influence on everyday experiences and the sense of belonging in host cities, thereby
enhancing perceptions of openness and livability (Mu et al., 2025).
For Melaka, strengthening tertiary education and deepening collaboration among universities, industry, and the
community can complement its heritage identity with a future-oriented knowledge-based narrative. Malaysian evidence from
an education hub setting  suggests that education clustering can increase access to education, foster employment
opportunities, and generate local spillovers that support perceptions of progress and prosperity (Rashid et al., 2024). Research
on educational city branding also shows that the urban environment, cultural heritage, and city image can influence student
satisfaction, implying that a well -managed education ecosystem can become part of a city’s wider reputation and branding
performance (Dhuoki et al., 2025).
Hypothesis 2: The education sector has a significant influence on the perception of Melaka city.

2.5.3. Tourism Sector

Tourism is one of the most influential sectors shaping how heritage cities are perceived, as it strengthens the local
economy, supports jobs and small businesses, and enhances a city ’s visibility through its cultural and heritage appeal. In
Malaysia, cultural and heritage tourism is widely discussed as a strategic national asset, with heritage resources positioned  as
a key part of destination value and differentiation (Zakaria et al., 2024). In historic cities such as Melaka, tourism is closely tied
to place identity because heritage attractions, historic streetscapes, and multicultural character create the core experience
that visitors associate with the city (Ibrahim et al., 2023).
Tourism also plays a symbolic and narrative role by translating history and culture into a “public image” that circulates
through marketing, events, and visitor experiences. Recent studies by Melaka highlight how heritage branding increasingly
uses event-based attractions and digitally mediated storytelling to shape visitor expectations and reinforce destination identity
while also raising questions about how “authentic” that experience feels (Arif et al., 2025) . In practice, this means that the
tourism sector contributes not only to economic outcomes but also to the meanings people attach to Melaka as a living heritage
city.
Moreover, tourism growth can weaken positive perceptions if it produces pressures associated with overtourism,
including congestion, resource overuse, and social friction , which reduce both the quality of life for residents and the visitor
experience. Research focused on Melaka’s UNESCO World Heritage Site empirically links overtourism impacts with perceived
risks and behavioral responses among tourists, showing that unmanaged in tensity can affect destination evaluations
(Azinuddin et al., 2022). Similarly, resident-focused research in Central Malacca emphasizes that sustainable tourism indicators
and local perceptions matter because community experience is part of what ultimately sustains a heritage city ’s reputation.
For Melaka, maintaining a strong and positive tourism image therefore depends on striking a balance between heritage
conservation and development, ensuring that visitor growth does not compromise authenticity, livability, or cultural integrit y.

2.5.4. Cultural sector

Culture forms the emotional and symbolic foundation of urban identity because it conveys the meanings that people
attach to places through their heritage, memories, everyday practices, and shared narratives. When cultural elements are
visible and valued, they strengthen a city ’s distinctiveness and deepen residents' sense of belonging, which in turn increases
positive perceptions of the city's image (Batool & Bahauddin, 2025; Bo & Abdul Rani, 2025).
Cultural vibrancy also shapes perceptions through lived experiences, such as festivals, arts programmes, museum
exhibitions, and heritage events that activate public space and encourage social interaction. These activities make culture “felt”
rather than merely “seen”, helping cities project an i mage of creativity, inclusiveness, and continuity between the past and
present. As a result, culture contributes not only to symbolic value but also to the experiential quality that people use to
evaluate a place (Batool & Bahauddin, 2025).
For Melaka, culture is central to how the city is understood and represented, as UNESCO -related heritage narratives
significantly influence branding, external recognition, and expectations of authenticity. Moreover, heritage branding can be
weakened if cultural assets are overcommodified or managed in ways that reduce authenticity and public welfare, making
careful cultural governance essential (Widodo, 2023).
Recent Malaysian scholarship further highlights that community participation and education -oriented approaches to
living heritage conservation help sustain cultural meaning and strengthen the relationship between heritage and local
communities. In parallel , digital mediation and event -based tourism strategies can amplify Melaka’s visibility, but they also
introduce tensions between spectacle and cultural integrity that can affect perception (Abdul Aziz et al., 2023; Arif et al., 2025).
Hypothesis 4: The cultural sector has a significant influence on the perception of Melaka city.

2.5.5. Social welfare sector

Social welfare reflects how well a city supports everyday life through safety, public health, inclusiveness, environmental
quality, and reliable access to essential services. These conditions are closely tied to livability and shape whether residents and
visitors perceive a city as comfortable, stable, and caring. In practice, people often judge a city ’s image through their daily
experiences of protection, fairness, accessibility, and well-being (Nicolás-Martínez et al., 2024).
Safety is especially influential because perceptions of security significantly impact how people use public space, their
confidence while navigating the city, and their overall evaluation of urban life. Malaysian evidence from an extensive,
multilocal-authority study demonstrated that perceived safety is measurable at scale and varies across demographic and
residential contexts, reinforcing the notion that safety perceptions are a core component of liveability and city evaluation
(Sakip et al., 2025).
In addition to  safety, satisfaction with public infrastructure and services also shapes urban perceptions, particularly
where residents rely on the consistent provision of facilities and urban systems. Malaysian research on neighborhood livability
and infrastructure provision reveals that residents’ assessments of services and facilities are directly linked to their perc eived
satisfaction and livability, which in turn influences broader judgments about city quality (Komak, 2024).
In heritage cities such as Melaka, social welfare holds additional importance because tourism growth can put pressure
on public spaces, cleanliness, mobility, and the well -being of residents. A people -centered approach that maintains safe
walkable environments and strengthens basic  urban support helps preserve positive perceptions while sustaining a city’s
reputation as both a heritage destination and a liveable community (Zakaria et al., 2025).
Hypothesis 5: The social welfare sector significantly influences the perceptions of Melaka city.

2.6. Research Gap

Although city branding has become an increasingly prominent topic in urban studies  worldwide, several important
research gaps remain unaddressed. First, much of the literature focuses narrowly on tourism or branding dimensions,
overlooking the ways in which different urban sectors interact to shape a city’s holistic perceptions. This sectoral isolation limits
the understanding of the complex, multidimensional nature of urban image formation, especially in contexts where economic,
educational, cultural, and social factors are deeply interlinked (Li et al., 2021).
Second, empirical evidence from heritage cities in Southeast Asia remains limited. Most existing studies have focused
on Western or economically advanced Asian contexts. Moreover, relatively few studies have explored how developing heritage
cities in this region integrate multiple sectors, such as the economy, education, tourism, culture, and social welfare, into their
city-branding strategies (Oliva et al., 2022; Lestari et al., 2022). Consequently, there is still a lack of understanding of how these

Finally, competition among regional heritage destinations such as George Town, Hoi An, and Luang Prabang is

3.3. Population, Sampling Strategy, and Sample Size

The target population of this study included residents, students, employees, and tourists in Melaka. These groups
represent the primary stakeholders whose perceptions collectively define and influence the city ’s urban identity. By capturing
perspectives from both local and external groups, this study ensures a comprehensive representation of the multiple
viewpoints that shape Melaka’s reputation as a livable and attractive city.
A stratified random sampling technique was used to ensure representativeness across different stakeholder groups. The
sample was stratified on the basis of stakeholder type (resident, student, employee, or tourist), age group, and geographical
location within Melaka. Within each stratum, respondents were randomly selected in proportion to their estimated share of
the total population. This method enhances sampling accuracy, reduces bias, and ensures that the findings reflect the diversity
of Melaka’s population.
The required sample size was determined via the Krejcie & Morgan (1970) formula, which recommends a minimum of
384 respondents for a population at a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. Data were collected through both online
and face -to-face methods to maximize inclusivity. Online surveys were distributed via Google Forms, while in -person data
collection was conducted at key locations, including Jonker Walk, Dataran Pahlawan, and Ayer Keroh, which are representative
of Melaka’s urban and tourist landscapes.
To ensure linguistic accuracy and cultural appropriateness, the questionnaire was prepared in both English and Malay.
A back-translation process and pilot testing with a small sample group were carried out to assess the clarity, accuracy, and

3.6. Limitations of the Study

Although this study provides valuable empirical insights into how city branding sectors shape the perceptions of Melaka
city, several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross -sectional design captures perceptions at a single point in time and
may not account for changes in attitudes over time. While stratified random sampling improved representativeness, the
findings are lim ited to the Melaka context and may not be directly generalizable to other cities with different cultural or
economic profiles. Furthermore, because the data were collected via self-report questionnaires, the responses may have been
affected by personal interpretation or social desirability bias.
Future research should consider employing longitudinal or mixed-method approaches to capture changes in perception
over time and explore causal relationships. Comparative studies involving other heritage cities, such as George Town or Penang,
could also broaden the understanding of how different policy and cultural contexts shape city-branding outcomes. Additionally,
incorporating qualitative methods such as interviews and focus groups may provide richer insights into stakeholder attitudes
and the emotional aspects of city perception.

5. Discussion

The findings of this study provide robust empirical evidence that the five key city branding sectors —economy,
education, tourism, culture, and social welfare —collectively and significantly influence the public perception of Melaka city.
The regression model (R² = 0.469) indicates that these factors account for nearly half of the variance in how residents and
visitors perceive the city ’s image. This result supports the theoretical proposition that city perception is a multidimensional
construct shaped by both functional attributes and symbolic meanings (Zhou, 2025). It also validates the conceptual framework
developed for this research, emphasizing the integrated and interdependent nature of urban branding and perception.
Tourism and culture emerged as the most influential predictors of Melaka’s image, reinforcing the city’s status as a
UNESCO World Heritage destination renowned for its historical and cultural richness. These results align with previous findings
that heritage-based tourism and cultural authenticity significantly strengthen emotional attachment and destination loyalty
(Chen et al., 2025) . This result is consistent with  Kavaratzis (2004) , who emphasized that city brands are shaped through

physical environments, institutional communication, and stakeholder experiences, and with  Anholt (2007), who argued that
culture, tourism, governance, people, and investment collectively form competitive urban identity. The high beta values for
tourism (β = 0.207) and culture (β = 0.197) suggest that visitors and residents primarily perceive Melaka ’s identity through its
festivals, architecture, and cultural diversity. However, such dependence on cultural -tourism appeal necessitates careful
management. Overtourism, environmental strain, and commercialization can erode authenticity and undermine long -term
brand credibility. Sustainable cultural governance and community-based tourism are thus essential for maintaining equilibrium
between growth and preservation (Alsharif et al., 2025).
The economy and education sectors also exert meaningful and statistically significant effects on public perception of
Melaka city. The positive influence of the economic sector (β = 0.168) suggests that respondents associate Melaka ’s urban
image with indicators of economic stability, employment opportunity, investment attractiveness, infrastructure readiness, and
overall development capacity. A city that demonstrates economic vitality is more likely to be perceived as progressive, resilient,
and capable of sustaining long-term urban competitiveness. Similarly, the significant contribution of the education sector (β =
0.163) indicates that educational institutions, learning opportunities, talent development, and knowledge -related activities
strengthen Melaka’s image as a centre for human capital formation, innovation, and future -oriented development.
These findings are consistent with city branding theory, which emphasises that a city ’s reputation is not constructed
solely through symbolic assets such as culture, heritage, and tourism, but also through functional attributes that shape
everyday stakeholder evaluations. From this perspective, the investment climate, governance quality, infrastructure provision,
educational capacity, and knowledge resources serve as important brand signals that influence how residents, students,
workers, tourists, and investors assess a city ’s credibility and attractiveness (Anholt, 2007; Kavaratzis, 2004) . In this regard,
Melaka’s city brand should not be understood only as a heritage -based or tourism-driven identity, but as a multidimensional
urban image formed through the interaction of symbolic and functional development sectors.
The results also support the concept of knowledge -based urban development, which argues that universities and
educational institutions play a strategic role in enhancing urban competitiveness by generating human capital, supporting
innovation, encouraging entrepreneurship, attracting talent, and strengthening regional development networks (Cinar &
Coenen, 2023; Duque Oliva et al., 2022; Peng & Xu, 2024) . In Melaka, the education sector contributes to the city ’s broader
reputation by complementing its historical and cultural identity with a knowledge -oriented narrative. This is particularly
important for heritage cities that seek to remain competitive in contemporary urban systems while preserving their cultural
distinctiveness.
Taken together, the significant effects of the economy and education sectors demonstrate that Melaka ’s public
perception extends beyond its UNESCO World Heritage status and tourism appeal. Economic opportunity provides the material
foundation for confidence, investment, and urban growth, while educational development enhances the city ’s intellectual,
social, and innovation capacity. The combination of these two sectors broadens Melaka ’s positioning from a predominantly
historical and cultural destination to  a more diversified, liveable, and future -oriented urban centre. Therefore, strengthening
economic resilience and educational capacity is an essential component of Melaka ’s integrated city branding strategy,
particularly in balancing heritage conservation with sustainable urban competitiveness.
Beyond these economic and knowledge -based dimensions, the social welfare sector further demonstrates that the
quality of everyday urban life shapes Melaka ’s city perception. Although social welfare recorded the smallest standardised
coefficient among the five predictors (β = 0.142), its statistically significant effect indicates that it remains an importan t
determinant of public perception. This result suggests that city perception is not shaped solely by highly visible sectors such as
tourism, culture, economic development, or education, but also by the extent to which residents, visitors, students, and
workers experience the city as safe, inclusive, accessible, healthy, and socially supportive. In this regard, social welfare reflects
the human -centred dimension of city branding, as factors such as public safety, healthcare access, social inclusion,
environmental quality, green public spaces, community partic ipation, and reliable public services are central to how
stakeholders evaluate the liveability, credibility, and social attractiveness of the city (Nicolás-Martínez et al., 2024; Schindler &
Dionisio, 2024; Sepe, 2025).
This finding is consistent with contemporary perspectives on urban branding, which increasingly emphasise quality of
life, social equity, inclusive public space, and human-centred development as essential components of a sustainable city image
(Yahia et al., 2025; Zhou, 2025). From this perspective, a city brand cannot be sustained solely through economic performance,
heritage promotion, or tourism attractiveness if the lived experiences of its users are weakened by poor accessibility, limit ed
public services, social exclusion, safety concerns, or declining environmental quality. Residents and visitors evaluate a city not
only through its landmarks, cultural assets, and promotional narratives but also through their daily encounters with public
spaces, mobility systems, healthca re facilities, safety conditions, environmental comfort, and community life. As such, social
welfare functions as both a practical and ethical foundation for city branding, as it determines whether the city is perceived as
caring, inclusive, trustworthy, and liveable.
In the context of Melaka, the social welfare sector is particularly significant because the city’s heritage and tourism must
be balanced with the needs of local communities. As a UNESCO World Heritage City, Melaka attracts a steady flow of visitors.
However, tourism growth may also place pressure on public spaces, transport systems, cleanliness, environmental quality, and

safety, provide green and inclusive spaces, support healthcare and community facilities, and encourage community

Safety, inclusiveness, accessibility, environmental comfort, public space quality, and reliable services influence whether

significance should not be underestimated. A heritage city that neglects social welfare risks weakening its brand's authenticity,
legitimacy, and credibility. In contrast, a city that protects community well-being can strengthen both resident satisfaction and
visitor confidence. Consequently, social welfare should be treated as an integral component of Melaka’s city branding strategy,
particularly in supporting inclusive growth, sustainable tourism, and long -term urban competitiveness.
The demographic profile of the respondents also adds interpretive insight. The predominance of younger respondents
(aged 18–24 years) highlights that perceptions of Melaka are shaped primarily by a generation fluent in digital communication
and visual storytelling. Young people are active creators and consumers of city narratives through social media, travel blogs ,
and online reviews (Kasemsarn et al., 2025). Their influence implies that city image formation is increasingly mediated by digital
platforms rather than traditional word-of-mouth platforms. Therefore, understanding digital perception dynamics is crucial for
city-branding strategies that target both local youth and global audiences.
Moreover, the intercorrelation results reveal that the five sectors are mutually reinforcing. For example, education is
strongly linked to culture and tourism, suggesting that academic institutions can serve as catalysts for the development of
creative ind ustries and heritage innovation. Similarly, the positive relationship between the economy and social welfare
indicates that financial prosperity supports improved public services and inclusivity. These relationships demonstrate that
successful city brandin g necessitates a systemic approach in which economic, educational, cultural, and social initiatives are
integrated rather than pursued independently. In this sense, Melaka ’s competitive edge lies in aligning these interdependent
sectors under a unified city vision.
Compared with other heritage cities such as Penang, George Town, and Chiang Mai, Melaka has a distinct branding
trajectory that combines historical authenticity with aspirations for modernization. The findings suggest that Melaka ’s long-
term competitiveness hinges on its capacity to transition from a tourism -dependent model to a diversified, knowledge -driven
economy, one supported by cultural innovation and inclusive policies. This transformation necessitates a governance model
that is participatory, transparent, and centered on a shared urban identity.
Compared with George Town, Penang, and Melaka, branding in Penang and Melaka remains more strongly centred on
historical tourism and heritage symbolism. George Town has diversified its heritage -city image through creative industries,
street art, cultural entrepreneurship, adaptive reuse of heritage buildings, and digitally visible urban experiences. Melaka can
learn from this model by expanding its brand beyond conventional heritage tourism toward creative -economy initiatives,
youth-led cultural production,  digital heritage trails, and community -based innovation. Such diversification would reduce
overdependence on tourism while strengthening Melaka ’s appeal to younger visitors, investors, students, and creative
professionals. However, Melaka should carefully adapt these lessons to preserve its multicultural identity, living heritage, and
local community values (Gönüllüoğlu & Arslan Selçuk, 2024; Ibrahim et al., 2023).
Hence, the study underscores that effective city branding is not merely a promotional exercise but also a reflection of
how well a city integrates development, heritage, and human well-being into its everyday practices.

5.1. Practical Implications

The empirical results of this study offer several practical insights for city managers, urban planners, policymakers, and
tourism authorities seeking to strengthen Melaka ’s image and enhance its competitiveness at both the national and
international levels. These implications bridge the gap between theoretical understanding and policy application, offering a
roadmap for developing a sustainable and inclusive urban brand that reflects the city ’s identity, values, and aspirations.
First, the results confirm that tourism and culture are the most potent drivers of city perception. This indicates that
Melaka’s cultural and heritage assets continue to define its identity and global recognition. The Melaka City Council and
heritage management authorities should therefore prioritize sustained investment in cultural preservation and tourism
development. Initiatives such as the restoration of historic landmarks, the promotion of cultural festivals, and the revitalization
of creative industries can strengthen Melaka’s brand appeal. Implementing responsible tourism frameworks that involve local
communities in planning  and decision -making can prevent overcommercialization and preserve cultural authenticity. By
focusing on sustainable tourism practices and community engagement, Melaka can maintain its heritage integrity while
ensuring that tourism continues to generate economic and social value.  In practice, this means Melaka should not market
heritage solely as a visual attraction. Instead, heritage should be connected to local livelihoods, community participation,

intellectual advancement, thereby appealing to investors, students, and global collaborators.
Third, the study emphasizes the importance of strengthening social welfare and community well -being as essential
components of Melaka ’s brand credibility. A city that provides equitable access to housing, safety, healthcare, and
environmental quality cultivates a positive image of livability and inclusiveness. Social well -being contributes directly to civic
pride and indirectly to visito r satisfaction, as both residents and tourists value safety, cleanliness, and accessibility.
Policymakers should integrate social welfare objectives into broader city branding strategies, showcasing Melaka as a caring,
inclusive, and community -centered city. Doing so aligns with global sustainability frameworks such as the United Nations
Sustainable Development Goals, particularly those related to sustainable cities and communities.
Fourth, given the youthful and digitally engaged demographic profile of Melaka ’s stakeholders, there is a clear need to
enhance digital communication strategies. City authorities should adopt digital marketing tools and innovative technologies to
promote Melaka’s heritage and modern attractions more effectively. Interactive experiences, such as virtual tours, augmented
reality heritage trails, and multimedia storytelling, can enrich public engagement and enhance global visibility. Encouraging
youth participation in digital content creation, such as through student -led social media campaigns or creative competitions,
will ensure that Melaka ’s image is portrayed authentically by the very people who shape and experience it most. A strong
digital presence can help Melaka reach broader audiences and establish a contemporary narrative that complements its
historical legacy.
Fifth, the findings suggest that successful city branding depends on an integrated governance approach. City branding
efforts should not be treated as separate marketing initiatives but should be embedded in broader policy frameworks that
connect culture, economy, education, and social welfare. A participatory governance model that involves stakeholders from
government, academia, business sectors, and local communities can ensure that branding strategies are inclusive and
representative of diverse perspecti ves. Establishing a permanent city branding council or interagency task force would allow
for consistent coordination, policy coherence, and shared ownership of Melaka ’s identity. This integrated model promotes
accountability, ensuring that development projects align with long-term sustainability goals.
Sixth, the study offers valuable lessons for other heritage cities in Southeast Asia. Melaka's experience illustrates that
sustainable urban branding requires striking a balance between economic growth, cultural preservation, and social inclusivity .
Heritage cities across the region can learn from Melaka ’s multisectoral approach, where culture and tourism coexist with
education, innovation, and community well -being. Policymakers in other contexts can adapt similar frameworks that link
economic diversification, cultural vitality, and social responsibility, creating holistic models of urban development that enhance
both image and quality of life.
Ultimately, the findings suggest that Melaka ’s path toward global recognition hinges on striking a balance among its
diverse urban functions. Sustainable city branding should reflect a harmony between tradition and modernity, conservation
and progress, and growth and empathy. This integrated and huma n-centered approach will enable Melaka to project itself as
a resilient, inclusive, and culturally dynamic city that values its heritage while embracing innovation. This strategy ensures that
Melaka remains competitive in the global urban landscape and con tinues to inspire pride among residents and admiration
among visitors.

6. Conclusions

This study aimed to examine how five key city -branding sectors, namely, economy, education, tourism, culture, and
social welfare, influence the overall perception of Melaka city. This research contributes both theoretically and empirically to
the understanding of city branding and urban perception, particularly in the context of heritage-based cities within developing
nations. The findings provide strong support for the proposed conceptual framework, confirming that these sectors collectively
shape Melaka’s urban image and contribute to its competitiveness as a sustainable and  liveable city.
The results reveal that tourism and culture play the most significant roles in shaping Melaka’s image, reflecting the city's
historical legacy and its recognition as a UNESCO World Heritage site. These sectors enhance Melaka ’s global reputation by
showcasing its cultural richness, historical authenticity, and vibrant creative industries. Economic performance and educatio n
also significantly contribute to perceptions of the city, signaling its progressiveness and potential as a knowledge-driven urban
center. Moreover, the social welfare sector, although less dominant, remains a vital foundation for fostering inclusivity, safety,
and civic trust, which are the qualities that enhance the emotional and social dimensions of city bra nding.
The study highlights that city perception is not solely determined by infrastructure or economic strength but also by the
integrated interaction of the tangible and intangible dimensions of urban life. For Melaka, this integration of culture, economy,

6.1. Future Research

Although this study provides meaningful insights into the determinants of Melaka’s city image, several areas remain
open for further investigation. The first limitation lies in its cross-sectional design, which captures perceptions at a single point
in tim e. Future studies should adopt longitudinal designs to track how perceptions evolve in response to policy changes,
infrastructure developments, or shifts in tourism patterns. This approach provides a deeper understanding of the dynamic
nature of city branding.
Second, while the study focused primarily on five core sectors, future research could incorporate additional dimensions,
such as governance quality, environmental sustainability, digital branding, and residents' emotional attachment to the area.
These variables may capture the emerging forces that shape modern urban perceptions, particularly as cities adopt innovative
technologies and implement climate-conscious policies.
Third, the present study used a quantitative approach, which provided robust statistical relationships but limited insight
into the deeper meanings behind participants' perceptions. A mixed -methods or qualitative approach involving interviews,
focus groups , or ethnographic observations could complement these findings by exploring the cultural and emotional
narratives that underpin the formation of city identity.
Fourth, the scope of this research was limited to Melaka, a single heritage city in Malaysia. Comparative research across
multiple heritage cities, such as George Town and Kuching, or regional counterparts , such as  Chiang Mai and Hoi An, could
reveal contextual similarities and differences in city branding strategies. Such comparative studies would enrich the regional
understanding of heritage -based urban competitiveness and help identify best practices for sustainable city branding in
Southeast Asia.
Ultimately, future scholars should examine the influence of digital communication and social media on city perception.
With younger generations shaping narratives through platforms such as TikTok, Instagram, and travel blogs, digital storytelling
has become a significant factor in how cities are imagined and experienced. Understanding these virtual dimensions will be
crucial in designing more adaptive, inclusive, and forward-looking city-branding strategies.
In conclusion, this study emphasizes that Melaka’s success as a sustainable heritage city hinges on its ability to integrate
economic resilience, educational advancement, cultural vitality, tourism innovation, and social inclusivity into a cohesive c ity
branding strategy. The findings reinforce that a ci ty’s image is both a reflection of tangible development and a product of
collective perception shaped by identity, experience, and aspirations. By adopting a balanced, human -centered approach that
values both heritage and innovation, Melaka can continue to strengthen its global standing as a vibrant, inclusive, and resilient
city that preserves its past while envisioning a sustainable future.
