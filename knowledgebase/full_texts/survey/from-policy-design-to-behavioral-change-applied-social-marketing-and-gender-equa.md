Abstract. Gender equality has become a very important goal of public policy programs at multiple levels,
leading to a significant adoption of legal strategies intended to promote equal opportunities. Despite this
noticeable policy development, a constant gap remains between formal policy designs and the actual
outcomes accomplished by public institutions. Existing strategies for implementing gender equality policies
continue to count on legal and administrati ve tools, focusing on procedural alignment while giving little
consideration to behavioral, cultural and institutional communication aspects. This paper claims  that
gender equality policies are not only working as administrative instruments but also as social interventions
that plan on influencing norms, attitudes and everyday organizational practices. Building on applied social
marketing theory, the study adopts a qualitative, conceptual research design based on a structured synthesis
of academic literature regarding gender equality policies, social marketing and public-sector governance.
The paper addresses the research question of how applied social marketing can support the actual
implementation of gender equality policies in public institutions. The main result in the study consists in the
development of a conceptual framework that incorporates all the fundamental concepts of applied social
marketing into the implementation stage of gen der equality policies. Instead of emphasizing formal
compliance, the framework reframes policy implementation as a process of behavioral change and
institutional engagement. By doing so, the paper contributes to the literature on social innovation and
public-sector impact, providing a theoretically based strategy for improving the effectiveness and
sustainability of gender equality programs in public organizations.

Keywords: gender equality, applied social marketing, public policy, behavioral change, social innovation.

Introduction
Gender equality has become a central goal of public policy agendas at the national, European and
international levels, leading to the legislative structures, strategic action plans and institutional
mechanisms aimed at reducing gender-based disparities, to be widely adopted in recent decades
(Walby, 2005; Verloo, 2007; Pollack & Hafner-Burton, 2000). In particular, within the European
Union, gender mainstreaming has been included as a primary plan in public governance sy stems
with the hope of changing organizational procedures and policy (Mazey, 2000; Lombardo & Meier,
2006).
In spite of existent policy development, a large body of research, emphasize that there are
still gaps between official policies  and real results in public institutions  (Moser & Moser, 2005;
Meier & Celis, 2011) . In addition, research literature reveals that gender equality policies hav e
little impact on the actual  organizational procedures, decision- making processes and power
structures, suggesting that they are only partially, unevenly and symbolically implemented (Walby,
2005; Lombardo & Meier, 2006; Verloo & Lombardo, 2007). However, further research suggests

Gender equality policies essentially seek to influence attitudes, behaviors and norms, yet they are

Considering this, the present  study argues that applied  social marketing might offer a

2020). This paper addresses the following research question: How can applied social marketing
support the implementation of gender equality policies in public institutions?
In order to address  the research question, a qualitative conceptual research was designed,
with the purpose to identify how primary concepts from applied social marketing can be translated
into policy implementation practices in the field of gender equality. This research is structured as
following: 1. Literature review – a focus on the main elements about gende r equality and gender
equality related policies; 2. Methodology –  a description of research design; 3. Results and
discussion – an overview of the main findings; 4. Conclusions – a summary of results.

Literature review
Gender equality policies and implementation challenges
Essential features of public governance systems, especially within the public institutions in Europe,
are the policies that promote gender equality. These policies are usually thought of official public
interventions that use organizational, administrative and legal measures to promote equal
opportunities and reduce gender -based disparities (Walby, 2005; Verloo, 2007). One of the most
well-known policy approaches, related to gender equality is gender mainstreaming, defined as the
systematic integration of a gender perspective into all stages of policymaking and institutional
practice (Mazey, 2000; Lombardo & Meier, 2006).
 While gender mainstreaming has been broadly institutionalized, a big number of research
highlight constant difficulties in its implementation. Studies frequently show that gender equality
policies often times remain restricted to formal structures , producing limited impact on everyday
organizational practices (Walby, 2005; Lombardo & Meier , 2006). This issue seems to keep
coming up in later work, indicating that while gender equality policy can be talked about
convincingly, the actual transformative impact of these policies can get lost in translation
(Lombardo, Meier & Verloo, 2009).  This difference between policy design and policy outcomes
has been explained in terms of the dominance of legal and administrative approaches that focus on
compliance rather than substantive change in organizations (Pollack & Hafner-Burton, 2000).
Moreover, research has shown that in terms of implementing gender equality policies, there
is often a tendency towards symbolic or performative practices. Public organizations may introduce
equality policies, reporting mechanisms, or equality roles that are essentially aimed at
demonstrating compliance with external expectations, rather than changing the underlying
dynamics of power and gender (Verloo & Lombardo, 2007; Meier & Celis, 2011). Such an
approach to compliance -oriented policy implementation reinforces the perception of  gender
equality as an administrative requirement rather than an institutional project. Inequality can also be

Institutional resistance and organizational structures
In addition to procedural compliance, research has also emphasized the significance of institutional
resistance and culture in shaping the outcomes of gender equality policies. Feminist
institutionalism has emphasized that institutions are governed not only by formal rules and
regulations but also by informal norms and practices that exert a powerful influence on institutional
behavior (Krook & Mackay, 2011).
Research on the im plementation of gender equality policies has shown that resistance to
policy implementation is rarely obvious. Instead, it is often expressed through indifference, lack of
engagement, or selective engagement, which reflects a deeper normative resistance to  change in
terms of gender roles, merit, and professional identity (Bacchi & Eveline, 2010; Lombardo, Meier
& Verloo, 2009). Organizational culture acts as a mediator in this process. More recent thought
suggests that this includes the importance of reflexivity in institutions in terms of internal learning
in order to move away from symbolism in policy to actual change (Hervías & Radulovic, 2023).
For example, informal understandings about leadership, work-life balance, and the division of labor
by gender may shape the implementation of equality policies. When such understandings are left
unchallenged, equality policies are less likely to result in behavioral change (Walby, 2005; Krook
& Mackay, 2011).

Discursive framing and policy interpretation
An important body of literature examines the discursive aspects of gender equality policy
implementation. From this viewpoint, policies are not assumed to have essential meanings; instead,
meanings are shaped by dominant frames within certain contexts (Verloo & Lombardo, 2007). The
discursive approach to gender equality policy implementation emphasizes that the framing of
gender equality is crucial in determining the legitimacy, relevance, and feasibility of policies.
Research on the discursive politics of gen der equality policies reveals the presence of various
competing frames, such as equality as fairness, equality as efficiency, equality as compliance, etc.
(Lombardo, Meier & Verloo, 2009; Dombos et al., 2012).  Similar patterns emerge in the study of
the internationalization of ideas in terms of gender mainstreaming in policy at the national level
(True & Mintrom, 2001; Tuori, 2007). When the issue of gender equality is perceived mainly as a
technical or legal issue, the more likely the institutional actor w ill behave instrumentally with
regard to the issue.
The results of the discursive analysis also suggest that the opposition to the implementation
of gender equality policies tends to be formulated in neutral or technical terms, which conceals the
underlying normative disagreements (Verloo, 2013). Such opposition can be considered one of the
most difficult to overcome via enforcement tools, since it refers to the level of meaning -making
rather than non-compliance. These results highlight the significance of the communication process
in the implementation of policy.

Behavioral perspectives on policy implementation
Recent studies on public policy have highlighted the significance of the behavioral approach in the
study of public policy implementation. Unlike the traditional approach, which refers mainly to the
rational compliance of the rules of public policy, the behavioral approach highlights the fact that
the responses of the institutional actor to public policy are based on perceptions, incentives, and
contextual constraints (Elgström, 2000; Bacchi, 2009).  This approach can be considered highly

Applied social marketing and the research gap
Social marketing, as a tool for influencing behaviors, has been defined as “an application of the
science of marketing to influence voluntary behaviors that benefit individuals or society” (Grier &
Bryant, 2005). The main principles of social marketing include being behavior -oriented,
segmenting the target audience, value exchange, and the strategic use of the social marketing mix.
These principles have been widely used in the implementation of public policies such as public
health and environmental sustainability, where individual and collective behaviors are the main
success factors (Gurrieri et al., 2020). Despite the clear relevance of the behavioral approach to the
implementation of gender equality public policies, the literature shows a clear lack of integration
of the theory of social marketing and the study of gender equality public policies. Social marketing
in governance and equality policy is not widespread but is becoming more common in public health
and sustainability policy (Gurrieri et al., 2020).
Existing studies on gender equality policies offer comprehensive diagnose s of the
challenges in the implementation process, including symbolic compliance, institutional resistance,
and discursive dilution, but rarely offer concrete mechanisms to overcome these challenges in the
implementation process (Bacchi & Eveline, 2010; Ve rloo, 2013). This is also evident in the lack
of conceptual models that explicitly link the objectives of gender equality policies with the
mechanisms of changing behavior in the context of the institution. Applied social marketing is
recognized as having a comprehensive framework to influence behavior; however, it has not been
considered in the discussions on the implementation of gender-responsive public policies.
 This study fills this gap by incorporating the principles of applied social marketing in th e
conceptual model for the implementation of gender equality policies in public institutions. In this
regard, this study introduces a new perspective on the implementation of gender equality policies
in the public sector.

Methodology
This paper follows a  qualitative conceptual research design with the objective of creating a
framework for the implementation of gender equality policies in public institutions. The research
process is based on the synthesis of literature from three main sources of academic l iterature:
gender equality and gender mainstreaming policies, public sector governance and change, and
applied social marketing theory. The sources were recognized and synthesized through a previous
systematic review process, which ensured transparency and  consistency in the selection of
literature. Only peer -reviewed journals, books, and authoritative institutional literature were
considered for the synthesis to ensure a strong theoretical basis for the research. Information was
approached in a deductive way, in order to assure a top to bottom covering of the literature, with
the purpose of developing the conceptual framework. (Saunders, et al., 2009).

The development of The Behavioral Gender Equality Implementation Framework (BGEIF)
was informed through a systematic review of 50 peer -reviewed academic papers, with a focus on
gender equality policies in the European public institutions, within the period 2000- 2025.  The
review was organized in 3 phases, according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews
and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA), to assure comprehensiveness and reproducibility. In the first phase,
searches were conducted across multiple databases (Scopus, Web of Science, ScienceDirect,
ResearchGate, OpenAlex), using compilations of the following keywords strings: “Public policies
and gender equality” + “social marketing”; “Gender equality in public policies” + “social
marketing”; “Policy framing” + ”gender equality” + ”public institutions”;  “Social marketing and
gender equality”; “Gender equality and public institutions”  +  “social marketing”. The second
phase referred to screening. During this phase, academic papers were included in this review based
on the following criteria: they are related to gender equality in public policies/institutions and social
marketing; they were published within the period 2000- 2025; they were available in full-text and
peer-reviewed formats. At the same time, during this phase  some academic papers were excluded
based on the following criteria: they were not available in full-text or peer-reviewed formats; they
were not published within the period of 2000- 2025; they were not related to gender equality in
public policies/ institutions and social marketing. Also, duplicates were excluded. From the initial
search, 50 articles were included in the final selection after filtering and screening of the studies in
the third phase.
The analytical strategy used in this study is based on a comprehensive approach. First, the
literature on gender equality policies has been examined in terms of common challenges in
implementation, with particular attention to practices focused on institutional resistance,
compliance and symbolic adoption. Second, the findings of behavioral policy and discursive policy
studies have been examined in terms of their implications for understanding the role of the culture,
norms and processes in policy results . Third, applied social marketing theory has been examined
in order to collect elements that are particularly relevant to behavioral change in institutions.
Instead of using each of these literatures as separate pieces, this study has focused on areas
of i ntersection, particularly in terms of challenges in implementation identified in the gender
equality policy and elements of behavioral change identified in social marketing theory. This led
to the identification of core analytical elements, such as behavio ral orientation, audience
segmentation, value exchange and implementation mechanisms that provide the basis for the
proposed conceptual framework.
Considering the conceptual nature of the research, no empiric al data collection,
measurement and statistical analysis were performed. The methodological strength of the paper
lies in its transparent selection of the literature, logical consistency of the analytical synthesis, and
the logical consistency of the proposed framework. This is appropriate for the rese arch aim and
objective of the conceptual and theory -building nature of the research and consistent with
traditional methods of conceptual and theoretical policy research.

implementation of gender equality policies using the social marketing approach. Rather than
providing empirical research findings, the outcome of the analysis is the theory -building
contribution to rethinking the implementation process of gender equality policies as an organized
process of institutional engagement and behavioral change. This conceptual framework on the
implementation of gender equality policies using the s ocial marketing approach is named the

The Behavioral Gender Equality Implementation Framework
The Behavioral Gender Equality Implementation Framework was derived from the
systematic synthesis described above and integrates recurring implementation challenges identified
in the review with core principles of applied social marketing. It is thought of as a continuous and
integral process that consists of four conceptual parts:  (1) policy design context, (2) institutional
audience segment ation, (3) applied social marketing mechanisms, and (4) behavioral and
institutional outcomes. These four parts of the conceptual framework offer a structured approach
to understanding the process by which gender equality policies can be taken beyond bare
compliance to institutional change.

As it can be seen in the figure above (Figure 1), The Behavioral Gender Equality
Implementation Framework (BGEIF) considers the implementation of gender equality policies as
a continuous and integral process. The process begins with the formal policy design context,
moving through insti tutional audience segmentation and finally to the application of social
marketing mechanisms. The integration of the value proposition, strategic framing, cost reduction
and the social marketing mix in the BGEIF redefines the implementation process as a process of
engagement rather than just formal compliance.
The first element of the framework refers to the context of policy design and involves the
formal objectives, legal ma ndates, strategic action plans and institutional requirements related to
gender equality policies. These aspects define the normative and regulator y basis of actions and
the legitimacy of gender equality policies. Yet, as extensively described in the literature on the
subject, the availability of legal and strategic frameworks does not ensure the successful
implementation of policies and the consistent behavioral change in public institutions.  Within the
proposed framework, the policy design is not seen as a direct factor of policy outcomes, but rather
as the context within which the strategies of policy implementation take place. In this way, instead
of concentrating attention on the design of the policy, attention is drawn to the way in which the
policy is implemented.
The second element of the suggested framework introduces the concept of institutional
audience segmentation as a key mechanism of imp lementation. This concept is directly inspired
by the applied social marketing theories that reject the assumption of a homogeneous public
institution context of implementation. Rather, the suggested framework conceptualizes public
institutions as entities  composed of different groups. Segmenting these audiences also facilitates
the development of implementation strategies that are adapted to the particular behavioral barriers
and motivations that are associated with them.  Moreover, by recognizing the diffe rences in the
perceived importance of gender equality between senior decision-makers, middle management and
operational employees, the framework tries to remediate an important limitation of compliance -
based approaches, which have standardized implementati on tools that do not take into account
audience differences.
The third component is the main analytical contribution of the framework and consists of
the applied social marketing mechanisms  that are intended to facilitate behavioral change within
an institutional context. One of the key mechanisms is the development of value propositions that
articulate gender equality in terms of its value to the institution and individuals. Such value
propositions can be advanced through multiple pathways, among which education plays a
particularly important part.  Individuals from institutions might become part of the behavioral

change and development. In this way, institutions can create mechanisms to avoid the so called
“hidden inequalities” generated by gender inequality  (Agarwal, 2026). Instead of focusing on
gender equality as a legal or moral requirement, the framework encourages the development of
value propositions that articulate the potential benefits of gender equality to the institution and
individuals. This addresses one of the main areas of resistance to gender equality, which are the
perceptions of irrelevance and cost.
Closely related to the use of value propositions is the use of discursive framing and
communication strategies. In this regard, the framework encourages the use of discursive strategies
to frame gender equality in terms of institutional effectiveness and social value, in order to reduce
normative resistance.
The framework incorporates a range of mechanisms to reduce the perceived costs of
behavioral change. These include the development of gender equality practices as part of existing
practices, the development of supportive organizational environments and the minimization of the
costs for implementation. Moreover, the framework draws on the social marketing mix and
imagines the gender equality practices as the “product”, the costs of implementation as the “price”,
institutional processes as the “place” and communication strategies as the “promotion”.
The fourth part of the framework deals with implementation outcomes . This is envisioned
in behavioral and institutional rather than procedural terms. In this way, it goes beyond traditional
outcomes such as policy development, reporting, or compliance. Rather, it focuses on outcomes
such as changes in practice, the internalization of gender equality norms and the harmonization of
gender-equal behaviors within organizational processes.
Each of the four components of the BGEIF has an explicit correspondence with the findings
of the systematic review:
1. Policy design context: The systematic review provided evidence that legal and strategic
frameworks exist but are inadequate. This provided support for placing policy design
in the contextual rather than causally determinative category.
2. Institutional audience segmentation:  Evidence of varying engagement levels of
leadership, middle management and staff, provided support for incorporating
segmentation as an integral component of the framework.
3. Applied social marketing mechanisms: Evidence of resistance, framing of conflicts and
inertia in implementation, provided support for incorporating value prepositions,
strategic framing, cost reduction and social marketing mix, as integral components of
the framework.
4. Behavioral and institutional outcomes : Evidence of dissatisfaction with  procedural
outcomes, such as reports and legal frameworks provided support for redefining
outcomes in behavioral and institutional terms.
From a discussion perspective, the Behavioral Gender Equality Implementation Framework
advances current approaches to gender equality policy implementation by explicitly implementing
dimensions that are often acknowledged but insufficiently theorized. While discursive approaches
have offered lots of signs of resistance, symbolic compliance and normative immobility, they have
rarely developed these ideas into concrete implementation approaches that focus on behavior
change. Therefore, the incorporation of applied social marketing addresses these issues. It also
provides a complementary role to legal and administrative instruments by seeking to ensure that
the strategies for implementation ar e aligned with the behavioral objectives that are implied in
gender equality policy. In this respect, the study contributes to the discussion on social innovation

Operationalization of the framework
Although the BGEIF is conceptual in nature, its components can inform the development of
practical implementation strategies in public institutions.  In order to demonstrate the practicality
of the framework, this section explains the operationalization of the components of BBGEIF in
real-life contexts.
The first step in the practical application of the BGEIF is translating the existing gender
equality policy design into behavioral objectives. Rather than accepting the policy design as a fixed
outcome, the existing policy design can inform the development of behavior-oriented objectives in
public institutions. For  instance, instead of accepting “compliance with reporting requirements”,
the objective can be translated into “enhancing inclusive decision -making in recruitment
committees” or “ensuring balanced participation in leadership paths”.
The second operational phase in this model entails the identification of unique internal
audiences within public institutions. Instead of viewing the implementation context as
undifferentiated, institutions should attempt to group their actors in terms of their functions,
decision-making authority, professional affiliation and level of influence in the achievement of
gender equality. Few common internal audiences are: senior leadership and political decision-
makers; middle management and department heads; human resource professional; employees and
operational staff. Each of these groups will likely possess unique motivational factors, costs and
levels of resistance. For example, the senior leadership group will likely be influenced by strategic
framing in terms of performance and legitimacy, while operational staff will likely be more
responsive to workload factors and fairness. In this regard, institutions should engage in a basic
level of internal segmentation analysis to understand the unique opportunities and barriers

associated with each group. This operational phase translates the theoretical principle of

The third operational phase in this model entails the crafting of special value propositions.
In the context of this model, the value exchange of gender equality is not  just a moral imperative
but also a means to achieve a unique exchange. In this regard, the model requires institutions to
articulate the benefits associated with the adoption of gender equality. For example:
• For senior leadership and political decision makers : institutional reputation, innovation
potential and compliance legitimacy;
• For middle management and department heads: team performance and conflict reduction;
• For employees: work-life balance, fairness and transparency in the workplace.
At the same time, costs associated with equality, such as time, administrative costs or perceived
role loss, should also be taken into consideration. Examples of practical solutions might include
the integra tion of equality considerations into existing workflows instead of creating parallel
systems, improving reporting tools or providing managerial training to minimize uncertainty.
At this stage, the social marketing mix is applied in practice:
• Product: gender equality practices in the organization;
• Price: costs associated with the process;
• Place: areas where behaviors take place;
• Promotion: communication channels used by the institution.
Finally, the results of the implementation process should be monitored from a behavioral
perspective rather than solely from a formal perspective. In other words, instead of measuring t he
formal adoption of equality plans or reports, institutions  can track the following behavioral
markers: changes in recruitment and promotion practices, participation rates in leadership training,
perceptions of fairness and equality and changes in daily practices and norms. There is also the
potential for using qualitative and quantitative methods, such as carrying out internal clima te
surveys, focus groups or diversity dashboards, in order to create feedback loops. In this way, there
is further emphasis on the cyclical and dynamic nature of the framework.
The practical application of the BGIF is in how it seeks to change the approach from a
compliance-oriented approach to implementing gender equality to a behavioral approach. It does
this by building on existing legal instruments and creating mechanisms that address normative
resistance, indifference and symbolism issues that have bee n extensively researched in the
literature base but have not been sufficiently operationalized.

Conclusion
This paper has sought to address a persistent challenge in the field of gender equality policies by
exploring the gap between the formal policy design and the effective implementation within public
institutions. The research has demonstrated the fact that although the issue of gender equality has
become an established concept in the making of public governance structures, the tools currently
used for implementation, are still based mainly on the use of law. It has also been demonstrated
that these tools have been insufficient for the implementation of the changes required for the
achievement of the goals set by these policies.
 The present research has taken the conceptual approach to the issue by arguing that gender
equality policies are not just tools for the regulation of the governance structures but also tools for
social intervention, with the aim of changing the attitudes and practices from these structures. It

policy implementation frameworks and ensures that policy implementation mechanisms are
aligned with the unspoken behavioral goals embedded in gender equality policies.
 From a theoretical point of view, this paper contributes to the body of literature on gender-
responsive public policy by bringing together elements of feminist institutionalism, discursive
policy analysis and applied social marketing. This study also adds to the policy implementation
literature by introducing a behavior change–oriented lens that has been absent in gender equality
policy studies. This is an important extension to the conceptual toolbox for policy implementation
studies.
The proposed framework also has significant implications for social innovation, which, in
turn, affects the public sector. The proposed framework indicates that the process of innovation in
the governance of gender equality does not necessarily need to rely on new legal instruments, but
rather on new ways of engaging institutional actors and transforming organizational practices. The
tools offered by the field of applied social marketing are proven to be effective in enhancin g
institutional ownership, minimizing resistance and internalizing gender equality norms. Thus, for
policymakers, this indicates a need to move away from the more traditional, inflexible and static
approaches to policy implementation, which are often based  on the application of the existing
regulatory instruments.
In conclusion, the present paper indicates that the process of enhancing the effectiveness of
gender equality policies should involve a redesigned process of policy implementation, which
should ta ke into account the behavioral and social aspects of the process. By offering a new
perspective on the process of implementing gender equality policies, the present study, based on
the tools of applied social marketing, offers a new perspective on the proc ess of achieving gender
equality, which might prove to be useful for both theoretical purposes, aiming to advance the field
of gender equality and for the purposes of enhancing the process of gender equality in the public
sector.

Limitations and future directions
It should be noted that there are a number of limitations to the present study, which should be
acknowledged. First, the proposed framework, being a concept ual paper, has not been tested and
its applicability might vary depending on the specific c ontext. Moreover, the present paper has
focused on the public sector, which raises the question of the applicability of the proposed
framework to other organizational contexts.
In this regard, the limitations of the present paper indicate a number of promising avenues
for further research, which might explore the application of the principles of applied social
marketing, either implicitly or explicitly, during the process of implementing gender equality
policies, aiming to assess their effects on organizational behavior. Further, the proposed framework
might be compared with other tools for governance, aiming to explore their interaction with the
tools of applied social marketing, aiming to enhance the process of gender equality.
