Abstract
Maritime logistics is a core component of the global supply chain, enabling logistics facilities to offer value-added services including maritime
transport and other modes, as well as to provide inbound and outbound logistics from the point of origin to the final destination. Despite significant
advancements in fields such as operational issues, risk management, sustainability, adaptability, and outcomes of innovative technologies, research
on digital marketing strategies in maritime logistics remains limited. This study aims to provide an overview of digital marketing strategies in
maritime logistics by analyzing digital technologies and marketing channels through a systematic literature review (SLR). By conducting an SLR,
this study aims to fill the gap concerning the importance of an integrated approach to digital marketing channels and technologies and to propose
essential frameworks for scholars and practitioners to improve digital marketing strategies in maritime logistics.
Keywords: Digital marketing strategies, maritime logistics, digital marketing, systematic literature review, digital marketing channels
1. Introduction
Maritime logistics constitutes a fundamental component of
the global supply chain, integrating transportation, finished
goods handling, and outbound logistics [1,2]. Digitalization
has considerably altered the maritime industry [3], enabling
productivity and sustainability [4], and overall cost
reduction [5], technological innovations, such as tracking
and tracing systems, facilitate operational optimization,
efficient transactions, and real-time information sharing
[6,7]. Regarding digitalization within maritime logistics,
scholars have concentrated on operational facilities [8], risk
management [9,10], sustainability [11], benefits [12], and
the drivers and challenges of digitalization [13]. Despite
this progress, digital marketing strategies remain mainly
unexplored [14,15], and the adoption of digital platforms
has not been strategically aligned with marketing functions
in maritime logistics [16].
This study seeks to address this gap by conducting an
systematic literature review (SLR)  on the influence of
digital technologies and channels in maritime logistics,
focusing on major trends, thematic focus areas, research
approaches used, and assessment of a conceptual framework
for digital marketing strategies. Next, the research questions
are developed. Research question (RQ1): What are the
major research trends and thematic focus areas, including
digital technologies and marketing channels, in digital
marketing strategies for maritime logistics? (RQ2): Which
research approaches are employed concerning digital
marketing strategies? (RQ3): How can insights from SLR
be synthesized to evaluate a conceptual framework related to
digital marketing strategies in maritime logistics?
2. Literature Review
2.1. Overview of Maritime Logistics
The maritime logistics industry brings together a wide
range of actors, including shipping companies, port
operators, freight forwarders, liner companies, terminal
operators, exporters, importers, and customs authorities,
whose complex and closely linked interactions make
maritime logistics a largely business-to-business (B2B)
oriented field [17-21]. It encompasses all ocean-based
activities, including liner shipping and port-related trade [7]

Maritime logistics, as a comprehensive maritime ecosystem,
necessitates a network-level analytical framework instead
of concentrating on individual entities or firm-specific
strategies [17]. While logistics broadly covers the planning,
execution, and control of the flow and storage of goods,
services, and information from origin to destination [24],
maritime logistics, as a growing subfield, builds on this
by managing the cross-ocean movement of goods in an
increasingly segmented and demand-driven environment,
requiring the deliberate use of supply chain management
concepts to stay competitive [25]. Where traditional logistics
focuses on operational flow, supply chain management
applies the systematic, the strategic coordination of business
process within and across firms [24] to drive long-term
performance and competitive differentiation [26,27].
Within this broader framework, the maritime supply chain
integrates all activities from raw material procurement
through manufacturing, maritime transport, port handling,
and final distribution [26,27], physically and informationally
distinct actors, whose combined performance shapes the
efficiency and competitiveness of the entire chain [18,26,28],
including logistics service providers, port operators, and
liner companies [18]. Given the complex and closely linked
interactions among these diverse actors, maritime logistics
operates mainly within a B2B context [17,18], where value
does not come from individual firms alone but grows
through coordinated exchanges of resources, knowledge, and
services across the network [29,30], as shown in maritime
logistics settings by Vural et al. [31].
2.2. Digital Marketing: Definitions and Channels in
Maritime Logistics
Digital marketing contains utilizing of digital and interactive
technologies, involving websites, email, social media,
mobile platforms, and search engines, to reach customers
and promote services [32,33], and drive action and advocacy
[34]. It includes tools such as social media marketing,
content marketing, search engine optimization (SEO), search
engine marketing, affiliate programs, and email marketing
[33], adjustable to the needs of the target market through the
digital marketing mix [17].
Digital marketing is managed differently in B2B contexts
than in consumer markets. Instead, it focuses on creating
and sustaining long-term relationships among companies,
increasing the visibility of services, supporting account-based
marketing, and enabling networked actors to co-create value
[17,29]. In a B2B setting, the concept of “brand” extends
beyond a single business or actor, since value is created
through interactions among multiple actors, rather than by
a single company alone [29,30]. This process works through
three connected areas: understanding customer journeys,
providing personalized digital content, and encouraging
customer engagement [35]. These areas support cooperation,
servitization, innovation networks, relational dynamics,
branding, and trust [17]. This network-level, multi-actor
approach fits with the broader digital marketing literature,
where studies such as Pandey et al. [29] and Cioppi et al.
[36] examine digital marketing across industries rather than
focusing on specific entities, an approach that is relevant to
maritime logistics, where digital marketing helps companies
stay competitive by adopting new technologies and bringing
stakeholders into value creation [14].
As maritime logistics shifts from purely operational models
to customer-centric networks [5], digital marketing has taken
on a bigger role, operating through relational dynamics
within B2B networks rather than at the individual company
level [17]. Recent studies reflect this shift: Hofacker et
al. [17] point to B2B trust and relational dynamics as key
digital marketing components, Zeng et al. [20] show how
Digital Freight Forwarders (DFFs) use platforms to organize
traditional service delivery, Yang and Lin [16] find that
digital logistics platform adoption impacts in organizational
performance, and Al Muala [37] regards digital marketing
integration as a core part of strategic management in
maritime logistics. Despite digital marketing strategies in
maritime logistics remaining understudied, existing studies
have mostly centered on operational efficiency [4], physical
distribution, and risk management [10], necessitating an
SLR that takes a network-based approach to map how digital
marketing is currently used within the maritime logistics
ecosystem.
3. Methodology
This study employs an SLR informed by the PRISMA
protocol [38] to investigate digital marketing strategies in
maritime logistics, focusing on the co-occurrence of digital
marketing channels and digital technologies. Integrating
content analysis for reliable thematic evaluation [39]
with bibliometric analysis for tracking large-scale trends
[40] provides the overall validity of the findings [41],
supports an ideal methodological approach for mapping
the interdisciplinary field of digital marketing in maritime
logistics.
For RQ1, bibliometric analysis (Biblioshiny/R) covers
general research themes, while content analysis traces the co-
occurrence of digital technologies and marketing channels.
For RQ2, content analysis was used to determine the main
research methodologies. To address RQ3, both analyses
are combined to assess a conceptual framework for digital
marketing strategies in maritime logistics.

3.1. Database and Search Strategy
The study is being carried out without time constraints
through October 25, 2025. During the SLR process, the two
leading academic databases in the world were used, namely
Scopus and Web of Science (WoS), to provide a wide coverage
related to the subject study, following the suggestions of Yu
et al. [40], and Zhang et al. [42]. Search terms were enclosed
in quotation marks and executed as separate and independent
queries in both databases, applied to the Title, Abstract, and
Keywords fields in Scopus, and to the Topic (Title, Abstract,
Author Keywords, and Keywords Plus) fields in WoS. The
Boolean AND operator was used throughout; parentheses
were applied only where OR operators were combined, as
in: “digital marketing” AND (“sea transport*” OR “sea
transportation*”), with the wildcard (*) capturing variant
endings, and the search strategy was guided by Bahoo et al.
[43]. Searches were restricted to Article and Early Access
document types and the English language. Results were
pooled and de-duplicated prior to screening [40].
Initially, a keyword search for digital marketing using
the term “maritime” was conducted; however, the term
“maritime” yielded very few results, as shown in Table 1. As
a result, the combinations “digital marketing” AND “supply
chain” and “digital marketing” AND “logistics” yielded
extensive results and formed the primary basis for the
literature analyzed. This combined keyword approach aligns
with established SLR practices in the maritime domain.
Muñoz-Sánchez et al. [44] paired “blockchain” with “supply
chain” and “logistics” without restricting to “maritime
logistics,” applying maritime relevance criteria during
screening. Basulo-Ribeiro and Teixeira [45] used “logistics,”
“supply chain,” and “transport” to investigate Industry 4.0
and smart port logistics, rather than port-specific terms.
Fruth and Teuteberg [13] combined digitalization-related
terms as “digital,” “big data,” “digitalization,” and “digital
transformation” with maritime-specific terms including
“maritime logistics,” “shipping industry,” and “maritime
transportation”.
3.2. Selection Criteria
The PRISMA protocol was employed to ensure rigorous
and transparent selection through multiple stages, database
search, title and abstract screening, and full-text evaluation
by minimizing selection bias [38], and providing conceptual
clarity for inclusion and exclusion criteria [46].
The search has no time constraint; however, no studies were
identified prior to 2019. Only English-language studies
were included. The literature covers multidisciplinary fields
including marketing, logistics, information technology (IT),
sustainability, economics, and operations management. The
review is limited to peer-reviewed journal articles and early
access papers; books, reports, and conference proceedings
are excluded, although selected books are cited in Section 2
for theoretical context. An actor-based conceptual mapping
was applied to filter the broad search results. Studies were
included if they addressed industrial actors inherent to
maritime logistics, namely shippers, exporters/importers,
freight forwarders, liner companies, and terminal operators
[13,20,21,25,31] or if they examined B2B interactions
directly relevant to international trade and shipping. The
term consumer behavior is used here as a screening criterion
to identify and exclude studies that focus exclusively on
individual consumer markets, as the study is grounded in a
B2B organizational context throughout. The only two studies
which are based on B2C were included as exceptions due to
their direct relevance to maritime B2B contexts: (1) Oliveira

4. Findings
In the initial stage of the database search, 187 records from
Scopus and 110 from WoS were identified, resulting in a
total of 297 raw records. After removing intra-database
duplicates (n=23 from Scopus; n=20 from WoS), 254 records
remained. Following cross-database de-duplication (n=62),
192 records were retained for title and abstract screening.
Using the PRISMA protocol (see Figure 1) [48], which
outlines the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 62
papers were identified as addressing the research questions.
4.1. Research Trends and Topics
To address RQ1, bibliometric analysis (Biblioshiny/R) and
content analysis were used to map keyword trends, abstract
themes, and co-occurrences between digital technologies
and marketing channels.
In the keyword analysis, the most frequent terms are digital
marketing, e-commerce, and supply chain. Thus, other
dominant keywords include small- and medium-sized
enterprises (SMEs), digital transformation, big data, digital
marketing capabilities, social media marketing, and digital
marketing strategies. Figure 2 shows that trend topic analysis
began in 2020, as the SLR identified only two studies
published in 2019. It illustrates shifts in research interests
over time, showing that SMEs, e-commerce, and supply
chains were the main topics in 2023-2024, while digital
marketing, social media marketing, and marketing channels
emerged in 2022. Fuzzy cognitive mapping and blockchain
technology emerged as research topics in 2022, while digital
transformation became important after 2021. From 2024
onward, recent developments include digital technology,
SEO, and sustainability.
Building on the bibliometric analysis, content analysis
examined digital marketing channels, applied technologies,
and their strategic outcomes using a coding scheme adapted
from Maas et al. [49]. Digital technologies and marketing
channels were coded according to the research question,
subject matter, or methodology of the reviewed studies.
Studies that engaged with digitalization in general terms,
without naming specific technologies or channels, were
categorized as “non-specific digital technologies (non-
SDTs)” and “non-specific digital marketing channels (non-
SDMCs)”, respectively.

5. Conclusion
This SLR examines the intersection of digital marketing
strategies and maritime logistics through bibliometric and
content analyses, revealing key thematic trends, research
gaps, and co-occurrence patterns between digital technologies
and marketing channels. With no time constraint applied,
publications were identified from 2019, with a marked
increase from 2020 onward.
Social media and websites are the most common marketing
channels, mainly used to increase brand visibility, awareness,
and customer interaction [55,59,60]. Big data analytics
supports behavioral data collection via social media, while
blockchain combined with big data enhances supply chain
transparency [61] and trust in the B2B context. SMEs
primarily rely on non-SDTs, with social media the preferred
channel. Content marketing has drawn little research attention
[57]; just one study ties it directly to SEO [62]. Thus, studies
on named technologies such as AI, blockchain, and IoT
tend to focus on competitiveness, risk management, and
sustainability, with limited attention to their integration with
specific marketing channels. Newer technologies, such as
digital twins and Generative AI (Gen AI), are not presented,
pointing to a gap between technological advancements
and their application in digital marketing within maritime
logistics. The literature indicates a lack of the interaction
between human resources and automation within maritime
logistics: evidence from other fields demonstrates that
human-robotics interaction [63] can improve digital
marketing outcomes [64], facilitate extensive customization
[65], and assist in predicting customer behavior [66], factors
largely missing from the review studies.
For RQ2, quantitative approaches are the most common,
with surveys and web analytics being the main methods
used. In survey-based studies of SMEs and supply chains,
digitalization and marketing capabilities have received the
most attention, whereas SDTs have largely been overlooked.
Fuzzy hybrid simulations [59], machine learning [67], and
AI are gaining ground in web analytics [60] for studying
customer behavior, marketing performance, and logistics
optimization. That said, predictive modeling of customer
behavior and customer perceptions in targeted marketing
strategies has yet to be sufficiently explored. The absence
of qualitative and mixed-method research constrains the
understanding of stakeholder viewpoints and contextual
dynamics in the adoption of digital marketing. Addressing
RQ3, the co-occurrence of digital marketing channels and
technologies found in the SLR points to a strategic alignment
that could support integrated digital marketing strategies,
yet many do not cover both dimensions within the same
study. Figure 5 presents a proposed framework drawn from
SLR findings, and supplemented by three external studies
included to fill technological gaps. Parola et al. [12] add
operational technologies, including robotization, smart
sensors, and human-machine interfaces, as marketing
infrastructure components, grounded in the service-
dominant logic framework [68]. Hermann and Puntoni [69]
and Cillo and Rubera [70] bring in Gen AI, which is still
underrepresented in the SLR but has clear implications for
hyper-customization and content-driven marketing strategies
in maritime logistics.
Based on the SLR findings, the effectiveness of the
interaction between digital technologies and marketing
channels is evaluated through marketing outcomes that are
categorized into two groups in Figure 5. Customer-related
outcomes include value derived from customers [32],
including satisfaction, brand awareness, visibility, loyalty,
participation, and value co-creation [68]. Internal business
outcomes deal with marketing [69], financial [71], and
operational [67] success, enabling measurable factors for
companies. Bridging these two outcome dimensions, digital
trade platforms function as interfaces that bridge between
digital marketing channels and emerging technologies
[53,54] by enabling end-to-end integration of marketing and
logistics functions within the maritime ecosystem [20], as
illustrated in Figure 5. The SLR reveals a lack of studies
that quantitatively examine marketing outcomes of channel-
technology integration, highlighting the need for future
research to establish measurable links between integration
and performance.
Limited research draws on established marketing theories,
such as the resource-based view [72,73] and service-
dominant logic [74], despite their relevance to value co-
creation and actor coordination in maritime logistics.
The DART model (dialogue, access, risk assessment,
transparency) [75] and marketing mix framework [76] are
similarly underutilized, discussing a theoretical gap in how
coordination and interaction among maritime logistics actors
are conceptualized in digital marketing research.
This study has several limitations. The SLR takes a broad,
phenomenon-driven scope to map the overall landscape of
digital marketing strategies across the maritime logistics
ecosystem [13], which enabled comprehensive thematic

coverage but does not capture actor-specific dynamics at the
macro-ecosystem level. Fruth and Teuteberg [13] also note

frameworks for particular actors. Both the database
selection (Scopus and WoS) and the search terms used may

adjacent terms such as B2B, service industry, and digital
transformation to broaden coverage. Social media is the most
common marketing channel; however, how specific actors
deploy these channels in practice falls outside the scope of
this review.
Future research could explore these gaps through several
avenues. Segment-specific studies could investigate how
digital marketing strategies differ across shipping market
segments in response to distinct customer expectations and
business structures [5]. Actor-specific studies are needed,
concentrating on individual participants such as ship
owners, operators, or freight forwarders, to explore their
digital marketing maturity and brand-building strategies;
while existing research has primarily examined these actors
from an operational perspective [6,44], digital marketing
strategies remain mostly unexplored. Comparative analyses
that bring together SLR findings and primary data from
official industry sources, and that are cross-referenced
with actual digital marketing activities of leading maritime
actors, would allow a more grounded evaluation across
different actor types. Furthermore, digital technologies
could be seen not merely as technical tools but as strategic
marketing components with measurable outcomes and a
special focus on Gen AI for hyper-customization and on
newer tools, such as digital quotation systems, online pricing
tools, and chatbot-based implementations. Evaluating
digital marketing performance using dual measures, namely
external indicators (satisfaction, loyalty, brand awareness)
and internal indicators (profitability, efficiency, business
performance), can contribute to a more extensive framework.
Thus, the digital marketing strategies of SMEs and DFFs
need to be compared with those of large incumbent players,
while also tracing adoption across geographically distinct
maritime markets [29,34].
This study contributes to the literature by mapping the
intersection of digital marketing and maritime logistics
and by presenting key research trends, gaps, and theoretical
deficiencies. From a practical perspective, the study
provides insights for maritime logistics firms and B2B
marketers to build more data-driven and customer-focused
digital strategies. It highlights how technologies such
as AI, big data analytics, and blockchain can strengthen
