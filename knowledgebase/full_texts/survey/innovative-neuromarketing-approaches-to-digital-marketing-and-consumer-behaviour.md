I.INTRODUCTION
Digital environments are now a primary interface between
firms and  consumers,  where persuasion  often depends  on
visual-first communication and rapid information processing.
Traditional analytics (e.g., clicks, dwell time) and self-reports
only partially capture the underlying mechanisms of attention
and interpretation,  so  neuromarketing  – particularly  eye-
tracking –  offers  a complementary lens on  how  consumers
orient to  stimuli  and  process  visual  information  under
different psychological states [1], [2].
Eye-tracking is  widely  used  to  study  attention to  brand
ele
ments, visual  hierarchy, and design  choices [3],  [4].
However, visual processing  is  state-dependent:  anxiety  –
especially in crisis contexts – can bias attention toward threat
monitoring, reduce attentional flexibility, and
increase
cognitive load.  These shifts may change not only how
consumers interpret digital content but also the reliability of
eye-tracking data (e.g., calibration/synchronisation quality
and usable trials), raising the risk of bi ased inference if
“measurement friction” is ignored.
Age and lived experience can further shape these effects.
Older adults may show slower processing and reduced
flexibility in allocating attention across multiple elements,
which can be reflected in practical eye -tracking indicators
such as calibration succ ess, synchronisation time, and trial
quality. Similarly, crisis -related experiences (e.g.,
displacement) may elevate anxiety and produce attention
patterns such as hypervigilance or avoidance  [7], [8] , yet
evidence linking these factors to eye -tracking out comes in
digital contexts remains limited.
The aim of this paper is to examine how anxiety and life-
context factors – specifically age and displacement – shape
visual information processing and the reliability of eye -
tracking measurement in digital media.  This study addresses
the gap through two experiments conducted in a real -world
crisis setting. Study 1 tests age -related differences in
coordination and recording quality under neutral visual
content. Study 2 compares internally displaced persons (IDPs)
and local residents during emotionally varied image viewing
to assess anxiety differences and anxiety -consistent attention
patterns. We test three hypotheses: older participants (60+)
will exhibit lower coordination/quality than younger
participants (H1); IDPs will exhibit greater anxiety and/or
stronger anxiety -consistent attention patterns than local
residents (H2); and self -reported anxiety will be positively
associated with synchronisation time (H3).
The study contributes by showing that constraints on
visual processing are systematically linked to age and anxiety-
related context, by positioning measurement reliability as a
theoretically meaningful outcome in applied eye -tracking
research, and by offer ing actionable implications for digital
communication design, particularly the value of simpler
layouts and stronger visual hierarchy for audiences under
cognitive strain.
II.LITERATURE REVIEW
A. Technologies and Methods of Neuromarketing
Neuromarketing sits at the intersection of marketing,
consumer psychology, and neuroscience and focuses on tools
and analytics that capture attention and emotion beyond self-

report. Empirical work shows how neurophysiological
techniques can support message effectiveness in applied
contexts, including social marketing in healthcare [9] and
packaging evaluation using neuroimaging [10].
Methodologically, EEG -based research increasingly uses
advanced modelling to decode affective states [11] and to
convert complex EEG outputs into interpretable emotional
summaries [12]. Visual design features remain central stimuli,
with evidence that colour and shape influence emotional
responses and perceived product quality [13]. Together, these
studies frame neuromarketing as a multimethod toolkit
combining neuroscience measurement and data -driven
interpretation.
B. Application of Neuromarketing in Consumer Behaviour
and Design
Applied research demonstrates neuromarketing’s value for
optimising digital communication and consumer experience.
Conceptual work argues that neuroimaging can reveal
multisensory and emotional mechanisms behind decision -
making, supporting strategy design aligned with nonconscious
preferences [2]. In practice, eye -tracking is widely used to
improve visual advertising and design elements [3] and to
understand how labelling/signage shapes emotions and
choices in services such as tourism [14]. Sector studies l ink
neuromarketing cues to purchase intentions [15] and show that
online shoppers prioritise product images over text [4]. At the
same time, reviews note fragmentation and gaps in web/UX
neuromarketing [16] and highlight barriers to adoption such
as cost and ethical concerns [17]. Related work discusses
market positioning of neuromarketing services [18] and
supports combining neuromarketing with declarative methods
for packaging and visual communication [19], [20], including
service applications where neuro -metrics add value beyond
surveys [21].
C. Theoretical, Ethical, and Review Studies
Conceptual and review research stresses that
neuromarketing’s credibility depends on ethical governance
and empirical rigour. AI -enabled pipelines are increasingly
proposed to model subconscious reactions at scale [22], while
work on perceptions and implicit influence processes
highlights disciplinary differences and the need for clearer
standards [23], [24]. Studies also explore how symbolic and
emotional congruence shapes digital advertising effects [25]
and how attention, emotion, and memory relate to decision
stages in virtual environments [26], supporting personalised
strategy development [27]. Ethical discussions emphasise
privacy and autonomy risks [28] and warn of weakening
ethical oversight under commercial pressure [29]. Broader
behavioural-neuroscience evidence shows that biological and
psychosocial states can shape attention and decision -making
[6], [30] , [31], reinforcing the relevance of state -dependent
processing. In crisis contexts, digital marketing is discussed
as supporting SME resilience and adaptati on [7], [8], while
attention research highlights the importance of early
engagement windows [32] and implicit motivational
influences on affective processing [5]. Finally, reviews call
for stronger empirical designs, standardised metrics, and
cross-cultural validation [1], [33,] alongside applied
discussions of AI -enabled real -time insight for mission -
driven communication [34].
Consistent with the proposed framework linking anxiety,
cognitive resources, and visual information processing, this
study tests three hypotheses aligned with the operational
measures available across the two studies.
H1 (Age → coordination/quality):  Participants aged 60+
will exhibit lower eye -tracking task coordination efficiency
than participants aged 18 –45 (e.g., longer synchronization
time, more calibration difficulty, and lower trial quality).
H2 (Displacement → anxiety and anxiety -consistent
attention patterns).  Internally displaced persons (IDPs) will
exhibit higher anxiety than local residents and display
anxiety-consistent visual attention patterns when viewing
emotionally varied images (e.g., hypervigilance, longer
fixation on anxiety -inducing images, and avoidance of
danger cues when present).
H3 (Anxiety → coordination): Self-reported anxiety will be
positively associated with synchronization time (higher
anxiety → longer synchronization time).
III.DATA AND METHODOLOGY
A. Research design and overview
This research employs a two -study experimental design
using eye -tracking to examine how age and crisis -related
anxiety context shape visual information processing in digital
media.
Study 1 (N=24) tests whether visual task coordination and
recording reliability differ by age (18 –45 vs 60+) under
neutral visual content (a short nature video), focusing on
calibration efficiency, synchronization time, and trial quality
as indicators of t ask coordination during eye -tracking. Self-
assessed anxiety ( rated 1–10) was collected in Study 1 and
analysed in relation to synchronisation time (H3).
Study 2 (N=40) tests whether individuals with
displacement experience (internally displaced persons; IDPs)
differ from local residents in anxiety level and anxiety -
consistent attention patterns when viewing emotionally varied
images, using the Anima platfo rm to capture eye -movement
indicators of hypervigilance, fixation tendencies, and
avoidance responses alongside an overall anxiety
classification. Two studies are used to (i) establish a baseline
demographic effect (age) under neutral content where
emotional confounds are minimised, and (ii) evaluate whether
crisis-related lived experience is associated with elevated
anxiety and distinct visual processing patterns under
emotionally salient stimuli, thereby strengthening the
interpretability and external rel evance of the findings for
consumer attention in real-world digital contexts.
B. Study 1: Age and visual processing under neutral
content
Study 1 involved 24 participants recruited in Ukraine and
tested in the Neuromarketing and Advertising Laboratory at
West Ukrainian National University. Participants were
equally distributed by gender (12 women, 12 men) and
assigned to one of two age groups: a you nger group (18 –45
years) and an older group (60+ years) (12 participants per
group). Participants were screened to minimise acute
emotional confounds: prior to the experiment, respondents
completed a brief check of current emotional state and did not
report strong emotions such as irritation, aggression,
happiness, or euphoria at the time of testing. Individuals
reporting anxiety disorders were not included. If an air -raid
alarm occurred during testing, the session was halted and

rescheduled to avoid distortion of attention measures by
immediate emotional arousal.
Participants viewed a 10 -second neutral nature video
designed to elicit minimal emotional activation and provide a
standardised visual stimulus for comparing attention -related
processing across age groups. The use of neutral content
reduces the risk that group differences are driven by affective
reactivity rather than by age -related differences in visual
processing and coordination.
Eye movements were recorded using an Eye Tracking Kit
with CoolTool NeuroLab software. Participants completed a
standard calibration procedure prior to stimulus presentation,
and a subsequent synchronisation step was used to register
sufficient pupil/attention signal for the system to initiate the
experiment.
Participants were seated in front of the display under
controlled laboratory conditions. Lighting was kept stable,
and participants were instructed to minimise head movement
and to watch the stimulus naturally. The experimental
sequence comprised: (1) a brief pre-check of emotional state;
(2) eye-tracker calibration; (3) a synchronization phase during
which the system registered a sufficient signal/attention level
to initiate recording; and (4) presentation of the 10 -second
nature video while gaze data were collected. Any interruption
due to external alerts (e.g., air -raid alarm) resulted in
termination of the session and rescheduling.
Measures. The primary outcome variables captured task
coordination efficiency and recording reliability in eye -
tracking:
• Calibration completion time (relative duration compared
across groups).
• Unsuccessful calibration attempts, including repeated
attempts required to achieve acceptable calibration.
• Synchronization time (seconds), defined as the time
required for the system to register sufficient
signal/attention to begin the experiment (reported as group
averages: 7s vs 19s).
• Trial quality rating, recorded on a 5 -star scale, where
higher ratings indicate higher -quality eye -tracking data;
the main indicator reported is the proportion of trials rated
4–5 stars (control 86% vs experimental 54%).
Analysis plan. Analyses compared outcomes between the
18–45 and 60+ groups. Descriptive statistics were reported
for each measure. Group differences were assessed using
independent-samples tests appropriate to the measurement
scale (e.g., independent t -tests for continuous measures and
proportion tests or non -parametric alternatives where
assumptions were not met). Statistical significance was
evaluated at α = 0.05 (two-tailed). Effect sizes (e.g., Cohen’s
d for mean differences and risk difference/odds rat ios for
proportions) are reported alongside p -values to support
interpretation. Outliers and unusable recordings were handled
via pre-specified rules based on failed calibration, excessive
signal loss, or low trial -quality ratings; results  are reported
both with and without exclusions where feasible as a
robustness check.
C. Study 2: Displacement status, anxiety, and emotionally
varied images
Participants. Study 2 involved 40 participants, equally
divided between men and women, tested at West Ukrainian
National University. Participants were assigned to two groups:
an experimental group of internally displaced persons (IDPs)
and a control group of local residents from a relatively safe
region.
Stimulus. Participants conducted a visual assessment of a
series of images with varying emotional tones. The stimulus
set was designed to elicit differential attention patterns
associated with anxiety (e.g., attention to emotionally intense
or threat-relevant cues) while enabling comparison between
IDPs and local residents.
Apparatus and software. Eye movements were recorded
using the Anima platform (video-camera-based eye-tracking)
within the immersive multifunctional hub of West Ukrainian
National University. The platform enabled the collection of
gaze-based indicators link ed to anxiety -related cognitive
processing during image viewing.
Procedure. After assignment to the IDP or local resident
group, participants viewed the image series while the Anima
platform recorded eye movements. The study was designed to
confirm or refute the hypothesis that IDPs exhibit higher
anxiety levels than lo cal residents, using eye -movement
indicators and the platform’s anxiety scoring approach.
Measures. To align tightly with what is reported in the
Results section, Study 2 focuses on anxiety-related measures
captured via the Anima methodology:
Overall anxiety level classification (low / moderate / high)
based on the platform methodology.
Eye-tracking indicators interpreted as anxiety -related
visual processing patterns, including:
• hypervigilance while viewing images,
• tendency to fixate longer on anxiety-inducing images, and
• avoidance of danger signals (when present).
Analysis plan. Data were processed using statistical
analysis to compare anxiety outcomes between IDPs and local
residents and to interpret group -level differences in anxiety -
consistent attention patterns derived from eye -tracking
outputs (e.g., heatmap/fi xation visualisations and the
platform’s anxiety classification).
Randomisation note: the manuscript describes image
viewing as a series but does not specify whether image order
was randomised; therefore, randomisation is not claimed in
the design description.
IV.RESULTS
A. Study 1 (Age): Eye-tracking task coordination and
recording quality (H1)
H1 predicted that participants aged 60+ would exhibit
lower eye -tracking task coordination efficiency than
participants aged 18 –45, reflected in longer synchronization
time, greater calibration difficulty, and lower recording
quality. In Study 1 (N = 24), the older group re quired
substantially more effort during the pre -experiment setup.
During calibration, participants aged 60+ took 80% longer to
complete the process compared to the younger group, and the
proportion of unsuccessful calibration attempts was 120%
higher than in the younger group. During synchronization, the
mean time required for the system to register sufficient
signal/attention to initiate the experiment was 19 seconds for
participants aged 60+ versus 7 seconds for participants aged
18–45 (Table I).

B. Study 1 (Age): Anxiety and synchronization time (H3)
H3 predicted that self -reported anxiety would be
positively associated with synchronization time. Using

D. Summary of hypothesis support
Across studies, H1 was supported by age -related
differences in calibration/synchronization effort and recording
quality. H3 was supported by a strong positive association
between synchronization time and self -reported anxiety. H2
was supported in terms of group differences in anxiety
distribution and anxiety -consistent attention patterns;
however, a direct test of “task coordination efficiency” by
displacement status would benefit from reporting the same
calibration/synchronization indicators used in Study 1 for the
Study 2 groups.
V.DISCUSSION
A. Summary of findings
This research examined how anxiety -related context and
demographic factors shape visual information processing in
digital media, using two eye-tracking experiments conducted
in a crisis setting. Study 1 demonstrated clear age -related
differences in eye -tracking task coordination and recording
reliability: participants aged 60+ showed longer calibration
completion time, higher rates of unsuccessful calibration
attempts, longer synchronization time, and a lower share of
high-quality trials compared with parti cipants aged 18 –45.
Study 1 also revealed a strong positive association between
synchronization time and self -assessed anxiety. Study 2
extended the crisis -context perspective by comparing
internally displaced persons (IDPs) with local residents. IDPs
exhibited substantially higher anxiety classifications and
attention patterns consistent with anxiety -related
hypervigilance and avoidance tendencies. Taken together, the
findings suggest that consumer visual processing in digital
contexts is not uniform across audiences; rather, it is sensitive
to anxiety and life-context factors, with implications both for
consumer behaviour theory and for applied neuromarketing
practice.
B. Theoretical implications
First, the findings contribute to consumer behaviour
research on affect and attention by showing that anxiety-related
states are linked not only to what individuals attend to (as
suggested by hypervigilance/avoidance patterns) but also to the
efficiency with which attention can be operationalised and
measured in technology -mediated settings. In eye -tracking
research, calibration and synchronization are often treated as
purely technical setup steps. The present evidence suggests that
these indicators also re flect psychologically meaningful
constraints that are likely to co-vary with anxiety and cognitive
load. Conceptually, this implies that “measurement friction”
can be informative about underlying consumer states and may
partially explain why digital messag es can be processed
differently across audiences under stress.
Second, the age -related findings align with cognitive
resource accounts in consumer psychology: older consumers
may face greater coordination demands in visually intensive
environments, which becomes visible both as reduced
recording reliability and as longer setup and synchronization
effort. From a consumer attention perspective, this supports
the view that the effectiveness of visual communication
depends not only on stimulus design but also on audience
capacity to process visual information under time an d
cognitive constraints.
Third, the displacement -related results highlight the
importance of integrating lived experience into consumer
behaviour models. Crisis exposure and displacement are likely
to shape consumer attention through persistent anxiety and
adaptation pressures. The observed differences in anxiety
distribution and visual attention tendencies suggest that
consumer response t o visual content in crisis contexts may
follow distinct patterns (e.g., heightened sensitivity to
potential threats, simultaneous fixation and av oidance
dynamics). This strengthens the argument that consumer
research should account for context -driven psychological
states when theorising attention and information processing in
digital environments.
C. Managerial implications for digital communications
and marketing design
The findings translate into several practical guidelines for
designing digital communications for audiences likely to
experience elevated anxiety or cognitive strain (e.g., older
consumers, crisis-affected populations, displaced consumers):
• Reduce visual complexity and clutter. Use fewer
competing visual elements per screen and avoid dense
layouts. Under anxiety and lower coordination efficiency,
visually complex designs risk reduced comprehension and
weaker message extraction.
• Strengthen visual hierarchy. Make the primary message
and action cues (headline, key benefit, call -to-action)
visually dominant through consistent placement,
whitespace, and clear salience. A strong hierarchy
supports faster orientation when attention is constrained.
• Limit emotionally intense imagery unless essential. Where
emotionally charged content is required (e.g., public
safety, crisis communications), ensure that supportive
structure is added: clear labels, predictable layouts, and
minimal competing cues.
• Design for “low-friction” attention. Use high-contrast but
simple focal points and avoid rapid multi -element
animations. Friction increases when coordination is
strained; designs should aim to reduce the time required to
locate meaning.
• Segment audiences by context, not only by demographics.
In crisis conditions, “context segmentation” (e.g.,
displaced vs stable living conditions) may be as important
as age segmentation for predicting attention and message
processing.
These implications are directly relevant to digital
marketing campaigns, public information design, and
customer experience interfaces where audiences may be under
stress and where message clarity and trust are critical.
VI.RESEARCH LIMITATIONS
Several limitations should be considered when
interpreting the results. First, the samples are relatively small
(Study 1: N = 24; Study 2: N = 40), which constrains statistical
power and generalisability. Second, Study 2 reports anxiety
distributions and qualitative attention patterns; a stronger test
of displacement differences in “task coordination efficiency”
would require reporting the same calibration, synchronization,
and trial-quality indicators used in Study 1 for the IDP and
local resident groups. Third, the crisis context in which the
research was conducted may increase variance in
psychological states and introduce unobserved heterogeneity
(e.g., individual coping resources, recent exposures, sleep
disruption), which may affect attention and measu rement
quality. Fourth, reliance on self -assessed anxiety and
platform-based anxiety classification, while practical, would

be strengthened by triangulation (e.g., validated anxiety
scales, physiological indicators such as heart rate variability
where feasible). Finally, the stimuli used include neutral video
and emotionally varied images; the extent to which these map
onto rea l digital marketing stimuli (ads, landing pages, e -
commerce layouts) could be strengthened by incorporating
explicitly marketing-relevant stimuli in future work.
VII.CONCLUSION
Overall, this study shows that visual attention in digital
media is shaped by anxiety -related context and age, with
implications for both consumer behaviour theory and the
applied use of neuromarketing tools. The evidence indicates
that under anxiety and c ognitive strain, consumers may
experience reduced coordination efficiency and altered
attention tendencies. Designing digital communications that
reduce cognitive load and strengthen visual hierarchy may
therefore improve message processing and engagement,
particularly for vulnerable audiences in crisis conditions.
