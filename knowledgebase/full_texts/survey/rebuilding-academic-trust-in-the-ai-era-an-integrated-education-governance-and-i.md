Abstract
The rapid integration of Generative Artiﬁcial Intelligence (GenAI) in higher education has triggered an
institutional crisis of academic trust. This challenge is characterized by an "adoption-trust paradox,"
where ubiquitous student usage coexists with deep ethical skepticism and concerns over academic
integrity. While existing research predominantly focuses on individual technology acceptance models
(e.g., TAM, UTAUT), this study shifts the analytical lens to institutional governance and its marketing
implications, framing academic trust as a vital strategic brand asset that drives organizational legitimacy
and student behavioral intentions. We propose and empirically test a multidimensional governance
framework consisting of four core pillars: ethical transparency, algorithmic accountability, digital AI
literacy, and adaptive AI governance. Utilizing a quantitative research design and Partial Least Squares
Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM), we analyze survey data from 380 higher education students in
Tunisia. The structural model reveals that Adaptive AI Governance is the most powerful predictor of
Institutional Trust (β = 0.297, p < 0.001), followed by Algorithmic Accountability (β = 0.258, p < 0.001) and
Ethical Transparency (β = 0.231, p < 0.001), while AI Literacy has a smaller but signiﬁcant effect (β = 
0.137, p = 0.006). Institutional Trust, in turn, exhibits an exceptionally strong positive relationship with
Perceived Institutional Ethicality (β = 0.741, p < 0.001). Together, trust and ethicality explain 61.2% of the
variance in students' behavioral intentions toward sustainable AI use, with perceived ethicality acting as
a signiﬁcant partial mediating mechanism (β_indirect = 0.337, p < 0.001). Beyond pedagogical
compliance, these ﬁndings demonstrate that robust governance directly enhances institutional brand
equity, student attraction, and procedural legitimacy. We outline strategic recommendations for
university marketers and educators to co-create proactive, values-led learning ecosystems that
strengthen academic reputation in the digital era.
1. Introduction
Higher education has entered a structural turning point in which Generative Artiﬁcial Intelligence (GenAI)
is no longer an emerging instructional novelty but a pervasive force reshaping how knowledge is
produced, assessed, communicated, and legitimized (Abdi & Abdullahi, 2026; Đerić et al., 2025; Maleki,
2026). The central disruption is not simply technological adoption; it is the erosion of academic trust that
occurs when institutions can no longer conﬁdently distinguish authentic learning from machine-assisted
performance, nor rely on inherited assessment and integrity mechanisms to preserve credibility (Koﬁnas
et al., 2025; Maleki, 2026). This tension has produced an "adoption-trust paradox" across higher
education. Students and educators increasingly use GenAI for writing, feedback, translation, idea
generation, and academic support, yet this growing dependence coexists with serious concerns about
cheating, misinformation, privacy, bias, weakened critical thinking, and the dilution of authorship and
originality (Abdi & Abdullahi, 2026; Sun et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025). Multicultural evidence shows that
many participants view GenAI as a potential facilitator of academic misconduct, while others
simultaneously recognize its educational value, conﬁrming that institutional responses must address
ambivalence rather than assume either resistance or acceptance alone (Yusuf et al., 2024).The problem,
therefore, is not merely whether GenAI will be used in universities, but whether its use will be governed in
Page 2/33

ways that sustain legitimacy, fairness, and conﬁdence in institutional judgment. Existing research shows
that trust in educational AI is shaped differently from general technology adoption because it depends
not only on performance or usability, but also on socio-ethical concerns, pedagogical values, and
institutional mediation (Lelescu et al., 2025; Sabri et al., 2026). Trust is also multifaceted, encompassing
beliefs about accuracy, privacy protection, and non-maliciousness, which makes it especially vulnerable
when universities offer inconsistent guidance or fail to align policy with actual classroom realities (Đerić
et al., 2025). Early institutional responses have been uneven. Universities have begun to articulate ethical
dimensions such as transparency, accountability, human oversight, and inclusiveness, yet many policy
environments remain fragmented, under-speciﬁed, or overly reactive (Dabis & Csáki, 2024; Nikolic et al.,
2024). Systematic reviews further show that formal training, systematic guidelines, and comprehensive
policy frameworks remain scarce, even though institutional strategies such as leadership support,
policies, and training are foundational drivers of trust (Lelescu et al., 2025; Nikolic et al., 2024). In some
contexts, visible institutional support still lags behind student adoption, suggesting that universities are
often responding after trust conditions have already deteriorated (Abdi & Abdullahi, 2026). For that
reason, the restoration of institutional trust requires a shift from punitive or ad hoc control toward a
formal governance architecture. Evidence increasingly suggests that sustainable trust is strengthened
when institutions operationalize transparent communication, digital accountability, literacy-building,
participatory norms, and responsible oversight as concrete organizational practices rather than symbolic
ethical commitments (Aprianto et al., 2026; Saddam et al., 2026). Trust-building also depends on
calibrating use appropriately: both overtrust and undertrust produce harmful outcomes, while guided,
transparent, and norm-based ecosystems encourage more reﬂective and sustainable engagement with
AI (Đerić et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2026). Against this background, this study addresses the core question:
which institutional governance pillars most directly shape students’ trust in their academic institution
during the age of GenAI? This study argues that Ethical Transparency, Algorithmic Accountability, AI
Literacy, and Adaptive AI Governance constitute the core structural assurances through which
universities can rebuild conﬁdence in the fairness, intelligibility, and ethical legitimacy of AI integration
(Lelescu et al., 2025; Maleki, 2026). Ethical transparency reduces uncertainty through clear disclosure
and honest communication (Dabis & Csáki, 2024; Shata & Hartley, 2025; Wang et al., 2025). Algorithmic
accountability reinforces conﬁdence by preserving human responsibility, oversight, and procedural
fairness in AI-mediated decisions (Dabis & Csáki, 2024; Xia et al., 2026). AI literacy equips students to
evaluate capabilities, limitations, and risks more critically, supporting informed rather than blind trust
(Alotaibi, 2025; Baig & Yadegaridehkordi, 2025; Sun et al., 2026). Adaptive AI governance enables
institutions to respond to rapid technological change with ﬂexible, educative, and context-sensitive policy
rather than static prohibition (Dabis & Csáki, 2024; Abdi & Abdullahi, 2026). Accordingly, this study
departs from individualist adoption models by treating trust not as a by-product of user familiarity alone,
but as an institutional outcome produced by governance quality. In doing so, it responds to growing calls
for higher education to approach GenAI integration not as a race toward adoption, but as a deliberate
process of trust-building that secures academic integrity, ethical sustainability, and institutional
legitimacy in digitally transformed learning environments (Lelescu et al., 2025; Maleki, 2026).

2. Literature Review and Hypothesis Development
2.1 The Crisis of Academic Trust in the AI Era
Academic trust is the cornerstone of the relationship between students, educators, and institutions.
However, the rise of GenAI has introduced systemic vulnerabilities (Coman et al., 2026; Francis et al.,
2025; Sejdiu et al., 2026). Scholars note that the "black-box" nature of Large Language Models (LLMs)
obscures the origin of information, leading to challenges in authorship, ownership, and accountability
(Maleki, 2026; Lelescu et al., 2025; Sejdiu et al., 2026). The risk of "hallucinations", where AI generates
plausible but false information, further undermines the reliability of academic research (Đerić et al.,
2025). Recent reviews argue that GenAI exposes systemic weaknesses in policy design, assessment
regimes, and institutional culture rather than merely creating isolated episodes of student misconduct
(Alyafei et al., 2026; Maleki, 2026; Sejdiu et al., 2026). This erosion of trust is intensiﬁed by the fact that
GenAI-generated content is often diﬃcult to distinguish from human work, undermining long-standing
assumptions that authorship is observable, veriﬁable, and individually attributable (Alyafei et al., 2026;
Maleki, 2026). Traditional safeguards such as plagiarism detection, proctoring, and static integrity codes
no longer provide suﬃcient assurance in environments where AI can generate original text, summaries,
and disciplinary writing on demand (Alyafei et al., 2026; Furze et al., 2024; Maleki, 2026). Scholars
therefore increasingly frame the problem as a transition from content veriﬁcation to process
accountability, where institutions must evaluate how work is produced, disclosed, and justiﬁed rather
than whether a text appears original on its surface (Maleki, 2026; Sejdiu et al., 2026). Trust is further
destabilized by the epistemic opacity of large language models. The literature repeatedly identiﬁes
concerns over accuracy, hallucinations, misinformation, privacy, and non-malicious use as core
dimensions shaping whether GenAI is seen as trustworthy in academic settings (Đerić et al., 2025).
When users cannot determine how outputs were generated, what data sources were used, or whether
fabricated content has been introduced, conﬁdence in both the technology and the academic process is
weakened (Coman et al., 2026; García-López & Trujillo-Liñán, 2025). Privacy and intellectual property
concerns deepen this problem, since fears of data misuse, ownership violations, and harmful or
unethical applications extend distrust from the tool itself to the institution that authorizes or normalizes
its use (Đerić et al., 2025; Furze et al., 2024). A second source of the trust crisis is the widening gap
between rapid student adoption and slower institutional readiness. Across contexts, students report high
familiarity with GenAI and broad support for its educational use, yet universities often lag in providing
coherent policy, training, and governance structures (Barus et al., 2025; Sejdiu et al., 2026; Yusuf et al.,
2024). The literature describes current institutional responses as fragmented, reactive, and unevenly
implemented, with many policies focusing narrowly on misconduct while neglecting broader
pedagogical, ethical, and regulatory questions (Coman et al., 2026; Furze et al., 2024;). While over 80% of
students engage with AI tools, a vast majority report a lack of awareness regarding their university's
speciﬁc ethical guidelines (Yusuf et al., 2024; Barus et al., 2025). This regulatory vacuum fosters an
environment where distrust persists despite the eﬃciency gains provided by the technology (Lelescu et
al., 2025). Systematic reviews also show that leadership engagement remains weak relative to the scale
Page 4/33

of the challenge, limiting the structural assurances needed for trust formation (Lelescu et al., 2025). This
mismatch between use and governance produces what can be described as an adoption-trust paradox.
Students and educators continue to use GenAI because of its convenience, eﬃciency, and learning
support, while simultaneously expressing concern that it facilitates cheating, misinformation,
overreliance, and inequity (Coman et al., 2026; Jayasinghe et al., 2026; Yusuf et al., 2024). Multicultural
evidence shows that perceptions of misconduct risk and appropriate regulation vary across cultural
settings, which means trust cannot be restored through universal prohibition or purely technical ﬁxes
alone (Yusuf et al., 2024). Trust in educational AI appears to depend on socio-ethical and institutional
conditions that standard adoption models do not adequately capture (Lelescu et al., 2025).
2.2 Construct Grounding and Theoretical Perspectives
To address these limitations, rebuilding academic trust requires a shift from individual-level acceptance
models toward an institutional governance perspective. Existing frameworks such as the Technology
Acceptance Model (TAM) and Uniﬁed Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) explain
perceived usefulness, ease of use, and behavioral intention, but they do not suﬃciently account for how
legitimacy, pedagogical values, and organizational safeguards shape trust in higher education (Lelescu
et al., 2025; Sabri et al., 2026; Sejdiu et al., 2026). Recent scholarship instead argues for integrated
approaches that connect ethics, pedagogy, assessment, and regulation within a shared institutional
architecture (García-López & Trujillo-Liñán, 2025; Sejdiu et al., 2026). In this study, four governance pillars
are advanced as the core structural antecedents of Institutional Trust: Ethical Transparency, Algorithmic
Accountability, AI Literacy, and Adaptive AI Governance. These pillars synthesize recurrent themes
across the literature on trustworthy AI, academic integrity, and responsible university policy (Dabis &
Csáki, 2024; Francis et al., 2025; Tran et al., 2025). Together, they move the analysis beyond the question
of whether students use GenAI toward the more consequential question of whether institutions govern
its use in ways perceived as fair, intelligible, and ethically legitimate (García-López & Trujillo-Liñán, 2025;
Maleki, 2026). Table 1 provides a comprehensive summary of the theoretical framing and empirical
basis of these four core pillars.

Ethical Transparency refers to the institution’s proactive disclosure of where, how, and under what limits
AI is used in academic and administrative processes. The literature treats transparency not as symbolic
openness but as a practical trust mechanism requiring clear communication about AI functionality, data
sources, limitations, and acceptable use boundaries (Coman et al., 2026; Đerić et al., 2025). Early
university responses show that transparency was operationalized through explicit syllabus
communication and acknowledgment norms, indicating that visible disclosure helps reduce uncertainty
and align expectations between institutions, teachers, and students (Dabis & Csáki, 2024; Francis et al.,
2025). Algorithmic Accountability denotes the institutional obligation to retain human responsibility for
AI-mediated judgments, recommendations, and assessment consequences. Research consistently
argues that universities must establish clear standards, monitoring mechanisms, and human-in-the-loop
oversight so that evaluative and moral agency is not delegated entirely to automated systems (García-
López & Trujillo-Liñán, 2025; Dabis & Csáki, 2024). This pillar also implies contestability: students must
be protected by procedures through which AI-supported outcomes can be questioned, reviewed, and
corrected, especially in contexts affected by bias, misinformation, or unfair automated inference (García-
López & Trujillo-Liñán, 2025; Tran et al., 2025). AI Literacy captures the competencies required to
understand, interrogate, and ethically use GenAI rather than merely operate it. Evidence from large
student samples shows that awareness and evaluative dimensions of AI literacy positively shape
attitudes toward GenAI, while broader models ﬁnd that literacy also strengthens perceived behavioral
control and downstream usage intention (Sun et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025a). At the
same time, the ethical dimension of literacy does not uniformly increase positive attitudes, suggesting
that literacy can also sharpen critical caution rather than simple acceptance (Sun et al., 2026). This
nuance is theoretically important because the present model is concerned with informed trust, not blind
trust (Đerić et al., 2025). Adaptive AI Governance refers to an institution’s capacity to revise policies,
Page 6/33

redesign oversight mechanisms, and coordinate stakeholders as GenAI evolves. Multiple reviews argue
that static or one-off regulations are inadequate because the risks, affordances, and social uses of
GenAI change too quickly for ﬁxed governance models (Sejdiu et al., 2026; Zlotnikova et al., 2025).
Effective governance therefore requires continuous evaluation, stakeholder collaboration, curriculum
integration, and ﬂexible frameworks that can be updated across disciplines and assessment types
(Furze et al., 2024; Zlotnikova et al., 2025). Evidence from assessment reform further shows that
structured but adaptable models can reduce misconduct while improving engagement, reinforcing the
value of agile rather than punitive governance (Furze et al., 2024).
2.3 Hypotheses Development
The proposed conceptual framework links these four governance pillars to Institutional Trust, which in
turn drives Perceived Institutional Ethicality and Student Behavioral Intention toward sustainable AI use.
We formally posit seven direct and indirect hypotheses below.
2.3.1. Ethical Transparency and Institutional Trust
Transparent communication of AI policies in course syllabi emerged as an early institutional good
practice among top universities responding to GenAI, directly linking disclosure norms to trust-building
(Dabis & Csáki, 2024). Perceived fairness and transparency in AI algorithms signiﬁcantly inﬂuence trust
and intention to adopt AI-based systems in educational institutions, positioning transparency as a direct
antecedent rather than a peripheral concern (Sabri et al., 2026). Positive ethical perceptions of AI,
including transparency and data privacy protection, strengthen the role of trust in shaping social
acceptance of AI technologies among students (Sun et al., 2026). Higher education institutions should
cultivate appropriate extrinsic trust by accurately communicating both learning opportunities and
potential negative issues such as plagiarism and privacy concerns, with transparent communication
equipping students with realistic expectations and informed judgment (Wang et al., 2025). Trust is
further strengthened through open communication channels and transparent academic and
administrative policies, though ethical values alone are insuﬃcient to sustain digital trust without
structural reinforcement (Aprianto et al., 2026). Thus, we propose (H1): Ethical Transparency signiﬁcantly
and positively inﬂuences Institutional Trust.
2.3.2. Algorithmic Accountability and Institutional Trust
Institutional policies that retain human moral and legal responsibility for AI-related wrongdoings,
combining top-down requirements with bottom-up instructor ﬂexibility, represent an accountability
mechanism that underpins trust (Dabis & Csáki, 2024). Algorithmic bias, data privacy concerns, and
adherence to ethical standards directly inﬂuence ethical trust in educational AI systems, beyond
technical performance alone (Sabri et al., 2026). Minimizing algorithmic bias and rigorously protecting
user data are recommended to foster trust, with developers urged to take proactive measures to address
ethical risks (Sun et al., 2026). Digital accountability functions as a component of institutional trust
governance, though weak oversight mechanisms and concerns regarding academic data security persist
Page 7/33

as challenges that can undermine accountability's trust-building potential (Aprianto et al., 2026).
Accountability appears alongside transparency, human oversight, and inclusiveness as one of the key
ethical dimensions that universities must address in their policy responses to GenAI (Dabis & Csáki,
2024). Accordingly, we propose (H2): Algorithmic Accountability signiﬁcantly and positively inﬂuences
Institutional Trust.
2.3.3. AI Literacy and Institutional Trust
AI literacy is proposed as a positive antecedent of Institutional Trust because informed users are better
able to evaluate AI outputs, interpret risks, and calibrate their expectations toward institutional AI
deployment. Technical knowledge positively affects both trust in technology and academic performance,
conﬁrming that students with higher technical proﬁciency tend to trust GenAI more (Alotaibi, 2025).
Personal perceptions exert the strongest inﬂuence on trust in technology among the studied
determinants, surpassing technical knowledge and personal experience (Alotaibi, 2025). Trust in
technology plays a signiﬁcant mediating role between technical knowledge, personal experience,
personal perceptions, and academic performance (Alotaibi, 2025). Ethical sensitivity within AI literacy
does not necessarily produce more positive attitudes; rather, it may generate hesitation or uncertainty in
certain contexts, suggesting that AI literacy is not a unidimensional construct (Sun et al., 2026). Valuing
knowledge and fostering collaborative dynamics positively inﬂuence AI governance and an ethical
institutional culture, which signiﬁcantly impacts students' AI literacy and legal awareness. Thus, we
propose (H3): AI Literacy signiﬁcantly and positively inﬂuences Institutional Trust.
2.3.4. Adaptive AI Governance and Institutional Trust
Institutional strategies, including leadership support, policies, and training, function as foundational
drivers of trust in GenAI within higher education, positioning governance as a structural antecedent
rather than a contextual moderator (Lelescu et al., 2025). AI governance must lead by embedding
assessment literacy and integrity education into institutional policy, ensuring that ethical capacity grows
with technological and cultural change (Maleki, 2026). AI governance should be educative, advisory, and
ﬂexible, empowering informed decision-making rather than imposing strict prohibitions, as guidelines
will evolve with the technology. Cultivating integrity should not rest on surveillance or deterrence but on
the co-creation of just and trustworthy systems, positioning adaptive governance as a trust-building
mechanism (Maleki, 2026). Organizational readiness, deﬁned as an institution's capacity to support AI
adoption through infrastructure, governance, training, and data management, moderates the effects of
data and knowledge processes on system outcomes (Sabri et al., 2026). Accordingly, we propose (H4):
Adaptive AI Governance signiﬁcantly and positively inﬂuences Institutional Trust.
2.3.5. Institutional Trust and Perceived Institutional
Ethicality
Embedding AI ethics into everyday academic practice enables universities to mitigate digital inequities
and strengthen trust in emerging technologies, linking trust directly to perceived ethical practice (Maleki,
2026). Institutional cultures that normalize disclosure and responsible AI use cultivate perceived
Page 8/33

ethicality, with the Higher Education Integrity Ecosystem Model positioning integrity as an emergent
property of interacting institutional layers (Maleki, 2026). Sustainable academic integrity depends on
recognizing ethics as a collective, renewable resource, with integrity serving as an indicator of
sustainability itself, reﬂecting how institutions balance fairness, innovation, and accountability (Maleki,
2026). Ethical trust reﬂects users' perceptions of the fairness, transparency, and reliability of AI systems,
with high ethical trust promoting adoption and eﬃcient use while low trust leads to rejection of
otherwise technically sound systems (Sabri et al., 2026). Inclusive leadership, open communication
channels, transparent policies, and responsible digital governance collectively strengthen trust,
conceptualizing ethical values as operational principles that guide institutional practice (Aprianto et al.,
2026). Hence, we propose (H5): Institutional Trust signiﬁcantly and positively inﬂuences Perceived
Institutional Ethicality.
2.3.6. Institutional Trust and Student Behavioral Intention
Trust predicts behavioral intention to continue using GenAI tools, with a moderate, statistically
signiﬁcant positive correlation suggesting that trust plays a central role in shaping users' willingness to
integrate GenAI into academic workﬂows (Đerić et al., 2025). Fostering trust is not merely an ethical
imperative but a practical necessity for promoting sustained and responsible use of GenAI technologies
in higher education (Đerić et al., 2025). Trust in GenAI signiﬁcantly inﬂuences university students' use of
GenAI for learning, with its effect on behavioral intention to use mediated through reliance and
resistance (Wang et al., 2025). Trust and social reinforcement strongly inﬂuence GenAI adoption
decisions and act as signiﬁcant mediators between perceived usefulness and willingness to adopt
technology (Shata & Hartley, 2025). Trust is a signiﬁcant positive predictor of behavioral intention to
adopt GenAI, alongside motivation, academic integrity, and access conditions (Abdi & Abdullahi, 2026).
Ethical anxiety is positively associated with responsible and continued use, indicating that moderate
concern about ethical issues motivates compliance rather than avoidance, suggesting trust and ethical
concern operate as parallel drivers of sustainable behavioral intention (Xia et al., 2026). Accordingly,
(H6): Institutional Trust signiﬁcantly and positively inﬂuences Student Behavioral Intention toward
sustainable AI use.
2.3.7. Perceived Institutional Ethicality as a Mediator
Attitude provides an evaluative ﬁlter that connects belief structures to behavioral intentions, which in
turn are linked to GenAI usage, suggesting that perceived ethicality functions as a mediating evaluative
mechanism between trust and intention (Sun et al., 2026). Ethical awareness signiﬁcantly predicts
behavioral intention, and an ethical mediation chain shapes students' behavioral intention,
demonstrating that ethical perceptions mediate between cognitive appraisals and adoption outcomes
(Qomariyah & Heriyanni, 2026). Performance expectancy, ethical awareness, habit, and AI ethical anxiety
serve as signiﬁcant predictors of behavioral intention, with ethical constructs operating alongside
technological perceptions in a chained mediation pathway (Qomariyah & Heriyanni, 2026). The overall
chain from perceptions through anxiety, trust, and risk to sustainable use intention is supported, with
perceived ease of use, information quality, and ethical awareness primarily inﬂuencing sustainable use
Page 9/33

intention indirectly through evaluative mediators (Xia et al., 2026). Academics' intentions to use GenAI
technologies are contingent upon the development of robust ethical guidelines and supportive
institutional policies, positioning perceived ethicality as a necessary mediating condition between
institutional trust and behavioral intention (Nikolic et al., 2024). Responsible use of GenAI tools can
enhance learning processes, but addressing concerns requires robust policies responsive to cultural
expectations, suggesting that perceived institutional ethicality bridges trust and sustainable use by
translating institutional commitments into culturally calibrated ethical signals (Yusuf et al., 2024).
Therefore, we propose (H7): Perceived Institutional Ethicality signiﬁcantly and positively mediates the
relationship between Institutional Trust and Student Behavioral Intention.

3. Research Methodology
3.1 Research Design and Sample Selection
This study adopts a rigorous quantitative, cross-sectional survey design to empirically test the
hypothesized relationships. The target population consists of university students currently enrolled in
public and private higher education institutions in Tunisia. A substantive eligibility criterion was applied:
participants were required to be aged 18 years or older, currently enrolled, and to have prior experience
using at least one generative AI or AI-based academic tool (e.g., ChatGPT, translation assistants,
intelligent tutoring systems) for scholarly activities. This target group was selected because students
who have never engaged with academic AI would lack the empirical basis necessary to evaluate their
university's structural practices, AI policies, or their own intentions regarding sustainable and responsible
AI utilization. To minimize sampling bias and promote heterogeneity across diverse academic contexts,
we employed a purposive quota sampling technique. Purposive sampling ensured that every respondent
was active and experienced with academic AI tools, while quota sampling was structured to enforce
diversity across major educational disciplines (e.g., social sciences, engineering, business, medicine,
law), study levels (bachelor's, master's, doctoral), and institutional aﬃliations. The ﬁnal analytical sample
consists of 380 valid responses, which represents a highly robust size. Using G*Power 3.1 and applying
the standard '10-times rule' popular in PLS-SEM literature, a minimum sample size of 380 provides
substantial statistical power (> 0.95) to detect small-to-moderate effects within our structural model.

3.3 Data Analysis Protocol
To test the hypothesized structural relationships, Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling
(PLS-SEM) was performed using SmartPLS 4. PLS-SEM is highly appropriate for this research because it
is robust to non-normal distributions, supports complex structural paths with multiple mediators, and
excels in exploratory and predictive scale validation. Data analysis proceeded via a standard two-stage
Page 13/33

protocol: ﬁrst, the measurement (reﬂective) model was rigorously assessed for reliability, convergent
validity, and discriminant validity. Second, the structural model was evaluated to test the standardized
path coeﬃcients (beta), coeﬃcients of determination (R²), effect sizes (f²), and predictive relevance (Q²).
Statistical signiﬁcance of the paths was veriﬁed using a non-parametric bootstrapping procedure with
5,000 resamples.

5. Discussion and Comparative Analysis
5.1 Discussion and Theoretical Alignment
The empirical validation of our integrated governance model provides robust, novel insights into the
structural dynamics of digital higher education. By shifting the unit of analysis from individual-level
technical attributes (e.g., perceived ease of use or technical utility) to systemic, institutional
responsibility, the study demonstrates that rebuilding academic trust in the era of GenAI is
fundamentally an organizational and moral challenge rather than a purely technological or pedagogical
problem (Al Zaidy, 2024; Kringen et al., 2026). Our structural ﬁnding that Adaptive AI Governance is the
most powerful antecedent of Institutional Trust (β = 0.297) strongly supports the conceptual
Page 22/33

propositions of Kringen et al. (2026). Kringen et al. (2026) argued that the widespread adoption-trust
paradox (where stakeholders actively use AI tools despite feeling intense skepticism and a lack of trust)
is primarily an "institutional governance failure" rather than a failure of student ethics. Our empirical
results validate this: when universities actively and dynamically review, monitor, and adapt their
administrative policies in response to technological change, student trust increases. This also aligns
with Shrestha (2026), who highlighted the importance of establishing integrated, agile regulatory
architectures in higher education through the REM (Responsible, Ethical, and Managed) framework. This
interpretation ﬁts the broader literature, which increasingly treats AI ethics in education as a systemic
governance problem involving accountability, transparency, autonomy, inclusion, and human oversight
rather than a narrow question of tool performance (Crompton et al., 2026; Holmes et al., 2022). It also
aligns with evidence that institutional responses to GenAI remain uneven, reactive, and often
underdeveloped, which makes adaptive governance a plausible source of trust variation across
universities (Crompton et al., 2026; Knox & Knox, 2025). Additionally, the signiﬁcant direct path from
Ethical Transparency to Institutional Trust (β = 0.231) extends the ﬁndings of Yu et al. (2025). While Yu et
al. (2025) established that user-level transparency regarding data usage and algorithmic boundaries is
essential for the individual adoption of AI-generated content (AIGC) platforms, our model demonstrates
that this effect holds true at the institutional level: clear administrative disclosure regarding the
boundaries and rules of AI use directly builds student trust in the university as a whole. This result
deepens a strong cross-paper consensus that trust depends on intelligible disclosure of how AI works,
what data it uses, where its limits lie, and how institutions expect it to be used (Dabis & Csáki, 2024;
Khosravi et al., 2022; Coman et al., 2026). Experimental evidence is especially useful here: transparency
appears to be the most inﬂuential predictor of student trust in AI-based assessment, and its effect is
stronger when paired with procedural control or user agency (Garcia, 2026). This means our institutional-
level transparency pathway is not merely symbolic; it reﬂects students’ demand for procedural
legitimacy. Furthermore, the robust effect of Algorithmic Accountability on Institutional Trust (β = 0.258)
empirically conﬁrms the qualitative assertions of Aysolmaz et al. (2023), proving that students require
clear structural assurance that their university accepts responsibility for AI-driven outcomes and
provides transparent pathways for academic grievance and appeal. Reviews of higher education AI
ethics show that accountability remains less operationalized than fairness, even though scholars identify
it as essential for oversight, explainability, and safety (Memarian & Doleck, 2023; Coghlan et al., 2021).
Empirical evidence from universities also shows that faculty and students express major concern about
weak transparency, privacy, and oversight, and call for audit protocols, ethical oversight structures, and
concrete regulatory mechanisms (Marín et al., 2025). In that context, our accountability coeﬃcient
suggests that trust rises when students believe the university can be held answerable for AI-mediated
harms, errors, and appeals. Interestingly, although the direct path from AI Literacy to Institutional Trust
was signiﬁcant (β = 0.137, p = 0.006), it was the weakest of the four antecedents. This represents an
important theoretical nuance: while fostering student-level technical competence and critical thinking is
indispensable (Wang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025), technical competence alone cannot substitute for a
robust, transparent, and accountable institutional framework. Student trust is predominantly driven by
structural, top-down governance safeguards rather than individual-level capabilities. This result
Page 23/33

highlights that individual competence must operate within a wider 'trust infrastructure' built by the
institution (Bhaskar et al., 2026; Troy et al., 2024). This is consistent with adoption studies showing that
AI literacy improves attitudes, perceived control, and behavioral intention, but does not by itself resolve
ethical concern or establish institutional legitimacy (Sun et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al.,
2025). Students with stronger awareness and evaluation literacy tend to develop more positive
orientations toward GenAI, yet privacy risk and ethical concern still depress trust unless institutional
protections are in place (Sun et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025). Our model therefore advances the literature
by showing that literacy functions best as an enabling capability within a broader trust infrastructure, not
as a substitute for governance. Finally, the partial mediation of Perceived Institutional Ethicality (β = 
0.337) between Institutional Trust and Behavioral Intention (H7) adds deep theoretical value. It shows
that institutional trust is not merely a passive psychological state. Instead, it actively shapes students'
cognitive evaluation of the university's moral standing (perceived ethicality), which then serves as a
primary, active driver of their own responsible and sustainable behavior. This path supports the values-
led organizational change model of Stout (2023), conﬁrming that values-driven leadership in technology
implementation fosters a self-reinforcing culture of compliance and academic integrity among students.
5.2 Practical Implications for Marketers and Senior
Administrators
In the highly competitive global landscape of digital higher education, academic trust and ethical
reputation are increasingly recognized as primary brand assets. As digital learning environments
become ubiquitous, institutions can no longer differentiate themselves solely based on technical delivery
or program diversity. Our ﬁndings suggest that institutional trust is a strong determinant of student
behavioral loyalty and brand equity. Higher education marketers and university leaders must strategically
position and brand their institutions as "ethical AI campuses" to attract and retain high-caliber students.
This supports reframing "ethical AI campus" positioning away from branding rhetoric alone and toward
auditable governance signals such as review committees, clear appeals pathways, transparent syllabus-
level guidance, and regular policy updates (Dabis & Csáki, 2024; Crompton et al., 2026). In competitive
higher education markets, trust becomes credible only when ethical commitments are institutionally
enacted. Marketing campaigns and brand messaging should proactively highlight the institution's
commitment to Ethical Transparency and Algorithmic Accountability. Rather than downplaying or
ignoring AI integration, marketing materials should transparently outline the university's robust 'AI Trust
Infrastructure', such as the active existence of an AI Ethics Governance Committee (Bhaskar et al., 2026)
and values-led data privacy policies (Stout, 2023). Positioning the university as a leading ethical actor in
the digital transition serves to enhance brand differentiation, elevate institutional legitimacy, and attract
ethically minded students, corporate partners, and research funding. For marketers and senior
administrators, the implication is not simply to publicize AI use, but to communicate procedural
legitimacy. Higher education faces socio-political pressure to use AI for quality assurance, retention,
satisfaction, and reputation, yet these same pressures can generate distrust when decisions appear
arbitrary, opaque, or externally driven (Khosravi et al., 2022). Transparent messaging should therefore
specify what AI is used for, what it is not used for, what data protections exist, how human oversight is
Page 24/33

retained, and how students can challenge decisions (Dabis & Csáki, 2024; Coghlan et al., 2021; Zhang et
al., 2025). Trustworthy positioning is strongest when it reduces uncertainty rather than when it
celebrates innovation abstractly.

5.3 Pedagogical Implications for Educators
For educators, the empirical support for the four pillars indicates that traditional, reactive, and punitive
approaches to AI management (such as blanket bans or reliance on unreliable AI-detection software) are
counterproductive. Multiple studies argue that detection systems and online proctoring are insuﬃcient
or counterproductive as standalone responses because they are unreliable, deepen alienation, and can
damage pedagogical relationships and student subjectivity (Leaton Gray et al., 2025; Lee & Fanguy,
2022). Early institutional policy studies reach a similar conclusion by recommending assessment
redesign and dialogue-based sanctions rather than a narrow misconduct-detection logic (Dabis & Csáki,
2024). This gives strong support to the argument for AI-resilient assessment. Educators must transition
toward 'AI-resilient' assessment designs and proactive digital pedagogy (Jain & Kiran, 2025; Sullivan et
al., 2023). This requires redesigning coursework to focus on higher-order critical thinking, reﬂective
writing, and real-time oral examinations—cognitive processes that machines cannot easily replicate.
Furthermore, our results show that embedding AI Literacy directly into the curriculum is a critical
requirement. Educators must actively train students in the core competencies of the Artiﬁcial
Intelligence Literacy Scale (AILS), focusing heavily on the 'Ethics' and 'Evaluation' dimensions (Wang et
al., 2023). Coursework should require students to critically evaluate AI-generated outputs, identify
Page 25/33

algorithmic biases, and explicitly document and reference their AI usage (Yu et al., 2025). By treating AI
as a collaborative learning assistant rather than a threat, educators can foster a sustainable learning
environment that preserves the intrinsic value of human scholarship.
5.4 Boundary Conditions: Surveillance, Equity, and Student
Agency
The systematic governance of GenAI in higher education is inextricably linked to broader, complex
societal ethics. A primary ethical risk is the escalation of digital surveillance: in their reactive attempts to
preserve traditional academic integrity, many universities have implemented intrusive algorithmic
proctoring and automated plagiarism detection tools. Surveillance-heavy governance tends to erode
trust, privacy, and educational relationships (Coghlan et al., 2021; Lee & Fanguy, 2022). Research on
proctoring shows that surveillance-based systems can undermine privacy, autonomy, fairness, and trust,
while also redistributing power toward institutions and vendors in ways that students experience as
coercive (Henry & Oliver, 2022; Coghlan et al., 2021; Coman et al., 2026). Other work argues that framing
ethics only through abstract principles is inadequate because AI ethics in education is enacted through
socio-technical arrangements and unequal distributions of power, care, and responsibility (Henry &
Oliver, 2022). This strengthens our claim that governance must be morally substantive, not merely
administratively present. Furthermore, the digital divide remains a profound social equity concern in
digital higher education. As advanced, highly capable GenAI platforms transition to premium,
subscription-only business models, a severe risk of cognitive inequality emerges. Wealthy students who
can afford premium cognitive tools gain access to sophisticated learning support, writing scaffolding,
and research assistance, while lower-income students are restricted to standard, less capable systems.
Sustainable learning ecosystems must establish equitable access policies, ensuring that access to vital
academic tools is democratized and does not exacerbate existing social and academic inequalities
(Pechenkina, 2023; Thaldar et al., 2025). Reviews and conceptual analyses warn that AI adoption can
amplify linguistic, socioeconomic, and infrastructural inequalities unless institutions deliberately design
inclusive access and support mechanisms (Knox & Knox, 2025; Crompton et al., 2026; Coman et al.,
2026). Human-centered models therefore emphasize equitable access, privacy, interoperability, and
critical thinking as design requirements rather than optional beneﬁts (Airaj, 2024). Finally, the
preservation of student agency is paramount. Effective AI governance must structurally guarantee that
AI remains a supportive learning 'mate' or cognitive assistant, while the student remains the active
intellectual 'driver' of their educational journey (Mahama & Amadu, 2025). Legitimate GenAI governance
must protect student agency by ensuring AI remains assistive, contestable, and pedagogically
subordinate to human judgment (Khosravi et al., 2022; Leaton Gray et al., 2025). Universities must design
learning frameworks that protect and celebrate the intrinsic value of human intellectual effort, creative
struggle, and critical thought, ensuring that technology serves to amplify, rather than replace, human
intelligence.
5.5 Limitations and Directions for Future Research
Page 26/33

While this study provides a highly rigorous, empirically validated framework, several limitations must be
acknowledged. First, because the data were collected through a cross-sectional survey design, we
cannot establish deﬁnitive temporal or causal relationships among our constructs. Future research
should utilize longitudinal designs to track how student trust and behavioral compliance evolve over time
as AI tools become deeply embedded within the fabric of academic workﬂows. Second, our sample was
limited to university students in Tunisia. While this provides a highly homogeneous and contextually rich
sample, cultural, economic, and institutional differences may affect the generalizability of our ﬁndings.
Comparative cross-national research (e.g., comparing highly regulated settings under the EU AI Act with
less centralized regulatory environments like the US or Africa) would provide valuable insights into the
performance of different policy architectures (Hristova, 2025; Li et al., 2026; Thaldar et al., 2025). The
broader literature repeatedly notes that empirical work remains thinner than normative discussion,
especially for transparency, accountability, and lived institutional practice (Marín et al., 2025; Memarian &
Doleck, 2023). Comparative studies across regulatory environments, institutional types, and disciplines
would test whether our trust architecture is stable or context-dependent, while mixed-method and
experimental work could examine how policy signals, transparency interfaces, and grievance
mechanisms alter trust over time (Crompton et al., 2026; Garcia, 2026).
6. Conclusion and Contributions
The central contribution of this study is to demonstrate that the current crisis of academic trust in the
age of Generative AI is, at its core, a governance challenge rather than a narrowly technical problem of
tool accuracy or a narrowly pedagogical problem of classroom control (Sejdiu et al., 2026; Crompton et
al., 2026; Coman et al., 2026). By validating a four-pillar model built on Ethical Transparency, Algorithmic
Accountability, AI Literacy, and Adaptive AI Governance, this study moves the debate beyond abstract
ethical principles and identiﬁes the speciﬁc institutional mechanisms through which trust can be
produced, sustained, and translated into responsible student behavior (Holmes et al., 2022; Sejdiu et al.,
2026; Crompton et al., 2026). More speciﬁcally, our ﬁndings demonstrate that active, adaptive
governance and structural accountability are the primary drivers of student trust and perceived
institutional ethicality, rather than student competence alone. This is a signiﬁcant theoretical advance
because much of the existing literature calls for transparency, accountability, literacy, and human
oversight, but fewer studies empirically demonstrate how these governance dimensions work together
as an integrated trust architecture in higher education (Crompton et al., 2026; Memarian & Doleck, 2023;
Marín et al., 2025). In that sense, the study helps close an important gap between normative frameworks
and empirical validation by showing that trust is not an automatic byproduct of AI adoption, but an
institutional achievement rooted in visible rules, review capacity, answerability, and ethical consistency
(Knox & Knox, 2025; Khosravi et al., 2022; Marín et al., 2025). The study also makes a practical
contribution by showing that sustainable AI integration cannot rely on punitive or surveillance-centered
responses alone. Evidence across higher education shows that detection-ﬁrst and proctoring-heavy
approaches can weaken trust, intensify perceptions of unfairness, and damage pedagogical
relationships, whereas more legitimate responses emphasize human oversight, authentic assessment,
Page 27/33

student agency, and clear procedural communication (Coghlan et al., 2021; Lee & Fanguy, 2022; Leaton
Gray et al., 2025). Against that backdrop, this study offers universities an empirically grounded
alternative: invest in a trust infrastructure that combines adaptive policy review, transparent
communication, accountable governance, and curriculum-embedded AI literacy so that innovation
remains aligned with academic integrity, equity, and human-centered educational values (Crompton et
al., 2026; Dabis & Csáki, 2024; Airaj, 2024). Ultimately, the broader importance of this study lies in its
reframing of what institutional readiness for GenAI actually means. Readiness is not simply access to
tools or willingness to innovate; it is the university’s capacity to govern AI in ways that students perceive
as legitimate, ethical, contestable, and educationally meaningful (Sejdiu et al., 2026; Crompton et al.,
2026; Garcia, 2026). By empirically showing that trust and perceived institutional ethicality are key
pathways through which governance shapes responsible behavioral intention, this study contributes a
timely and policy-relevant model for protecting the credibility of higher education in an AI-saturated era
(Sejdiu et al., 2026; Crompton et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026).
