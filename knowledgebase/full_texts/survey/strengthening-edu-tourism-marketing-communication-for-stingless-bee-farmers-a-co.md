 Purpose of Study:  This research on community service program aims to
accompany and empower the KTH Madu Sari forest farmer group as the community
partner in Katongan Village, Nglipar District, Gunungkidul Regency, by
strengthening productive communication skills, utilizing  artificial intelligence and
augmented reality, and mentoring community -based edu -tourism and digital
marketing practices based local needs. From the outset, the partner faced challenges,
including limited active member participation, centralized educational roles held
by the group leader, low digital literacy, and dependence on external honey
suppliers.
Methodology: The program was implemented using a Community Action Research
approach a cross three cycles, encompassing participatory planning, training and
co-creation, and implementation with reflection. Concrete activities included step -
by-step public speaking training using a structured rubric, collaborative
development of simple promoti onal media assisted by artificial intelligence tools,
integration of augmented reality content into edu-tourism packages, and hands-on
mentoring in stingless-bee colony management and social media-based marketing
activation.
Main Findings:  The results demonstrate improvements in members’
communication performance based on the public speaking rubric, the emergence
of two new edu-tourism instructors, increased honey production capacity, and the
establishment of more organized digital marketin g channels. Practically, this
program contributes to decentralizing educational and marketing roles within the
group, reduc ing reliance on external suppliers, and forming a contextual,
technology-enabled learning and entrepreneurship ecosystem.
Novelty/Originality of This Study:  This empowerment model offers a replicable
framework for other rural, resource-based community groups seeking sustainable
capacity development.

INTRODUCTION
Edu-tourism has increasingly become an important strategy for connecting local economic
development, community -based learning, and sustainable tourism. In rural areas, tourism is no
longer limited to sightseeing but also includes educational experiences that help visitors understand
local production systems, environmental practices, and community entrepreneurship. Within this
context, stingless -bee farming has strong potential to be developed as an edu -tourism attraction

because it combines honey production , biodiversity conservation, pollination services, and local
economic empowerment. Studies on beekeeping and stingless-bee livelihoods show that beekeeping
can contribute to household income diversification, ecological conservation, sustainability education,
and biodiversity support, particularly when supported by appropriate colony management and
community-based enterprise development (Chami et al., 2022;  Koffler et al., 2021;  Rizkuna et al.,
2025; Arsyad et al., 2025; Garrigues & Garrigues, 2025; Patel et al., 2021; Prodanović et al., 2024; Rai
et al., 2025; Singh & Singh, 2024). In addition, nectar diversity, seasonality, and colony management
practices influence honey yield and quality, while integrated training and cooperative support can
improve production outcomes among beekeeping communities (Hidalgo et al., 2022; Nurdiansyah et
al., 2024; Mekinç et al., 2023).
KTH Madu Sari in Katongan Village, Nglipar, Gunungkidul, represents a rural community group with
considerable potential to develop stingless -bee edu-tourism. The group is located within a honey -
production cluster and is situated near other rural attractions, such as the Punthuk Kepuh viewpoint
and Aloe Vera cultivation area. This geographical proximity provides opportunities to develop
integrated tourism packages that combine nature-based tourism, hands-on stingless-bee education,
and local product entrepreneurship. However, the group still faces several internal challenges.
Although KTH Madu Sari has 25 registered members, only four members are actively involved in
production, marketing, and edu -tour guiding, while the remaining members mainly contribute as
passive suppliers of bulk honey through community forestry volunteers. The group’s average
production ranges from 70 to 100 bottles per month , equivalent to approximately 17.5 to 25 liters,
indicating that its current production capacity still depends partly on external supply from PKSM.
This condition reflects the need for regeneration, stronger member participation, and more evenly
distributed technical capacity among members.

The challenges faced by KTH Madu Sari are not limited to production. Edu -tourism activities and
external communication are still largely centralized around the group leader, Mr. Sugeng Apriyanto,
which makes practical knowledge and public communication ski lls unevenly distributed among
members. At the same time, the group has limited digital branding capacity, low confidence in public
presentation, and varying levels of digital literacy. The adoption of AI and AR was considered realistic
in this rural conte xt because the program relied on familiar, low -cost, and mobile -accessible tools
that could be introduced through step -by-step guided practice rather than complex technical
training. AI was limited to practical tasks such as poster and caption development,  while AR was
implemented through printable markers and web -based access, thereby reducing technical and
financial burdens for farmers with limited digital literacy. These conditions constrain the group’s
ability to communicate its edu -tourism value to vis itors and wider markets. In tourism marketing
communication, local actors need to be able to explain products, practices, and local knowledge in
ways that are clear, engaging, and relevant to the audience. Therefore, strengthening productive
communication, storytelling, and digital branding becomes essential for transforming stingless -bee
farming from a production-based activity into an edu-tourism experience.
Previous studies have shown that digital technologies such as artificial intelligence and augmente d
reality can support contextual learning, immersive communication, and practical training, especially
in rural and vocational settings where learning resources are often limited (Chen et al., 2023; Galvão

community partnerships have also been found to improve entrepreneurship and community
empowerment (Buhalis et al., 2023; Prasetyo & Setyadharma, 2022; Zhang et al., 2024). In addition,

they are locally tailored, skill -oriented, and capable of fostering innovation within the community

In the area of  communication training, prior research indicates that structured speaking practice,
feedback, audience adaptation, and digital-supported learning can improve participants’ confidence,
speaking performance, and audience engagement. Public speaking assessme nt can also be used to
observe clarity, structure, confidence, visual support, and interaction in communication practices
(Bylkova et al., 2021; Jayanti et al., 2022; Juwito et al., 2022; Laske & Reed, 2022; Muna et al., 2022;
Wei & Fan, 2024). For digital branding, AI-based design tools have been reported to reduce costs and
technical barriers, allowing rural SMEs to produce culturally relevant promotional materials more
independently (Tuomi, 2023;  Jorgensen et al., 2022;  Ilieva et al., 2024) . Meanwhile, d igital and
interactive platforms, including AR -supported media, have been reported to support business
digitalization, user engagement, and technology adoption among SMEs and service -oriented
organizations. However, their implementation in low -resource com munity settings requires
attention to usability, infrastructure, bandwidth, and users’ digital readiness (Abbad et al., 2021; Jaén
et al., 2023; Oyekunle & Tiamiyu, 2022; Scutella et al., 2023). In the context of digital marketing, social
media-based promotion has also been shown to influence consumer interest and purchase intention,
underscoring the importance of strengthening farmers’ capacity to produce attractive, audience -
oriented promotional content (Li, 2023).
Taken together, previous studies suggest  that AI, AR, communication training, and community
empowerment should be understood as interconnected components in community -based edu -
tourism. AI and AR provide digital support for producing promotional materials and enriching visitor
learning experiences, while productive communication enables local actors to transform these digital
outputs into clear, engaging, and meaningful tourism narratives. Within this relationship, community
empowerment ensures that the use of technology and communication skills is participatory, locally
relevant, and sustainable, so that farmers are not merely technology users but active co -creators of
edu-tourism communication.
However, previous studies still indicate several limitations when viewed from the perspective of
community-based edu -tourism communication. Studies on AI, AR, and digital technologies in
tourism, education, and digital promotion have discussed technological adoption, visitor experience,
value co -creation, digital branding, and learning innovation, but thes e discussions have not yet
sufficiently explained how rural community actors can develop the communication capacity needed
to use such technologies in edu -tourism marketing contexts (Buhalis et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023;
Galvão et al., 2020;  Ilieva et a l., 2024;  Tuomi, 2023) . Studies on public speaking and productive
communication have shown the importance of speaking performance, confidence, audience
engagement, and personal branding; however, their application among farmers who act as edu -
tourism guides and local marketing communicators remains underexplored (Bylkova et al., 2021;
Jayanti et al., 2022;  Juwito et al., 2022;  Laske & Reed, 2022;  Muna et al., 2022;  Wei & Fan, 2024) .
Similarly, studies on stingless -bee farming and melitourism have emphasized  honey production,
ecological value, biodiversity, sustainable livelihoods, and agrotourism potential, yet the connection
between these potentials and AI-assisted branding, AR-based visitor learning, and storytelling-based
tourism marketing communication still requires further development (Arsyad et al., 2025; Garrigues
& Garrigues, 2025 ; Hidalgo et al., 2022;  Nicolas et al., 2022;  Nurdiansyah et al., 2024;  Patel et al.,
2021; Prodanović et al., 2024; Rizkuna et al., 2025; Singh & Singh, 2024).
In response to these gaps, this study develops a Community Action Research -based empowerment
model that integrates AI -assisted content creation, AR -based edu -tourism media, and productive
communication training for stingless-bee farmers. The contribution of this study lies in showing how
digital tools, communication capacity, and local knowledge can be co -created with community
members to strengthen edu -tourism marketing communication in a low -resource rural context. By
positioning farmers as co-creators rather than passive technology users, the model connects digital

METHOD
This study employed a Community Action Research (CAR) framework to guide the co-creation of AI-
and AR-supported communication training with the KTH Madu Sari beekeeping community. CAR was
selected because the study required a participatory process in whic h researchers and community
members could jointly identify needs, design relevant actions, implement training, observe progress,
and reflect on improvements. The process followed the iterative sequence of Plan, Act, Observe, and
Reflect, allowing the inter vention to be adjusted to participants' digital readiness, communication
needs, and local edu -tourism context (Fairey et al., 2022;  Stockton et al., 2021;  Scaramuzzi et al.,
2020).
The study involved 25 members of KTH Madu Sari in Dusun Ngrandu, Katongan V illage, Nglipar,
Gunungkidul. The group, led by Mr. Sugeng Apriyanto, focuse s on stingless -bee

(Trigona/Geniotrigona thoracica) beekeeping and edu -tourism. Before the intervention, only four
members were consistently active in production, marketing, and ed u-tour guiding. The 25
participants were involved in the CAR process according to their roles and availability across
production, marketing, education, and tourism communication. All participants joined voluntarily
and provided informed consent, while the program upheld inclusivity, transparency, and respect for
local culture (Hananto et al., 2024; Khoong et al., 2025).
The empowerment process was conducted over eight months, from May to December 2025, through
three connected CAR cycles. In each cycle, the Plan stage involved focus group discussions,
preliminary observation, digital literacy mapping, identification of communication problems, and
preparation of the training plan with KTH Madu Sari members. These activities helped determine
participants' needs related public speaking, edu-tourism storytelling, digital branding, and readiness
to use AI- and AR-supported media.
The Act stage was carried out through productive communication training, public speaking practice,
poster design using Canva and Dreamina  AI, caption writing using ChatGPT, and AR media
development using MyWebAR. Participants practiced preparing tourism messages, arranging
storytelling scripts, designing simple promotional posters, developing short captions for digital
promotion, and creati ng marker -based AR content for visitor learning. These applications were
selected because they are accessible for rural community members, require limited technical skills,
and support low-cost edu-tourism marketing communication.
The Observe stage was con ducted by recording participant involvement, assessing microteaching
performance using a public speaking rubric, reviewing the quality of posters, captions, and AR
markers, and collecting participant responses during practice and simulation. The Reflect st age
involved discussions between participants and facilitators to identify difficulties, improve
communication materials, simplify the use of AI/AR tools, adjust edu-tourism narratives to visitors'
needs, and formulate follow -up actions. In this way, the f indings from observation and reflection
were used to refine the training materials, communication scripts, visual products, and AR content
for subsequent implementation.

Data analysis combined descriptive, i nferential, and qualitative thematic techniques. Each
participant’s overall public speaking score was calculated as the mean of the five rubric indicators:
Clarity, Confidence, Structure, Visual Support, and Engagement. Quantitative data from the public
speaking rubric were summarized using means and standard deviations. A paired-samples t-test was
conducted to examine whether the difference between participants’ overall pre -test and post -test
public speaking scores was statistically significant, with the significance level set at p < .05. Cohen’s
dz was calculated to estimate the magnitude of the within-participant change. Quantitative data from
the digital readiness checklist and simple digital engagement indicators were analyzed descriptively.
Qualitative data from focus group discussions, interviews, observation notes, and reflection sessions
were analyzed thematically to identify patterns related to participation, communication confidence,
technology adoption, collaborative learning, and sustainability. Triangulation was conducted by
comparing rubric results, checklist data, observation notes, interviews, reflection records, AI/AR
product documentation, and supporting colony records to strengthen the validity of the findings
(Agostino & Costantini, 2021; Kim et al., 2024).
Ethical considerations were embedded throughout the research process. The program upheld
informed consent, transparency, data security, and shared ownership of outputs. Digital posters, AR
visuals, documentation photos, and training materials were co-created with KTH Madu Sari members
and treated as community assets. Local knowledge of stingless-bee care was respected to ensure that
the communication materials remained relevant, accurate, and useful for the community. Overall, the
CAR app roach positioned KTH Madu Sari members as active partners who shaped the planning,
implementation, evaluation, and refinement of the program.

To empirically examine the improvement in public speaking performance, a paired -samples t-test
was conducted using the pre -test and post -test scores of 25 participants. The analysis indicated a
statistically significant increase in the overall public speaking score from the pre-test (M = 2.44, SD =
0.40) to the post-test (M = 3.78, SD = 0.48), t( 24) = 10.80, p < .001. The magnitude of the difference
was very large (Cohen’s dz = 2.16), suggesting that the improvement was not only descriptively
apparent but also statistically and practically meaningful. At the indicator level, the largest mean
gains were observed in Visual Support (Δ = 1.50) and Confidence (Δ = 1.40). These improvements
corresponded with the implementation of AI -assisted promotional media development, structured
microteaching, and guided feedback; however, because the study did not i nclude a control group or
isolate the effects of individual activities, the observed gains cannot be attributed exclusively to any
single component of the intervention.
The public speaking rubric comprised five indicators  Clarity, Confidence, Structure, Visual Support,
and Engagement rated on a 1 –5 performance scale. For interpretive purposes, the present study
operationally categorized mean scores of 1.00–2.00 as Basic, 2.01–3.00 as Developing, 3.01–4.00 as
Proficient, and 4.01–5.00 as Advanced. These categories were used to facilitate the interpretation of
performance progression and were informed by the broader use of five-point speaking-performance
rubrics in public -speaking and vocational learning contexts (e.g., Juwito et al., 2022; Jayanti
et al., 2022). Accordingly, the pre-test mean of 2.44 represents the Developing level, indicating that
the targeted communication skills were present but demonstrated inconsistently. In contrast, the
post-test mean of 3.78 falls within the Proficient level, indicating more consistent demonstration of

Discussion
Implications
The findings of this study have practical implications for rural community empowerment, edu -
tourism development, and digital marketing capacity-building. The integration of AI-assisted content
creation, AR -based visitor learning media, and productive commu nication training indicates that
affordable digital technologies can be adopted by rural producer groups when they are introduced
through guided practice, local content, and sustained mentoring. This finding is consistent with
previous studies showing that  digital tools can support promotional content development, user
engagement, and small -business communication when adapted to users’ readiness and local
conditions. For universities, extension services, non -governmental organizations, and local
governments, the model can be adapted to support community -based tourism, local product

when combined with human facilitation, peer mentoring, and context -sensitive comm unication
training rather than implemented as stand-alone technological solutions.
Research contribution
This study contributes to the literature by integrating productive communication, AI-assisted digital
branding, AR -based edu -tourism media, and Communi ty Action Research within a single rural
empowerment model. Previous studies have commonly examined digital technology adoption,
visitor experience, public speaking, or community empowerment separately. In contrast, this study
demonstrates how rural community members can act as co-creators of technology-enabled tourism
communication by combining local knowledge, storytelling, visual media, and interactive visitor
learning. The study also extends the discussion on stingless -bee farming by showing that its ed u-
tourism value depends not only  on honey production, but also on farmers’ ability to communicate
colony-care practices, biodiversity, product characteristics, ecological values, and local
entrepreneurship to visitors. Therefore, the contribution of this study lies in positioning farmers not
merely as technology users or program beneficiaries, but as active producers of edu -tourism
narratives and digital promotional content in a low-resource rural context.
Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single rural community with a
relatively small participant group, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, the study
did not include a control group, meaning that the observed improvements cannot be attributed
exclusively to the intervention. Third, although the paired -samples t -test provides statistical
evidence of pre-test and post-test differences, the study did not isolate the individual effects of AI -
assisted design, AR media, microteaching, o r guided feedback. Fourth, several outcomes relied on
short-term observations, participant reflections, and practical outputs, while longer-term changes in
visitor engagement, market reach, household income, and community participation were not yet
systematically measured. Variations in digital literacy, internet connectivity, device access, and
participant availability also influenced the consistency of implementation.
Suggestions
Future studies should replicate and expand this empowerment model across different stingless-bee
farmer groups or other rural producer communities to examine its applicability in broader contexts.
Further research is also recommended to use comparative or quasi-experimental designs in order to
provide stronger empirical evidence regarding the effectiveness of AI - and AR -supported
communication training. In addition, long -term evaluation is needed to determine whether
improvements in public speaking, storytelling, digital branding, and AR -based edu-tourism media
can contribute to visitor engagement, digital market reach, pro duct branding, community
participation, and household -level economic benefits. Future empowerment programs should also
develop offline or low -bandwidth versions of AI/AR-supported training materials to accommodate
rural communities with limited infrastructure, varied digital readiness, and unequal access to digital
devices. Finally, collaboration among universities, local governments, extension services, community
organizations, and technology partners should be strengthened to develop simple, adaptable, an d
sustainable training modules for rural edu-tourism and digital marketing communication.

CONCLUSION
This study, conducted as a community service program , demonstrates that an integrated
empowerment approach combining productive communication training, AI - and AR -assisted
learning tools, and participatory Community Action Research can strengthen KTH Madu Sari ,s
capacity and sustainability  as a rural edu -tourism and honey -producing community. Through
iterative cycles of planning, action, and reflection, the program helped improve communication skills
among members, supported the emergence of new edu -tourism trainers, strengthened digital
marketing readiness, and increased honey production capacity, while reducing reliance on
centralized roles and external su pply. Beyond immediate outcomes, the program fostered a self -
