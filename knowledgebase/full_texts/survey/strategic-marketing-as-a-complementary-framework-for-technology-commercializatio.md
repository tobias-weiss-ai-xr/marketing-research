1. Introduction
During the last few decades, Research, Development, and Innovation (R+D+i) ecosystems
have steadily increased their scientific, technological, and financial capabilities. However,
this growth has not translated into a proportional improvement in the commercial success
rates of the developed technologies. Various studies show that a high proportion of
innovations fail to achieve sustained market adoption, even after satisfactorily passing the
stages of research, development, or technological validation (Cooper, 2017; CB Insights,
2024; OECD/Eurostat, 2018).
The literature does not ignore marketing in innovation. The Oslo Manual recognizes
marketing innovation as a type of business innovation, while research on market orientation
has long examined the generation, dissemination, and response to market intelligence (Kohli
& Jaworski, 1990; Narver & Slater, 1990). Likewise, technology-commercialization practice
encompasses market assessment, business-case development, intellectual-property
marketing, licensing, product development, and market dissemination (WIPO, 2026). The
gap addressed here is therefore narrower: the fragmented use of Strategic Marketing as
an integrated, strategic and operational analytical lens for interpreting commercial risk in
technology commercialization and transfer.
Innovation scholarship has emphasized technological maturity, funding,
organizational capabilities, governance, diffusion, and transfer mechanisms (Rogers, 2003;
Chesbrough, 2003; Cooper, 2017). Marketing scholarship, in turn, has developed concepts
for need identification, segmentation, competitive analysis, value propositions, adoption,
and exchange. These bodies of knowledge are complementary, but they are frequently
brought together only at later stages of commercialization.
This gap is highly relevant because numerous activities currently integrated
into innovation management—such as needs identification, market segmentation,
competitive analysis, value proposition development, technology adoption, and knowledge
commercialization—correspond to concepts developed in the marketing literature several
decades before the consolidation of innovation management as an academic discipline
(Cherington, 1920; Kotler, 1967; Stanton et al., 2007; Lambin et al., 2009).
This article focuses on the market-facing dimensions of technological innovation
rather than on all determinants of innovation performance. It does not equate marketing
failure with overall technology-transfer failure, nor does it assume that public and private
organizations face identical barriers. National innovation-system conditions, institutional
incentives, regulation, financing, absorptive capacity, and regional industrial structure can
be decisive, particularly for publicly generated technologies.
The research question is: Do selected concepts that structure technological
innovation management and technology commercialization present a functional
correspondence with Strategic Marketing principles, and does the joint consideration of
strategic and operational marketing dimensions help explain factors historically associated
with commercial failure of technologies?
To answer this, the study is developed in two complementary phases:
• First Phase: Conducts a syntactic-comparative analysis of the main concepts
of innovation management and their functional equivalents in marketing.

2. Theoretical Framework
2.1.  Marketing, Market Orientation, and Innovation
Marketing should be differentiated from marketing innovation. Marketing innovation refers
to the implementation of a new marketing method; Strategic Marketing, by contrast,
concerns the analysis of needs, segments, competitors, value propositions, and long-
term market choices. Operational Marketing translates these choices into product, price,
distribution, and communication decisions (Lambin, Gallucci, & Sicurello, 2009; Kotler &
Armstrong, 2018). This distinction addresses the concern that recognition of marketing
innovation in the Oslo Manual already resolves the problem addressed by this article: it
does not, because the present analysis concerns a broader decision framework rather than
a single innovation category.
Technological innovation management consolidated as a field of study during
the second half of the 20th century, driven by the development of national innovation
systems, the strengthening of technology transfer mechanisms, and the growing economic
importance of knowledge (Rogers, 2003; Chesbrough, 2003; OECD/Eurostat, 2018). Its
evolution gave rise to various methodologies aimed at increasing the probability of adoption
and commercial success of innovations.
In parallel, marketing began its conceptual development several decades earlier.
Following the contributions of Shaw (1916) and Cherington (1920), it evolved from a focus
centered on exchange toward models of strategic market analysis and value creation.
Subsequently, Kotler (1967) consolidated the Marketing Management paradigm, while
Lambin et al. (2009) expanded this perspective through the Market-Driven Management
model, distinguishing two complementary dimensions: Strategic Marketing and Operational
Marketing.
Strategic Marketing comprises needs identification, market segmentation,
competitive analysis, and the formulation of long-term strategies, whereas Operational
Marketing transforms these decisions into commercial actions through product, price, place
(distribution), and promotion (communication) management (Lambin et al., 2009; Kotler &

5
Armstrong, 2018). Both dimensions are complementary and constitute a framework for
understanding the relationship between the organization, the market, and value generation.
According to Lambin et al. (2009), the integration of both dimensions is
indispensable for reducing commercial risk. An operational execution without a strategic
foundation tends to generate technically viable products that are disconnected from
market needs, while a strategy without effective implementation rarely produces results.
Consequently, analyzing the commercial performance of innovations requires considering
both dimensions as independent yet complementary factors.
In turn, innovation literature incorporates concepts with equivalent functions within
the technological generation and adoption process. The Oslo Manual defines innovation as
the effective introduction of a new or significantly improved product or process (OECD/
Eurostat, 2018), while the Association of University Technology Managers (AUTM) defines
technology transfer as the process through which research results are transformed into
products and services with economic and social impact (AUTM, 2024).
Market-orientation research reinforces this positioning. Kohli and Jaworski (1990)
describe market orientation as organization-wide intelligence generation, dissemination,
and responsiveness; Narver and Slater (1990) emphasize customer orientation, competitor
orientation, and interfunctional coordination. Evidence also suggests that market and
technology orientations interact rather than compete: market orientation may support
technology-based innovations, while organizational context shapes the nature of this
relationship (Zhou et al., 2005). Thus, the article does not claim that technological
development should be subordinated to marketing. It argues that market-facing capabilities
must be integrated with technological capabilities when commercial outcomes are at stake.
Recently, Zelada (2026) formulated a series of marketing principles for technological
innovation, taking into account buyer behavior in contexts characterized by nuanced supply
with uncertainty.
On this basis, the present study analyzes whether the main constructs of
Technological Innovation Management present a conceptual convergence with the
foundations of Strategic Marketing and whether this convergence helps explain part of the
factors associated with the commercial performance of innovations.
The present study is grounded in a core body of foundational literature comprising
the works of Shaw (1916), Cherington (1920), Kotler (1967), Rogers (2003), Chesbrough
(2003), Lambin et al. (2009), the Oslo Manual (OECD/Eurostat, 2018), and Cooper (2017).
Collectively, these contributions trace the evolution of thinking on marketing, innovation,
technology transfer, and market orientation, providing the conceptual framework that
underpins the analysis developed in this study.
2.2. Technology Commercialization, Transfer, and Market Intelligence
Technology transfer converts inventions, scientific knowledge, and research results into
applications with economic or social value. It can occur among universities, firms, public
entities, and across national borders. Transfer includes, but is not limited to, licensing,
collaborative development, spin-offs, contractual arrangements, and knowledge exchange.

Technology transfer should not be reduced to a conventional sale. It is better
understood as a specialized exchange and coordination process involving technology owners,
adopters, intermediaries, intellectual-property arrangements, development commitments,
and final markets. Selling and technology transfer share an exchange structure, but transfer
also includes valuation, rights allocation, adaptation, absorptive capacity, and post-transfer
development. This qualification preserves the functional comparison while avoiding an
overstated equivalence.
Technology intelligence and foresight are relevant upstream complements.
They help identify technological trajectories, application fields, competitors, partners, and
emerging needs; Strategic Marketing uses related market intelligence to choose segments,
articulate value propositions, and design routes to adoption. Together, these practices can
improve commercialization decisions without eliminating contextual constraints.
Servitization provides a complementary perspective for understanding the market-
facing transformation of technological offerings. Originally defined as the transition from
product-centered businesses toward integrated product-service offerings, servitization
emphasizes value-in-use, customer relationships, and the organizational capabilities
required to commercialize complex solutions (Vandermerwe & Rada, 1988; Baines et al.,
2009). More recent research on digital servitization shows that digital technologies can
strengthen this transition by enabling data-driven services, lifecycle management, and new
solution-oriented business models (Paschou et al., 2020; Favoretto et al., 2022). From the
standpoint of technological innovation management, servitization innovation illustrates how
technological value is not fully realized through the technical artifact alone, but through the
integration of market needs, complementary services, delivery mechanisms, and adoption
processes (Xing et al., 2023). Zelada (2024) analyzes the transaction costs and barriers
to entry associated with technological innovation, drawing on some of the arguments
proposed by Xing et al. and adopting a perspective focused on problem resolution and
strategic reorientation. These perspectives are consistent with the present study’s
argument that Strategic Marketing can contribute to reducing commercial risk. However,
servitization is not operationalized as an independent variable in the present model and is
therefore considered a complementary theoretical perspective rather than an explanatory
construct.
2.3. Contextual Determinants of Commercialization
Commercial outcomes are multicausal. In addition to market-related factors, technology
commercialization can be affected by technological readiness, intellectual-property quality,
organizational capabilities, public-policy instruments, funding, regulation, culture, and
the structure of the national or regional innovation system. The model developed below
deliberately addresses only a subset of commercial-risk factors. Its findings must therefore
be interpreted as complementary associations, not as a complete account of technology-
transfer outcomes.

3. Research Hypotheses
The present research poses two complementary hypotheses:
Hypothesis 1 (H1): Selected concepts of Technological Innovation Management
and technology commercialization exhibit a consistent conceptual and functional
correspondence with Strategic Marketing principles.
H1 is restricted to the selected concepts and dimensions examined in the
comparative matrix. It does not posit disciplinary identity, historical priority, or theoretical
dependence between Innovation Management and Marketing.
Hypothesis 2 (H2): The joint consideration of Strategic Marketing and Operational
Marketing variables is significantly associated with a substantial share of the factors
historically associated with commercial failure of technological innovations.
H2 is exploratory and associational. It does not establish that these marketing
dimensions are the sole or causal determinants of commercial failure.
These hypotheses do not seek to establish absolute causal relationships between
Strategic Marketing and Innovation Management. Rather, their purpose is to examine the
conceptual consistency and explanatory capacity of an integrated analytical framework
that provides a complementary perspective for interpreting the processes associated with
technological innovation management.
4. Methodology
4.1. Research Design
The study adopted a sequential mixed-method design with two articulated phases. First, a
comparative conceptual analysis examined functional correspondences between selected
innovation- and marketing-related constructs. Second, an exploratory multiple linear
regression model evaluated the explanatory contribution of two macro-constructs derived
from the Market-Driven Management model.
4.2. Phase 1: Comparative Conceptual Analysis
Two conceptual pairs were selected because they represent market-facing dimensions
of the innovation ecosystem: Innovation-Marketing and Technology Transfer-Commercial
Exchange. Definitions were obtained from the Oslo Manual (OECD/Eurostat, 2018), the
American Marketing Association (2017), AUTM (2024), and marketing literature. Each
definition was decomposed into five dimensions: subject, principal action, object, recipient,
and purpose. The analysis evaluates functional correspondence, not terminological identity.
This procedure made it possible to compare the functional structures of the
concepts while avoiding limiting the analysis to terminological similarities. Consequently,
the evaluation focused on the function that each concept performs within its respective
disciplinary body.
The analyzed definitions were obtained exclusively from institutional documents
and widely accepted literature within each knowledge area, including the Oslo Manual

The comparison indicates a functional correspondence between the selected concepts.
Innovation concerns the implementation of a new or significantly improved product
or process; marketing encompasses activities through which offerings are created,
communicated, delivered, and exchanged. Both involve an offering, an external recipient,
and a process through which value is recognized and adopted.

The result should be read as a functional convergence rather than equivalence. Innovation

While innovation is defined as the effective introduction of a new or significantly
improved product or process, marketing describes the set of activities through which value
is created, communicated, delivered, and exchanged—a sequence clearly oriented toward
achieving the stated effective introduction.
Although both concepts stem from different perspectives, the syntactic
comparison shows a functional convergence in three main dimensions:
• They are structured around a transformation process whose expected
outcome is the adoption of an offering by a user.
• They require the existence of an external recipient whose response
determines the success of the process.
• They implicitly incorporate the notion of value creation as a necessary
condition to achieve that result.

5.3. Quantitative Analysis Results
The second phase of the research examined the explanatory capacity of the integrated
Market-Driven Management model through a Multiple Linear Regression analysis based on
secondary information and technological audit records. As established in the methodology,
the model incorporated two independent macro-constructs: Strategic Marketing and
Operational Marketing.
The statistical processing yielded an adjusted coefficient of determination (Adjusted
R^2 = 0.77), indicating that the model explains a substantial proportion of the variability

5.4. Convergence of Evidence
The main contribution of this research lies in the convergence between the conceptual
analysis and the statistical model. While the former identifies a structural correspondence
between the main constructs of Technological Innovation Management and Strategic
Marketing, the latter demonstrates that Lambin's integrated model explains a significant
proportion of the factors historically associated with the commercial failure of innovations.
In combination, both approaches indicate that Strategic Marketing principles do
not merely constitute tools for technology commercialization, but also provide an analytical
framework capable of contributing to the understanding of knowledge generation,
evaluation, and transfer processes. Consequently, the evidence supports the central thesis
of the study and provides a foundation for analyzing the theoretical and practical implications
derived from this conceptual and empirical convergence.

6. Discussion
The results obtained allow for an interpretation of the relationship between Strategic
Marketing and Technological Innovation Management from a different perspective than the
one that predominates in specialized literature. Traditionally, both disciplines have evolved
as relatively independent fields, establishing points of intersection mainly during the final
stages of innovation commercialization. However, the conceptual and quantitative evidence
presented in this study suggests that this relationship could be deeper and could begin
from the earliest phases of formulating a novel product that may ultimately culminate in an
innovation.
The conceptual and quantitative evidence converges on a limited but useful
proposition: Strategic Marketing offers a complementary analytical language for examining
market-related decisions in technology commercialization. It should not be interpreted as
an alternative to innovation-management theory or as a universal explanation for transfer
outcomes.
From this perspective, the findings are consistent with the Market-Driven
Management approach proposed by Lambin et al. (2009), according to which understanding
the market constitutes a parallel process to developing the offering rather than a post-
innovation activity. Likewise, the evidence is consistent with the propositions of Narver
and Slater (1990) and Day (1994), who maintain that market orientation constitutes an
organizational capability associated with sustainable competitive performance.
The contribution is threefold. First, it distinguishes Marketing Innovation from
Strategic Marketing as a decision framework. Second, it treats technology transfer as a
specialized commercialization and coordination process rather than as a simple sale. Third,
it empirically explores the joint relevance of strategic and operational market dimensions
for commercial-risk factors. These clarifications delimit the article’s claim and make its
interdisciplinary contribution more precise.
The quantitative results complement this interpretation. The explanatory capacity
achieved by the integrated model of Strategic Marketing and Operational Marketing indicates
that both dimensions contribute concurrently to explaining an important proportion of the
causes historically associated with the commercial failure of innovations. Particularly, the
greater weight observed in strategic variables is consistent with research identifying the
absence of clearly defined needs, competitive pressure, and the timing of market entry as
recurring failure factors (CB Insights, 2024; Cooper, 2017).
In short, the results allow for broadening the traditional interpretation of marketing
within innovation processes. Rather than a tool oriented exclusively toward commercializing
already developed technologies, Strategic Marketing can be understood as an analytical
framework that helps guide decisions from the initial stages of opportunity identification,

needs evaluation, segment selection, and value proposition design. This interpretation does
not alter the foundations of Technological Innovation Management; instead, it proposes
a conceptual articulation with a previously consolidated theoretical body with which it
demonstrates a symbiotic relationship.
The practical implications of this integration are also relevant. The systematic
incorporation of Strategic Marketing tools during the early stages of technology evaluation
could strengthen project prioritization, reduce uncertainty associated with adoption, and
improve resource allocation for technology transfer. Similarly, maintaining Operational
Marketing variables as independent components of the model confirms that market
understanding alone does not guarantee the commercial success of an innovation if it is
not accompanied by adequate execution of commercial decisions.
Consequently, the evidence obtained suggests that articulation between both
disciplines can constitute a promising line of research to understand more precisely
the factors conditioning the commercial performance of technological innovations
and to strengthen the models currently used by R+D+i ecosystems. For instance, the
mechanisms and methodologies for measuring the problems causing innovations to fail
due to commercial or market issues deserve a closer approach and open a crucial line of
reflection and research for a better understanding of the subject.
For publicly originated technologies, these market-related dimensions interact with
system-level barriers. Weak institutional incentives, limited commercialization capabilities,
regulatory constraints, scarcity of risk capital, and low firm absorptive capacity can impede
transfer even when market analysis is sound. Future studies should test the framework
across university, public-research, corporate, and start-up settings, and include contextual
variables at organizational and national-system levels.
7 . Limitations of the Study
The quantitative analysis relies on secondary information and available audit records. The
sources are complementary but heterogeneous, and the results cannot establish definitive
causal relationships. Constructing macro-constructs required coding and conceptual
aggregation, which may simplify differences among technologies and innovation contexts.
The study does not use interviews, field observations, or a longitudinal primary
database. Accordingly, it should not be described as involving full methodological
triangulation. Future research can combine the present coding logic with multi-case
studies, interviews with technology-transfer offices and adopting firms, and longitudinal
commercialization data. The analysis also excludes several potentially important institutional,
regulatory, cultural, financial, and macroeconomic conditions.
These estimates should be interpreted as exploratory associations within
the constructed model. They do not establish causal exclusivity, nor do they imply that
marketing-related variables outweigh technological, institutional, regulatory, financial, or
national innovation-system conditions that may also affect technology commercialization
outcomes. The present analysis deliberately delimits its scope to the market-related
dimensions specified in the hypotheses. Therefore, the findings indicate the explanatory
relevance of Strategic and Operational Marketing within this defined analytical framework,

8. Conclusions
This study examined the possible conceptual correspondence between the key principles
of Technological Innovation Management and those previously developed by Strategic
Marketing theory. It also researched the explanatory capacity of the Market-Driven
Management model in relation to the commercial failure of technological innovations.
The evidence obtained through the conceptual analysis showed that innovation
and technology transfer processes maintain a consistent functional correspondence with
concepts extensively developed by Strategic Marketing theory. This convergence manifested
in aspects related to needs identification, value generation, exchange, information gathering
for decision-making, and the market adoption of solutions. Consequently, the results are
consistent with the first hypothesis raised and support the existence of a conceptual
underlying nature between both disciplinary bodies.
Complementarily, the quantitative analysis revealed that integrating Strategic
Marketing and Operational Marketing dimensions significantly explains the variability
observed in the historical causes of commercial failure in technological innovations.
Estimating the model revealed that both dimensions maintain independent and
complementary effects, suggesting that the commercial performance of an innovation
depends both on the quality of the prior strategic analysis and on the capacity to execute
commercial decisions adequately.
From a theoretical perspective, the results prompt a reconsideration of the role
of Strategic Marketing within Technological Innovation Management. Rather than a tool
applicable solely during technology commercialization, the presented evidence is consistent
with interpreting it as a conceptual framework that can contribute to the understanding of
processes present from the initial stages of opportunity identification, needs evaluation,
and development strategy design.
From an applied perspective, the explicit incorporation of segmentation tools,
competitive analysis, needs validation, and value proposition design during the first phases
of innovation could strengthen technological evaluation, improve articulation between
knowledge-generating and knowledge-adopting organizations, and contribute to reduce the
commercial risk associated with technology transfer.
The central conclusion is deliberately bounded: Strategic Marketing can serve
as a complementary framework for reducing market-related commercialization risk, but it
cannot replace technological assessment, institutional capability, financing, regulation, or
innovation-system conditions. This framing strengthens the article’s theoretical contribution
while preserving its practical relevance for universities, technology-transfer offices, firms,
and innovation-policy stakeholders.
