# Primal Marketing

Primal Marketing
Drawing on 25 years of research, consultancy, and teaching experience in the
fast- moving consumer goods sector, Primal Marketing reveals the hard- wired
motives driving customer behaviour and the secrets of leveraging these to
craft higher- impact marketing strategies.
While markets, technologies, and media channels have transformed rap -
idly, the human mind that buys, chooses, and connects has remained funda-
mentally the same. By introducing an evolutionary approach to marketing,
the book argues that effective marketing works not by manipulating desire but
by aligning brands with deep, enduring human needs. Structured around ten
major product categories –  including food, fashion, health, home, play, tech-
nology, transport, and finance –  the book shows how everyday consumption
reflects primal drives related to survival, belonging, status, attraction, care,
and learning. These insights are organised through the PRIMAL framework, a
practical strategic tool designed to help marketers diagnose what truly moti -
vates their audiences and translate insight into clear positioning, storytelling,
and brand decisions.
Written primarily for marketing practitioners, brand leaders, and strategists,
and supported by examples suitable for students and educators, the book
bridges theory and application. Readers will gain a powerful lens for under -
standing consumer behaviour, sharpening brand strategy, and future- proofing
marketing decisions by grounding innovation in timeless human psychology.
Michael Nicholson  is a consumer psychologist and Professor of Strategic
Marketing at Durham University, specialising in evolutionary decision-
making, brand strategy, and inclusive, accessible marketing.
Sarah Hong Xiao is a consumer psychologist and Professor of Marketing at
Durham University, where she is Head of the Department of Management
and Marketing, specialising in consumer learning, behaviour analysis, and
AI- driven marketing.

Chapter 1
The PRIMAL Consumer
Why Evolution Sells
Awakening the Ancient Consumer
Walk into any modern shopping centre, open Amazon on your phone, or
scroll through Instagram, and you’ll see a wide array of choices. Vegan
snacks, designer trainers, cryptocurrency wallets, mindfulness apps –  all feel
new, tailored to a fast-  moving, digital world. Yet the mind browsing them
hasn’t changed significantly in tens of thousands of years.
Our ancestors didn’t have credit cards or broadband, but they had the same
psychological toolkit we do. That toolkit was forged over millennia to help
humans solve recurring problems: finding food, avoiding danger, securing
mates, caring for children, learning from experience, and gaining respect
from peers. It’s the same brain that once judged whether berries were edible,
or whether a stranger was friend or foe, that now judges whether an advert is
trustworthy or whether a brand is desirable.
This is the paradox of the consumer: the environment has changed, but the
mind has not.
Why This Matters for Marketing
Marketers often assume people are rational, modern decision-  makers. We
talk about “choice architecture”, “purchase journeys”, and “value prop -
ositions” as if consumers carefully weigh every option. But research from
behavioural science and neuroscience shows the opposite. Most decisions
are quick, emotional, and rooted in instinct.
Think about the last time you bought a coffee on the go. Did you compare
calorie counts, or calculate the precise price-  to- taste ratio between Costa
and Starbucks? Probably not. You acted on habit, brand familiarity, or even
mood. These are shortcuts our brain uses –   and they’re shaped by the same
primal circuits that helped our ancestors make life- and- death choices on the
savannah.
Recognising this gap between the “modern marketplace” and the “ancient
brain” is the starting point for marketing that works.

A Consumer Older Than Civilisation
The consumer brain we’re speaking to is old –  far older than civilisation, agri-
culture, or writing. Humans have spent roughly 95% of their evolutionary
history as hunter-  gatherers. Only in the past few thousand years have we
invented farming, cities, and mass production. Only in the last few decades
have we added the internet and smartphones.
In evolutionary terms, that’s a blink of an eye. The brain hasn’t had time to
evolve to these new conditions. Instead, it reuses the same old adaptations in
new contexts. Psychologists call this mismatch theory: instincts designed for
one environment spilling over into another.
This explains why calorie-  dense foods are so tempting in a world where
obesity is a problem. Our brains evolved to seize rare, energy- rich opportun-
ities. It’s why we are drawn to status symbols like luxury watches or sports
cars. In small tribes, reputation meant survival. And it’s why social media is
addictive: we evolved to track reputation cues in groups of 150, not thou -
sands of digital “friends”.
The Marketing Challenge
For marketers, the challenge is simple but profound: consumers don’t tell
you what really drives them. When asked, they’ll say they chose a brand for
its quality, convenience, or price. But beneath those rational justifications
lie deeper motives. A parent doesn’t buy baby food only because it’s nutri -
tious –  they buy it because it reassures them they are protecting their child.
A graduate doesn’t buy an expensive handbag only because it lasts –  they buy
it because it signals success and identity.
Brands that speak only to surface reasons risk being forgettable. Brands that
tap into ancient motives can become unforgettable.
A New Lens
That is what this book is about: giving marketers a lens to see through the noise
of features and benefits and into the primal motives driving choice. When we
understand that consumers are ancient creatures in a modern marketplace,
marketing becomes less about manipulation and more about resonance.
The best brands don’t trick the brain –   they tell stories that align with the
instincts already embedded within it.
In the chapters that follow, we’ll explore these instincts in detail. But for
now, hold onto one simple truth: the consumer you’re speaking to is the same
one who once hunted, gathered, courted, and cared in small ancestral tribes.
The packaging is modern, but the psychology is ancient.
And until you awaken that ancient consumer, your marketing will struggle
to stick.

What Evolutionary Psychology Brings to the Party
If marketing is about understanding what makes people tick, then psych -
ology has always been its close cousin. For decades, marketers leaned on
the work of Freud, Maslow, and Skinner to decode consumer behaviour. But
in the past 30 years, a new voice has joined the conversation: evolutionary
psychology.
At first glance, it might feel remote from marketing. Evolutionary psychology
asks: Why do humans think, feel, and act the way they do? Its answer: because
those thoughts, feelings, and actions helped our ancestors survive and repro-
duce. We crave sugar because calories once meant survival. We worry about
reputation because status once meant resources. We are drawn to certain
shapes, faces, and stories because they once meant opportunity or threat.
When applied to marketing, this perspective is powerful. It tells us that
consumers are not blank slates shaped only by advertising. They are carriers
of ancient adaptations. And those adaptations guide what products appeal to
customers, what messages stick, and what stories sell.
Gad Saad: Darwin in the Marketplace
One of the pioneers here is Gad Saad, whose book The Evolutionary Bases of
Consumption (2007) laid the groundwork. Saad argued that many consumer
categories are best understood as modern expressions of ancient needs.
Cosmetics are mating tools. Luxury goods are status displays. Comfort foods
are survival strategies. Entertainment itself –   from jokes to horror films –   is a
way of rehearsing adaptive challenges.
Saad’s contribution was to put Darwin into the shopping basket. He showed
that buying is not just economic but evolutionary. We may think we buy
mascara to “feel good”, but beneath that lies an evolved motive: enhancing
attractiveness to potential mates.
Douglas Kenrick: Reinventing Maslow’s Pyramid
If Saad gave us examples, Douglas Kenrick and colleagues gave us a frame -
work. In 2010, they proposed the “fundamental motives” pyramid, a rework-
ing of Abraham Maslow’s famous hierarchy of needs ( Kenrick et al., 2010 ;
Maslow, 1943). Unlike Maslow’s speculative ladder, Kenrick’s pyramid was
rooted in evolutionary logic and supported by empirical research.
At the base are survival motives like self- protection and disease avoidance.
Above them come social motives like affiliation (belonging) and status. At the
top sit reproductive motives: mate acquisition, mate retention, and parenting.
The insight was clear: we are not motivated by a vague drive to “self-
actualise”. We are motivated by ancient tasks. And those tasks cascade from
surviving threats to reproducing successfully.

The Value –   and the Limitations
Together, Saad and Kenrick did something important for marketing. They
showed us what is appealing. They mapped consumption back to the deep
motives it serves. They helped us see patterns that cut across cultures and
product categories.
But here is the limitation: their frameworks are diagnostic, not prescriptive.
They can tell us why lipstick matters, but not how to advertise it. They can
show that people seek affiliation, but not whether a campaign should focus
on humour, nostalgia, or shared rituals. They identify the motive, but they
don’t tell us how to activate it.
For a marketer, this creates a gap. Knowing that food appeals to survival is
not enough to design a successful brand strategy. You need to know whether
to frame your snack as indulgent comfort, fuel for performance, or a bonding
ritual with friends. That is not something Kenrick’s pyramid alone can deliver.
This book exists to bridge that gap. It takes the evolutionary insights of
scholars like Saad and Kenrick and turns them into an applied framework
marketers can actually use. That framework is our set of PRIMAL Motivations.
From Diagnosis to Strategy: The Marketer’s Dilemma
Knowing what motivates consumers is only half the story. The real challenge
is translating that insight into strategies, stories, and campaigns that actually
work. Their frameworks explain why people buy, but not how to act on that
knowledge. It’s like diagnosing a patient without prescribing treatment.
Imagine you’re marketing a new brand of sunscreen. Evolutionary psych -
ology tells you that people want to avoid disease and protect their health.
That’s your diagnostic insight. But how do you turn that into a campaign?
Should you emphasise safety for children, appeal to vanity by preventing
wrinkles, or highlight freedom to enjoy the outdoors? Each approach presses
the “protection” button in a different way. Choosing the right one requires
more than theory –  it requires a usable framework.
That is why we created our PRIMAL Motivations model. It takes the evo -
lutionary insights of scholars like Saad and Kenrick and distils them into six
clear, actionable motives marketers can apply directly. These motives retain
their evolutionary grounding –   the idea that consumer desires spring from
ancient adaptations –  but they translate that science into a language market -
ers can actually work with.
PRIMAL: Our Six Core Motivations
Every human decision –   from the trivial to the transformative –   is driven
by deep- rooted motives shaped over hundreds of thousands of years. Our
PRIMAL Motivations framework identifies six of the most fundamental. They

operate beneath conscious awareness, guiding choices in ways consumers
themselves rarely articulate. For marketers, they are the hidden engines of
persuasion.
Protect: Survival and Safety
The most basic human motive is to stay alive and keep those we love safe.
Our ancestors evolved to detect threats quickly and act decisively to avoid
them. That instinct remains hardwired into the consumer brain. We seek
security in everything from home alarms and financial products to safe cars,
healthy foods, and trusted healthcare brands. Products that reduce risk or
promise reassurance –  however mundane –  plug directly into this primal cir-
cuitry. Marketers who understand this know they are not selling features; they
are selling peace of mind.
Relate: Belonging and Identity
Humans are profoundly social. Belonging to a tribe once meant the diffe -
rence between life and death. That legacy lives on in our hunger for con -
nection, recognition, and shared identity. Brands that build communities,
signal membership, or help people express who they are harness this motive.
Whether it’s a football jersey, a social network, or a coffee chain that “gets”
your lifestyle, products that fulfil Relate make people feel seen, accepted, and
part of something bigger.
Impress: Status and Prestige
Status has always been a route to survival. High-  status individuals gained
access to resources, allies, and mates. Today, consumers still seek products
that help them stand out, command admiration, or signal achievement. From
luxury goods to cutting- edge tech, from professional qualifications to exclu -
sive memberships, Impress is about more than vanity –   it is a deeply rooted
drive to show value and competence. Successful brands know how to turn
their products into visible badges of prestige.
Mate: Attraction and Connection
Reproduction is the ultimate evolutionary imperative, and much of human
behaviour is shaped by its pursuit. The Mate motive underpins entire
industries –   from fashion, beauty, and fitness to hospitality and entertain -
ment. Products that enhance attractiveness, spark desire, or strengthen
romantic bonds speak to this drive. They appeal to ancient circuits that
once governed courtship and now shape consumer behaviour in subtle
but powerful ways.

Adore: Care and Nurture
Humans survive because we care. Parenting, kinship, and altruism are woven
deep into our species’ success. We are hardwired to protect and nurture not
only our offspring but also pets, partners, communities, and causes. This
motive fuels markets for baby care, pet food, home safety, healthcare, and
charity. Brands that tap into Adore position themselves not just as products
but as partners in care, enabling people to express one of the most funda -
mental human instincts.
Learn: Curiosity and Growth
Curiosity is the engine of human progress. Our ancestors’ willingness to
explore, experiment, and innovate allowed us to adapt to changing environ -
ments and pass knowledge forward. Today, the Learn motive drives demand
for education, travel, books, tools, and technologies that promise discovery
and self- improvement. Consumers seek not just information but transform -
ation –  and brands that help them grow, understand, or master something
new satisfy this deeply human urge.
Storytelling as the Bridge
Understanding these six motives is only the beginning. The real power lies in
knowing how to activate them. And the most effective tool humans have ever
developed for doing that is the story.
Stories are not decorative. They are ancient technologies of survival. Long
before writing or science, humans told stories around fires to share vital
knowledge: which plants were poisonous, how to hunt dangerous animals,
whom to trust, and how to cooperate. Stories compressed experience into
memorable lessons and spread them across generations. They taught, warned,
bonded, and inspired –  and our brains evolved to pay attention to them.
Modern neuroscience confirms this. Stories engage multiple areas of the
brain –  emotional, sensory, and motor –  in ways that data and arguments do
not. They make information felt, not just understood. They also help people
make sense of products and brands: not as lists of features, but as characters,
journeys, and transformations in which they themselves play the leading role.
For marketers, this matters profoundly. A product can theoretically appeal
to all six primal motives –   but effective brands choose one as their centre of
gravity. They identify the motive with the deepest resonance for their audi -
ence and build a story around it. A snack bar could be positioned as protec -
tion (fuel for survival), belonging (a ritual shared with friends), or status (the
choice of athletes) –  but the brand that tries to do all three risks dilution. The
brand that picks one and tells a powerful story around it can achieve focus,
clarity, and emotional depth.

The Marketer’s Advantage
This is the promise of our PRIMAL Motivations framework: it bridges the gap
between evolutionary theory and marketing practice. It gives marketers a way
to decode what consumers truly want and a method to connect with them on
the deepest possible level.
Consumers are not blank slates shaped only by ads and algorithms. They
are carriers of ancient adaptations. They seek safety and belonging, status and
love, care and discovery –   just as they always have. Brands that understand
this don’t just sell products; they become part of the stories people tell about
themselves and their lives.
Every great campaign, every enduring brand, taps into one of these PRIMAL
currents. They speak to the ancient consumer in a modern world –   and in
doing so, they don’t just persuade. They endure.
Over the coming ten chapters, from food through to education, we shall
see this in action. Each chapter explores a category of goods, along with key
products within that category. We’ll learn the evolutionary principles market-
ers need to know about. We’ll explore how each of the six PRIMAL elements
can be applied to an individual product. And we’ll see how great brands –
some we’ve worked with, others we just love –  have crafted great marketing
creatives that align around a single motivation.
Above all, we’ll see how great brand stories are told.
