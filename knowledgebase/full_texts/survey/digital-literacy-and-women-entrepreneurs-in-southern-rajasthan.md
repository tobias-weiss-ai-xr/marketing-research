Abstract
This paper develops a case  study of business -related digital literacy among women entrepreneurs in
seven districts of Southern Rajasthan. Its starting point is a central distinction: access to a phone or
payment application does not establish the ability to use digital tools indepe ndently for business tasks.
The chapter on which the paper is based defines digital literacy across five domains: basic device
operation, internet and information use, online marketing, digital financial literacy, and digital
communication and content creation. It also separates entrepreneurship effectiveness, perceived business
performance, and economic empowerment, while treating social, financial, access, and technology
barriers as contextual conditions. The case is bounded geographically to Udaipur, Chi ttorgarh,
Rajsamand, Dungarpur, Banswara, Pratapgarh, and Salumber. The underlying study specifies a
purposively recruited survey of 300 women entrepreneurs and approximately 20 –25 semi -structured
interviews, using a cross -sectional mixed-method design. Th is paper contributes a coherent case -study
logic, a measurement map, and an analysis plan that connect these elements without claiming results that
are not present in the source chapter. The propositions address capability, business outcomes,
empowerment, and the potential conditioning role of barriers. The design can identify sample
distributions and associations, but it cannot by itself establish temporal order or causality. The paper
therefore provides a publication-oriented framework for reporting the eventual empirical analysis and for
interpreting it within the limits of the available evidence.

Keywords: digital literacy; women entrepreneurs; Southern Rajasthan; micro and small enterprises;
economic empowerment; mixed methods; case study

1 Introduction
Digital tools have become part of ordinary business activity. A small enterprise may use messaging to
coordinate orders, search engines to compare suppliers, social media to display products, and a digital
payment service to receive customer funds. These a ctivities are often presented as evidence of digital
inclusion. Yet the presence of a device or an account alone says little about who controls it, what
business tasks the owner can complete, whether she can evaluate online information, or whether a tool i s
safe and affordable to use. Research on digital inequality has long distinguished access from skills and
meaningful use [1]–[5]. That distinction is especially important for women who operate small businesses
with limited capital and who may depend on sh ared devices, household permission, local connectivity,
and informal customer networks.

Economic empowerment covers reported income improvement, household contribution, participation in

separate outcomes avoids assuming that a useful business practice automatically produces stronger
commercial performance or personal control over income. Empowerment research likewi se cautions
against reducing agency to a single economic indicator [16], [17].
The source chapter specifies a purposive survey of 300 women entrepreneurs and an intended set of
approximately 20 –25 semi -structured interviews. The design is cross -sectional a nd mixed -method.
These details support a case-study manuscript centred on the research question, construct alignment, and
evidential boundaries. The chapter does not report completed statistical outputs or interview themes. For
this reason, the paper does not manufacture findings. It instead sets out the case logic and propositions
that the completed data can test, and clarifies how the eventual results should be reported.
The paper asks: how should business -related digital literacy, entrepreneurial outcome s, and contextual
barriers be examined together among women entrepreneurs in Southern Rajasthan? It makes three
contributions. First, it presents a five -domain capability model aligned to enterprise tasks. Second, it
distinguishes effectiveness, perceived performance, and economic empowerment as separate outcomes.
Third, it provides an evidence-bounded integration plan for a cross -sectional survey and interviews. The
paper is organised as a literature review, conceptual framing, case context, methodology, p ropositions
and analysis plan, discussion, implications, limitations, and conclusion.

2 Literature review
2.1 Digital literacy as capability rather than access
Definitions of digital literacy have moved beyond the ability to operate a computer. Eshet -Alkalai’s
conceptual account emphasises the range of skills needed to navigate, understand, evaluate, and create in
digital environments [1]. Work on the second -level digital divide similarly demonstrates that online
skills vary even when people have access to the internet [2]. Later research distinguishes operational,
information, communication, content creation, and strategic skills, while showing that use patterns are
influenced by education and social resources [3], [4]. These strands support a multidimensio nal measure
rather than a binary classification of respondents as digitally included or excluded.
For an enterprise owner, the relevant unit of analysis is not abstract technological competence. It is the
ability to carry out business tasks with digital to ols. Such tasks include searching for market or supplier
information, communicating with customers, presenting products, receiving payments, and maintaining
a degree of control over digital transactions. Enterprise -oriented measures must therefore capture both
practical task competence and the conditions under which use occurs. Technology may be present in the
household without being available to the entrepreneur at the time she needs it. Likewise, routine use for
personal communication does not prove that the user can create business content or assess the credibility
of an online offer.

2.3 Gender, entrepreneurship, and context
Women’s entrepreneurship research has increasingly examined the gendered assumptions embedded  in
definitions of entrepreneurial success and the institutional conditions shaping women’s business activity
[18]–[20], [22], [24], [25]. Ahl’s review shows that studies can reproduce gendered explanations when
women are treated as a special category whil e the institutional context of entrepreneurship remains
unexamined [18]. Jennings and Brush identify the need to study women’s enterprises in relation to the
wider field of entrepreneurship, while attending to the social structures that shape opportunity [ 19].
Welter’s contextual approach similarly argues that entrepreneurship is embedded in place, time, and
social relations [20].
2.4 Digital finance, agency, and empowerment
Digital financial literacy includes the ability to use financial tools and understa nd the associated risks. It
is related to, but distinct from, having a bank account or making a transaction. Oggero, Rossi, and
Ughetto connect financial literacy and digital skills to entrepreneurial activity among women and men
[12]. Drydakis examines wh ether business app training can improve entrepreneurs’ digital skills and
firm competencies [13]. These studies support the value of task -specific skill development, while
leaving open how skill use interacts with local access and social conditions.
Research on mobile money in Kenya demonstrates that digital financial services can affect women’s
economic opportunities under certain institutional and market conditions [14]. The finding is important
as evidence that digital finance may matter, but it should n ot be transferred directly to Rajasthan. India’s
financial inclusion context differs, and a cross -sectional self-report study cannot establish comparable
long-run effects. Global Findex provides country -level indicators of account ownership and financial
service use that help describe the broader environment [15].
Empowerment is not equivalent to enterprise income. Kabeer defines empowerment through resources,
agency, and achievements, stressing the importance of women’s ability to make strategic life choic es
[16]. Sen’s capability approach likewise focuses on substantive freedoms and the real opportunities
people have to pursue valued ways of living [17]. In this case, household contribution, financial
decision-making, and control over business earnings sho uld therefore be measured separately and
interpreted as reported indicators, not as a complete account of agency. A positive business -performance
association may coexist with limited control over the proceeds.
2.5 Barriers as conditions of conversion
The case chapter identifies mobility restrictions, limited personal -device access, family discouragement,
internet cost, training access, and fear of online fraud. These barriers can influence both skill acquisition
and skill application. Their theoretical role can be expressed as a conversion condition: digital capability
is more likely to correspond with reported business performance when a respondent has practical access
and room to use it. This proposition does not imply that all barriers have equal eff ect or that a composite
barrier score identifies the mechanism. Domain -level measurement and qualitative accounts are needed
to determine whether the score captures materially different experiences.

2.6 Research gap
The literature provides conceptual and c omparative foundations for examining digital capability, small -
firm digitalisation, gendered constraints, and empowerment. The case chapter identifies a more specific
gap: limited regional evidence on the five dimensions of business -related digital literacy among women
entrepreneurs in the selected Southern Rajasthan districts, considered alongside separate entrepreneurial
outcomes and contextual barriers. It further identifies a measurement gap in treating perceived business
performance and empowerment as distinct constructs, and an interpretive gap in connecting quantitative
patterns with respondents’ accounts.

3 Conceptual framework and research propositions
3.1 Capability and context
The conceptual model treats business -related digital literacy as a mult idimensional capability. Its five
domains describe what a respondent reports being able to do. These capabilities may support
information access, customer interaction, promotion, transactions, and the creation of business materials.
The model then separate s three outcome constructs: entrepreneurship effectiveness, perceived business
performance, and economic empowerment. Digital literacy is expected to be positively associated with
each outcome, but the expected associations remain hypotheses rather than established effects.

Business performance may be one route through which digital capability is associated with
empowerment. For example, improved customer reach or efficiency could coincide with higher reported
income, which may be associated with greater household co ntribution. The mediation hypothesis is
nonetheless provisional. Cross-sectional data measure the constructs at one point, leaving temporal order
uncertain; omitted factors may influence both business performance and empowerment. Any indirect
effect should therefore be reported as a statistical decomposition, not proof of a causal mechanism.
Barriers are represented in two roles. First, the barrier index may be negatively associated with
economic empowerment. Second, barriers may moderate the relationship b etween digital literacy and
perceived business performance. If a respondent has skills but limited device control or high
connectivity costs, the fitted association between her reported capability and her reported performance
may differ from that of a resp ondent facing fewer constraints. The model does not presume that this
interaction will be statistically significant.

3.2 Research questions and hypotheses
The case is guided by six questions: (1) What is the level and domain pattern of reported business -
related digital literacy in the recruited sample? (2) How is digital literacy associated with
entrepreneurship effectiveness and perceived business performance? (3) How are digital literacy,
performance, and barriers associated with reported economic empowerment? (4) Do these measures vary
across residence, education, district, or business -experience groups? (5)  Does business performance
statistically account for part of the association between literacy and empowerment? (6) Do barriers alter
the fitted association between literacy and perceived performance?
The directional propositions follow the source chapter. H1 proposes a positive association between
digital literacy and entrepreneurship effectiveness. H2 proposes a positive adjusted association between
digital literacy and perceived business performance. H3 proposes a negative association between the
barrier index and economic empowerment. H4 proposes a positive association between digital literacy
and economic empowerment after accounting for other included predictors. H5 proposes an indirect
statistical association between digital literacy and empowerment th rough perceived business
performance, subject to the limitations of cross -sectional mediation. H6 proposes that higher barrier
scores weaken the fitted association between digital literacy and business performance. Null hypotheses
should be retained and evaluated using the prespecified decision rule and reported uncertainty intervals.
The hypotheses do not assert that improving a skill will cause an outcome to change. They do not assert
that a specific training programme works, that one district is more emp owered than another, or that a
platform produces higher profits. These questions require intervention, longitudinal, or objective
administrative data that the current design does not include.

4.4 Ethics and data quality
The source chapter identifies interviews and a structured survey but does not provide complete ethics
documentation. The final  empirical paper should report institutional approval or the applicable ethics
review decision, informed consent, confidentiality protections, voluntary participation, data storage, and
procedures for participants who prefer oral explanation or local -language interviews. Because questions
concern household authority, financial control, and online safety, interviews should be conducted in a
setting that does not expose participants to avoidable social risk.
Data quality procedures should include interviewer training, consistent eligibility screening, range
checks, documentation of non -response, and procedures for resolving inconsistent entries. The article
should state whether the survey was piloted, how translation was handled, and whether the instrument
was adapted from validated measures or developed for the thesis. These details are essential for
evaluating the evidence and cannot be supplied from Chapter 1 alone. Self -report procedures also
warrant attention to common-method bias [30].

Measurement evaluation should precede substantive modelling. The final paper should report reliability
estimates and evidence relevant to factor structure, explain whether item scoring followed a prespecified
rule, and avoid treating a high internal -consistency coefficient as proof that a construct is
unidimensional. Where the instrument is newly assembled, content coverage and translation procedures
should also be documented. If a barrier index combines different kinds of constraints, the authors should
explain whether the index is reflective, formative, or simply a descripti ve sum, since these choices carry
different assumptions.
Bivariate associations can address whether the main scores vary together. Regression models can
estimate adjusted relationships between digital literacy and the outcome measures, using covariates
specified in advance and available in the data. The chapter names residence, education, district, and
business experience as relevant comparison variables; the final paper should also report any included
enterprise characteristics. Model assumptions, collinea rity diagnostics, confidence intervals, and robust
or alternative specifications should be reported as appropriate. A statistically significant coefficient
should be interpreted with its scale, uncertainty, and practical meaning, not as automatic evidence of an
important effect.
The proposed moderation analysis should include the main effects for literacy and barriers and their
interaction, with any centring or scaling procedure documented. The interaction should be interpreted
with conditional estimates or  a plot across the observed range, not only by citing a p -value. Mediation
analysis should report the estimated indirect association and its interval, while explicitly acknowledging
that cross-sectional data do not establish the temporal order required for  causal mediation. Sensitivity
analyses can examine whether conclusions change under alternative plausible model specifications, but
they cannot eliminate unmeasured confounding.

5.2 Qualitative analysis and integration
Interview analysis should begin with a record of transcription, translation, familiarisation, coding, and
theme development. Braun and Clarke’s thematic -analysis approach offers a transparent process for
identifying patterns while retaining attention to the research er’s interpretive role [28]. A codebook may

with inductive codes that emerge from participants’ accounts. The final manuscript should document
who coded the interviews, how disagreements were handled, and how quotations were selected. It
should not imply inter-coder reliability unless that procedure was actually used.
Integration should occur at the interpretation stage through a joint display or structure d comparison. For
each principal quantitative pattern, the analysis can identify whether interviews offer contextual
explanation, a contrasting account, or no relevant evidence. Interview material should not be used to
claim prevalence, and qualitative examples should not be presented as proof of a regression mechanism.

5.3 Case-study propositions
The case can be examined through six propositions aligned with the study hypotheses. Proposition 1 is
that reported digital literacy will be positively  associated with entrepreneurship effectiveness. The
anticipated pathway is that information search, digital communication, and task execution may assist
routine decisions. The final empirical report must show whether this association is observed and whether
it persists under adjustment.
Proposition 2 is that literacy will be positively associated with perceived business performance. The
strength of this association may vary across domains and enterprise context. A composite score may be
positively associat ed while one domain remains weakly related, or the association may differ by
customer channel or business type. This is why domain-specific descriptive reporting matters.
Propositions 3 and 4 concern economic empowerment. Higher barrier scores are expected  to be
negatively associated with reported empowerment, while digital literacy is expected to show a positive
adjusted association. These propositions separate capability from control over resources. If the data
reveal that literacy aligns with business co mmunication but not with income control, the interpretation
should retain that distinction rather than treating it as a failed measure of general success.
Proposition 5 is that perceived business performance may statistically account for part of the relationship
between digital literacy and economic empowerment. This is a conditional statistical claim. It would be
stronger to test with longitudinal or intervention data that establish time ordering, but the current design
can provide an initial estimate of w hether an indirect association is compatible with the observed
covariance structure.
Proposition 6 is that barriers may weaken the fitted association between literacy and performance. A
weaker association at high barrier levels would be consistent with a c onversion constraint, though
alternative explanations would remain possible. A null interaction would not establish that barriers do
not matter; they may have direct associations, vary by domain, be measured imprecisely, or produce
non-linear patterns. The result should be read with confidence intervals and the distribution of the barrier
score.

The seco nd implication is that business outcomes should remain distinct. Digital tools can support

constructs co-vary and whether the association remains after including digital literacy and barriers. It can
also identify where evidence is absent.
The third implication concerns context. Gendered constraints should not be treated as residual noise
around an individual skills measure. R esearch on gender and entrepreneurship has shown that
opportunity is shaped by institutional and social context [18] –[20]. The chapter’s barrier domains reflect
a similar logic. Skills can be acquired at an individual level, but the opportunity to apply th em may
depend on household device arrangements, connectivity costs, social expectations, customer safety, and
training access. A barrier index can help test whether constraints correlate with outcomes, while
interviews may describe what those constraints mean in respondents’ daily routines.
The regional focus provides a contribution through contextual specificity. National reports can describe
overall connectivity or gender differences, but they cannot indicate how women running informal or
home-based enterprises across these districts use digital tools. Nor can findings from another country or
state be assumed to apply uniformly. The case can add descriptive detail about the sample and show
which associations merit further study. Because recruitment is purposive, the contribution is analytic and
contextual rather than statistically representative.
A useful interpretation of any eventual findings will compare the overall literacy association with
domain-level patterns and interview accounts. If an association is stronger for information and marketing
skills than for device basics, one possible explanation is that basic use has become common while
business-specific use differentiates respondents. If the business -performance relationship is positive but
empowerment associations are weak, that pattern would suggest a need to examine control over earnings
and household decision -making separately. If the barrier interaction is significant, it would support
further study of enabling conditions; if it is not, direct b arrier effects and measurement limits still
warrant examination. These are conditional interpretive examples, not claims about the data currently
available.
The paper’s mixed-method design can strengthen interpretation when the two strands are used with ca re.
Survey results can establish patterns within the sample. Interview accounts can explain how skills and
constraints are experienced and can identify meanings omitted by predefined response options. When
both strands converge, the interpretation gains co ntextual coherence. When they diverge, the
discrepancy should be reported and considered rather than forced into agreement. The integration
remains bounded by the recruitment strategy and data quality of each strand.

8 Limitations and future research
The present paper is limited by its source material. It is derived from Chapter 1 of a thesis and therefore
has access to the research problem, definitions, scope, planned method, objectives, and hypotheses, but
not the completed survey dataset, validated final instrument, regression outputs, or interview transcripts.
It is consequently a case -study framework manuscript rather than a completed empirical report. No
results, sample profile, reliability coefficients, statistical s ignificance values, or qualitative themes are
reported here.
The underlying design also has constraints. Purposive recruitment limits population -level generalisation,
and cross -sectional self -report data can be affected by recall, social desirability, comm on-method
variance, and uncertain temporal order. Perceived business performance is not the same as audited
financial performance. Reported empowerment cannot fully capture household bargaining or agency. A
composite barrier index may combine constraints t hat operate differently, and district comparisons may
be limited by sample sizes. These limitations should be reported alongside, not after, the conclusions.
Future studies can improve on these limits through repeated measurement, probability -based sampling
where a viable frame exists, objective business indicators collected under clear consent, and carefully

9 Conclusion
This paper presents a case -study fr amework for examining women entrepreneurs’ business -related
digital literacy in seven districts of Southern Rajasthan. Its central analytical choice is to treat digital
literacy as a set of five distinct capabilities and to separate entrepreneurship effect iveness, perceived
business performance, and economic empowerment as different outcomes. It also brings contextual
barriers into the model and uses interviews to interpret, rather than replace, quantitative analysis.
The study design specified in the sourc e chapter can describe capability patterns and estimate
associations within a purposively recruited sample of 300 women entrepreneurs, with approximately 20–
25 interviews planned. It can assess whether the proposed relationships are consistent with the dat a, but
it cannot establish causal impact or represent all women entrepreneurs in the region. Since completed
findings were not supplied with the chapter, this paper states propositions and an analysis plan instead of
presenting invented results. The comple ted empirical paper should add the actual sample profile,
measurement evidence, statistical estimates, interview themes, and integrated interpretation before
claiming findings. This boundary keeps the manuscript aligned with its evidence and makes the regi onal
case a sound basis for further research.
