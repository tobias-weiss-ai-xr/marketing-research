Transformation of Georgia’s Marketing Strategy as a

By Mikheil Koplatadze1, Paata Chaganava2

ABSTRACT
This study examines how Georgia’s marketing strategy as a tourist destination changed between 2015
and 2025 and assesses the degree to which observable outcomes correspond with the strategic
objectives set at the beginning of the period. The research uses a qualitatively dominant mixed-method
design that combines thematic analysis of national and regional strategy documents, descriptive
comparison of official tourism statistics, a strategic-alignment matrix, and supporting evidence from a
2025 survey of 300 international tourists in Ajara. The decade is analysed in three phases: growth and
international positioning (2015–2019), crisis adaptation (2020–2022), and post-pandemic recovery and
renewed transformation (2023 –2025). The findings show a clear, though incomplete, shift from a
volume-led model toward wider m arket diversification, stronger digital communication, experience -
based products, four-season positioning and a greater emphasis on visitor value. Progress was uneven.
International communication and digital visibility developed faster than integrated data  systems,
consistent KPI-based evaluation, service-quality coordination and the measurement of public–private
outcomes. Georgia’s experience is therefore best described as a partial and uneven transformation:
strategic direction changed substantially, while the institutional systems needed to measure and manage
value developed more slowly. The paper offers an integrated framework for interpreting this transition
and identifies practical implications for national and regional destination management organizations.

Keywords: destination marketing; marketing transformation; Georgia; DMO; digital marketing; market
diversification; tourism resilience.

1. Introduction

The period from 2015 to 2025 brought a substantial change in the environment
in which tourist destinations compete. International visitor growth remained important,
but it became less useful as a stand -alone measure of destination performance. Digital
platforms altered the way travellers  discover, compare and discuss destinations; online
reviews and user -generated content reduced the control that official organizations have
over destination image; and the COVID -19 crisis made safety, flexibility and reliable
information part of the touris m offer itself. At the same time, destinations faced greater
pressure to connect marketing decisions with seasonality, local value creation and long -
term sustainability. These developments have moved destination marketing away from a
narrow promotional function toward a broader system that links markets, products, brand
communication, partnerships and visitor experience (Pike & Page, 2014; Li et al., 2017;
Pawłowska-Legwand et al., 2024).
 Georgia is a useful case through which to study this change. At the start of the
period, the country was experiencing rapid tourism growth but remained strongly exposed

and greater penetration of higher-value international markets (Georgian National Tourism

communication, brand architecture, partnerships with airlines and tour operators, regional
product development and the use of performance indicators (GNTA, 2018).
 The existence of these objectives, however, does not by itself demonstrate that
the marketing model was transformed. A strategy can change on paper while the
underlying system continues to rely on the same markets, channels and performance
measures. This d istinction is particularly important for Georgia because the decade
included both rapid pre -pandemic expansion and an exceptional disruption of
international mobility. It also included a subsequent recovery during which the geography
of source markets, the role of digital communication and the emphasis placed on regional
and four-season products changed visibly. The analytical problem is therefore not simply
whether tourism grew, but whether the logic by which the destination selected markets,
designed products, communicated value and evaluated performance also changed.
 Research on Georgia has often examined visitor growth, destination branding,
digital promotion, crisis recovery or particular source  markets separately. A longitudinal
assessment that connects strategic intent, implementation and observable outcomes is less
common. This paper addresses that gap by asking: how, and in which directions, did
Georgia’s marketing strategy as a tourist desti nation transform between 2015 and 2025,
and to what extent did the observed outcomes align with the objectives established at the
beginning of the period? The analysis considers changes in target markets, positioning,
tourism products, communication channe ls, crisis response, regional branding, public –
private coordination and performance measurement.
 The paper makes three contributions. First, it treats destination marketing
transformation as a connected process rather than a sequence of campaigns. Second, it
brings together national and regional strategy documents, official statistics and supporting
visitor evidence within one ten -year case. Third, it uses a strategic -alignment matrix to
distinguish between objectives that were broadly achieved, partly achieved and not yet
assessable because of data limitations. This distinction is central to the paper ’s
interpretation of Georgia’s movement from a predominantly volume -oriented model
toward a more diversified and value-conscious one.

2. Literature Review and Conceptual Framework
2.1. From destination promotion to integrated destination management
Destination marketing has traditionally been associated with awareness, image
building and visitor acquisition. Yet a destination is not a single product controlled by one
organization. It is assembled from transport, accommodation, food, attractions, publ ic
spaces, local communities and many independent firms. The experience promised in a
campaign is therefore delivered by a network of actors. Buhalis (2000) and Pike and Page
(2014) show why destination competitiveness depends not only on communication but
also on coordination, product consistency and the capacity to turn dispersed resources

into a coherent visitor proposition. In this sense, destination marketing is better
understood as the management of demand, relationships and experience than as
advertising alone.
 The changing role of destination management organizations follows the same
logic. A DMO may lead research, branding, product development, digital communication
and partnership building, but it rarely controls price, service delivery or the full product
chain. Its influence is therefore exercised through leadership, information sharing and the
alignment of stakeholders rather than direct administrative command (Pike & Page, 2014).
The service-dominant perspective similarly treats tourism value as something c o-created
through interactions between the visitor, businesses, residents and place. Marketing assets
include knowledge, relationships and organizational capabilities as well as physical
attractions (Line & Runyan, 2014).
 Technology has widened this managerial role. Early destination websites mainly
distributed information, whereas social media, online platforms and digital traces now
support interactive communication, segmentation and continuous feedback. Li et al.
(2017) describe this progression from web -based information provision toward
technology-enabled experience and engagement. Evidence from regional DMOs also
shows that the presence of a social -media account is not enough: performance depends
on content, timing, interaction and strategic integration (Mariani et al., 2016). More recent
work reaches a similar conclusion, identifying a persistent gap between the widespread use
of social media and the existence of structured strategies linked to organizational objectives
and performance measures (Pahor Zvanut & Zabukovec Baruca, 2026).
 As communication becomes more segmented, data quality and organizational
readiness become more important. Digital marketing capacity depends on infrastructure,
managerial support, staff competence and the ability to use information in decisions, not
only on access to technology (Abate et al., 2025). This point is consistent with dynamic -
capabilities theory, where adaptation rests on identifying change, acting on new
opportunities and reconfiguring resources (Teece, 2007). In tourism firms, such
capabilities are associated with organizational learning, human capital and the ability to
respond to environmental dynamism (Nguyen et al., 2023). These ideas are relevant to
destination organizations that must regularly reconsider target markets, budgets, products
and channels.

2.2. Crisis, digitalization and sustainability as drivers of change
The COVID-19 pandemic compressed several long -term changes into a short
period. International mobility collapsed, health risk became central to travel decisions, and
destination organizations had to communicate rules and uncertainty while maintaining
future demand. Research on tourism crises emphasizes that marketing under such
conditions cannot be separated from risk management, institutional coordination and
recovery planning (Ritchie & Jiang, 2019). A scoping review of travel intention likewise
shows that perceived health risk, trust, personal control and destination safety became
important determinants of travel decisions during the pandemic (Seyfi et al., 2025).
 For DMOs, the crisis expanded the meaning of marketing. Pawłowska-Legwand
et al. (2024) show how destination organizations used local experience, domestic demand
and adapted communication to sustain relationships during disruption. Evidence from

 Digitalization supported this shift by providing continuous channels for
information, reassurance, inspiration and feedback. Its effects, however, extend beyond
communication. New digital tools increasingly influence personalization, visitor flows,
product design and management decisions (Law & Chen, 2025). At the same time,
platform-based tourism can support the visibility of smaller firms and less -visited places
while creating new dependencies on global intermediaries. The sustainability effects of
digital tourism therefore depend on governance, data practices and how benefits are
distributed among local participants (Polukhina et al., 2025; Zeqiri et al., 2025).
 Sustainability also changes how marketing success is assessed. Visitor numbers
alone do not show whether tourism reduces seasonality, supports local businesses, extends
length of stay or protects cultural and natural assets. Research on Mediterranean coast al
destinations shows how difficult the transition can be when destinations remain
economically dependent on high-volume demand and governance is fragmented (Mangion
et al., 2025). For a destination seeking higher value, the relevant issue is therefore not the
rejection of growth, but the quality, distribution and resilience of that growth.

2.3. Conceptual framework
The conceptual framework used in this study treats marketing transformation as
a sequence rather than a single intervention. External pressures —crises, technology,
changes in traveller behaviour, sustainability requirements and geopolitics—create a need
for strategic response. That response is expressed through target -market selection,
destination positioning, product development, communication channels and visitor -
experience management. Whether these changes produce durable results depends on
enabling capabilities: DMO competence, data and KPI systems, human capital and public–
private coordination. The expected outcomes include wider market diversification,
stronger awareness and trust, longer stays, higher spending, lower seasonality and greater
resilience.
 The framework does not assume a direct causal line from a marketing activity to
a tourism outcome. Air access, prices, service quality, security and macroeconomic
conditions also shape performance. Marketing domains are therefore treated as
intermediate m echanisms, while organizational capabilities affect the extent to which
strategic intent is converted into observable results. This caution is especially important in
a ten-year national case that includes both a global pandemic and geopolitical change.
 To make this relationship explicit, the framework is presented as an
interconnected strategic-alignment results chain. Public, national and regional strategies
are translated into implementation activities, which interact with enabling conditions such
as a ir accessibility, service quality, human capacity, safety, infrastructure and market
intelligence. These factors jointly influence tourism  outcomes, while the outcomes
themselves provide feedback for later strategic decisions. The framework therefore treats

3. Methodology
3.1. Research design and periodization
 The study uses a qualitatively dominant mixed-method design and a longitudinal
single-case approach. Georgia is the case, and the main unit of analysis is the
transformation of the national destination marketing strategy between 2015 and 2025. The
design c ombines thematic analysis of strategy documents, descriptive comparison of
official statistics, a strategic -alignment matrix and supporting interpretation of a
quantitative survey of international tourists in Ajara. The purpose is not to isolate the
causal effect of individual campaigns. It is to assess the direction, scale and internal
consistency of a decade of change using several complementary forms of evidence.
 To make the rating procedure more transparent, the different sources were
integrated according to their analytical role rather than through fixed numerical weights.
Strategy documents were used to identify intended objectives, implementation materials
to verify whether related actions were undertaken, and official statistics, air-route data and
the Ajara survey to assess whether corresponding changes could be observed. Ratings were
assigned from the convergence of this evidence: “broadly achieved” indicates  substantial
implementation supported by observable results, “partly achieved” indicates incomplete
or mixed progress, and “not achieved” indicates that meaningful implementation or the

 The period begins in 2015 because the Georgia Tourism Strategy 2025 established

product diversification, service improvement and greater visitor spending. The analysis
ends in 2025, allowing comparison between the original objectives and observable
conditions at the end of the planning horizon. For analytical clarity, the decade is divided
into three phases: 2015 –2019, characterized by growth, international positioning and
market expansion; 2020–2022, characterized by pandemic disruption and crisis adaptation;
and 2023–2025, characterized by recovery, renewed international communication and a
stronger emphasis on diversification, digital marketing and experience-based products.

 The analytical sequence is strategic intent → implemented change → observable

3.2. Data sources and analytical procedures
 The evidence base was constructed from strategy and policy documents, official
tourism statistics, marketing implementation materials, peer -reviewed literature and two
author datasets. The principal policy sources are the Georgia Tourism Strategy 2025, the
national marketing, branding and advertising implementation plan, and Ajara’s tourism
brand, communication and development strategies. Official series from GNTA (2025a,
2025b) and the National Bank of Georgia (2025) were used to track visitor and receipt
dynamics, while airport and route information from United Airports of Georgia (2025)
supported interpretation of changes in market accessibility.
 A central quantitative component is a comparison of Georgia’s international
source markets across two three -year periods, 2016 –2018 and 2023 –2025. Using three -
year windows reduces the weight of a single anomalous year and makes the structural
direction easier to interpret. Regional market groups and selected countries were compared
by volume and percentage change. These changes are used as evidence of market
diversification, not as proof that marketing activity alone caused the observed growth.
The study also uses a 2025 survey of 300 international tourists in Ajara. The survey was
conducted through a non -probability purposive -quota design among foreign tourists
reached through visitor-centre settings in Batumi. It covered repeat visitation, seasonality,
product attractiveness, information sources, consistency between marketing promise and
on-site experience, post -crisis travel behaviour  and service quality. Results were
summarized through frequencies, percentage distributions and mean ratings. Because the
sample is regional and non-probability based, it is used as supporting evidence and is not
generalized to all international tourists in Georgia.
 Documentary material was coded around destination positioning, market
diversification, branding, digital communication, product development, service quality,
public–private coordination, data and KPIs, sustainability and crisis adaptation. The
strategic-alignment matrix compares each major objective with implemented changes and
observable results. Ratings are expressed as broadly achieved, partly achieved, not
achieved, changed in relevance, or not fully assessable because of limited data. Where
possible, each judgement was based on at least two different types of evidence.

3.3. Limitations
 The study relies substantially on official and secondary data, and the pandemic
creates a major break in the longitudinal series. Marketing effects cannot be separated
cleanly from changes in air access, prices, geopolitics and broader economic conditions.
Campaign-level budgets, conversions and ROI measures are not consistently public. In
addition, the visitor -perception evidence comes from one regional survey rather than a
repeated national panel. These constraints are incorporated into the interpretation and into
the strategic-alignment ratings.

4. Results
4.1. Growth, awareness and market expansion, 2015–2019
 The first phase established the strategic foundations of the later transformation.
Georgia sought to strengthen its international position while sustaining rapid visitor
growth and increasing tourism’s economic contribution. The 2015 strategy did not define
success only in terms of arrivals. It also called for more valuable visitors, higher spending,
longer stays, stronger air access, better service and a broader tourism product. In practice,
however, the starting market structure remained concentrated in neighbouring countries

and land -border travel, which supported volume but also exposed the destination to
regional economic and political shocks (GNTA, 2015).
 From 2015 to 2019, international awareness became a central marketing objective.
Georgia’s proposition combined cultural heritage, wine and gastronomy, mountain and
coastal landscapes, hospitality and the possibility of experiencing varied products within a
relatively small territory. The 2018 implementation plan developed this positioning into a
more operational marketing system that linked market research, advertising, digital media,
public relations, airlines, tour operators and brand implementation (GNT A, 2018). The
shift is important because it shows that marketing was being organized around market
knowledge and relationships as well as media exposure.
 Higher-value and more distant markets became more prominent in strategic
planning. European Union markets, North America, the Middle East and Asia received
increasing attention, but the pace of development differed widely because remote markets
required air access, larger budgets, longer awareness cycles and more precise product -
market matching. Airline partnerships therefore served both transport and marketing
functions. Direct connections and joint promotions were part of the practical mechanism
through which Georgia sought to reduce dependence on nearby land markets.
 Digital channels also gained a more explicit role during this phase. Websites, social
media, digital advertising and online content increasingly complemented exhibitions,
roadshows, familiarization trips and media relations. Regional branding developed in
parallel. Ajara’s brand strategy, for example, set out to broaden the region’s identity beyond
a sea-and-summer association by connecting Batumi with mountain, cultural, urban and
year-round experiences (Department of Tourism and Resorts of Ajara, 2017a, 2017b,
2019). By 2019, Georgia had therefore changed the strategic direction of its marketing
system, but the value -based model was still only partly institutionalized: visitor volu me
remained the most visible performance measure.

4.2. Crisis adaptation, 2020–2022
 The pandemic interrupted the growth model and changed the immediate purpose
of destination marketing. When international mobility was restricted, promotional
messages alone could not create demand. Communication had to address entry rules,
health requirements, cleanliness, flexibility and uncertainty. The practical task shifted from
stimulating travel toward maintaining trust, providing reliable information and preparing
for gradual recovery. This change is consistent with international evidence that risk
perception and institutional trust became central to travel decisions during the pandemic
(Seyfi et al., 2025).
 The crisis also accelerated the centrality of digital communication. Social media,
websites and direct online updates became the fastest channels through which DMOs
could explain changing conditions and stay connected with potential visitors. Content
became more practical and empathetic, while domestic and relatively accessible markets
gained temporary importance. Nature, small groups, open spaces and short flexible trips
became more relevant components of the offer. These shifts mirror the broader DMO
experience documented by Pawłowska-Legwand et al. (2024), where crisis communication
worked best when it was connected to real local products and operational readiness.
 The Ajara survey suggests that several crisis-era concerns remained relevant after
the immediate emergency. Respondents rated safety at a mean of 4.62 and cleanliness at
4.47, and 89% reported that the pandemic and subsequent crises had changed their travel
behaviour in some form (Koplatadze, 2026b). These results support the view that safety
communication did not disappear with recovery; it became part of the wider evaluation of
destination reliability. The crisis also widened the DMO role from branding and market
development toward information coordination, crisis communication and partner support.

4.3. Recovery and the renewed marketing model, 2023–2025
 The recovery phase was not simply a return to the pre -pandemic model. As
demand returned, market diversification, digital communication, experience -based
products and broader measures of value became more prominent. The strongest structural
evidence comes from the comparison of source -market groups. Between the 2016–2018
and 2023–2025 three-year periods, the combined volume of European markets rose by
54.7%, Central Asia by 298.1%, Asia by 218.8% and the Middle East by 141.2%, while the
combined volume of neighbouring-country markets fell by 28.8% (Koplatadze, 2026a).
These changes indicate a broader geographic structure and lower reliance on proximity
and land access, although they should not be interpreted as the isolated effect of marketing.
 Within Europe, the source comparison records particularly strong long -term
growth from Germany (+110.6%), Spain (+102.2%), France (+72.8%) and the United
Kingdom (+58.8%). Poland remained an important market but was more volatile, while
some Baltic markets weakened in 2025. In the Middle East and Asia, growth was evident
from Israel, Saudi Arabia, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, India, China and South Korea. The
mixed pattern matters: market size, growth and purchasing power do not guarantee
strategic value unless they are considered together with air access, seasonality, length of
stay, spending and product fit.
 Communication became more digitally embedded. The 2018 implementation
framework had already envisaged a national digital hub, multilingual content, social media,
search advertising, CRM functions, video, user -generated content and advanced digital
tools. By 2023–2025, these channels were more closely aligned with how travellers actually
searched for inspiration, checked credibility and shared experience. The Ajara survey
confirms that social media, personal recommendations, online reviews and other digital
sources jointly played a major role in the travel decision (Koplatadze, 2026b). This also

coastal-summer identity toward urban, mountain, cultural and year-round experiences. In
the 2025 survey, 63% of respondents agreed or strongly agreed that Ajara was no longer
only a sea-and-summer destination, although summer remained the most attractive season
and only about one quarter considered the region attractive in all four seasons (Koplatadze,
2026b). The evidence therefore points to partial rather than complete repositioning.
 Visitor perceptions also reveal a gap between brand communication and
operational delivery. Sixty-seven per cent of respondents judged marketing information to
be mostly or fully consistent with their actual experience. Food and accommodation were
evaluated more positively, while transport and tourist information appeared as weaker
components (Koplatadze, 2026b). The result is important for strategy because stronger
brand awareness does not guarantee that the brand promise is delivered consistently at
every touchpoint. The quality of destination marketing ultimately depends on the service
system that visitors encounter after arrival.

5. Discussion and Strategic Interpretation
5.1. From volume to value: how far did the transformation go?
 The central finding is that Georgia did move away from a purely volume -led
understanding of destination marketing, but the transition was incomplete. The intention
to raise spending, length of stay and the share of higher-value markets was present in the
2015 strategy. Market diversification, product development and digital communication
subsequently advanced in directions consistent with that objective. Yet public performance
reporting continued to make total visits the most visible and regularly available  indicator.

Country-level spending, length of stay, seasonal economic effects and campaign
conversion were less consistently integrated into one public evaluation system.
Market structure is the clearest area of transformation. The rise of European, Central
Asian, Middle Eastern and Asian markets alongside the decline in the combined volume
of neighbouring markets shows that the inbound portfolio became geographically broader.
This is strategically important because it reduces concentration in a small number of land-
access markets. Geographic diversification, however, is not the same as value
diversification. A fast-growing market may still be highly seasonal or characterized by short
stays; a smaller market may generate more spending or wider regional travel. The next
stage of value -based management therefore requires a common market portfolio that
combines volume, spending, length of stay, seasonality, risk and product fit.
 The same unevenness appears in digital transformation. Georgia’s use of social
media, video, user -generated content and online campaigns expanded substantially, and
visitor evidence confirms that digital and social sources matter in trip decisions. This i s
consistent with international research showing that digital marketing capability depends
on strategic integration rather than channel presence alone (Abate et al., 2025; Pahor
Zvanut & Zabukovec Baruca, 2026). The remaining gap is measurement: attention metrics
need to be linked more consistently to website behaviour, booking intention, conversion,
spending and retention.
 Georgia-specific evidence makes this uneven pattern clearer. Surmanidze et al.
(2024) found that successful digitalization in Georgian tourism companies depends not
only on the use of digital channels, but also on flexible organizational structures, leadership
commitment and the systematic analysis of indicators, feedback and sales metrics. This
confirms our finding that wider digital communication does not automatically mean
integrated digital capability. At destination level, Khelashvili and Okroshidze (2025) show
that 23 leading source countries accounted for 87.6% of Georgia’s foreign tourists and
explicitly identify market diversification as essential for competitiveness; at the same time,
Georgia held only moderate positions in the main competitiven ess components and a
relatively low position in the component combining tourism resources and sustainability,
with infrastructure and resource development identified as areas for improvement. This
supports our conclusion that market expansion can progress faster than the systems that
support and measure it. Khartishvili et al. (2019) provide a similar governance perspective:
in the Georgian rural -tourism context, they identified fragmented development, weak
national–local coordination, limited capacity and digital-marketing skills, and the need for
stronger DMOs, trainin g, standards and stakeholder cooperation. Thus, Georgia mainly
confirms recent research on digital capability, resilience and governance, while extending
the dynamic -capability perspective by showing that sensing and seizing new market
opportunities can ad vance faster than organizational reconfiguration, integrated
measurement and sustainable destination management. This unevenness is also visible
beyond digitalization.
 Product and regional branding also advanced more quickly than the measurement
of their economic effects. The widening of the product portfolio toward wine,
gastronomy, culture, nature, adventure and MICE creates more reasons to visit and can
support year-round travel. Ajara’s survey evidence suggests that the sea -only image has
weakened, but summer remains dominant. The more appropriate conclusion is not that

repositioning failed; it is that repositioning is underway and has not yet fully changed
seasonal perception or behaviour.

5.2. Georgia in relation to international research
 Georgia’s experience broadly follows the direction described in the destination -
management literature. The pandemic widened the DMO role from promotion toward
trust, information and coordination, matching the pattern identified by Pawłowska -
Legwand et al. (2024). The rapid expansion of digital channels, accompanied by slower
development of integrated measurement and organizational readiness, also mirrors the
distinction between technology adoption and digital marketing capability discussed by
Abate et al. (2025).
 The case also supports the dynamic-capabilities view that adaptation depends on
more than choosing the right strategic direction. Market changes must be sensed, resources
and partnerships must be mobilized, and organizations must learn from new data (Teece,
2007; Nguyen et al., 2023). Georgia’s stronge st changes—market diversification, digital
communication and product branding —were areas where strategic action could be
implemented relatively quickly. More institutionally demanding changes, such as integrated
data systems, common KPIs and consistent service delivery, developed more slowly.
The sustainability literature helps explain why this difference matters. Mangion et al. (2025)
show that the move from high -volume tourism to a more sustainable model is difficult
when economic dependence on visitor numbers remains strong and governance is
fragmented. Georgia’s regional products and four -season positioning create a basis for
distributing demand more widely in time and space, but sustainability cannot be inferred
from product labels alone. It requires measurable evidence on local benefit, re source
pressure and seasonal distribution. Digital platforms can assist with visibility and flow
management, but their contribution likewise depends on governance and data practices
(Polukhina et al., 2025; Zeqiri et al., 2025).
 Taken together, the evidence suggests a hybrid transformation. Contemporary
destination-marketing tools and ideas coexist with the legacy of a volume -based growth
model. For a small tourism economy exposed to air connectivity, geopolitics and external
demand shocks, the depth of transformation depends less on adopting new terminology
than on turning market and digital changes into institutional knowledge, coordinated
service standards and measurable economic, social and spatial outcomes.

5.3. Integrated model and policy implications
 The integrated model derived from the case has four linked levels. Strategic intent
defines the desired movement from volume toward value, diversification and year -round
demand. Implementation mechanisms convert that intent into market selection, digital
communication, air -access partnerships, product development and regional branding.
Institutional capacity—data quality, shared KPIs, DMO competence, coordination and
human capital —determines how consistently these mechanisms operate. Observable
outcomes app ear in market diversification, awareness, trust and recovery, but their
conversion into higher visitor value and service consistency is uneven when institutional
capacity lags behind strategic ambition.

CRM logic and a standard set of KPIs that connect communication with behavioural and
economic outcomes. Predictive analytics and AI -based tools should be introduced
gradually alongside improvements in data quality and staff capability rather than treated as
substitutes for them. Public –private campaigns should be tied to specific prod ucts and
target markets, and four -season positioning should be evaluated through actual seasonal
demand and experience delivery rather than communication reach alone.

6. Conclusions

This study asked how Georgia’s marketing strategy as a tourist destination
changed between 2015 and 2025 and how far the observable outcomes aligned with the
objectives established at the start of the period. The evidence supports a clear answer: the
strategic direction changed substantially, but the transformation was uneven and not fully
institutionalized. The marketing model moved beyond a narrow focus on visitor growth
toward a broader combination of market diversification, digital communication,
experience-based products, regional and four -season positioning, crisis resilience and
visitor value. At the same time, the systems used to measure and manage that value did
not advance at the same pace.
The incomplete institutionalization of the transformation is concentrated in four closely
related weaknesses: insufficient integration of data systems, limited standardization of

outcome measurement. These weaknesses do not mean that strategic change did not
occur; rather, they reduce the ability to measure, compare and manage its results
consistently. Figure 8 summarizes how these institutional gaps constrain the shift from

 The four weaknesses affect the transition to visitor value in different but
connected ways. Fragmented data systems make it difficult to link target markets and
marketing activities with spending, length of stay, repeat visitation and retention. Non -
standardized KPIs reduce comparability across national and regional levels and make
overall implementation effectiveness harder to evaluate. Weak coordination of service
quality limits the ability to monitor whether the destination promise is delivered
consistently across the visitor journey, while limited public–private outcome measurement
makes it difficult to determine whether partnerships with airlines, operators and businesses
generate higher spending, longer stays or broader local benefits. As a result, Geo rgia has
moved clearly beyond a visitor -volume logic at the strategic level, but the institutional
capacity to measure and manage visitor value has developed more slowly.
 The first major change was in market structure. Compared with 2016 –2018, the
2023–2025 period shows substantial growth in European, Central Asian, Middle Eastern
and Asian markets and a decline in the combined volume of neighbouring -country
markets. This is the clearest evidence that Georgia’s international visitor portfolio became

geographically more diversified. The objective of expanding international flows was
broadly achieved, while the higher -value-market objective was only partly verifiable
because consistent country-level evidence on spending and length of stay remains limited.
The second major change was in the content and channels of marketing. Social media,
video, user-generated content, online reviews and influencer partnerships became integral
to destination communication, and the Ajara survey confirms that social and digital sources
are important in travel decisions. Product communication also broadened toward wine,
gastronomy, culture, nature, adventure, business tourism and regional experience. These
changes support the conclusion that Georgia’s tourism offer became more multi-product
and more experience-oriented. Four-season repositioning also progressed, but the regional
evidence shows that seasonal perceptions have not disappeared: 63% of respondents saw
Ajara as more than a sea-and-summer destination, while summer remained dominant.
The third major change followed the pandemic. Between 2020 and 2022, destination
marketing had to manage safety, reliable information, flexibility and trust as well as
promotion. Survey results suggest that these concerns remained part of travel behaviour
after the immediate crisis. The period therefore expanded the coordinating role of DMOs
and made resilience a practical part of destination marketing rather than a separate crisis -
management issue.
 The principal weakness is institutional. Digital visibility, international
communication and product branding developed faster than integrated data systems,
standardized KPIs, consistent service quality and the measurement of public –private
outcomes. The finding that 67% of surveyed visitors saw marketing information as mostly
or fully consistent with actual experience is positive, but the weaker ratings for transport
and tourist information show that the brand promise is still delivered unevenly across
touchpoints. For this reason, the movement from quantity to value is best described as
substantial but incomplete.
 For policy and management, the next stage should focus on measurement and
coordination rather than a simple increase in promotional intensity. Market prioritization
should combine volume with spending, length of stay, seasonality, risk and product fit .
National and regional DMOs should use compatible data and standardized KPIs, while
CRM, predictive analytics and AI-supported tools should be introduced alongside stronger
data quality and staff competence. Joint public–private campaigns should be connected to
specific products and markets, and four -season claims should be tested against observed
demand, service delivery and local value creation.
 The study has several limitations. It depends heavily on official and secondary
sources, the pandemic creates a structural break in the time series, and marketing effects
cannot be separated fully from air connectivity, prices, geopolitics and wider econom ic
conditions. Campaign-level budgets and conversions are not consistently available, and the
visitor-perception evidence comes from one purposive -quota regional survey. Future
research should therefore use repeated multi -year visitor surveys, primary rese arch with
DMOs and private firms, campaign-level conversion analysis and market-specific models
of visitor economic value. These extensions would allow the strategic transformation
identified here to be tested with greater causal and longitudinal precision.
 Future research should build on these findings through a more explicit
longitudinal and comparative design. Repeated annual or biennial visitor surveys could be

conducted across several entry points and destination regions, including Ajara, Tbilisi,
Kakheti and mountain destinations, using stratified or calibrated sampling by source
market, season and travel purpose to improve national representativeness. A rotati ng-
panel or repeated cross -sectional design would make it possible to track changes in
spending, length of stay, repeat visitation, service evaluation and off-season demand over
time. Comparative case studies with other small or emerging destinations could test
whether the pattern identified in Georgia —rapid market and digital adaptation followed
by slower institutional integration —also appears under similar conditions. Where
campaign timing, new air routes or major policy interventions provide identifiable
treatment points, interrupted time -series, difference -in-differences or synthetic -control
approaches could be used to estimate effects more rigorously. Visitor -level survey data
could also be linked, where possible, with CRM, booking, digital -conversion and
administrative data to examine retention and service outcomes beyond stated intentions.
Finally, tourism resilience should be assessed not only through the speed of visitor
recovery after shocks, but also through market diversification, volatility, seasonality,
spending stability and the ability of destinations to maintain service quality and local value
creation. Such research would move the analysis from documenting strategic
transformation toward testing which institutional and marketing capabilities actually
produce durable visitor value over time.
