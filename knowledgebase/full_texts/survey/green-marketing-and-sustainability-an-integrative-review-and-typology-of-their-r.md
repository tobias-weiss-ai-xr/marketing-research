1Batman University, Vocational School of Social Sciences, Department of Accounting and Tax Applications, Batman, Türkiye

Abstract: Green marketing and sustainability are frequently treated as interchangeable in both scholarship and practice, yet they
describe different scopes of action. Green marketing is largely an operational lever centred on environmentally framed produc ts,
claims and communications, whereas sustainability marketing denotes a broader, long -term and societal orientation organised around
the triple bottom line. This conceptual conflation obscures how the two are actually related. The present study is an integra tive
literature review of work indexed in Scopus and Web of Science between 2021 and 2026. Studies were screened for direct relevanc e to
the green marketing and sustainability relationship, and their findings were synthesised thematically rather than aggregated
statistically. The review shows that recent research links green marketing to sustainability through six recurring domains: gr een
products and green innovation; green branding and green brand equity; green communication, advertising and greenwashing; gree n
consumer behaviour; digital and artificial -intelligence-enabled green marketing; and circular -economy, Sustainable Development Goal
and strategic -orientation concerns. Drawing on these themes, the study develops a typology that classifies how green marketi ng
contributes to broader sustainability objectives, ordering the domains by their level of analysis and by whether their orient ation is
mainly instrumental or transformative. The typology in turn supports a green -to-sustainability marketing continuum on w hich green
marketing appears as one developmental stage within a wider sustainability trajectory. The study closes with implications for  theory
and practice.

Keywords: Green marketing, Sustainability marketing, Green brand equity, Greenwashing, Circular economy, Integrative review

1. Introduction
Concern for the natural environment has moved from the
margins of marketing thought to its centre. Firms now
compete on environmental credentials, consumers
increasingly read products through an ecological lens,
and regulators scrutinise th e claims that accompany
green offerings. Within this setting, two terms recur so
often that their boundaries have blurred: green
marketing and sustainability. They are routinely used as
synonyms, both in managerial language and in parts of
the academic lit erature, even though they originate in
different problem framings and imply different scopes of
organisational action.
Green marketing developed as a response to specific
environmental problems attached to products and their
promotion. Its vocabulary is built around green products,
green claims, eco -labels and the environmental
positioning of brands. Sustainability, by con trast, is
anchored in the definition advanced by the WCED
(1987), which frames sustainable development  as
meeting present needs without compromising the ability
of future generations to meet their own. Sustainability
marketing, accordingly, is wider in sco pe: it is organised
around the triple bottom line of economic, environmental
and social value and around a long -term,
intergenerational horizon. White et al. (2025)  trace how
marketing scholarship on sustainability has historically
centred on resource cons ervation and green marketing,
and how its scope has more recently expanded to include
the circular economy, anti -consumption, regulation,
innovation and carbon emissions. Their account makes
clear that green marketing and sustainability marketing
are related but not identical.
Treating the two as equivalent has consequences. It
encourages firms to present narrow, product -level green
claims as evidence of organisational sustainability, which
is one of the mechanisms through which greenwashing
spreads. It als o fragments the research field, because
studies that examine the same underlying relationship
attach themselves to different labels and rarely speak to
one another. A reader who surveys the recent literature
finds a large number of empirical studies and se veral
reviews, but little agreement on how green marketing
connects to sustainability as a broader project.
The purpose of this paper is therefore conceptual rather
than descriptive. Rather than counting publications or

2. Feature Selection
2.1. Green Marketing
Green marketing refers to the set of marketing activities
through which firms develop, price, distribute and
communicate products whose environmental attributes
are made salient to th e market. The construct has an
operational character: it is bound to particular offerings
and to the claims made on their behalf. Recent work
continues to treat green marketing as a strategic
orientation that firms adopt in order to translate
environmental concern into market outcomes. Negi et al.
(2023) show that the dimensions  of a green marketing
orientation feed green innovation and, through it,
organisational performance, while Nuryakin and Maryati
(2022) report that gre en innovation and green
competitive advantage mediate the effect of a green
marketing orientation on the green marketing
performance of small and medium -sized enterprises. In
both accounts green marketing is a lever that operates at
the level of the firm and its products.
This op erational framing is also visible in how the
construct is measured and reviewed. Alkhatib et al.
(2023), reviewing green marketing  in the digital age,
identify strategies, challenges, promotion, consumers and
digital media as the categories that structure the field.
The emphasis throughout is on what firms do and say
about specific green offerings, rather than on the wider
societal consequences of consumption.
2.2. Sustainability Marketing
Sustainability marketing is broader. It takes the triple
bottom line  as its organising principle and extends the
marketing horizon beyond the point of sale to the full life
cycle of consumption and its social effects. Velenturf and
Purnell (2021)  articulate principles  for a sustainable
circular economy in which value is re tained and
regenerated rather than extracted and discarded, a logic
that moves marketing well beyond the promotion of
individual green products. White et al. (2025)  similarly
describe a literature that has grown to encompass anti -
consumption, regulation an d infrastructure, carbon
emissions and the social and ethical dimensions of
consumption. In this framing the unit of concern is not
the green product but the sustainability of the system
within which products are made, used and disposed of.
These different  scopes pull the two constructs toward
different levels of analysis. Green marketing is typically
studied at the level of the consumer and the firm;
sustainability marketing draws in institutional and
societal actors, including regulators and the
infrastructures that make sustainable behaviour feasible.
The two are not opposed, but they sit at different points
on a scale that runs from the tactical to the paradigmatic.
2.3. A Contested Boundary
Green marketing and sustainability overlap, and that
overlap is the source of the difficulty. Green marketing is
one of the historical roots of sustainability marketing,
and many green marketing activities do contribute to
sustainability outcomes. The problem arises when
narrow green activity is presented as sufficien t evidence
of sustainability. The literature on greenwashing
documents this slippage directly: Kwon et al. (2024), in a
content analysis of the social -media advertising of
leading global corporations, find that a large majority of
green claims were misleading, which indicates how
readily green communication detaches from substantive
environmental action. The present revi ew treats this
contested boundary not as a problem to be resolved by
definition, but as the empirical question that the recent
literature implicitly addresses through its choice of
topics.

3. Review Methodology
This study adopts an integrative literature review, a
design selected deliberately rather than by default
(Torraco, 2005 ; Whittemore and Knafl, 2005 ; Snyder,
2019). An integrative review is the method of choice
when the  aim is to synthesise conceptually diverse
evidence and to generate new framework s or typologies,
rather than to estimate an average effect or to enumerate
an exhaustive corpus. The research question here is
conceptual, namely how the recent literature connects
green marketing to sustainability, so an interpretive and
purposive synthes is is more appropriate than a
systematic or meta -analytic protocol, both of which are
designed for the replicable measurement of a defined
effect across a closed set of comparable studies. For this
reason the review does not pursue exhaustive coverage
and does not report a numerical screening flow; its
contribution is to organise a fragmented field into a
typology, not to take a census of it. This positioning

follows established guidance on integrative and
conceptual reviews, which treat purposeful, theory -
oriented selection as a legitimate and rigorous
alternative to systematic counting when the goal is
theory development (Torraco, 2005; Snyder, 2019).
The literature was searched in the Scopus and Web of
Science databases, with the window set to the period
2021 to 2026 in order to capture the current state of the
field. Search strings combined green and sustainability
vocabulary with marketing vocabulary, including the
terms green marketing, sustainability marketing, green
brand equity, greenwashing, green c onsumer behaviour
and circular -economy marketing, applied to titles,
abstracts and keywords. From the records returned,
studies were eligible when they satisfied all of the
following inclusion criteria: (i) they addressed the
relationship between green mar keting activity and
sustainability outcomes rather than either construct in
isolation; (ii) they were published in peer -reviewed
journals; (iii) they were written in English or Turkish;
and (iv) their full bibliographic details could be verified
at source.  Studies were excluded when they were
editorials, notes or conference abstracts without a full
paper; when environmental terminology appeared only
incidentally and was not central to the argument; or
when the full text was not retrievable. Within the eligi ble
pool, a purposive selection was then made of the studies
that most directly and representatively addressed each
emerging theme, giving priority to recent peer -reviewed
work and to review articles that consolidate larger
bodies of evidence. A small numb er of foundational
works that predate the window, namely the definition of
sustainable development by the WCED (1987)  and the
green brand equity model of Chen (2010), together with
the organisational  and marketing theories used to
ground the typology, were retained as conceptual
anchors because the analysis builds directly on them.
Because the aim was conceptual organisation rather than
exhaustive enumeration, the selected studies were read
closely an d coded for the substantive domain in which
they located the green marketing and sustainability
relationship. Recurring domains were then grouped, and
the groups were compared in order to identify the
analytical dimensions along which they differ. This
procedure produced the six domains presented in the
next section and the two dimensions used to construct
the typology. As a conceptual review without primary
data collection from human or animal participants, the
study did not require ethics committee approval.

4. Thematic Synthesis of the Green
Marketing and Sustainability Relationship
Six domains recur across the literature published
between 2021 and 2026. They are presented here in
turn, from the most product -centred to the most system -
centred, which previews the ordering used later in the
typology.
4.1. Green Products and Green Innovation
The most direct link between green marketing and
sustainability runs through the product and the
innovation that produces it. Green innovation converts
an environmental orientation into tangible offerings, and
recent work treats it as the mechanism that makes a
green marketing orientation consequential. Negi et al.
(2023) find that green marketing ori entation dimensions
drive green innovation, which in turn improves
organisational performance, while Nuryakin and Maryati
(2022) report that green innovation and green
competitive advantage m ediate the path from green
marketing orientation to performance among smaller
firms. In this domain sustainability is approached main ly
as product -level environmental improvement, and the
level of analysis is the firm.
4.2. Green Branding and Green Brand Equity
A second domain concerns how environmental value
accumulates at the level of the brand. The green brand
equity construct, introduced by Chen (2010) through the
drivers of green brand image, green satisfaction and
green trust, continues to organise much of this work.
Khan et al. (2022)  show that healthy green practices and
green brand attachment raise consumer -based green
brand equity while green scepticism erodes it. Ha (2022)
extends the same logic to the negative case,
demonstrating that greenwashing weakens green brand
equity through its damage to green brand image, green
satisfaction and green trust, with green concern
moderating the effect. Qayyum et al. (2023)  likewise find
that greenwashing and green confusion  reduce green
brand equity, with brand credibility moderating the
harm. Here sustainability is mediated by perception and
trust, and the level of analysis combines the consumer
and the firm.
4.3. Green Communication, Advertising and
Greenwashing
The third domain is communicative. Green advertising is
the most visible expression of green marketing, and it is
also the site at which the green marketing and
sustainability relationship most often breaks down. Ktisti
et al.  (2022), reviewing gree n advertising on social
media, map the field and its persuasive mechanisms,
while Li et al.  (2022) show that scepticism and
transparency shape green brand authenticity and green
brand evangelism. The risk of detachment between claim
and substance is documented by Kwon et al. (2024), who
find that most green claims by major corporations were
misleading. Reviewing the business -to-business setting,
Vangeli et al.  (2023) argue that the move from
greenwashing to credible green marketing depends on
greater transparency and on dur able, verifiable
sustainability commitments rather than on
communication alone. This domain operates at the
interface of the firm and the consumer, and it is where
the instrumental use of green marketing is most evident.

4.4. Green Consumer Behaviour
A fourth domain locates the relationship in the consumer.
Work here examines the antecedents of green purchase
intention and behaviour and the persistent gap between
the two. Lavuri (2022)  shows that environmental
protection emotion and price sensitivity m oderate
organic green purchasing, and Michel et al.  (2023)
identify the antecedents of green consumption intention
among younger consumers in a developing country. Lee
and Haley (2022)  segment green consumers by their
motivations for purchasing pro -environmental products.
In this domain sustainability is enacted, or not, through
individual choice, and the level of analysis is the
consumer.
4.5. Digital and Artificial -Intelligence-Enabled Green
Marketing
A fifth domain reflects the digital transformation of the
field. Alkhatib et al. (2023) review green marketing in the
digital age and show how digital media reconfigure green
promotion, consumer engagement and the diffusion of
green information. Gündüzyeli (2024)  takes this further
into the question of artifi cial intelligence, reporting that
AI-enabled personalisation and optimisation in digital
marketing can serve environmental, economic and social
sustainability ends, while also drawing attention to the
resource costs of the technology itself. The same digit al
infrastructure that amplifies authentic green
communication also amplifies misleading claims, which
connects this domain to the greenwashing literature. The
level of analysis spans the firm and the consumer, with
platforms and algorithms acting as an in termediary that
shapes both.

4.6. Circular Economy, Sustainable Development
Goals and Strategic Orientation
The sixth domain is the most system -oriented and is
where green marketing meets sustainability marketing
most fully . Velenturf and Purnell (2021)  set out
principles for a circular economy in which marketing is
responsible for value retention across reuse, repair and
remanufacture rather than for a single p oint-of-sale
transaction. Kumar Kar and Harichandan  (2022) connect
green marketing innovation to sustainable consumption,
and White et al. (2025)  place the circular economy,
regulation and social sustainability  at the centre of the
contemporary sustainability marketing agenda. In this
domain the relevant actors extend beyond the firm and
the consum er to institutions and infrastructures, and
sustainability is treated as a systemic property rather
than a product attribute.

5. A Proposed Typology and Integrative
Framework
The six domains differ along two dimensions that
together explain why the green marketing and
sustainability relationship is described so inconsistently.
The first dimension is the level of analysis at which the
relationship is located, which runs from the micro level of
the individual consumer and the firm to the macro level
of insti tutions and society. The second is the dominant
orientation of the activity, which ranges from
instrumental and transactional, where environmental
framing serves a market objective, to transformative and
paradigmatic, where marketing is reorganised around
sustainability as an end. Table 1 positions each domain
within this two -dimensional space and lists
representative studies from the review window.

Across the rows of the table the domains do not

behaviour occupies an intermediate position because
individual choice can serve either a transactional or a
transformative logic.
This pattern suggests that green marketing and
sustainability ar e best understood not as synonyms and
not as opposites, but as points on a developmental
continuum. Table 2 sets out that continuum as four
stages, each defined by its focus, its marketing logic and
the breadth of sustainability it addresses.
The continuum  reframes the relationship in a way that
the two -term debate cannot. Green marketing is not a
lesser version of sustainability marketing but an earlier
developmental stage from which sustainability
marketing grows, a reading consistent with the historical
account of White et al. (2025).

A firm can occupy more than one stage at once, and
movement along the continuum is neither automatic nor
irreversible. Greenwashing, in this light, is the failure to
progress beyond the tactical stage while claiming to
operate at the systemic one, which is why the
communication domain is the most prone to decoupling.
These two dimensions are not merely descriptive; they
rest on established bodies of theory, a grounding that
clarifies what the typology is organising. The
instrumental pole reflects a resource -based logic, in
which environmental capabilities are cultivated to secure
competitive advantage and economic returns ( Barney,
1991); green products and green innovation, green brand
equity and green advertising all fit this firm -centred,
profit-oriented reading. The transformative pole aligns
instead with institutional theory, in which normative and
coercive pressures move organisations to treat
sustainability as a legitimate end in itself ( DiMaggio and
Powell, 1983 ), and with the macromarketing  tradition,
which judges marketing by its consequences for society
and the natural environment rather than for the
individual firm (Mittelstaedt et al., 2014 ). Positioning the
typology between these traditions explains why
movement along the continuum is a lso a shift between
theoretical frames, from a resource -based, firm -centred
rationale toward an institutional, society-centred one.
Figure 1 renders the typology as a two -by-two
conceptual matrix, with orientation on the horizontal axis
and level of analys is on the vertical axis. The micro and
instrumental quadrant gathers green consumer
behaviour, green branding and green brand equity, and
green communication, advertising and greenwashing.
Green products and green innovation, digital and
artificial-intelligence-enabled green marketing, and the
circular-economy, Sustainable Development Goal and
strategic-orientation domain sit toward the macro and
transformative quadrant, which captures their stronger
link to systemic change.

6. Discussion
Beyond organising  the literature, the synthesis bears on
how the relationship is theorised and managed, and it
brings into view the parts of the field where evidence is
still scarce.
6.1. Theoretical Contributions
The review advances the field on several connected
fronts. It makes explicit a distinction that much of the
literature leaves implicit, separating green marketing as
an operational, product -centred lever from sustainability
marketing as a systemic, intergenerational orientation.
Building on that distinction, it of fers a two -dimensional
typology, organised by level of analysis and by dominant
orientation, which gives the field a shared coordinate
system in which studies using different labels can be
located and compared. The typology then grounds a
developmental rea ding of the relationship, the green -to-
sustainability marketing continuum, which replaces a
binary contrast with a graded one. A further consequence
is conceptual rather than additive: greenwashing is
reframed not as a separate phenomenon but as the gap
between a claimed and an actual position on the
continuum, a reading consistent with evidence that most
corporate green claims are misleading ( Kwon et al.,
2024) and that greenwashing  erodes green brand equity
(Ha, 2022; Qayyum et al., 2023).
6.2. Practical Implications
For managers the framework works as a diagnostic. A
firm can locate its current activity on the continuum and
then ask whether its communications claim a stage that
its products and operations do not yet support. Because
later stages build on earlier ones, the continuum also
implies a sequence: investment in green innovation and
green brand equity is a foundation for, not a substitute
for, the systemic commitments associated with the
circular economy and the Sustainable Development Goals
(Velenturf and Purnell, 2021 ; White et al., 2025 ). Read
this way, the framework discourages the shortcut of
communicating sustainability that has not been built.
6.3. Limitations
Several limitations qualify these contributions. The
review is integrative and purposive rather than
systematic, so it favours conceptual organisation over
exhaustive coverage, and it does not report a formal
selection count; a different reviewer might gro up the
domains differently or include additional studies. The
evidence base is also weighted toward consumer and
firm-level work, which leaves the institutional and
societal end of the continuum resting on fewer empirical
studies and in need of dedicated i nvestigation. Because
the relationship is evolving quickly, particularly under
the influence of digital and artificial-intelligence tools, the
typology is best treated as a provisional map. A natural
next step would convert the present integrative review
into a systematic review with a documented selection
protocol and a quantitative mapping of the field, which
would test the stability of the six domains and the
continuum against a larger corpus.
6.4. Future Research Agenda
The six domains suggest a structu red agenda in which
each theme yields a concrete question for future work.
On green products and green innovation, future research
could ask how far green innovation translates into
measurable sustainability outcomes rather than into
market positioning alo ne. On green branding and green
brand equity, it could examine which signals allow
consumers to distinguish substantive green brands from
cosmetic ones, and how durable that distinction is. On
green communication, advertising and greenwashing, it
could tes t which disclosure formats and third -party
verifications most reliably narrow the gap between green
claims and green practice. On green consumer behaviour,
it could investigate why the intention -behaviour gap
persists across cultural and income contexts, a nd which
interventions close it. On digital and artificial -

7. Conclusion
Green marketing and sustainability are related but
distinct, and treating them as interchangeable obscures
both how they connect and where green activity falls
short of sustainability. Drawing on work published
between 2021 and 2026, this review identified six
domains through which the two are linked and organised
them by level of analysis and by dominant orie ntation.
The resulting typology and the green -to-sustainability
marketing continuum recast green marketing as a
developmental stage rather than a synonym.
Sustainability should therefore be seen not as the
outcome of isolated green marketing actions but as  a
broader strategic trajectory within which green
marketing represents one stage. The framework is
offered as a shared vocabulary for researchers and as a
diagnostic for practitioners, and work that strengthens
the institutional and societal end of the co ntinuum,
ideally through a systematic mapping of the field, would
do most to test and extend it.
