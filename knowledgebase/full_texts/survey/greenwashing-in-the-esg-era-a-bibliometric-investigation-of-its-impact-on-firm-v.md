Abstract:
Greenwashing has emerged as a significant problem in
corporate sustainability. Companies frequently emphasize
their eco -friendly practices to enhance their public image.
Most studies on greenwashing have concentrated on
marketing strategies, corporate social responsibility (CSR),
and sustainability communication. However, there has been
less focus on its impact on a company's value. The current
literature is also scattered and lacks a thorough review of
research trends and themes, as well as the overall structure of
this field. Moreover, there are few bibliometric studies on
greenwashing, especially in developing countries where
regulations and p ublic awareness differ from those in
developed nations. This study aims to provide a systematic
bibliometric analysis of greenwashing research, specifically
looking at its link to firm value. By reviewing published
literature from major academic databases,  this study
highlights key research trends, influential authors, important
journals, and emerging themes. The findings offer insights
into the evolution of greenwashing research and identify
significant gaps for future studies, particularly in developing
countries. This research contributes to the existing literature
by providing a clearer understanding of greenwashing and its
effects on firm value. It will help researchers, policymakers,
and practitioners make better-informed decisions.

Introduction:
1.1 Background of the Study
In recent years, environmental sustainability has become a
major concern for businesses, stakeholders, and society.
Companies are increasingly adopting environmental practices
and disclosing sustainability information to enhance their
corporate image and meet stakeholder expectations.
However, not all environmental claims made by companies
reflect their actual environmental performance. This has led
to the emergence of greenwashing, which refers to misleading
or exaggerated environmental claims made by firms to create
a false impression of environmental responsibility [9].
Greenwashing has beco me a widespread issue due to
increasing pressure from regulators, investors, and consumers
for environmental transparency. Organizations often use
environmental communication strategically to gain
legitimacy and competitive advantage without making
substantial environmental improvements [3]. Firms may
selectively disclose positive environmental information while
hiding negative aspects, which misleads stakeholders and
creates information asymmetry [4]. This selective disclosure
affects stakeholders ’ ability to evaluate the true
environmental performance of firms.
Furthermore, greenwashing negatively affects consumer trust
and increases consumer confusion and perceived risk
regarding green products and services [1]. When companies
exaggerate environmental claims, it reduces the credibility of
sustainability initiatives and harms the overall effectiveness
of environmental policies. Greenwashing exists in different
forms and often operates in a grey area where it is difficult to
distinguish between genuine and m isleading environmental
communication [15].
At the same time, environmental, social, and governance
(ESG) performance and environmental transparency have
gained importance as key indicators of firm value. Investors
increasingly consider ESG disclosures whe n making
investment decisions, and firms with better ESG transparency
tend to have higher firm value [6]. Similarly, strong ESG
performance positively influences financial performance and
enhances firm valuation [7][14]. Environmental disclosure
also plays a significant role in improving corporate reputation
and financial outcomes [8].
However, greenwashing can distort the relationship between
environmental performance and firm value. Some firms may
benefit from greenwashing in the short term by improving
their image and attracting investors without actual
environmental improvements [5]. On the other hand,
greenwashing may negatively impact financial performance
and firm value when stakeholders discover misleading claims
[12]. Investor attention and environm ental governance also

1.2 Meaning and Concept of Greenwashing
Greenwashing refers to the practice where companies mislead
stakeholders by presenting false, exaggerated, or incomplete
information about their environmental performance [3][9]. It
is a strategy used by firms to appear environmentally
responsible without making significant environmental efforts.
Greenwashing can occur through various methods, including:
 Misleading advertisements
 Selective disclosure of environmental information
 False environmental claims
 Lack of transparency
These practices reduce stakeholder trust and affect the
credibility of sustainability reporting [1].
1.3 Importance of the Study
The study of greenwashing is important due to the increasing
focus on sustainability and environmental responsibility.
Greenwashing affects multiple stakeholders, including
investors, consumers, regulators, and society.
From an investor perspective, ESG transparency and
environmental performance influence firm value and
investment decisions [6][7]. Greenwashing may mislead
investors and affect their financial decisions.
From a corporate pers pective, greenwashing may provide
short-term benefits but can damage firm reputation and
financial performance in the long run [5][12].
From an academic perspective, bibliometric analysis helps in
understanding the research trends, key themes, and gaps in
greenwashing literature [16][19][20].
Therefore, it is important to systematically analyze
greenwashing research to understand its impact on firm value
and sustainability.

Literature review:
2.1 Introduction
Greenwashing has emerged as a significant issue in the field
of sustainability, corporate governance, and environmental
management. It refers to misleading environmental claims
made by companies to create a false impression of
environmental responsibility [9]. With increasing stakeholder
awareness and demand for transparency, greenwashing has
attracted considerable attention from researchers. This
chapter reviews the existing literature on greenwashing, its
drivers, consequences, and its relationship with firm value.
2.2 Concept and Evolution of Greenwashing
Greenwashing was first identified as a practice where
companies mislead stakeholders about their environmental
performance [9]. It occurs when firms exagg erate their
environmental achievements or selectively disclose
environmental information to improve their corporate image
[3].
Greenwashing exists in different forms and often operates in
a grey area where it is difficult to distinguish between genuine
and misleading environmental communication [15]. Over
time, greenwashing has evolved as a strategic tool used by
firms to gain legitimacy and competitive advantage [2].
Bibliometric studies have shown that greenwashing research
has increased significantly in recent years, reflecting growing
academic interest in environmental sustainability and
corporate behavior [19][20].
2.3 Drivers of Greenwashing
Several factors motivate companies to engage in
greenwashing. One of the major drivers is stakeholder
pressure, including pressure from investors, customers, and
regulators [3].
Companies often engage in selective disclosure to present a
positive environmental image while hiding negative
information [4]. This helps firms maintain legitimacy and
attract investors.
Investor attention also plays a significant role in influencing
corporate environmental disclosure. Firms may engage in
greenwashing to attract investors and improve their market
value [18].
Managerial motivations and strategic considerations also
influence greenwashing practices, as managers aim to
enhance corporate reputation and financial performance [11].
2.4 Impact of Greenwashing on Consumers
Greenwashing negatively affects consumer trust and creates
confusion among consumers regarding environmental claims
[1].
When consumers realize that companies are making false
environmental claims, it reduces their trust and confidence in
green products. Sustainability ratings and environmental
claims significantly influence consumer perception, but
misleading information can harm consumer confidence [10].
As a result, greenwashing reduces the effectiveness of
environmental marketing and damages corporate credibility.

2.5 Greenwashing and Firm Financial Performance
The relationship between greenwashing and financial
performance is complex. Some studies suggest that
greenwashing may provide short -term financial benefits by
improving corporate image and attracting investors [5].
However, in the long term, greenwashing can negatively
impact financial performance when stakeholders discover
misleading environmental claims [12].
Greenwashing creates information asymmetry between firms
and stakeholders, which affects investment decisions and
corporate performance.
2.6 ESG Performance, Environmental Disclosure, and
Firm Value
Environmental, social, and governance (ESG) performance
plays a significant role in determining firm value. Firms with
better ESG performance tend to have higher firm value and
better financial performance [7][14].
Environmental transparency also positi vely influences firm
value, as it improves investor confidence and reduces
information asymmetry [6].
Environmental disclosure helps firms improve their
reputation and financial performance [8].
Corporate environmental governance also plays a crucial role
in improving firm value and sustainability performance [13].
However, greenwashing can distort ESG information and
mislead investors, affecting firm value.
2.7 Bibliometric Analysis in Greenwashing Research
Bibliometric analysis is a quantitative method used to analyze
research trends, themes, and patterns in academic literature
[16].
It helps in identifying influential authors, journals, and
research areas.
Bibliometrix is one of the most widely used tools for
conducting bibliometric analysis and science mapping [17].
Several bibliometric studies have analyzed the development
and evolution of greenwashing research and identified key
research trends and gaps [19][20].
These studies highlight the need for further research on the
relationship between greenwashing and firm value.
2.8 Research Gap
Although many studies have examined greenwashing, ESG
performance, and environmental disclosure, the existing
research is fragmented and lacks comprehensive analysis.
There is limited bibliometric analysis focusing on

Research Methodology:
3.1 Research Design:
This study uses a bibliometric analysis approach to
systematically review the existing literature on greenwashing
and firm value. Bibliometric analysis is a quantitative
research method that evaluates large volumes of academic
publications. It identifies key trends, influential authors,
important journals, collaboration patterns, and the evolution
of themes within a specific research area. This method gives
a clear overview of th e intellectual structure and growth of
the field. It is especially useful for spotting research gaps,
emerging themes, and the overall increase in scholarly output
over time.
This study employs bibliometric techniques such as
publication trend analysis, ci tation analysis, co -authorship
analysis, co-occurrence of keywords, and source analysis to
understand the development and direction of research related
to greenwashing and firm value.
2.2 Data Source: Scopus Database
The data for this study was gathered fr om the Scopus
database, one of the largest and most reliable abstract and
citation databases for peer -reviewed literature. Scopus was
chosen for its extensive coverage of high -quality journals,
conference papers, and review articles across multiple
disciplines, including business, management, accounting, and
environmental studies.Relevant documents were selected
using keywords like “greenwashing” and “firm value,” along
with related terms. The search was limited to peer -reviewed
publications to ensure the quality and reliability of the data.
3.3 Timeline of the Study
The study covers the period from 2005 to 2026. This timeline
was chosen to capture the early development, growth, and
recent trends in greenwashing research. The selected period
allows for a detailed analysis of how the research field has
changed over the past two decades, including the rise in
publications and new research themes.
3.4 Data Description
A total of 654 documents were retrieved from the Scopus
database. These documents came from 335 different sources,
including journals, conference proceedings, and review
publications. The dataset includes various types of
publications such as research articles, review papers,
conference papers, and book chapters.
This dataset provides a strong and trustworthy foundation for
conducting bibliometric analysis and understanding the
structure, trends, and development of research on
greenwashing and firm value.

Conclusion:
This bibliometric analysis offers a thorough summary of the
body of research on greenwashing and how it relates to firm
value between 2005 and 2026. The results show that,
especially since 2017, greenwashing has developed from a
minor marketing idea into a significant and quickly growing
field of study. Sustainabi lity, ESG disclosure, and corporate
transparency are becoming increasingly important in business
practices, as evidenced by the notable annual growth rate and
the sharp increase in publications after 2021.
The analysis shows that research output is highly
concentrated in a small group of leading journals, particularly
the Journal of Cleaner Production, Business Strategy and the
Environment, and Corporate Social Responsibility and
Environmental Management. These journals serve as the
intellectual core of the  field, confirming that greenwashing
research is strongly embedded within sustainability and
environmental management disciplines. At the country level,
China and the USA dominate in terms of production and
citation impact, indicating their central role in shaping global
discourse on corporate environmental behavior.
Keyword and thematic analyses highlight “greenwashing” as
the dominant and central concept, closely linked with
sustainable development, corporate social responsibility,
ESG, and green marketin g. Over time, the focus has shifted
from general CSR discussions to more critical examinations
of corporate accountability and misleading environmental
claims. The co -occurrence and thematic maps confirm that
the field is structured around a few highly con nected core
themes, while several niche and emerging topics remain
underexplored.
Despite the rapid growth in publications, the collaboration
network reveals a fragmented structure, with many small
research clusters and limited cross -group interaction. Thi s
suggests opportunities for stronger international and
interdisciplinary collaboration. Furthermore, while
greenwashing’s impact on consumer perception and brand
reputation has been widely studied, direct empirical
investigation into its long-term effects on firm value remains
relatively limited, especially in developing countries.
Overall, this study contributes to the literature by mapping the
intellectual structure, major contributors, and evolving
themes in greenwashing research related to firm value. It
identifies important research gaps and encourages future
studies to explore the financial consequences of
greenwashing more deeply, strengthen cross -country
comparisons, and enhance methodological rigor. The findings
provide valuable guidance for resear chers, policymakers,
regulators, and corporate managers seeking to promote
transparency, reduce misleading sustainability practices, and
protect long-term firm value in an increasingly sustainability-
driven global economy.
