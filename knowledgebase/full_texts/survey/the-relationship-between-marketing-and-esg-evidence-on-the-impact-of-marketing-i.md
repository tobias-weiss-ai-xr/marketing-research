Abstract: In today’s competitive business environment, firms increasingly integrate ESG (Environmental, Social,
and Governance) principles into their marketing strategies to build reputation and gain stakeholder legitimacy.
This study examines the impact of marketing intensity on firms' ESG social performance scores, conceptualizing
marketing expenditures as a strategic signaling mechanism. Using data from Borsa Istanbul indices, the research
employs a robust methodological framework including Multiple Linear Regression (OLS), lagged models to
account for time -dependent effects, and Two -Stage Least Squares (2SLS) regression to address potential
endogeneity. The empirical results demonstrate that an increase in marketing intensity has a statistically positive
and significant impact on firms' social scores. This relationship remains robust when controlling for financial
indicators such as profitability (ROA) and leverage, and it persists across various model specifications. The
findings conclude that marketing intensity serves as a strategic antecedent that substantiates a firm’s social
commitments, thereby strengthening corporate legitimacy and operational resilience. Consequently, marketing
budgets should be managed as strategic investments for long -term value creation rather than mere tactical tools
for short-term sales.
Keywords: Marketing intensity; ESG; Corporate Sustainability; CSR
1.  Introduction
In today's business world, the success of enterprises is measured not only by their capacity to generate
financial profit but also by the extent to which they fulfill their environmental, social, and governance
(ESG) responsibilities. In particular, the 'social' dimension of sustainability performance has become
one of the cornerstones of corporate reputation, as it encompasses critical areas such as workforce
diversity, human rights, product safety, and community relations. In this context, businesses are
subjecting their marketing strategies to an ESG -oriented transformation in order to gain legitimacy in
the eyes of stakeholders and achieve a competitive advantage.
The massive financial scale reached by ESG -oriented investments in global markets strikingly
demonstrates the significance of the relationship between sustainability and marketing intensity. In
2020 alone, global assets under management (AUM) in this field reached $35.3 trillion, representing a
15% increase within two years, and this figure is projected to exceed $40 trillion by 2030 [1]. Parallel to
this growth momentum, internet searches for the concept of ESG have increased fivefold since 2019;
today, more than 90% of S&P 500 companies and approximately 93% of Russell 1000 companies have
actively begun publishing sustainability reports [2]. Furthermore, the fact that approximately 70% of
recent academic studies confirm a positive correlation between ESG performance and financial
performance indicates that this issue has evolved from a purely ethical matter into a fundamental
profitability strategy [3]. In such a vast ecosystem where trillions of dollars in capital are being

Although existing literature includes studies addressing the relationship between marketing

The primary objective of this study is to empirically analyze the impact of firms' marketing intensity
on their ESG social scores. Within the scope of this research, we examine how resources allocated to
marketing activities shape a firm's social performance, the evolution of this effect over time (lagged
effects), and its implications within the context of sectoral differences. Furthermore, a comprehensive
model is presented by incorporating the controlling effects of financial dynamics, such as profitability
and leverage, on the social score. The significance of this study stems from its substantiation of the
interaction between marketing and sustainability through corporate financial data. The findings
demonstrate to managers that marketing budgets serve as a strategic lever not only for commercial
purposes but also for building corporate social reputation. In particular, the employment of
Instrumental Variable (IV) regression models to address endogeneity issues enhances the statistical
reliability of the results, providing a robust foundation for both academic literature and professionals
engaged in sustainability reporting.
2.  Theoretical Background
2.1. The Concept of Marketing Intensity and Its Strategic Importance
Marketing intensity represents the entirety of activities carried out by enterprises to build brand
awareness, acquire loyal customers, and establish long -term relationships with their stakeholders [4],
[5].  While marketing intensity was traditionally viewed merely as advertising and marketing
expenditures, contemporary literature defines it as the firm's strategic and holistic involvement in
marketing-related processes [6]. It encompasses a wide range of activities, including public relations,
branding, the revitalization of distribution channels, and the integration of the internet into
international business processes, such as after -sales services [7], [8].  Expenditures on these activities
vary across firms and possess the characteristics of discretionary costs, independent of the cost of
goods sold [9].
From a strategic perspective, marketing intensity functions as a 'signaling mechanism,' particularly in
markets characterized by high information asymmetry [10]. High-cost marketing activities voluntarily
undertaken by enterprises transmit signals of high quality and growth, thereby enabling stakeholders
to distinguish between high -quality and low -quality firms [11], [12] . These signals can enhance a
firm’s financial function and may even serve as a national benchmark in the relationship between debt
structure and market valuation. However, management’s endeavor to remain committed to market
obligations entails a mandatory reallocation of limited resources toward innovation and production
capacity. This phenomenon, defined by Zhu et al. [13] as 'resource commitment escalation,' forces the
firm into a highly aggressive position within the technological development arena. The rapid gains
provided by marketing intensity may lead to a managerial illusion of market control; as short -term
success evolves into cognitive bias, it can encourage managers to take risks in investment decisions
[14].
Marketing intensity holds critical importance due to its association with corporate social responsibility
and sustainability strategies. Enterprises engaging in corporate social responsibility activities must
prioritize the marketing of sustainable products and services to increase their visibility among
stakeholders and enhance their corporate image [15], [16]. It is emphasized that marketing intensity is

an effective strategy not only for increasing productivity and improving firm
performance—particularly for SMEs —but also for fostering social innovation [17], [18], [19] .
Marketing strategies centered on advertising and promotions serve as a dynamic lever that
strengthens customer relationships while shaping market perception regarding a firm’s strategic
alliance announcements and reinforcing its competitive advantage [7], [20].
2.2. The Strategic Importance of ESG and the Social Dimension within the Framework of Corporate
Sustainability
ESG principles, which constitute a multi -dimensional system analyzing how enterprises structure
their environmental performance, social contributions, and managerial ethical processes, have become
an indispensable element of contemporary corporate strategies [21], [22] . Often used synonymously
with corporate sustainability, ESG encompasses a broad set of risks and opportunities ranging from
climate change to workplace safety, and from inequality to customer relations [23]. ESG emerges not
merely as a reporting tool, but as a significant indicator that creates long -term value, guiding
investment decisions, corporate strategies, and consumer perceptions [24], [25], [26], [27].
For firms, ESG performance offers a bilateral strategic advantage that strengthens both operational
competency in internal processes and trust -based relationships within the external stakeholder
universe. Comprehensive ESG practices facilitate access to product innovation and favorable policy
networks while supporting transparency -oriented sustainable growth by mitigating agency problems
[28], [29] . According to signaling theory and social identity theory, ESG performance serves as a
critical signal that reduces information asymmetry and adverse selection, thereby strengthening
corporate legitimacy by building trust among external stakeholders [30], [31], [32] . Consequently, it
provides firms with 'downside protection' during periods of shock, such as crises and pandemics,
thereby improving operational resilience [33], [34].  However, some enterprises utilize these concepts
as a reputation -oriented 'greenwashing' strategy rather than undergoing a genuine corporate
transformation[35].
The social (S) dimension of ESG is rooted in directly human -oriented foundations, such as working
conditions, human rights, employee well -being, diversity, and supply chain responsibility [36], [37] .
Within the framework of Stakeholder Theory, these social practices should be evaluated not as
strategic obstacles, but as a source of relational capital that rises above shared values between the
institution and its stakeholders, thereby nurturing competitive advantage [38]. Relational capital,
which contributes to the development of elements such as brand image, customer loyalty, and
innovation capacity, provides enterprises with a significant competitive edge [39]. Venture capital
investors and long -term fund providers perceive a firm’s social performance as an indicator of
governance quality; this perception, in turn, improves access to capital and market valuation [40], [41],
[42].
The alignment of social norms and ethical values with corporate actions enhances the determinacy of
the social dimension in consumer decisions. As emphasized by White et al. [43] and Stern [44]
consumers with social responsibility awareness utilize their purchasing processes as moral approval
mechanisms, gravitating toward brands that align with their beliefs. Consumers develop positive
attitudes toward firms that offer fair working conditions and contribute to social development, which
in turn leads to direct purchasing and brand loyalty [45], [46] .  While social sustainability -oriented
strategies support corporate productivity internally through employee motivation, they contribute to
the success of the enterprise externally by strengthening the brand image [47].
2.3. The Impact of Marketing Intensity on the ESG Social Dimension
Marketing intensity is a strategic tool that transforms an enterprise’s social commitments into tangible
values in the eyes of stakeholders. According to the Resource -Based View, marketing intensity
converts corporate capabilities into outputs and acts as a strategic moderator that shapes the
relationship between ESG activities and financial performance [48], [49]. Social dimension investments
supported by effective marketing efforts function as a dynamic value -creation mechanism that
enhances a firm’s financial performance and brand value through employee well -being and
community engagement [50], [51].

Marketing intensity functions as a strategic signaling mechanism that reduces information asymmetry
and solidifies corporate transparency by conveying ESG strategies to stakeholders through
multi-dimensional communication channels [49], [52].  According to Xiong and Bharadwaj [53], this
mechanism enables the development of target -oriented communication models by improving
stakeholder engagement. As supported by Liu et al. [54], marketing intensity transforms social
responsibility expenditures from a passive cost item into a dynamic value element that reinforces the
firm’s market resilience and strategic superiority.
Studies exist indicating that marketing intensity has a significant and positive relationship with
environmental and social sustainability [55], [56] . Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that social
media and relational marketing strategies enhance ESG performance [57]. Within the framework of
Signaling Theory, incorporating Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) efforts into marketing activities
is regarded as transmitting a constructive message to the market, signifying that the company remains
committed to its ethical values and social obligations [58]
3. Methodology and Data Set
This study aims to empirically test the relationship between firms' marketing intensity and the social
dimension (social score) of their Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) performance. The
analysis process is designed with a stepwise methodological flow to eliminate potential biases and test
the robustness of the findings.
3.1. Definition of Variables and Model Specification
In the analysis, log_sscore, which represents the social responsibility performance within the ESG
ratings of firms, is used as the dependent variable. The primary explanatory variable (independent
variable) is log_marksale_exp, which denotes the ratio of firms' marketing expenditures to their sales
revenue and serves as a proxy for marketing intensity. This variable is evaluated as an indicator of the
extent to which firms allocate resources to marketing activities relative to their sales revenue. To
control for corporate and financial factors that may influence firm performance, the following control
variables are included in the model:
Firm Size (logtassets): The logarithm of total assets.
Financial Leverage (debt_asset): The ratio of total debt to total assets.
Profitability (roa): Return on assets.
Market Visibility (BIST30 and BIST100): Firms' membership in the Borsa Istanbul indices.
The baseline econometric model estimated in this study takes the following general form. Following
the standard panel data model specification outlined by Wooldridge [59] and Greene [60], the model
is expressed as:

log_sscoreit = α + β1 log_marksale_expit + β2 logtassetsit + β3 debt_assetit + β4 roait + β5 BIST30it + β6 BIST100it + γs + εit (1)

where i denotes the firm, t denotes the time period, α is the constant term, β₁ –β₆ are the slope
coefficients, γ s represents sector fixed effects, and ε it is the idiosyncratic error term. Robust
(heteroscedasticity-consistent) standard errors are employed throughout, as recommended by
Wooldridge [59] to obtain reliable inference in the presence of non-constant variance.

3.2. Empirical Strategy and Analysis Stages
Stage 1: Baseline OLS Model with Sector Fixed Effects
In the first stage, a Multiple Linear Regression (OLS) model was estimated to examine the relationship
between the dependent variable, log_sscore, and the primary explanatory variable, log_marksale_exp.
Along with logtassets, debt_asset, and roa, the BIST30 and BIST100 dummy variables were incorporated
as controls. Sector fixed effects (γ s) were included to absorb time -invariant, sector-level heterogeneity
that could otherwise bias the coefficients — a standard approach in the fixed -effects panel literature
[61], [63]. The estimated model corresponds to Equation (1). Robust standard errors were employed to
mitigate heteroscedasticity, as discussed in Greene [60]

5

Stage 2: OLS Model with Sector and Year Fixed Effects
In the second stage, the model was expanded by incorporating year fixed effects (δ t) to control for
time-dependent factors — such as macroeconomic shocks, regulatory changes, or economic crises —
that may affect all firms simultaneously. Controlling for both cross -sectional (sector) and temporal
heterogeneity through a two-way fixed-effects specification is standard practice in panel econometrics
[61- 63] The extended model is specified as:

log_sscoreit = α + β1 log_marksale_expit + β₂–β₆ Controlsit + γs + δt + εit       (2)
where δt denotes a full set of year dummy variables. As Baltagi [61] notes, omitting year fixed effects
when unobserved aggregate time effects are present leads to inconsistent estimators; the two -way
specification addresses this concern.
Stage 3: Lagged Marketing Intensity Model
In the third stage, based on the assumption that the impact of marketing activities on social
responsibility performance may manifest over time rather than instantaneously, the one -period lagged
value of the marketing intensity variable (L. log_marksale_exp) was substituted for the
contemporaneous regressor. The use of lagged explanatory variables to capture dynamic,
time-delayed effects in behavioral and organizational processes is well established in the panel data
literature [59,62]. Furthermore, this substitution constitutes a preliminary step toward addressing reverse
causality — since the lagged value precedes the dependent variable in time, it cannot be caused by the
current-period social score[59]. The lagged model is specified as:

log_sscoreit = α + β1 log_marksale_expi,t−1 + β₂–β₆ Controlsit + γs + δt + εit      (3)
where log_marksale_expi,t−1 is the one-period lagged value of the marketing intensity variable for firm i.

Stage 4: Instrumental Variables (IV) / Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS) Estimation
In the fourth stage, the Instrumental Variables (IV) regression method was applied to rigorously
address the potential endogeneity of log_marksale_exp. Endogeneity may arise through several
channels: reverse causality (firms with higher social scores may strategically increase marketing
expenditures), omitted variable bias, or measurement error. As Wooldridge [59] and Greene [60]
emphasize, OLS estimates are inconsistent when the key regressor is correlated with the error term,
making IV estimation necessary.
The one -period lagged value of the endogenous variable (L. log_marksale_exp) was employed as an
instrument. This choice is motivated by two standard validity conditions for an instrument: (i)
relevance — the lagged marketing intensity must be significantly correlated with its current -period
value (testable via the first -stage F -statistic); and (ii) exogeneity  — the instrument must be
uncorrelated with the current -period error term ε it. Both conditions are standard requirements for IV
validity, as detailed in Baltagi [61] and Wooldridge [59].
The model was estimated using the Two -Stage Least Squares (2SLS) method. The procedure consists
of two stages:

First Stage : The endogenous variable log_marksale_expit is regressed on the instrument and all
exogenous regressors:
log_marksale_expit = π0 + π1 log_marksale_expi,t−1 + π₂–π₆ Controlsit + γs + vit     (4)
where π1 captures the partial correlation between the instrument and the endogenous variable, and v it
is the first-stage error term. The first-stage F-statistic is reported as a standard diagnostic: an F-statistic
exceeding 10 is the conventional threshold for a strong instrument [59], [60].

Second Stage: The fitted values log_marksale_exp̂it from the first stage replace the endogenous variable
in the structural equation:

log_sscoreit = α + β1 log_marksale_exp̂it + β₂–β₆ Controlsit + γs + εit       (5)

6

where log_marksale_exp̂it denotes the instrumented values from Equation (4). As Baltagi [61] explains,
substituting fitted values from the first stage eliminates the correlation between the regressor and the
error term, thereby restoring consistency of the estimator.

Stage 5: IV/2SLS Model with Full Fixed Effects
In the final stage, the 2SLS model was expanded to include both sector and year fixed effects
simultaneously, yielding the most comprehensive specification:

log_sscoreit = α + β1 log_marksale_exp̂it + β₂–β₆ Controlsit + γs + δt + εit      (6)

This two-way fixed-effects IV specification is the most demanding of the five stages and follows best
practice in applied panel econometrics  [59], [61], [6 3]. Controlling simultaneously for sector
heterogeneity, aggregate time shocks, and the endogeneity of marketing intensity provides the
strongest basis for causal inference available within this empirical setting. Comparing coefficients
across all six specifications allows a systematic robustness check of the core finding.
3.3. Findings
Table 1 presents three different OLS regression models estimated to examine the relationship between
the marketing expenditure -to-sales ratio and the social score (log_sscore), which represents the social
dimension of firms' ESG performance. In the first model, along with the primary explanatory variable
log_marksale_exp, control variables including logtassets (representing firm size), debt_asset (denoting
the financial leverage ratio), roa (representing profitability), and BIST30 and BIST100 (indicating the
firms' index memberships in Borsa Istanbul) were incorporated. Additionally, sector fixed effects were
added to the model to control for structural differences across sectors. In the first model, the
coefficient of the log_marksale_exp variable was estimated at 0.0433. Since the variables are utilized in
logarithmic form, this coefficient can be interpreted as elasticity. Accordingly, a 1% increase in the
marketing expenditure-to-sales ratio is associated with approximately a 0.043% increase in the firms'
social scores. In other words, an increase in firms' marketing intensity is associated with a small but
positive improvement in their performance regarding the social dimension of ESG.
In the second model, year fixed effects were included in addition to the variables in the first model.
This approach ensures control over factors that could affect all firms simultaneously, such as
macroeconomic conditions, economic crises, or regulatory changes. When year fixed effects were
incorporated into this model, the coefficient of the log_marksale_exp variable was estimated at 0.0354.
In this case, a 1% increase in the marketing expenditure -to-sales ratio is associated with approximately
a 0.035% i ncrease in the social score. Although a slight decrease in the magnitude of the coefficient is

the positive relationship between marketing intensity and the social score persists even when

impact of marketing activities may manifest over time, the one -period lagged value of the

the social score in the current period.Regarding the control variables, the roa variable was found to be
positive and highly significant across all mod els. The debt_asset variable is also positive and
significant, suggesting that the social score may increase to a certain extent as the leverage ratio
increases. When examining index memberships, the BIST30 variable was found to be negative and
significant, while the BIST100 variable was positive and significant. The explanatory power of the

4. Discussion
The initial OLS model in Table 1 examines the relationship between marketing intensity and social
score by controlling for financial indicators, stock market indices, and sector effects. The analysis
reveals that marketing expenditures have a positive and significant impact on social performance.
This result indicates that increases in the marketing expenditure -to-sales ratio are associated with an
increase in firms' ESG social scores. In other words, allocating more resources to marketing activities
relative to sales revenue may contribute to a higher social responsibility performance.
In the second model, year fixed effects were added to control for macroeconomic factors and
time-dependent external shocks. Despite this addition, the impact of marketing intensity on the social
score remained positive and significant. This result suggests that the positive effect of the marketing
expenditure-to-sales ratio persists even when common economic effects related to specific years are
controlled.
In the third model, lagged marketing intensity (L.log_marksale_exp) and year fixed effects were
employed, assuming that the impact of marketing activities may manifest over time. The results
showed that the effect of prior -period marketing expenditures on the current -period social score is
also positive and significant. This finding indicates that increases in the marketing
expenditure-to-sales ratio in the previous period can also enhance firms' social scores in the current
period. Therefore, it can be argued that the impact of marketing activities on social responsibility
performance emerges not only in the short term but also over time.
Regarding the control variables, it was found that return on assets (ROA) has a positive and highly
statistically significant impact on social performance across all models. This suggests that as firms'
profitability levels increase, their social scores also tend to rise. The debt_asset variable is also positive
and significant, indicating that the social score may increase to a certain extent as the leverage ratio
rises. This can be explained by the tendency of firms with higher leverage to prioritize social
responsibility activities to build trust among investors and other stakeholders. When evaluating stock
market index memberships, being listed in the BIST 30 index was observed to have a negative and
significant impact on social performance, whereas BIST 100 membership was found to affect the social
score positively and significantly. This result indicates that firms in the BIST 30 index may have lower
social scores compared to others, while firms in the BIST 100 index tend to have higher social scores.
5. Conclusion

9

This study empirically analyzes the interaction between marketing intensity and the social dimension
of ESG performance within enterprises. The research findings clearly demonstrate that an increase in
the marketing expenditure -to-sales ratio has a statistically positive and significant impact on firms'
social scores. This relationship maintains its validity not only in current -period data but also in lagged
models, which assume that the effects of marketing activities may manifest over time. Furthermore,
analyses conducted using the Two -Stage Least Squares (2SLS) method prove that the findings are
robust against endogeneity issues and that marketing intensity serves as a strategic antecedent in
enhancing social performance. Consequently, marketing expenditures function not merely as a sales
tool, but as a critical signaling mechanism that communicates a firm’s social commitments to its
stakeholders and strengthens its corporate legitimacy.
5.1. Theoretical Contributions
By positioning marketing intensity as a 'signaling mechanism' within the ESG context, this study
contributes to the literature by explaining how marketing efforts reinforce social responsibility
perception in areas characterized by high information asymmetry. Within the framework of signaling
theory and social identity theory, the role of ESG performance in strengthening corporate legitimacy
by building trust among external stakeholders is theoretically supported. Moreover, this study
empirically validates theoretical approaches that redefine marketing expenditures —moving beyond a
passive cost item to a dynamic value element and a source of relational capital that reinforces a firm’s
market resilience and strategic superiority. Methodologically, the employment of lagged variables and
instrumental variable (IV) models places the causal relationship between marketing and social
performance on a more robust foundation compared to existing studies in the literature.
5.2. Managerial Implications
The findings suggest that managers should plan marketing budgets not only for short -term sales
targets but also as strategic investment tools to enhance ESG social scores and corporate reputation.
The positive and highly significant impact of profitability (ROA) on the social score indicates that
financially successful firms can create a 'virtuous cycle' by channeling their financial strength into
social responsibility activities. Additionally, considering the impact of the leverage ratio on the social
score, firms operating with high financial leverage are advised to utilize their social performance as a
balancing element to establish trust and gain legitimacy among stakeholders. Finally, the contribution
of BIST 100 membership to the social score serves as a reminder that firms with increased market
visibility can achieve a competitive advantage by complying with social responsibility standards.
5.3. Limitations and Directions for Future Research
Although this study presents significant findings, certain limitations pave the way for future research.
The current analysis is confined to the 'social' dimension of ESG performance; therefore, future studies
could offer a more comprehensive perspective by modeling the effects of marketing intensity on
environmental and governance dimensions as well. The data utilized in the research is limited to a
specific market and index framework; thus, comparative analyses across different emerging markets

of the messages used —and the impact of risks such as 'greenwashing' on social scores —would add
significant depth to the literature.
