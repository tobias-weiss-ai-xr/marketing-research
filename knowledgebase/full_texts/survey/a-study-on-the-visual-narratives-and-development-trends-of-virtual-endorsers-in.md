  Abstract
Virtual endorsers are a new form of advertising that brands use to promote their
progressive values in the age of the metaverse and digital human influencers. This
paper will explore them as a brand marketing tool for feminism and how visual
images and narrative approaches influence the perception of consumers. The re-
search involves a literature review and analysis of six virtual endorsers: Shiseido
(SHI), SK-II (Yumi), L'Oréal (Miss M), Prada Candy, Lil Miquela, AYI. Collec-
tively, these six cases suggest that they can be seen as not only breaking the stere-
otype of the female gender by presenting different types of females who are em-
powered but also creating an emotional bond with the audience through the use of
various types of narratives such as personal development or career success. The
paper focuses on the need to create a balance between the commercial aspect and
the feminist value expression. It is cautio ned about the femvertising , which is a
shallow approach. In this article, I will explain why it is necessary to create virtual
endorsers in three ways: (1) Create characters with affective backgrounds ; ( 2)
Brand story and actual brand actions should be con sistent; (3) Consumers should
have opportunities to participate in the creation of the brand.
Keywords
Feminism; virtual endorsers; brand marketing; visual image; narrative strategy

1. Introduction
1.1 Research Background
Virtual endorsers are also being used in brand communication as the digital world is emerging and it is changing
social culture. Virtual endorsers can be able to communicate more brand emotions  and concepts than human ones,
since they will be able to use visual images and narrative strategies to present the image and message of the brand.
Simultaneously, the creation of female empowerment images has become a trend as brands react to the increasing
consciousness of women being independent. Research indicates that a large portion of consumers believe that brands
must be involved in addressing social issues, particularly gender equality (Kantar, 2020). Therefore, ethical and com-
mercial issues are presented to brands using feminist discourse as a marketing tool. However, current literature pre-
dominantly concentrates on the technological applicat ion and effectiveness of virtual endorsers in marketing.

Systematic design studies and gender studies research are  not present in terms of how visual images and narrative
strategies of virtual endorsers transmit and communicate feminist values. This paper attempts to fill this gap.
Feminist marketing is not merely the inclusion of female faces in advertisements or making women appear visually
attractive as consumers. Instead, it requires communication to reconsider the relationships between women, body,
career, family, consumption, and self-identity.
1.2 Research Status and Questions
Within the theoretical framework of feminist marketing, brands face the dual challenge of economic returns and
social responsibility. In the context of feminist marketing, virtual endorsers may help brands break through traditional
gender images, but they may also reproduce old forms of female body discipline under a new technological appear-
ance. Therefore, this study raises four specific questions:
1) How do international beauty brands construct feminist discourse systems through the visual images and narrative
strategies of virtual endorsers?
2) What differences exist among different types of virtual endorsers in terms of visual image, personality setting,
and narrative mode, and how do these differences influence consumers’ perception of and identification with
brands?
3) Why do virtual humans have distinctive research significance in feminist brand marketing, as compared with
human celebrities and traditional animated brand mascots?
4) From a feminist perspective, how can a virtual endorser system be constructed that both communicates progres-
sive values and effectively enhances brand identification?
These four questions are important to consider, and the first one is that it is not whether the virtual endorsers look
like humans or can sell products, but rather how these virtual endorsers have been used in the process of creating
women by means of the visual image and the narrative techniques.
This study explores the conceptualisation of virtual endorsers and their depiction in brand marketing based on a
literature review, with a focus on the link between feminist marketing and endorsers. This paper conducts a case
analysis to choose Shiseido, SK-II, L'Oréal, Prada, Lil Miquela, and AYI as exemplary brand characters for discus-
sion.
2. Literature Review
Virtual endorsers began as cartoon mascots but are now very realistic digital humans, and their use has been extended
to consumer industries such as fashion and beauty. However, virtual endorser issues also exist. Consumers may doubt
the authenticity of their emotional expression and value positions because they lack real-life experiences and embod-
ied sensations. In a feminist marketing context, when virtual female images only appear as young, perfect , and ide-
alised figures, they may put real women under even more unattainable digital aesthetic standards.
This paper does not regard virtual endorsers as a replacement for human endorsers, but as a new kind of personal-
ised brand communication in the digital era.
First, Mulvey’s (1975) classic discussion of the “male gaze” provides an important theoretical basis for under-
standing how female bodies are viewed in advertising. Since virtual endorsers are not naturally existing bodies, their
faces, body shapes, postures, clothing, and temperaments are all consciously designed. They therefore reveal more
directly how brands judge what kind of women deserve representation.
Second, studies on feminist marketing and anti-stereotypical gender representation have shown that breaking tra-
ditional gender roles and presenting diverse female images can enhance consumers’ identification with and loyalty
to brands. However, existing st udies still lack systematic analysis of how the gendered temperament of virtual en-
dorsers is coded and how it relates to product attributes and brand narratives.
Finally, the rise of femvertising indicates that feminist ideas have become an important resource in brand commu-
nication. Yet when brands borrow feminist discourse, they may simplify it into consumer symbols. Sobande (2019)
critically points out that commercial brands often transform feminist issues with social critical significance into con-
sumable emotional symbols, turning feminism into a marketised style and attitude—a phenomenon she terms “woke-
washing”. Comparative research among different types of vir tual endorsers remains particularly insufficient. Based
on these research gaps, this paper selects six typical cases for analysis.

3. Research Methodology
The paper will use case analysis, textual analysis, and narrative analysis. The choice of cases is based on the concept
of typicality, difference, and comparability. The six cases were selected to maximise diversity along multiple dimen-
sions. They include beauty brands (Shiseido, SK-II, L'Oréal), a luxury fashion house (Prada), a platform-native virtual
influencer (Lil Miquela), and a Chinese hyperrealistic digital human (AYAYI). This range allows comparison across
different brand sectors, cultural contexts, and degrees of technological realism.
The paper has three layers of analysis. In the first place, in terms of visual coding, it is an examination of female
subjectivity through body image, face, clothing signs, posture, gaze , and position of the virtual endorsers. Second,
from a narrative construction perspective, it looks at how the virtual endorser deals with the conflict between the
virtual and the actual. Lastly, in the context of feminist discourse translation, there is a study on the ways in which
the various instances convert abstract principles of feminism into images, personages, stories, and actions of brands.
4. Visual Representation and Narrative Analysis of Feminist Discourse in Virtual Endorsers
4.1 Visual Coding and the Construction of Gendered Subjectivity
In terms of visual subjectivity, the six cases reveal different levels of transformation from “objects to be viewed” to
“subjects capable of active expression”. Shiseido’s SHI is positioned as a virtual woman with cross-cultural charac-
teristics (Marketing Interactive, 2022). Her overall temperament presents an independent and confident urban pro-
fessional image. She often appears looking directly into the camera and occupying the visual centre of the image. Her
image moves flexibly among scenarios involving travel, nature, cities, and business, echoing Shiseido’s advocacy of
diverse beauty and its “SHI for SHE” charity initiative (Marketing Interactive, 2022).
By contrast, although AYAYI features highly realistic skin, refined makeup, and cinematic lighting, she empha-
sises coolness and a sense of high -end distance (Shenzhen Daily, 2021). She tends to wear soft makeup , stylish
clothes, and artistic backgrounds, but emotional expression is a rather minor aspect of her image. The visual image
of AYI demonstrates that digital humans possess technological and aesthetic maturity. According to feminist mar-
keting, assuming virtual women are always young, slim, perfect, and emotionally detached, even though the form of
brand communication is updated, female image production logic can remain unchanged.
Prada Candy provides another surreal path. Unlike SHI and AYAYI, who aim to approach the appearance of real
women, Candy does not primarily pursue high-tech human likeness. Instead, she embodies Prada’s avant-garde tem-
perament through glitch aesthetics and  surreal visual language (Prada, n.d.; Cosmetics Business, n.d.). She is more
than an object of sexual interest, as she has to make her own world and create order in the visual world.
The visual subjectivity of L'Oréal’s Miss M tends more toward affinity, service, and functionality. Miss M appears
as a young Chinese female figure with a cute, lively, and relaxed style (VOGUE China, n.d.). Miss M is a young
Chinese female character who has a cute, lively, and relaxed style. Her dress often contains culturally familiar aspects
of Chinese, her humorous behavior and cartoonish details create a gap between consumers of the brand. Nevertheless,
such affinity also has its constraints: Miss M seems more like a friend in society and an aid to consumers, instead of
being an independent agent with high agency.
Yumi is designed as a digital figure who can interact with users and provide skincare advice (Hypebeast, 2019).
Her visual image is similar to that of a kind and trustworthy skincare consultant. She is not characterized by the
appeal of celebrities or a fashion-conscious personality, but is characterized by a technological image, which indicates
that women can contr ol their condition themselves. Combined, Miss M and Yumi suggest that virtual endorsers in
feminist marketing do not have to be powerful and defiant. But their danger is that they are making female subjectivity
less rigid into friendship, help, and self-management.
Lil Miquela’s visual image lies between the human and the digital. She was created by the American technology
company Brud and appeared on Instagram in 2016 (HYPEBEAST, 2018). Her facial features, skin tone, hairstyle ,
and styling are like those of a real person, but there are still some traces of the digital in her face texture and expres-
sions. She is a young woman with mixed-race characteristics and generally dressed in streetwear and high-end brand
styling.
The various visual styles are not necessarily associated with feminist importance. Hyperrealism, surrealism , and
cartoonisation are merely visual techniques. The question is whether these visual means change the power status of
women’s images.

4.2 Narrative Tensions Between Virtuality and Authenticity
There is an element of contradiction in feminist marketing as virtual endorsers. On one side, virtual endorsers are not
physical and do not have any life experiences. In contrast, it requires the brand to be involved in the reality of the
issues that women are facing, which is the case with feminist marketing. Thus, the influence of virtual endorsers on
communication is determined by the fact whether the brand can convey its values through a story or overcome the
deficiency of authenticity of the brand because of its virtuality.
Shiseido’s SHI establishes credibility through everyday storytelling and functional interaction. Her narrative strat-
egy involves sharing lifestyle information through various online communication channels. In social media content,
SHI does not mainly rely on direct slogans about independent women. Instead, she embeds brand values naturally
into everyday scenarios through travel experiences, observations of nature, cross -cultural activities, and sustainable
living (Daily Vanity, 2022). Shiseido’s regional ch arity initiative “SHI for SHE” further strengthens this narrative
(Marketing Interactive, 2022).
SK-II Yumi’s authenticity comes more from functional interaction that provides professional services to consum-
ers. SK-II launched the AI virtual ambassador Yumi in collaboration with Soul Machines, and also collaborated with
the Japanese virtual influencer Imma (Hypebeast, 2019). Within this paradigm, Yumi is not only a technological tool
but also a technological representation of the values of SK -II. However, the feminist articulation of Yumi remains
largely at the personal change and self-management level.
L'Oréal’s Miss M compensates for the distance of virtuality through community-oriented and everyday narratives.
Her narratives are closer to product explanation, livestream interaction, and user Q&A (VOGUE China, n.d.). Unlike
SHI’s global value narrative and Yumi’s technology-service narrative, Miss M’s authenticity comes more from con-
tinuous companionship and high-frequency interaction.
The platform-native virtual influencer Lil Miquela adopts another approach to authenticity construction. She is not
entirely attached to a single brand. Instead, she first exists as a social media personality and then enters brand collab-
oration scenarios (HYPEBEAST, 2018). In terms of personality setting, she is constructed as a young woman who
loves music, follows fashion, and supports social issues. According to HYPEBEAST (2018), she has used her plat-
form to voice support for transgender rights, the LGBTQ+ community, racial equality, and the “Black Lives Matter”
movement, while also participating in real- world charitable projects. At the same time, Lil Miquela’s case also ex-
poses the ethical boundaries of authenticity for virtual endorsers. When a virtual figure controlled by a commercial
team publicly discusses sensitive social issues, consumers may further ask whether these positions come from the
character herself or from the commercial strategy of the company behind her.
The case of AYAYI shows that a high degree of visual realism does not necessarily bring narrative credibility.
AYAYI first appeared on social media in 2021 and was created by Ranmai Technology (Shenzhen Daily, 2021). The
skincare service offered by AYI is different than the one offered by Yumi. AYI is not focused on addressing the
issues of consumers. This ideal body of women in digital form is what people imagine as perfect but cannot feel any
emotional attachment to what happens to them.
It is a fact that can be noted in this comparison : the visual and narrative credibility are not positively associated
with the design of feminist virtual endorsers.

Yumi and Miss M are examples of technology-based and localised community-based paths; one is Yumi, the other
is Miss M. Their common benefit is that they both shift virtual endorsers to not only show images but also engage in
interactive roles. But there are also their shortcomings. They are more likely to explain products, support users , and
drive consumption. The issue-oriented and conflictual modes of expression are offered by Candy and Lil Miquela.
Candy uses surreal imagery and a rebellious attitude to express the meaning of feminism, which is a visual manifes-
tation of self-creation, aesthetic rebellion, and rejection of discipline. In the meantime, her freedom should still be
achieved through consumption. This is a big problem in feminist marketing by luxury brands because it is a con-
sumption paradox. Lil Miquela involves a combination of feminism and identity politics as well as youth culture and
platform communication. The visual strategy of Lil Miquela is more issue- oriented than the depoliticised aesthetics
of diversity practiced by SHI. According to HYPEBEAST (2018), she has used her platform to voice support for
transgender rights, LGBTQ+ communities, racial equality, and the “Black Lives Matter” movement, while also par-
ticipating in real-world charitable projects. However, this form of expression is also more vulnerable to doubts about
commercial manipulation and authenticity. AYAYI points to another risk: the high -end visual quality of virtual en-
dorsers does not equal a deepening of feminist expre ssion. Although she constructs a refined and futuristic digital
female image, this image serves high-end aesthetics and brand tonality more than it expresses real women’s experi-
ences. AYAYI’s case shows that the more technologically mature virtual endorsers become, the more brands need to
maintain awareness of aesthetic ethics and gender expression.
Therefore, it is possible to note that the fundamental principle of superficial feminist marketing is not only the
employment of feminist slogans by brands. Feminist marketing will not be on the level of superficial packaging
unless the visual presentation , narrative expression, and brand action of virtual endorsers are supportive of one an-
other.
5. Discussion
According to this study, virtual endorsers are shifting towards not only product display images but also as carriers of
brand values, digital personalities, and social issue expression. The fundamental value of virtual endorsers does not
lie in whether they are realistic enough or not, but whether they can bring consumption conversion, and whether they
can be involved in the construction of female subjectivity through visual image and narrative strategies. The

appearance, posture, language style,  and value expression of virtual endorsers are easier for brands to control than
those of human endorsers. Virtual endorsers have more personalised characteristics and social media communication
capabilities than ordinary brand mascots.
The paper is a theoretical contribution in that it expands the research on virtual endorsers to be not only related to
technology acceptance models and persuasion but also has an important critical insight into gender discourse produc-
tion. It was established by the study that brands are able to create feminist discourse through the use of virtual  en-
dorsers; therefore, they must be guided by a co -constructive process, which implies the visual representation as the
narration process, followed by action.
As the above analysis indicates, this article asserts that the optimisation of feminist virtual endorsers can be viewed
in three ways: the visual subjectivity, the authenticity of the narrative,  and the consistency of the brand. First of all,
it is important to mention the issue of visual subjectivity, as a brand should not make virtual women look like young,
slim, perfect bodies. Second, the fact of the matter is that the story of the authenticity of the brands has to recognize
the fact that there are no r eal bodies and lives behind the virtual endorsers , and these virtual people have to be used
as a means of communicating with the experiences of the actual women. Lastly, on the topic of the actions of the
brand being consistent, feminist marketing cannot simply follow the trend of the slogans, but instead it must react to
its value expression by taking some action.
6. Conclusion and Prospects
The paper has also been a research work that focused on six typical cases and analyzed the visual representation of
virtual endorsers in feminist marketing as well as the narrative construction of the same. The research indicates that
the feminist nature of the virtual endorsers is not based on how advanced their technology  is, how realistic they are
in their pictures, or even their physical appearance. It rests upon the possibility of creating the visual coding, strategy
of narration, and brand action in an identical manner. In other words, the shallowness of feminist marketing is nothing
more than a gap between image, story, and behaviour.
The paper also has its weaknesses. The first one is that the examples of cases are not very diverse and are limited
to those industries where people can easily consume with their eyes , such as beauty, fashion , and luxury goods. In
the future, there may be a development in the direction of games, automobiles, consumer electronics, and communi-
cation, among others. Second, the case analysis, visual textual analysis, and narrative analysis used in this paper have
not been followed by any further research on the effects on consumers in terms of actual reception through question-
naires, detailed interviews , and experimental studies. Further investigation could incorporate the use of audience
research approaches to explore how different age groups, gender groups, cultures , and media habits of audiences
perceive the feminist voices of the virtual endorsers.
