Abstract—Retail banks often judge customer acquisition by activity counts such
as calls made or visits completed. These counts reveal little about prospect
quality or customer need. This paper examines how prospect identification,
telecalling, business profiling, field prospecting, institutional outreach and follow-
up work together in acquisition and need -based cross -selling at one retail bank
branch. It uses a descriptive and exploratory field -based case design drawn from
a Sales and Marketing internship at HDFC Bank’s Pink City Road Branch,
Wakad, Pune  (18 May–10 July 2026). The evidence base is a consolidated 53-
entry activity log (46 dated entries and seven undated summaries), narrative
workstream records and summary tables. It is not a customer survey. Telecalling
extended reach, with dated Personal Loan call counts of roughly 30 to 60 -plus on
selected days. Probe42-based profiling helped shortlist businesses before contact.
Padyatra and institutional visits supplied context that calls could not. The source
report records one Credit Card conversion and one converted three -wing society
proposal; other product discussions remain opportunities, and no conversion rate
can be calculated. The paper contributes a Need-Based Customer Acquisition and
Lead Optimisation Framework that links structured prospect information,
requirement assessment, lead classification, product matching and follow -up
scheduling. The framework is conceptual  and untested. Branch managers can use
it to define lead stages and record next actions before they rank channels by
performance.
Keywords—customer acquisition; cross -selling; retail banking; lead management; data -supported prospecting; relationship
marketing; HDFC Bank

1. INTRODUCTION
1.1 Background of the Study
Retail banking grows through new relationships. A
branch must find people and organisations with a banking
need, start a conversation, assess the need, offer a suitable
product and stay in touch until the customer decides.
Customers differ widely. Salaried employees, shopkeepers,
hospitals, schools and residential societies each need
different services, so a branch rarely relies on one contact
channel. It combines calls, business information, field visits
and follow -up, and each channel yields different
information at a different cost.
Branches still tend to judge these channels by activity
counts. Calls dialled, visits completed and names listed are
easy to record. They do not show whether a prospect was
relevant, whether a need was confirmed or whether the next
step was taken. Research gives good reasons to look beyond
counts. The channel through which a customer is acquired is
linked to later retention and cross -buying [1], and the
balance between acquisition and retention spending affects
customer profitability [2]. A branch that counts contacts
without qualifying them can mistake effort for progress.
1.2 Customer Acquisition and Cross-Selling in Retail
Banking
Customer acquisition is the process of identifying
prospects, establishing communication, understanding their
requirements and converting suitable prospects into
customers [3]. In retail banking this process runs through
telephone calls, branch visits, databases, referrals and field
activity. Cross-selling is the offer of additional products to
an existing or newly acquired customer. Banking customers
tend to acquire products in a sequence [4], and customer
databases can help predict which product a customer may
want next [5]. The two activities are therefore linked.
Acquisition creates the first relationship, and cross -selling
can deepen it when a further need exists.
Cross-selling in banking carries an ethical and practical
condition. A product that does not fit the customer’s need,
eligibility or circumstances damages trust and may lead to
complaints or early closure. This study therefore treats
cross-selling as the identification of a genuine additional
requirement, not as a target to place as many products as
possible. The distinction matters for measurement too. A
product mentioned in a conversation is not a product sold,
and a record that treats the two as equal overstates results.
1.3 HDFC Bank and the Research Context
HDFC Bank Ltd. is a large private -sector bank in India.
This study concerns one of its branches, the Pink City Road
Branch in Wakad, Pune. The branch serves a catchment of
shops, business establishments, offices, hospitals, schools
and residential societies, so acquisition work involves
individual, business and institutional prospects.
The author worked at the branch as a Sales and Marketing
trainee from 18 May to 10 July 2026. The assigned work
covered Personal Loan, Credit Card and top -up loan
telecalling; business profiling with Probe42, a platform used
during the internship for prospect identification; padyatra,
an on -foot field -prospecting activity; visits to shops,
hospitals and a school; society -level outreach; social -
security-scheme (SSS) activity; and follow -up [6]. The
products involved were Personal Loans, Credit Cards,

1.4 Research Problem and Significance
Activity metrics capture effort but not relevance. A call
count does not show how many prospects had a real need.
Field visits give richer context but have no comparable
denominator. Business -information tools list possible
customers but do not confirm intent. The research problem
is how these unlike activities can be interpreted together,
using the evidence a single branch actually holds, and what
that interpretation implies for lead quality and follow-up.
The study matters for three reasons. For branch managers,
it separates contact effort from qualified opportunity and
shows which records are needed to manage the difference.
For researchers, it shows how routine internship records can
yield transparent, bounded findings without invented rates
or samples. For multidisciplinary work, it treats acquisition
as a process -design and information problem, in which
structured data capture and technology -supported steps
could improve branch decisions while the empirical claims
stay within the limits of the evidence.
1.5 Relevant Literature and Theoretical Background
Relationship marketing and CRM. Relationship
marketing places trust and commitment at the centre of
lasting exchange [7]. Customer relationship management
(CRM) turns this view into practice. Payne and Frow
describe CRM as a set of connected processes that integrate
strategy, multichannel interaction, information management
and value creation [8]. Boulding et al. caution that CRM
involves more than technology and needs cross -functional
coordination [9]. Buttle and Maklan describe CRM in
strategic, operational and analytical forms, with sales and
marketing support among its operational uses [10].
Multichannel contact. Neslin et al. argue that
multichannel management requires coordinated channel
design and integrated customer information [11]. In
banking, customers who hold more and more varied
financial products tend to use more channels [12]. Personal
contact keeps its value beside remote channels: Durkin et al.
found that retail bank customers still gave considerable
weight to face -to-face interaction [13]. For a branch, these
findings imply that telephone, field and data-based contacts
serve different purposes and should share information.
Acquisition, customer selection and cross -selling.
Verhoef and Donkers found that retention and cross-selling
differ across the acquisition channels of a financial-services
provider [1]. Reinartz et al. show that the split of resources
between acquisition and retention affects profitability [2],
and Venkatesan and Kumar propose a lifetime -value
framework for selecting customers and allocating marketing
resources [14]. On cross -selling, Li et al. model the
sequence in which consumers adopt banking products [4],
and Kamakura et al. propose a database-marketing model to
predict which customers will buy further products [5]. These
works support targeting by customer need and value instead
of contact volume.
Personal selling, lead management and technology.
Moncrief and Marshall trace the evolution of the classic
steps of selling, from prospecting through to follow-up [15].
D’Haen and Van den Poel present a model -supported
approach to business -to-business prospect lists that aims to
produce prospects easier to convert into leads, with a
feedback loop that improves the list over time [16]. Ngai et
al. classify data-mining applications in CRM into customer
identification, attraction, retention and development [17].
Lemon and Verhoef describe customer experience as a
journey across pre -purchase, purchase and post -purchase
touchpoints [18]. Together, these studies frame acqu isition
as a staged, information -dependent process in which lead
quality and continuity matter.
1.6 Research Gap
The literature offers strong strategic accounts of CRM
and multichannel management, and quantitative models of
cross-selling and prospect selection. Most models, however,
need customer-level data, such as purchase histories or lead
outcomes, that a branch sales team may not hold in usable
form. Within the sources reviewed, the author found limited
process-level evidence on how heterogeneous front -line
activities connect in an Indian branch: calls, business -
information profiling, on -foot prospecting and institutional
outreach, all feeding one follow-up routine. This study does
not claim that no comparable work exists. It addresses the
gap in two ways. It reports what a single branch’s ordinary
records can and cannot show, and it proposes a low -
technology lead -management framework that a branch
could adopt and later test.
1.7 Research Objectives and Research Questions
The study has five objectives, which follow those of the
underlying OJT report [6]: (O1) to describe the customer
acquisition process at the branch; (O2) to analyse the roles
of telecalling, Probe42 and field-based prospecting; (O3) to
examine how cross -selling identifies additional
requirements; (O4) to examine follow -up and lead
management; and (O5) to propose practical strategies for
improving lead quality, acquisition and cross-selling.
Three research questions guide the analysis. RQ1: What
distinct roles did the documented acquisition channels play
in identifying and developing opportunities? RQ2: How did
customer-requirement identification relate to the cross -
selling opportunities observed? RQ3: What lead -
qualification and follow -up practices does the evidence
support, and how can they be organised into a usable
branch-level framework?

source report states. The exploratory element looks for
patterns in how the activities relate. The author acted as a

2.2 Research Setting and Internship Duration
The setting is the Pink City Road Branch of HDFC Bank

recorded absence on 30 June 2026 were excluded. Seven
further entries are undated summaries of exposure. This
paper therefore refers to 46 dated entries and 53 total entries.
It does not treat the seven summaries as extra working days.
2.3 Primary and Secondary Data Sources
The primary data are the author’s own internship records
as consolidated in the OJT report [6]: the date-wise activity
log, the fortnightly progress report, the workstream analysis
tables and narrative sections, and the recommendations
chapter. The secondary data are the published works cited in
this paper, which frame concepts and interpretation but
supply no findings. The study collected no questionnaire
responses, conducted no interviews and holds no customer-
level or account-level data.
2.4 Field Observation and Data Collection
The data arose during assigned sales duties, not under a
prospective research protocol. The author carried out the
calling, profiling and field work and recorded activities and
impressions in daily work notes. The OJT report states that
the log was consolidated and reorganised from those notes
[6], so the log is a reconstructed record. It lists activities and
learning points for each working day. It does not record call-
by-call outcomes, the number of Probe42 profiles reviewed,
the number of visits made or the status of individual leads.
Daily call counts appear for selected days only and are
approximate. These features limit what the analysis can
claim.
2.5 Scope of the Study
The study covers the Sales and Marketing workstreams of
one branch and its catchment over about eight weeks.
Prospects included individuals, shop owners and business
personnel, three hospitals, one school and one residential
society. The products are those listed in Section 1.3. Loan
underwriting, credit decisions, risk assessment, collections
and other back-end functions fall outside the scope because
the internship did not cover them [6]. The study does not
measure customer attitudes, satisfaction or loyalty, and it
makes no claim about HDFC Bank as a whole.

2.7 Research Ethics and Confidentiality
The study uses no customer names, account details,
identity documents, income information or credit records,
and it reports no customer -level data. Institutions are
described by type, not by name. The use of the bank’s name
and branch details in an external publication is subject to
clearance by the university and the host organisation, as
noted in the Declarations. Any future prospective study of
leads would need approval under the bank’s data -handling
and customer-contact procedures and, where customers are
surveyed, informed consent.

3.3 Probe42-Based Business Prospect Identification
Probe42 appears in 10 dated entries, beginning with an
introduction on 19 May and continuing through catchment
profiling, business profiling for Current Account, Business
Loan and OD opportunities (9 June) and repeated sourcing
until 4 July. Profiling gave the author a preliminary view of
business type, location and possible banking needs before
any approach. The report links retail businesses to Current
Account and QR or Soundbox needs, other businesses to
financing needs and salaried individuals to Personal Loans,
and it notes that prospect lists helped plan field visits instead
of approaching an area without information [6].
The report is equally clear about the limit. Profile
information indicated potential, and actual requirements
could be confirmed only through interaction. A business
might already hold a banking arrangement, lack an
immediate need or prefer another solution. The log gives no
count of profiles reviewed or businesses later approached,
and it links no conversion to Probe42. The supportable
finding is therefore about role: Probe42 helped source and
prioritise prospects, and conversation was still required to
qualify them.
3.4 Padyatra and Catchment-Based Prospecting
Field prospecting and padyatra appear in 15 dated entries.
They covered retail shops (25 May), shop owners and
business personnel (17 June), a camp-style activity (1 July)
and repeated catchment walks through early July. The report
states that face -to-face contact exposed requirements that
Personal Loan or Credit Card calls did not, notably Current
Account, Business Loan, OD and QR or Soundbox needs,
and that the nature of the business, its transactions and its
existing banking arrangements shaped which product was
relevant [6].
Direct conversation also allowed the author to observe
questions and reactions immediately. Some prospects had
no immediate requirement or already banked elsewhere,
which the report treats as a reason for follow-up and not as a
failure. The 18 June entry records a Personal Loan
requirement of about INR 10 lakh identified during field
prospecting. It is an opportunity: the log does not record its
outcome. The log gives no count of visits or contacts, so the
paper cannot compare the yield of field work with that of
calling.

4. DISCUSSION
4.1 Interpretation of Findings
Complementary channels. The findings fit the
multichannel view that channels should be coordinated and
share customer information [11]. Calling created the widest
contact surface. Probe42 narrowed the field before contact.
Field visits and institutional outreach added context that a
short call could not supply, which echoes the continuing
value of personal interaction in banking [13]. The roles are
complementary in practice, but this study cannot say which
channel performed best. The channel through which a
customer is acquired may matter for later retention and
cross-buying [1], so the lack of channel-tagged outcomes is
a real gap and not a minor omission.
Lead quality over activity volume. Call counts of 30 to
60-plus show effort, yet the log holds no figure for relevant
prospects found. Research on resource allocation and
customer selection argues that profit depends on whom a
firm acquires and how it spends, not only on how much

predict the next product [5], but a branch without such data
relies on listening. The practical consequence is that
requirement assessment is the junction stage between
acquisition and cross -selling. If it is skipped, cross -selling
becomes product pushing. If it is recorded, the same
information serves both purposes.
Relationship orientation. Trust and commitment sustain
exchange relationships [7], and CRM depends on connected
processes more than on tools [8], [9]. The society proposal
illustrates the point. It reached a reported outcome after
coordination and continued follow -up with several
stakeholders, though one case cannot show cause. The
attention the report gives to eligibility, charges and customer
choice also shows that, in financial services, relevance and
clarity are part of the sales process and not an extra.
Follow-up and lead development. The classic steps of
selling end with follow -up [15], and the customer -journey
view treats post -contact touchpoints as part of the
experience [18]. The report’s evidence agrees: leads often
needed more than one interaction, and business, institutional
and society leads needed the most. Yet follow -up was the
least formally recorded stage. Where a prospect passes from
a Probe42 shortlist to a call and then to a field meeting, only
a shared record can carry the requirement across.
Operational challenges. Four challenges stand out.
Several activities ran on the same day, so a lead cannot be
assigned to one channel. Calls, profiles, visits and proposals
are different units, so no common denominator exists.
Banking confidentiality limited the data available. Long -
cycle opportunities extend beyond a short placement. These
are features of branch -level acquisition in general, not
defects of one internship, and they explain why the
framework below begins with definitions and records.
4.2 Theoretical Contribution
The study makes four modest contributions. First, it
applies an explicit evidence ladder (activity, observation,
reported outcome, interpretation) to practitioner records,
which keeps opportunities from being read as conversions.
Second, it separates sourced prospects from qualified leads,
a distinction the acquisition and selling literature implies
[15], [16] but rarely shows in front -line records. Third, it
identifies requirement assessment as the junction at which
acquisition and cross -selling research meet in branch
practice. Fourth, it shows that the absence of a common
denominator is itself a finding, one that explains why
branch-level channel comparisons are often unreliable. The
study builds no causal theory. Following case -study logic
[20], it offers propositions for later testing.
4.3 Managerial Implications
Six implications follow. (1) Define lead stages in writing,
so that a contact, a qualified lead, an application and a
completed outcome are different things. (2) Record the
source and the next action for every lead, with an owner and
a date. (3) Qualify before cross -selling: assess the primary
need, then raise an additional product only if a further need
and eligibility appear, and explain charges and terms first.
(4) Segment the catchment into retail, business and
institutional groups and plan field days by segment, using
business-profile data to prepare visits. (5) For institutions
and societies, keep a separate list that names the decision -
maker, the stakeholders and the stage. (6) Review quality
indicators with volume: needs verified, qualified leads,
follow-ups done and opportunities converted, beside calls
and visits. None of these requires new technology. All
depend on approved bank procedures for customer contact,
consent and product suitability.
4.4 Proposed Customer Acquisition and Lead
Optimisation Framework
The study proposes a Need -Based Customer Acquisition
and Lead Optimisation Framework (Fig. 1). It is a
conceptual and managerial framework derived from the
documented observations and the OJT report’s
recommendations [6]. It is not a system that was built, run or
tested during the internship, and no algorithm, automated
platform or predictive model was used. Its originality lies in
the way it joins components that are individually
established: structured prospect identification, requirement-
first qualification, need -based product matching and
scheduled follow-up in a single lead record.

5. CONCLUSION
This study examined customer acquisition and cross -
selling at one retail bank branch through a descriptive, field-
based case drawn from an internship record. Regarding O1
and O2, the evidence shows that telecalling, Probe42
profiling, padyatra and institutional outreach played
different and complementary roles: reach, targeting, context
and group -level access. Regarding O3, cross -selling
opportunities arose from identified requirements, which
makes requirement assessment the link between acquisition
and cross -selling. Regarding O4, follow -up connected the
channels but was the least formally recorded stage.
