ABSTRACT
Academic research in marketing and in business and management has been criticized for its lack of relevance for
practice, with calls for stronger interaction between academia and practice, to improve impact and benefit to the
world. The practical or managerial implications included in journal articles are important in bridging this divide.
Yet the role of these implications in ensuring academic research has wider economic and social impact is
significantly under-researched. This article addresses this gap by developing a generalizable conceptual frame-
work for examining the practical implications associated with academic research findings. A systematic literature
review that focuses on digital marketing is used to assess the usefulness of this practical implications framework.
Insights are provided into the dimensions that underpin how effectively these practical implications bridge the
theory-practice divide and support economic and social impact. Multilevel archetypes of practical implications
are developed to guide best practice in developing and reporting practical implications for researchers, journal
reviewers, editors and publishers.
1. Introduction
Academic research in marketing and in business and management
has been criticized for its lack of relevance, with calls for stronger
interaction between academia and practice to improve marketing’s
impact and benefit to the world (Chandy, Johar, Moorman, & Roberts,
2021; Deighton, Mela, & Moorman, 2021). This discussion resides
within wider debates on how the theory-practice divide in marketing
can be bridged (Dziubaniuk et al., 2021; Jedidi, Schmitt, Ben Sliman, &
Li, 2021; Kriz, Nailer, Jansen, & Potocnjak-Oxman, 2021; Pedersen,
Ellegaard, & Kragh, 2020; Van der Borgh, Sch¨afers, Lindgreen, & Di
Benedetto, 2022; Van der Borgh, Xu, & Sikkenk, 2020), and on achieving
research impact, which is “the good that researchers can do to the world”
(Reed, 2018, p. 15). The practical or managerial implications included
in journal articles are one way to translate academic research findings
into relevant knowledge for practice (Baraldi, La Rocca, & Perna, 2014).
Yet how these practical implications are conceptualized and their spe-
cific role in bridging the theory-practice divide are under-researched.
This article addresses this gap through an empirical examination of
how practical implications are discussed within industrial marketing
journals. We develop a novel conceptual framework of the practical
implications that we then apply using a systematic review of the three
main industrial marketing journals. This practical implications frame-
work can be used to support good practice in presenting practical im-
plications within marketing journals and more broadly in business and
management.
We follow a multistage process. We begin by drawing on the existing
marketing, business and management literature to develop a general-
izable conceptual framework for examining the practical implications
within academic journals. Next, we undertake a systematic literature
review within the industrial marketing field to apply and assess the
conceptual framework’s usefulness. For illustrative purposes, we focus
our review on digital marketing within the industrial context. We chose
this topic because it provides a coherent sample frame and is an inter-
esting field attracting a substantial number of articles within industrial
and B2B marketing journals. This topic also provides a ‘tough test’ of the
framework, as digital marketing research in industrial marketing set-
tings deals with complex and demanding marketing situations, where
the relationship between theory and practice can be hard to harmonize
(Gummesson, 2014). Using insights from this analysis about the re-
lationships between research relevance and wider economic and social
impact, we develop multilevel archetypes of practical implications to

Several contributions to knowledge are made. First, we address calls

2. Conceptual framework
Academic research in marketing and in business and management
has been criticized for its lack of relevance, with calls for greater
interaction between academia and practice to improve marketing’s
impact and benefit to the world (Chandy et al., 2021; Deighton et al.,
2021). This debate resides within a wider conversation about the need to
bridge the theory-practice divide in marketing (Dziubaniuk et al., 2021;
Jedidi et al., 2021; Kriz et al., 2021; Pedersen et al., 2020; Van der Borgh
et al., 2020; Van der Borgh et al., 2022), and alongside a growing in-
terest in research’s role in enhancing economic and social outcomes for
businesses and other stakeholders. In this section, we consider the
bridging role of practical implications in narrowing the theory-practice
divide and in supporting the translation of relevant academic research
into knowledge that will have an impact on practice and support the
creation of societal value.
2.1. Practical implications and the theory-practice divide
Various reasons have been given to explain the theory-practice
divide. These include major differences in academic and practitioner
priorities concerning career goals, the nature of the knowledge sought or
prioritized, and the type of language used (Lindgreen, Di Benedetto,
Brodie, & Naud´e, 2020). The substance of academic research itself also
creates challenges when applying the findings to managerial problems,
as do the suitability of academic articles for practitioners and the way
findings are presented within them (Dziubaniuk et al., 2021). Other
difficulties include instances of poor connection between research and
practice (Kriz et al., 2021; Van der Borgh et al., 2022), and the fact that
market actors use different lenses to view and enact marketing realities
(Wieland, Nariswari, & Akaka, 2021).
Solutions proposed to address these problems include a Relevance to
Marketing (R2M) Index, which focuses on the topic and timeliness of
academic research for marketing practice (Jedidi et al., 2021). Industrial
marketing researchers have also investigated the praxis of interorgani-
zational B2B marketing practices (Pedersen et al., 2020), while
increasing their calls for rigorous research that fulfils both societal and
business and needs (Kriz et al., 2021) and tackles the bigger challenges
facing the world (Lilien & Wuyts, 2022). A design science approach has
also been introduced to demonstrate how industrial marketing scholars
can rigorously address practical problems (Van der Borgh et al., 2020).
Recent systematic literature reviews that address these issues have
examined the social impact of inter-organizational relationships (e.g.,
Siemieniako, Mitręga, & Kubacki, 2022) and considered the alignment
of B2B marketing scholarship with the UN sustainable development
goals (SDGs) (e.g., Voola, Bandyopadhyay, Voola, Ray, & Carlson,
2022). The readability and translation effectiveness of B2B marketing
articles (Dziubaniuk et al., 2021) have also been considered. Meanwhile,
Wieland et al. (2021) have called for a reconceptualization of manage-
rial relevance, in which marketing academics, practitioners and other
market actors reconsider the emphasis on theorizing and focus more on
how theories are created. More recently, Van der Borgh et al. (2022)
introduced a new type of academic-practitioner s that aim to bridge the
rigor-relevance gap by ensuring “end-to-end collaboration between aca-
demics and practitioners” (p. A1).
Overcoming communication problems between academics and
practitioners is a commonly recurring theme in the theory-practice
debate, with the need to understand the differences between these
groups considered a vital step (Lindgreen et al., 2020). Given that “the
goal of scholarly research should be to facilitate and encourage communi-
cation between academia and practice” (Lindgreen & Di Benedetto, 2017,
p. 1), a main premise of our study is that the effective communication of
practical implications within journal articles is crucial. However, despite
the intensification of interest in research impact and even though many
business and management journals encourage authors to provide prac-
tical implications (see Table 2, p. 13), research on this issue remains
fragmented and scarce (Baraldi et al., 2014; Salminen, Oinonen, &
Haimala, 2014).
Examining the attention that academic journals devote to practical
implications is revealing. Given the ongoing interest in research rele-
vance, we might expect these practical implications to be prominently
located within journals. Yet typically we find them languishing towards
the end of articles, as if ‘afterthoughts’, with academic or theoretical
contributions featured earlier and more visibly (Nicholson, LaPlaca, Al-
Abdin, Breese, & Khan, 2018). As Lindgreen and Di Benedetto (2018, p.
2) comment, “too often, the ‘managerial implications’ section of theoretical
articles consist of simple rewording of the results section and little else; it
appears to be a section bolted on after the fact”. Lindgreen et al. (2020)
regard this prioritization of theoretical contribution as a point of
disconnect between academics and practitioners, which risks de-
emphasizing the practical relevance of research. We argue that prac-
tical implications provide a means to bridge the theory-practice divide
and address this disconnect.
To demonstrate the importance of practical implications and as a
precursor to our analysis, we now discuss the related and interrelated
concepts of research relevance and impact.
2.2. Relevance, stakeholders and practical implications
Relevance has received extensive attention in the management
literature. In marketing, Jaworski (2011, p. 212) defines managerial
relevance as “the degree to which a specific manager in an organization
perceives academic knowledge to aid his or her job-related thoughts or actions
in pursuit of organizational goals”; indicating that relevant knowledge
must be suitable for the needs and roles of specific managers. In a Journal
of Business & Industrial Marketing special issue that explored how the gap

between managers and researchers could be bridged (Åge & Cederlund,
2014), two classification schemes for practical implications were
included, one organized according to managerial usability (Baraldi
et al., 2014) and a second which used Jaworski’s (2011) framework for
role-relevant research to classify implication types (Salminen et al.,
2014). Here, we note an important distinction between research rele-
vance and impact, where the former reflects the topicality of research
content to practice (Jedidi et al., 2021), and “describes the outcome of the
research production process and precedes any company action regarding this
outcome” (Wiegand, Becker, Imschloss, & Reinartz, 2020, p. 6), while the
latter refers to the tangible change or outcome that occurs. Such impact
might range from simple changes in managers’ awareness about an issue
to more fundamental changes to how they operate. Thus, research might
be relevant to practitioners both as direct applications to solve problems
and through its potential to improve decision making (Wiegand et al.,
2020).
While Jaworski’s (2011) conceptualization has proven popular in
viewing relevance from the perspectives of practicing managers, rele-
vance for a wider range of organizations or stakeholders has also been
considered. This development is important because it identifies the po-
tential to extend how we think about the scale and scope of practical
implications within journal articles. For example, Wiegand et al. (2020)
argue for managerially relevant research at the level of groups of or-
ganizations to enable industry practice to be affected; while Kohli and
Haenlein (2021) operationalize relevance according to its usefulness to
practice, in terms of the number, types and status of stakeholders, and
the magnitude of expected change. Although the relevance debate has
focused primarily on practicing managers, organizations and sectors,
there is growing interest in broadening its applicability to a wider range
of societal stakeholders. Thus, Lindgreen et al. (2021) introduce the
term ‘relevance for society’ and call for industrial marketing research
which has greater societal value, is transdisciplinary, socially account-
able, and applicable.
On the question of which societal stakeholders to consider, Chandy
et al. (2021) regard firms, customers, employees, suppliers, commu-
nities, as well as the world at large, as important stakeholders; while
Lindgreen et al. (2020) suggest that society, education and practitioners
should be considered. While acknowledging the importance of business
practitioners, Dziubaniuk et al. (2021) expand these stakeholder types to
include consultants, governmental and non-governmental organiza-
tions, students, and educators. In a similar vein, Voola et al. (2022) refer
to governments, firms, marketing practitioners and students as research
stakeholders, with Kriz et al. (2021) grouping stakeholder into three
types: researchers; wider society, such as social stakeholders (govern-
ments, regional communities, taxpayers and employees); and
practitioners.
In considering the role of practical implications within the research
relevance debate, we take a broad view of stakeholder groups that
extend beyond academic scholars and practicing managers. On this
basis, we see managerial relevance as a subset of wider practical rele-
vance. Reflecting the rising interest in wider societal impact, we argue
that the practical relevance of scientific articles should extend beyond
practicing managers to include industries, customers, policy makers
such as governmental and non-governmental organizations, and wider
society. Accordingly, we use the term ‘practitioners’ to include stake-
holders from any kind of organization or body, covering the commercial,
public and third sectors, e.g., managers, policy makers, agents, consul-
tants and charity workers.
2.3. Impact, societal value and practical implications
Discussions about research relevance and societal value connect with
the interest in research impact within business and management, which
has been defined as “the good that researchers can do to the world” (Reed,
2018, p. 15). Although no commonly accepted definition of social
impact exists in marketing (Siemieniako et al., 2022), we regard impact
as sitting within the higher-level concept of societal value. Accordingly,
societal value is made up of societal relevance, which reflects the in-
terest of what is being researched to others; and societal impact, which is
research’s value in monetary, quantitative or qualitative terms
(Lindgreen, Di Benedetto, Clarke, et al., 2021).
Siemieniako et al. (2022, p. 191) view social impact as involving
“significant social changes that are commonly perceived as positive or
negative and that go beyond the internal boundaries of a single company”.
Thus, impact involves the translation of academic research into benefits
for individuals, organizations, and/or nations (Lindgreen, Di Benedetto,
Clarke, et al., 2021). There is then a growing expectation for research to
create impact across a broader range of stakeholders, such that its effects
are felt across the economy, health, environment and culture (e.g.,
Siemieniako et al., 2022; Voola et al., 2022).
The connection between relevance, societal value and impact em-
phasizes the move away from focusing on practicing managers as the
predominant key stakeholders, to impact involving a wider range of
societal groups. This breadth is evident in the UK’s The Economic and
Social Research Council (ESRC) (2022) definition of research impact,
“…as the demonstrable contribution that excellent research makes to society
and the economy”; and in Reed et al.’s (2021, p. 3) reference to,
“demonstrable and/or perceptible benefits to individuals, groups, organisa-
tions and society (including human and non-human entities in the present and
in the future)”. Such distinction between academic contribution and
wider economic and societal impact is important because it signals the
role of practical implications in translating academic findings for a va-
riety of non-academic stakeholders.
This interest in impactful outcomes for different stakeholders un-
derlines the need for practical implications to bridge the theory-practice
divide and support more effective translation of relevant academic
knowledge into usable knowledge for practice (Baraldi et al., 2014). To
more closely examine this bridging role, it is necessary to navigate the
plethora of terms academic journals use to describe these implications.
At its simplest, an implication is defined as the “possible effect or result of
an action or a decision” (Oxford Learner’s Dictionaries, 2023). However,
most journals qualify the nature of the implications being claimed, as in
‘practical contributions’, ‘practical implications’, ‘managerial implica-
tions’, ‘managerial contributions’, and even ‘impact’. For several rea-
sons, we adopt the overarching term ‘practical implications’ here,
deliberately avoiding the term ‘contribution’, which has been more
generally associated with academic contribution (e.g., Nicholson et al.,
2018). We also use ‘practical implications’ as our umbrella term because
it encompasses ‘managerial implications’ for practicing managers but
also includes implications for other stakeholder groups. Accordingly, we
view practical implications as a translational bridge between relevant
research and the achievement of practical outcomes that lead to impact.
Thus, practical implications are involved in translating academic
research findings into usable knowledge for managers, organizations,
and other societal stakeholders, to enable social or economic impact (see
Fig. 1 below).
We argue that research in academic articles should have relevance
beyond academic scholars and practicing managers, and therefore our
systematic review adopts Lindgreen, Di Benedetto, Clarke, et al.’s
(2021) wider view of societal impact stakeholders. As a precursor to this
systematic review, we introduce our conceptual framework. This prac-
tical implications framework, which builds on the existing body of work
(Baraldi et al., 2014; Jaworski, 2011; Salminen et al., 2014 and Wiegand
et al., 2020), is the basis for a nuanced examination of the scope and
characteristics of the practical implications included within industrial
marketing journals.

Although conceptual frameworks are essential to building academic
knowledge, their failure to bridge theory and practice has been criticized
(Lindgreen, Di Benedetto, Brodie, & Jaakkola, 2021). We address this

organized around the four meta categories, ‘What’, ‘Why’, ‘How’ and
‘When’, proposed by Lindgreen, Di Benedetto, Brodie, and Jaakkola

25 separate measures drawn from the work of Baraldi et al. (2014),
Jaworski (2011), Salminen et al. (2014) and Wiegand et al. (2020) (see
Table 1). Thus, for the ‘What’ meta category, we used two dimensions to
adapt the types of overall knowledge and managerially relevant
knowledge measures from Jaworski (2011), which were also adopted by
Salminen et al. (2014) and Baraldi et al. (2014). For the ‘Why’ meta
category, we used Wiegand et al.’s (2020) study, which considers why
managers and other stakeholders should use scientific research. The
‘How’ meta category draws on dimensions and concepts from Baraldi
et al.’s (2014) study of managerial implications, which resulted in nine
dimensions grouped into three larger groups. For the ‘Who’ meta cate-
gory, we used the most common stakeholder types identified in the
business and management literature.
Our study uses these dimensions and measures to examine the
characteristics of practical implications that enable them to act as a
translational bridge between relevance and impact, rather than
providing an explicit numerical scale of relevance or impact. Therefore,
our main proposition is that for academic research to deliver societal
value, practical implications should act as a translational bridge be-
tween relevance and impact. To ensure practical implications act as a
translational bridge, we suggest the following four questions need to be
addressed when developing these implications: What type of knowledge
do implications deliver? Why should practitioners use these implica-
tions? How are these implications presented? Who are the targeted
practitioners for these implications? To address these questions, we use
the meta categories, dimensions and measures, as included in the
practical implications framework in Fig. 2, to systematically review and
analyze the practical implications content within the main three in-
dustrial marketing journals as explained in the methodology.
4. Methodology
The study unfolded in four stages. First, the comprehensive and
consolidated conceptual framework was developed by reviewing the
literature on practical implications, relevance and impact. Through this
abductive phase, dimensions and measures were identified for the
conceptual framework for use and elaboration in the subsequent phase.
Second, a systematic literature review of the top three industrial mar-
keting journals considered the practical implications content within
articles focused on digital marketing in the industrial marketing context.
Third, the findings were synthesized into multilevel practical implica-
tions archetypes, that indicate best practice in developing and reporting
more effective and offer action-oriented practical implications for re-
searchers, reviewers, at operational level and editors with publishers at
strategic level. Fourth, following guidance from design science research
(Van der Borgh et al., 2020; Van der Borgh et al., 2022), and more
specifically design science research add-on (Van Aken et al., 2016), we
field test our main proposition about the role of practical implications as
a translational bridge, and check the applicability of our recommenda-
tions for practice (alpha testing) and their wider suitability for use
within the business and management discipline (beta testing).
The systematic literature review followed a domain-based approach
to identify, select and analyze relevant articles (Palmatier, Houston, &
Hulland, 2018). Rigorous systematic review procedures were adopted to
ensure the quality of the study (Hulland & Houston, 2020; Snyder, 2019;
Tranfield, Denyer, & Smart, 2003). The selected approach involved
planning, conducting and reporting the review (Tranfield et al., 2003).
How the review was planned and conducted is shown in Fig. 3, with the
reporting and dissemination of findings presented in the following
section.
As a part of the planning, critical decisions were made concerning the
target journals, timescale and search strategy (Paul & Criado, 2020;
Snyder, 2019; Tranfield et al., 2003). The selected scientific journals
were Industrial Marketing Management (IMM), Journal of Business and
Industrial Marketing (JBIM) and Journal of Business-to-Business Marketing
(JBBM), each of which allowed a coherent frame of reference, having
previously been used in other B2B systematic reviews (e.g., Nicholson
et al., 2018 and Piekkari, Plakoyiannaki, & Welch, 2010). These are also
the highest rated industrial marketing journals according to the Char-
tered Association of Business Schools Academic Journal Guide (AJG), a
widely used UK rating scheme of business and management journals. Of
these journals, IMM was the first dedicated to industrial marketing
(Lindgreen, Di Benedetto, Brodie, & Jaakkola, 2021) and is the most

Decisions about the search strategy and inclusion and exclusion
criteria are crucial to ensuring a systematic review’s quality and rigor
(Snyder, 2019; Tranfield et al., 2003). The Web of Science Core Collection
database, which covers all three journals, was searched. The keyword
selection was guided to ensure all possible digital and social media
marketing articles within industrial marketing were identified by the
search (Snyder, 2019; Tranfield et al., 2003). To ensure the necessary
breadth, the Boolean search combination searched for “digital market-
ing” OR “social media”, identified in all articles where these phrases
appeared, anywhere within the text. This search yielded 226 articles in
total, the results of which were organized in order of relevance to the
keywords. The search results, including details of the authors, article
title, source, author keyword, abstract, publication date and DOI link,
were exported into an Excel spreadsheet for further scrutiny.
Next, a detailed screening of all exported articles took place, using
inclusion and exclusion criteria to rigorously assess suitability (Snyder,
2019; Tranfield et al., 2003). First, all articles other than research arti-
cles, such as review articles, editorials, opinions, and commentaries
were excluded, since these articles primarily target academic re-
searchers, rather than practitioners. This resulted in 22 articles being
excluded. The remaining 204 articles were then screened using the in-
clusion and exclusion criteria. The first suitability criterion was to
include articles which predominantly focused on digital marketing and/
or social media in the industrial marketing context. This suitability was
assessed by reviewing the article title, keywords and abstract. Among
those excluded at this point were articles that mentioned using digital as
part of a wider set of marketing practices, but which were not deemed
sufficiently focused on the core topic area. Those excluded included
articles covering big data and big data analytics, digital transformation,
business models, e-marketplace, crowdsourcing and business networks.
The second criterion for inclusion was the need for articles to include
practical implications, whether as a separate section or as a part of other
sections, such as discussion or conclusion.
Both researchers took part in the screening process. Initially, we
separately screened 20 exported articles against the inclusion and
exclusion criteria, comparing the results to ensure consistency.
Following a period of discussion and reflection, the lead researcher then
screened the remaining 182 articles. 13 articles for which suitability was
more difficult to assess were then discussed by both researchers until
agreement was reached. To ensure inter-rater agreement across the
article pool, the second researcher then reviewed 37 of the excluded
articles, selecting every third article to ensure further consistency in the
application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Initially, the two
researchers disagreed about one excluded article (interrater agreement
of 0.97), which after discussion they decided to include. Following this
screening process, 102 articles were included for further analysis.

5. Findings
5.1. Findings summary
The systematic literature review provides interesting insights, pat-
terns and trends regarding the discussion of practical implications. Fig. 4
summarizes the number of digital marketing articles published annually
in each journal from 2011 to 2023. More than half (61) were published
within IMM, suggesting it is the leading publisher for this subject matter.
While the first study was published in 2011, no other B2B digital and/or
social media marketing articles were found between 2011 and 2013.
JBIM was second in the number of published digital marketing articles,
with only half of those found in IMM (34). JBBM published only three
digital marketing articles in 2019 and 2021, and one in 2023. Across the
journals, 2015 and 2016 saw a steady and increasing interest in digital
and/or social media marketing research; and from 2019 onwards, there
have been between 11 and 20 articles per year.
Over half of the reviewed studies (60) used a quantitative research
design, while a third (36) were qualitative. IMM included twice as many
quantitative studies as qualitative, while JBIM had similar numbers of
each. The vast majority of studies in JBBM were quantitative (3). Only
six of the analyzed studies adopted a mixed methods approach.
Table 3 summarizes the key findings regarding the coded 102 articles
and their practical implications. The findings reveal that practical im-
plications were discussed and integrated into articles in very different
ways. We found examples of what we consider to be very good practice,
in which the discussion of the implications was very specific, with clear
details provided as to the actions managers and other stakeholders
needed to take to improve their practice (e.g., Jarvinen and Karjaluoto,
2015). Some articles included tables or figures within their practical
implications to describe issues, such as social media tensions and the
specific actions managers could take to overcome them (e.g., Luo et al.,
2021), while others developed tools or matrices regarding social media
strategies and actions (e.g., Zhang and Du, 2021). In many other cases,
the articles lacked clarity and gave few of the details needed for
actionable practical implications, even though the authors often claimed
their articles provided clear managerial guidance (e.g., Ancillai et al.,
2019; Silva et al., 2020). Others focused on conceptual model and
hypothesis testing as a part of their practical implications (e.g., Nguyen,
2017). The sections below, which provide more details of the analysis,
are organized according to the conceptual framework categories and
dimensions.
5.2. ‘What’ category
5.2.1. Knowledge type
The analysis showed that 94 % of the analyzed practical implications
focused on conceptual research and specific concepts, such as social
media in sales (e.g., Guesalaga, 2016; Agnihotri, 2016). Typically, these
implications started with phrases such as, ‘managers should be aware of’
or ‘our findings provide insights to B2B SME managers’. Other conceptually
oriented implications presented the conceptualization as the implica-
tion, such as “the developed conceptualization of social selling provides
detailed insights into how salespeople can strategically leverage social media
for selling” (Terho et al., 2022).
Only six articles provided any kind of tool for managers, with just
two providing implications for a specific managerial process. Some of
the manuscripts with tools were among those we regarded as showing
good practice. One example (Jarvinen and Karjaluoto, 2015), provided a
detailed discussion of the process managers should adopt and use in
relation to web analytics. Another (Luo et al., 2021), presented detailed
implications in table format, which illustrated social media related
tensions and proposed specific actions to overcome them; while a third
(Zhang and Du, 2020) developed a matrix to illustrate four types of
social media strategies for B2B firms. Articles that provided tools tended
to be specific in their implications, as typified in this example: “Our
findings provide practical managerial implications for social media marketers
to execute effective social media marketing content strategy” (Kwon et al.,
2022).
Several articles referred to academic conceptual frameworks and/or
hypotheses, suggesting authors considered these were associated with
useful practical implications. For example, Meire et al. (2022) included
the following statement: “Our conceptual framework of similarity deepened
our knowledge of the creation of compelling social media posts while also
controlling for previous findings in this area”. However, in some cases au-
thors claimed to provide detailed guidance to managers but instead
offered rather abstract measures that might be difficult to apply in
practice. In instances where conceptual frameworks or models from
elsewhere in the manuscript were mentioned, but not fully explained,
we categorized the practical implications as of the ‘conceptual research’
knowledge type. One example (Lacoste, 2016) developed a customer
engagement model, which the authors presented as a useful tool for key

5.2.2. Practically relevant knowledge type
In general, the results demonstrate a focus on present action and/or
thinking, with only two articles that specifically looked to the future.
While 47 % of articles were categorized as including present action, 33
% as present thinking, and 18 % combined both these elements. Prac-
tical implications categorized as present action, typically included the
following kinds of phrases, suggesting they: ‘offer guidance to B2B mar-
keters’ or ‘provide practical managerial implications… to execute’ or indi-
cated that ‘managers must plan a systematic process’. Meanwhile articles
with a present thinking orientation used phrases such as: ‘our findings
provide insights’ or ‘we suggest that B2B companies rethink’ or ‘managers
should be aware of’.
Future action was found in only two articles. In one of these (Sia-
magka et al., 2015), the implications were aimed at “B2B organizations
that do not currently use social media, but plan to invest in the near future”.
The other (Eid, Abdelmoety and Agag, 2019), included very long im-
plications, the first part of which related to future thinking for “B2B
organizations that do not currently use social media, but plan to invest in the
near future”; while the second part involved present actions to help firms
already using it “…to maximize their performance”. Overall, these find-
ings suggest significant underrepresentation of implications that focus
on future thinking or actions.
5.3. ‘Why’ meta category
5.3.1. Research type
Of the analyzed articles, 92 % were categorized as educational
research because they aimed to enhance practitioners’ general under-
standing of issues. For example, the implications focused on under-
standing social media and examined social media’s use in areas such as
increasing sales (Ancillai et al., 2019); brand innovation (Nguyen et al.,
2015); and to support marketing during the pandemic (Hu et al., 2022).
Only eight articles were of the problem-solving type, six of which pro-
vided some kind of managerial tool. Examples included a process to
adopt and use web analytics (Jarvinen and Karjaluoto, 2015), actions for
overcoming social media tensions linked with specific managerial
problems (Luo et al., 2021), and detailed guidance on social media
content for branding strategy, message appeals, selling strategy and
information search (Swani et al., 2017).
We noted that most implications categorized within the ‘What’
knowledge type (83 articles) as conceptual, were also of the educational
research type in the ‘Why’ meta category. Those which provided tools
were more likely to be of a problem-solving nature. Perhaps surpris-
ingly, no articles were categorized as inspirational, which would have
required authors to provide insights to stimulate practitioners to gain
new ideas and perspectives and understand future trends and issues.

5.4.2. Location
The targeting of implications at practicing managers and other
stakeholders could be undermined if content is difficult to locate or in-
tegrated into sections that explicitly target an academic audience. There
was a separate section for practical implications in 14 % of articles (e.g.,
Itani et al., 2021; Brink, 2017; Silva et al., 2020). Usually, these were
located towards the end of the article. Findings reveal that 21 articles
mentioned implications within the conclusion (e.g., Diba et al., 2019;
Cowan et al., 2022), while 42 included them as part of the discussion (e.
g., Rose et al., 2021; Kwon et al., 2022). Just five articles combined the
discussion and conclusions or the discussion and implications (e.g.,
Barac et al., 2017; Keinanen and Kuivalainen, 2015), while a very few
combined the conclusion and implications. These sections were vari-
ously labelled as: ‘theoretical and managerial implications’, ‘integrating and
discussing the findings’, ‘implications, limitations and future direction’, and
‘research contributions, limitations and future research’.
Where practical implications were located seemed indicative of their
perceived importance. Some articles combined the managerial impli-
cations with the academic contributions in the discussion section,
implying that both were important. However, in others, the practical
implications appeared as part of the limitations and future research
section, perhaps indicating they were of less value than the theoretical
contributions, which were more prominently featured within the dis-
cussion. In some cases, the theoretical and practical implications
appeared sequentially in the discussion or conclusion, indicating that
both were of equal importance. Yet others, featured the practical im-
plications less prominently within the very last section. The practical
implications were positioned before the theoretical contributions in only
two cases (Brink, 2017; Kooli et al., 2019).
5.4.3. Length
The length of the practical implications’ discussion varied from just
83 words (Wang et al., 2016) to 1652 words (Itani et al., 2021), with an
average of 452 words. Only four articles (e.g., Huotari et al., 2015; Nath
et al., 2019) discussed these issues in 100 words or less, with just three
(e.g., McShane et al., 2019; Hollebeek, 2019) having more than 1000
words. Some implications warranted less than a single paragraph, while
others spanned several pages of the manuscript. Even very short impli-
cations of 173 words could include clear actions (e.g., Hsiao et al.,
2020), thus, there was no indication that longer practical implications
were more action and practice orientated. Sometimes other factors
contributed to increasing the length. For example, the article with the
longest practical implications content (McShane et al., 2019), included
specific and detailed actions to B2B social media managers, but also
linked these recommendations back to academic contributions and
identified future research avenues. There were examples of short and
long practical implications across all three journals, with no relationship
found to the stated editorial guidelines or the manuscript length
requirements.
5.4.4. References
Just over one third (36%) of articles did not include references as a
part of their practical implications, while two thirds (64%) did. The
number of included references varied from one to 26. In 29 articles, just
one or two references were used (e.g., Rose et al., 2021; Brink, 2017),
four articles included three references (e.g., Karjaluoto et al., 2015;
Cowan et al., 2022), seven articles had four (e.g., Swani et al., 2017;
Itani et al., 2017), six articles had five (e.g., Agnihotri et al., 2016; Zhang
and Du, 2020), four articles had six (e. g., Luo et al., 2021; Quinton and
Wilson, 2016), and four articles had seven (e.g., Swani et al., 2014;
Cheng et al., 2021). Nine articles had between eight (e.g. Hu, 2022) and
twelve references (e.g., Serbetcioglu and Gocer, 2020), and one article
each 17 (Barac et al., 2017) and 24 (Itani et al., 2021) references.
Some of the practical implications, especially those that were more
academic, included larger numbers of references. The example (Itani
et al., 2021) with the largest number of references (24) was also the
longest practical implications section. Where academic references were
used, the purpose often seemed to be to justify, defend or argue more
convincingly for the recommendations being made. In other cases (e.g.,
Agnihotri et al., 2016), articles used practitioner references in their
implications to provide extra explanation of their meaning for practice
and practitioners. In one unusual case (Quinton and Wilson, 2016),
references were included in the practical implications section but not in
the theoretical contributions.
5.4.5. Similar or different to academic contributions
This dimension captured whether there was a connection or overlap
between the academic contributions and the practical implications.
Given academics and practitioners have fundamentally different prior-
ities, we would expect differences between the academic contributions
and practical implications. The reality was less straightforward, as the
extent of integration between the two and the degree to which the
practical implications built on the academic contributions emerged as
more important than whether they were similar or different per se.
Careful probing of the content was required to achieve this
understanding.
We found evidence of confusion about the nature and purpose of
practical implications within the reviewed articles. Some implications
were very academic in style, often summarizing or repeating the aca-
demic contributions, albeit using slightly different language. This might
indicate poor understanding among researchers about what practical
implications should deliver or how they should be presented. In one
example (Robertson et al., 2019), which did not include a separate
theoretical contribution section, there was instead an ‘implications for
business marketing practice’ section, which content focused primarily
on academic contribution and included academic references. Similarly,
Nguyen et al. (2015) included the results of hypotheses testing within
their practical implications, using the following rather academic
description of the study’s contribution: “this study is the first to develop a
conceptual model examining the relationships between knowledge acquisition
from social media, market orientation (proactive and reactive), social media
strategic capability, and brand innovation strategy on social media in a single
study”.
Some articles (e.g., Chuang, 2020; Lashgari et al., 2018) presented
the practical implications in two parts, where the first summarized the
conceptual contribution and the second detailed the implications for
practice. Others (e.g., Ancillai et al., 2019; Juntunen et al., 2020; Terho
et al., 2022) claimed to offer detailed guidance and advice for managers,
yet the content lacked detail and was rather generic and academically
focused. For instance, Upreti et al. (2021) suggested their findings would
“serve as a practical tool” for B2B marketers, despite making relatively
little attempt to explain or translate them for practitioners.
The practical implications were substantially different to the aca-
demic contributions in only 19 articles. The remainder were rather
scientific and theoretically oriented, and not obviously designed with
managers and practitioners in mind. Seven articles did not have a
separate ‘theoretical contributions’ section, meaning it was not possible to
capture the similarities between the practical implications and theo-
retical contributions.
In one interesting example (Rose et al., 2021), the practical impli-
cations were connected to the academic contribution and linked to the
model items being tested through the article’s quantitative methodol-
ogy, but then the implications were fully explained and translated into
suggested marketing practice. Another example (Luo et al., 2021)
identified social media tensions as the conceptual contribution and then
proposed practical solutions to each these challenges. While similar
practical and academic contributions were provided by Chaker et al.

5.4.6. Actions or concepts orientation
This dimension considered whether the implications involved di-
rection for practitioners’ actions or emphasized conceptual under-
standing and ideas. Two thirds (66 %) of the reviewed practical
implications focused on concepts and were ideas oriented (e.g., Duncan
et al., 2019; Kumar and Srivastava, 2022; De Zubielqui and Jones,
2022), while only one third provided detailed actions (e.g., Agnihotri
et al., 2016; McShane et al., 2019; Guenzi and Nijssen, 2020). Given
practical implications are intended to offer specific guidance or
recommend actions practitioners can take to improve their practices, we
regard this is problematic.
This emphasis on concepts and ideas rather than on specific actions
for practice, was evidenced when authors invited practitioners to think
for themselves about how to apply the implications to their specific
situation. Thus, practitioners were encouraged to think differently about
certain concepts, such as the use of social media for branding, sales or
relationship building, but authors stopped short of proposing how these
outcomes should be achieved. This approach was reflected in the
phrases used to introduce the implications, which included: ‘managers
should be aware of’, ‘managers should consider’, ‘companies should ensure’,
or ‘companies should recognise’, and the suggestion that these implica-
tions could provide: ‘insights’, ‘markers to explore’, or ‘help manager to
see’. This finding aligns with the categorization of many articles as either
‘conceptual’ or ‘educational’ research and their tendency to include
rather abstract implications.
We do not consider implications that had an actions orientation to
necessarily be preferable to those that are concepts oriented, as both
conceptual and educational studies could deliver action-oriented prac-
tical implications (e.g., Barac et al., 2017, Hsiao et al., 2020; Jarvinen
and Taiminen, 2016). However, we did observe that some articles with
actions-oriented implications were also categorized as ‘tools’ within the
knowledge category (e.g., Luo et al., 2021; Zhang and Du, 2020, Kwon
et al., 2022).
5.4.7. Normative or non-normative language
Language normativity followed similar patterns, with more than two
thirds of the articles (74 %) using normative language, while the
remainder used non-normative language. This pattern is encouraging, as
it indicates the large majority of articles suggested specific recommen-
dations and actions that practitioners should/could/must take to
improve their practice. Normative implications typically included pre-
scriptive language: ‘managers should primarily focus on’, ‘managers must
plan’, ‘managers should ensure’, ‘manufacturers should consider’, ‘manu-
facturers should pay more attention to’, ‘marketers should place more
attention’, ‘marketers are recommended to’. Some implications did not use
normative words (e.g., Pitt et al., 2019; Ancillai et al., 2019; Silva, 2022;
Badran et al., 2023) or used them only at the end of the implications. The
implications with no normative words typically resulted in rather
generic implications, especially in cases where no specific stakeholders
were identified (e.g., Cowan et al., 2022; Quinton and Wilson, 2016).
Despite these different degrees of normativity, the use of normative
language alone did not necessarily result in implications that were ac-
tion oriented or easier for practitioners to act on.
5.4.8. Simplistic or complicated message
The message efficacy dimension captured whether the scientific re-
sults were translated into easy to grasp and simplistic language for
practitioners and managers. Reflecting the differences in language used
by academics and practitioners, the vast majority of the analyzed
implications (82 %) used simplistic language that differed from the more
complex academic language found elsewhere in the articles. Those that
did not vary the language (e.g., Kalra et al., 2013; Hollebeek, 2019;
Meire et al., 2022), tended to have more academic content within the
implications. Some mentioned conceptual frameworks, hypothesis
testing and robustness checks. The use of academic acronyms in these
implications, often with no explanation, suggested that authors expected
practitioners to be familiar with these terms and to be prepared to read
the whole of the article. However, even when simpler language was
used, the implications were often concept focused or emphasized theo-
rizing (e.g., Ancillai et al., 2019; Rose et al., 2021; Itani et al., 2020).
These findings suggest that simplifying the language alone, may not be
sufficient to make practical implications actionable.
5.4.9. Abstract or concrete measures
This dimension concerned the extent to which suggested measures
were abstract and generic or concrete and applicable to practical prob-
lems and decisions. Of the analyzed implications, 75 % implications
were found to be abstract and to discuss generic and abstract ideas
without offering specific measures (e.g., Lone et al., 2022; Suh and
Chow, 2021; Rusthollkarhu et al., 2022). Only 25 % included concrete
measures to address practical problems (e.g., Zhang and Du, 2020, Luo
et al., 2021; Jarvinen and Karjaluoto, 2015), which is not particularly
surprising given most articles were conceptually oriented. In contrast,
articles which included concrete measures tended to have an actions
orientation; for example, Swani et al. (2017) divided the practical im-
plications into subheadings aligned with the article’s conceptual
framework and summarized specific implications for each element in
table format.
5.4.10. Visual elements
Although not specifically mentioned in the underpinning literature
for our framework, the benefit of presenting practical implications in a
visual way emerged as important. This includes the use of visuals, such
as tables, figures, bullets/numbered points or specific subheadings; as
well as the provision of linked resources, such as presentations, blogs or
vlogs. Including such visual aspects was shown in the analysis to elevate
the practical implications and/or clarify the recommended actions. We
found some exceptional examples of this approach. One case (Kwon
et al., 2022) used a table and six separate illustrative figures to illustrate
the effectiveness of using warm and cold colors in social media
photography. Another example (Yu et al., 2020) used bullet points to
illustrate and make the recommendations appear more actionable. One
successful example (Zhang and Du, 2021) illustrated a diagnostic tool
for practitioners as a matrix; while others (e.g., Barac et al., 2017; Swani
et al., 2017; Luo et al., 2021), provided summary tables that aligned the
practical implications with the academic or conceptual contributions,
helping demonstrate specific action-oriented implications.
5.5. ‘Who’ meta category
5.5.1. Targeted stakeholders
Although the vast majority of implications were aimed generically at
managers, firms, or organizations, some targeted specific stakeholders;
for example, ‘firms in developing and transition economies’, ‘professionals
involved in marketing and communication departments of SMEs’, ‘B2B or-
ganizations that do not currently use the social media’, or ‘logistic service
providers in embryonic stage’. The most widely cited stakeholder category
was managers, appearing in 81 out of 102 articles. Sometimes the type of
manager was specified, such as ‘B2B SME practitioner’, ‘key account
managers’, ‘managers of new ventures’, or ‘managers operating in B2B ser-
vice industry’. Other articles preferred the term ‘management’, either used
on its own or in a qualified form, such as ‘omnichannel management’ or
‘sales management’. Companies were the second most commonly
mentioned stakeholder type (59), using either generic terms such as
‘organization’, ‘firm’, ‘company’, ‘manufacturer’, or specific ones, such as

6. Conceptual contribution and practical implications
6.1. Conceptual contribution
This article highlights the important role of practical implications in
translating academic findings into relevant knowledge for practice.
Though this focus, we have addressed calls to bridge the theory-practice
divide and improve marketing’s impact and benefit to the world. In
examining dimensions that underpin how effectively this divide is
bridged by practical implications, we make several conceptual contri-
butions to knowledge. First, by drawing together insights from across
the literature, we develop and apply a new framework for practical
implications. Second, through this conceptualization, we enrich under-
standing of the existing – and potential – role of practical implications in
addressing the theory-practice divide and promoting the societal value
of industrial marketing research. We do so by showing how practical
implications act as a translational bridge between relevant research and
research impact, and by identifying the dimensions that support effec-
tiveness. We further highlight the largely unrealized potential for re-
searchers to extend the range of stakeholders they consider, beyond
practicing managers to include industries, customers, policy makers
such as governmental and non-governmental organizations, and wider
society. Third, by drawing widely from the business and management
literature on research relevance, the theory-practice divide, and on
research impact, we show our conceptualization is applicable beyond
this initial focus on industrial digital marketing.
6.2. ‘Typical’ practical implications
The vast majority of reviewed practical implications were of the
educational research type. They mainly focused on present action,
although many emphasized present thinking. The most commonly
occurring section title was ‘managerial implications’, with this content
typically located within the discussion section. The average length of the
practical implications was 450 words, with most of these sections
including academic references. The large majority of articles provided
practical implications that were similar or overlapped with the academic
contributions. Often, these implications were focused on concepts, ideas
or theorizing. Although the language used was usually simplistic and
accessible, many of the presented ideas were rather abstract.
Managers and companies were the most commonly mentioned
stakeholders, often referred to generically, rather than more granularly
at a functional or specific hierarchical level. Other commercial stake-
holder types, such as marketing agencies, industry bodies or other ser-
vice providers, were less likely to be included; while beneficiaries from
wider society, such as policy makers and community organizations,
were significantly under-represented in the reviewed articles. In terms of
scale and scope, relatively few practical implications provided specific
tools or included detailed actions to improve current business practice,
perhaps suggesting authors saw these tools as more relevant to applied
research. There was also relatively little problem-solving research, with
no inspirational research identified.
The analysis revealed some other interesting patterns, with the vast
majority of practical implications categorized as conceptual research
within the ‘What’ category, also identified as educational research
within the ‘Why’ category, and regarded as concepts oriented and ab-
stract within the ‘How’ category. On the other hand, practical implica-
tions including tools in the ‘What’ category, were often categorized as
problem solving and actions oriented within the ‘Why’ and ‘How’ cat-
egories respectively. Other patterns included an alignment between
conceptual research the ‘What’ category and problem-solving research
within the ‘Why’ category; and conceptual research that focused on
present thinking in the ‘What’ category, also found to be actions-
oriented in the ‘How’ category.
6.3. Developing better practical implications: multilevel archetypes
In this section, we present three multilevel practical implications
archetypes to explain our findings and to underpin our subsequent
recommendations for good practice. We identify three levels of practice
aligned with the conceptual framework, which we label: ‘repeating’,
‘translating’ and ‘elevating’ archetypes (see Fig. 5).
6.3.1. Low level – repeating
This lowest level of practical implications is by far the most
commonly occurring in the articles we reviewed. The implications are
predominantly oriented towards present thinking rather than to present
action and are typically concerned with enhancing managers’ (or com-
panies’) general understanding of certain issues. Often these implica-
tions are difficult to locate within the articles, due to poor signposting or
because they are integrated or spread through other sections. In many
cases, the content of these practical implications is very similar to the
conceptual contributions, with ideas or recommendations oriented on
theorizing. Non-normative, complex and overly academic language
tends to be used, often repeating the academic contributions and
including academic references. Although the length of these implica-
tions varied, recommendations usually concentrate at a very abstract
level.
Practical implications of this type might, for example, come from a
study of the adoption and use of social media as a part of the B2B selling
process. The practical implications might simply repeat the key findings
and/or academic contribution, perhaps in a simpler or abridged set of
words. Such implications would be unlikely to offer detailed action to be
taken and/or offer guidance on how social media might be adopted or
used within B2B sales. None, or very limited, detailed information about
targeted stakeholders, types of companies or managers within the or-
ganization would be given.
6.3.2. Mid level – translating
At this level, there is greater emphasis on translating the article’s
findings into relevant practical knowledge. Although many implications
are educational, there is also some problem-solving research designed to
address concrete practical problems. The implications are clearly
labelled and usually contained within a bespoke and separate section. In
comparison to the low – repeating – level, these implications are likely to
be distinctly different and more action oriented than the academic
contributions. Another difference is the provision of practical tools, in-
struments or models for managers. Both normative and non-normative
language can be used, however, clear efforts are made to translate the
findings for non-academic stakeholders, whether they be managers,
companies, industry bodies and/or customers. These efforts include
explaining scientific results using simple wording and providing

concrete measures or recommendations that are applicable to practical
problems and decisions. Any included references aim to specifically
support the practical implications rather than repeating more academic
points. The length of these implications varies considerably.
Returning to the example of a study of the adoption and use of social
media as a part of B2B selling processes, the practical implications at this
level would go further in translating key findings and academic contri-
bution into action-oriented guidance on how to adopt and use social
media as a part of B2B sales. The focus would extend beyond simply
considering the use of social media, to provide more specific and
detailed actions, perhaps in the form of flow diagram to demonstrate
immediate steps to be undertaken. The companies’ type, size and sector
would probably be considered, along with the role of the manager, e.g.,
key account manager. The implications of these potential actions might
be considered for other stakeholders, such as the customers/clients and
wider sector/industry.
6.3.3. High level – elevating
At the highest level is an elevated set of practical implications that
extend beyond the identified academic contributions. Although our
study found very few examples of this level, we make the following
suggestions as an aspirational target for researchers. As well as focusing
on present applications, implications at this level are future oriented
where possible. To provide the most effective translational bridge, these
implications should solve a specific problem and inspire practitioners by
offering innovative perspectives or ideas on existing issues or future
problems. These solutions could include detailed tools, models or in-
struments, oriented to the needs of specific practitioners, who should be
quickly and readily able to apply them to their own situation. There is
potential to include empirical findings within these implications,
perhaps in the form of aggregated data, results or study findings that are
processed into higher-order insights or recommendations for practi-
tioners. The practical implications must be well sign-posted, easy to
locate and to stand out from the rest of the article. The provision of
visual information, such as tables, figures or even simple bullet points,
should be considered. Their content should include simple, action-
oriented messaging, outlining concrete measures and using normative
language. There is also potential to include a wide range of targeted
stakeholders, which include the usual managers, companies and in-
dustries, but extend beyond to consider other policy, practice and so-
cietal groups.
Returning once again to the example of a study of the adoption and
use of social media as a part of B2B selling processes, at this level a
detailed and specific tool should be developed for managers and com-
panies with clearly laid out action on how to successfully adopt and
effectively use social media as a part of B2B selling. These actions should
be either immediately actionable or be future facing, with the capacity
to inspire practitioners with innovative perspectives on social media use
as a part of their sales. This might include showing their sales strategy
could be transformed to become a social media first company. Action-
oriented, specific implications should be developed, including using
visual representation, ideally in the diagram, model, matrix, or table
format. The implications for other stakeholders beyond those commer-
cially involve might be considered. An example could include consid-
ering the role of policy in supporting organizations to improve their
social media adoption and to benefit wider business growth.
6.4. Recommendations for authors, reviewers, editors and publishers
The systematic review and the multilevel archetypes indicate the
need for a step-change in the way practical implications are written,
reviewed and presented within academic journals. In this section we first
present operational recommendations for authors to improve the scope
and effectiveness of practical implications. Acknowledging the impor-
tance of the review process, we also extend these operational recom-
mendations to reviewers, who can support this development. We follow

with strategic recommendations for journal editors and publishers,
suggesting ways they might influence how practical implications are
presented and prioritized within academic publications.
The operational recommendations for authors and reviewers have
been informed by good practices from the multilevel archetypes and are
organized according to the ‘What’, ‘Why’, ‘How’ and ‘Who’ meta cate-
gories. Specifically, we make operational suggestions for authors and
reviewers to improve how practical implications bridge research rele-
vance and impact. In doing so, we note a significant under-
representation of variations and types of practical implications and
encourage researchers to use the conceptual framework creatively to
develop their approach to practical implications.
6.4.1. ‘What’
The knowledge type is an indicator of the research’s relevance and its
potential to generate usable practical implications. Although we do not
regard certain knowledge types as more or less useful for practitioners,
we note potential for more practical implications that provide specific
tools, such as models or frameworks. To ensure the applicability of these
implications, it is important for authors to clearly signal the knowledge
type. For example, there is a distinction between a tool that can be
directly used to change practices and more conceptually oriented or
empirical implications, where an explanation is needed of how the
findings can be used to develop practitioners’ thinking or ways of
working. Authors should also clarify whether recommended changes in
thinking or action are focused on the present or on the future. Here
again, we note that a greater focus on future dimensions – whether
future actions or thinking – could lead to inspirational implications. We
also see an opportunity for greater emphasis on empirical research
within practical implications. Examples might include the development
of industry averages and benchmarks, that could be processed into
higher-order insights or recommendations. To support how effective
these implications are as a translational bridge, reviewers should check
for clarity on these matters.
6.4.2. ‘Why’
The way knowledge from research findings is to be used is closely
connected with the research relevance, meaning that authors should
clearly signal the intended purpose of the knowledge. Possibilities might
include to educate by drawing together different knowledge about an
issue or problem, to inspire by providing new insights and perspectives
on how practitioners think about an issue or problem, or to solve a
problem by developing tools to assist in implementing this knowledge.
Whether these implications go beyond informing practitioner thinking
and propose tangible actions to be taken, also needs to be explained.
Reviewers should ensure the actions practitioners need to take as a
consequence of the knowledge are clearly explained.
6.4.3. ‘How’
The written articulation and presentation of the practical implica-
tions are crucial, especially as non-academic stakeholders may lack the
time and other resources to engage with them. Considerations for au-
thors include the signposting and positioning of the implications within
the manuscript, their length, use of language, concreteness and the
extent to which they build on, or are similar to, the academic contri-
butions. Practical implications that focus too much on the scientific
aspects of research, or that over-use of complex academic language or
references could be off-putting for practitioners. Although a connection
between the academic contributions and practical implications is
important in demonstrating the integrity of the implications, simply
repeating the academic contributions in different words is not sufficient.
We note that some of the reviewed sections of practical implications
were effective in presenting the academic contributions and then
translating and elevating them into practical implications. Although we
did not formally analyze whether visual implications visually repre-
sented, we note the very effective use such presentation in some articles,
and we comment further on this point below.
Clear signposting of the practical implications is crucial so they can
be readily located. Including these implications within a clearly marked
section or sub-section can be helpful. Reviewers should assess whether
this material is easy to find, rather than dissipated across multiple parts
of the manuscript. The length of the practical implications should be
sufficient to explain the recommended changes in thinking or actions
and provide detailed insights into how practitioners might implement
the suggestions. Authors need to be aware that concrete and action-
oriented recommendations can support the translational effectiveness
of these practical implications. Their relevance to the practical problems
and decisions faced by practitioners should also be obvious, as more
abstract implications are likely to be less usable. Reviewers should check
the clarity of recommendations and consider whether sufficient detail is
provided for practitioners to make changes.
6.4.4. ‘Who’
Authors should clearly identify the stakeholders who can benefit, so
practitioners understand which findings are relevant to them.
Describing the stakeholders, their sector and/or managerial function is
useful. While the predominant focus is likely to remain on managers and
companies, there is potential to extend the commercial stakeholders to
consider the implications for stakeholders, such as marketing agencies,
industry bodies or other service providers, which are important to the
smooth operation of firms and their markets. We also call for greater
granularity in the targeted stakeholders; for example, to include impli-
cations for managers at specific levels within the organizational hier-
archy, or from certain functions, industries or regions. Give that societal
stakeholders were significantly under-represented in the reviewed arti-
cles; we encourage researchers to consider situations in which their
research could have societal implications for policy makers, the third
sector, communities and the public. Reviewers should question whether
the stakeholder beneficiaries of the research are readily evident and if
obvious stakeholder types have been missed.
There are also strategic implications for editors who have an
important role in supporting reviewers and authors, and in highlighting
the practical relevance and potential of academic work for economic and
social impact. With many articles still appearing without practical im-
plications (45 of the 204 we reviewed), these should be a compulsory
element, as even conceptual or literature review articles can benefit
from including such recommendations. Clearer mandatory guidance for
authors and reviewers could clarify expectations about the presentation
and usability of these implications for non-academic stakeholders,
including that practitioners are likely to find a standalone section more
easily. The appointment of practitioner reviewers or advisors could also
be considered, although these can be difficult to retain. Another option
could be to appoint a ‘practice panel’ to oversee how a journal handles
practical implications and make recommendations to editors on how
best to guide authors and reviewers.
Other strategic questions for editors and for publishers include where
these practical implications should be located within journal articles,
their relative importance compared with academic contributions, and
whether these implications would attract greater attention if published
in a different format. We recommend requiring practical implications to
be mentioned in abstracts, and also for consideration of creative new
formats to improve the visibility, accessibility and suitability of practical
implications for managers, organizations and other stakeholder types.
Some progress has been made in altering the location and prominence of
practical implications within articles, with some journals requiring short
bullet point highlights at the start of articles and others including
practical implications as part of their structured abstract format. Other
options could include mandating authors to produce a separate practical
implications output, written in accessible language and including action
points for non-academic stakeholders. Some journals, including IMM,
have also developed separate academic-practitioner sections. We also
recommend that other media formats be used to supplement the

mations, videos or infographics. Meeting the needs of practitioners who

share relevant research findings through clear and actionable practical
implications.
6.5. Field testing and validation of practical recommendations
Our main guiding assumption was that for academic research to
deliver societal value, practical implications should act as a translational
bridge between relevance and impact. In examining the effectiveness of
practical implications, we argued that four questions needed to be
addressed: What? Why? How? and Who? To validate our findings, in line
with design science research add-on (Van Aken et al., 2016), we con-
ducted alpha testing using one-to-one discussions with practitioner ex-
perts and beta testing with other business and management researchers
(Van der Borgh et al., 2020; Van der Borgh et al., 2022).
The alpha testing was used to confirm the effectiveness of our main
assumptions and test the suitability of our practical implications with a
group of expert practitioners. Collectively, these experts had private and
public sector experience from a range of manufacturing and services
businesses, and had worked in senior positions with small, medium and
large enterprises. All claimed to be interested in the findings and prac-
tical implications generated by academic research. We showed these
practitioners examples of the reviewed practical implications sections
and provided details of the recommendations we made to improve the
scope and effectiveness of these implications. They were able to identify
dimensions we had used to analyze the practical implications and all
strongly endorsed our recommendations. In doing so, they emphasized
that time-poor practitioners need readily accessible insights tailored to
their needs, that they can quickly apply in practice.
To examine whether our findings were replicable in other areas of
marketing and management, we conducted beta testing with academics
from other areas of marketing (consumer behavior, tourism marketing);
and other management sub-disciplines (strategic management, finance,
accountancy). Having been shown the same practical implications ex-
amples as the practitioners, these academics unanimously endorsed the
included areas. For example, they recognized the need for practical
implications to be written in non-academic language and explained why
the provision of detailed and readily applicable actions are necessary. At
the same time, they acknowledged that practical implications tend not
to receive the same level of attention devoted to academic contributions.
When shown the practical implications framework, they agreed it would
be applicable to their own academic sub-disciplines. All considered the
four meta categories to capture the characteristics necessary to ensure
sufficiently action-oriented and specific implications, with potential to
be applied in practice.
7. Conclusions, limitations, and future research directions
The results of the study – including the practical implications
framework and the multilevel archetypes – contribute significantly to
the debate about research and societal value and shed new light on the
translational role of practical implications. There is potential for a step-
change in thinking that elevates the practical benefits and societal value
to be gained from industrial marketing research, renewing our focus on
research relevance, and embracing impact for more stakeholder types
into our practices. However, our study is not without its limitations.
First, for practical reasons it was appropriate to focus our review on a
specific topic within industrial marketing – in this case, digital mar-
keting. We acknowledge that these results will to some extent be influ-
enced by the characteristics of research undertaken within this area.
Although our field testing provides early indications the findings are
applicable beyond this context, we encourage researchers to apply this
framework to other areas of industrial marketing research, such as
supply chain management, sales management, and to B2C marketing.
Such reviews could involve the use of single or multiple combinations of
different journals. Although we acknowledge that marketing involves a
particular customer-facing function, which highlights the importance of
practical implications, we hope this approach will also be of value for
other business and management disciplines, such as organizational
behavior, operations management or accounting and finance.
Second, as the aim was to identify different characteristics of prac-
tical implications and their role in bridging relevance and impact, our
analysis relied on binary measures. We therefore consider there is po-
tential to develop more complex scales for evaluating these implications.
For example, the measure which assesses complicatedness of the mes-
sage could be adapted to incorporate approaches from readability tests
that aim to determined how easy or difficult the text is to understand (e.
g., Kincaid, Fishburne, Rogers, & Chissom, 1975).
Third, while this study focused on practical implications in isolation,
further work could explicitly explore the relationship between practical
implications and theoretical contributions (Nicholson et al., 2018),
enabling the ways in which different types of theoretical contribution
facilitate development of different kinds of practical implications to be
examined.
Finally, while this study’s findings and frameworks help to kickstart
a conversation about the role of practical implications within academic
journals, an important next step is to extend this discussion to other
researchers, reviewers, editors and publishers. Such wider and deeper
engagement is crucial to ensure practical implications are taken seri-
ously, and that their translational role in bridging the theory-practice
divide is secured.
