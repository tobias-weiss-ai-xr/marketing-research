Abstract
The productivity of manufacturing companies across the globe has been of concerns despite
the importance of the sector to economic development. The poor productivity that has greeted
these companies has led to questioning whether strategic capabilities dimensions: managerial
capabilities, innovation capabilities, technological capabilities, marketing capabilities and
human resources capabilities could be of influence in boosting productivity. The study therefore
seeks to examine the role strategic capabilities plays in boosting productivity of manufacturing
companies. The study adopts the qualitative approach by reviewing recent studies on strategic
capabilities dimensions and productivity. The study used over 50 articles with over 60 authors
from high-impact journals to explore the possibility of strategic capabilities dimensions having
a role in improving productivity. The study theoretical review includes the resource base view,
dynamic capability theory and the stakeholder theory. The outcome of the study shows that
strategic capabilities dimensions have  a significant impact on the productivity of
manufacturing organizations. The study recommended that management of manufacturing
companies, should invest in modern technology, skills and capabilities.

Review of Literature
Productivity
Productivities in literature have been proxies for operational performance, operational success,
or operational capabilities (Ali et al., 2019; Berndt et al., 2022; Yi et al., 2023). Productivity is
the extent to which a manufacturing process achieves its objectives (Berndt et al., 2022).
Productivity, or operational capability, refers to the fundamental ability of a company to carry
out the complete process of manufacturing and selling a product (Yi et al., 2023). Productivity
refers to a company's capacit y to optimise its essential technology, resources, and critical
procedures in order to meet consumers' needs in a highly efficient and cost -effective manner
(Ali et al., 2019). Productivity refers to the capacity to produce a higher level of value or output
while using a reduced number of resources or inputs. It’s been noted that productivity is not
everything, but in the long term, it is nearly everything ( Oulton, 2022). In terms of the labour
perspective, productivity is defined as labour productivity mea sured by company sales per
worker (Amin & Ulku, 2019). Productivity refers to labour productivity defined as the log of
total annual revenue of the firm (in currency value) during the previous financial year divided
by the total number of permanent full -time staff employed by the firm at the end of the most
recent financial year (Amin & Ulku, 2019; Amin & Khalid, 2023).
Productivity corresponds to the ratio of outputs to inputs (Goolsbee & Syverson, 2023;
Rathnayake & Middleton, 2023 ). In the computation of efficiency and productivity, the
underlying idea of returns to scale occupies an integral part. If the technology provides constant
returns to scale throughout, the technology is global, implying that the same benchmark applies
to all firms, irrespective of their scale of operations. However, if the returns to scale are varying,
then the reference units can be different  for different units under study. In the same vein, the
returns to scale displayed by a unit have significant implications for the average productivity
of the company in question (Sinha, 2021). Productivity is a major predictive algorithm of
corporate surv ival since it assesses a firm’s efficiency and profitability. Furthermore,
productivity gives essential information about the efficiency of the market in which companies
operate (Muzi et al., 2021; Pérez -Pons et al., 2020). In a functioning market with equ al
competition, more productive firms remain while less productive firms leave the market. Such
dynamic permits constant reallocation of resources to their highest value of usage. With the
threat of entry, established firms are under pressure to discover ways to boost their efficiency,
frequently through innovative activity (Muzi et al., 2021).
On the characteristics, productivity is related to a combination of attribute factors, such as
process improvements, product development efficiency, shorter lead times, and quality
standards (Berndt et al., 2022). Also, productivity can be related to efficiency and effectiveness.
In essence, a company's operational success depends on the selection of the right strategy based
on external factors, in which the objective is to use its resources as efficiently and effectively
as possible (Pradana & Safitri, 2023). Operational capability is conceptualised by two features:
process management and performance management (Ali et al., 2019). Berndt et al. (2022) are
of the view that operational performance is not just about product quality but also about leading
to less costly and faster processes.

Productivity is characterised by delivery time performance, speed to introduce new products
or services into the operational process, compliance with product or service specifications,
flexibility to change the product or service mix, unit cost of producti on, and flexibility to
change the volume of operations (Berndt et al., 2022). In the energy sector, productivity was
viewed as the difference between the monthly system's actual and planned efficiency in terms
of peak solar hours and normalised specific final energy production (Mohamed et al., 2024).
On the advantage of productivity, at the firm level, productivity is often associated with
improved profitability, cost efficiency, and competitiveness ( Handoyo et al., 2023).
Productivity growth allows firms to produce more with the same or fewer resources, which in
turn leads to increased output, reduced unit costs, and improved margins. This efficiency
contributes not only to organisational sustainability but also to broader macroeconomic
outcomes, such as GDP growth and employment expansion (OECD, 2021) . Moreover,
productivity gains enable firms to invest more in innovation, employee training, and
infrastructure development, thereby fostering long-term strategic advancement.
Despite these advantages, rising productivity can also yield unintended negative consequences,
particularly when driven by excessive cost -cutting or overutilisation of labour and capital
(Ogbonnaya et al., 2022). The pursuit of productivity at all costs ma y lead to job insecurity,
work intensification, and employee burnout, especially in labour -intensive sectors. In such
cases, the quality of work life may deteriorate, undermining employee morale and engagement,
which are themselves critical to sustained pe rformance (Begum et al., 2025; Kremer et al.,
2021; Niazi et al., 2023). Additionally, when productivity improvements are achieved through
automation, there may be adverse implications for employment, particularly for low -skilled
workers whose roles are mo st susceptible to technological displacement (Jiang et al., 2024;
Sedik & Yoo, 2021).
In accordance with the above thoughts and opinions of scholars, this study defined productivity
as the capabilities for firms to achieve greater results with fewer resources with alignments to
the desired goals and objectives of the firm. A firm will not w ant to achieve efficiency and
effectiveness that contradict their desired goals and objectives. This goes to entail that getting
results that do not align with an organization’s desired goals can be a waste of resources.

Strategic capabilities
The concept of strategic capabilities is associated with the resource-based view (RBV), where
both approaches focus on establishing the core competencies that cannot be duplicated by
competitors. Besides, it necessitates the optimal use of tangible and int angible organizational
resources (Abazeed, 2020). Strategic capabilities are a sophisticated and collected set of skills
and knowledge that allow an organization or business unit to coordinate activities and leverage
its assets to generate economic value a nd a sustainable competitive edge (Abazeed, 2020;
Runtu & Ellitan, 2021). Strategic capabilities are defined as the organization’s unique internal
talents and operations that are not available to competitors. Furthermore, strategic capabilities
are those specific organizational resources and competencies that assist in creating relevant
strategies for achieving better organizational performance (Abazeed, 2020). Strategic
capability is defined as the adequacy and suitability of an organization’s resources an d
competencies for survival and prosperity. Resources are an organization’s assets, while
competences are the effective manner in which those assets are employed, or 'what the
organization does well' (Imbambi et al., 2019).
Strategic capability is dependent on an organization’s capacity to accomplish a series of
coordinated tasks and employ organisational resources with the objective of obtaining specified
final results (Runtu & Ellitan, 2021; Wulaningrum et al., 2020). Internal power in the
organization (i.e., this capability) becomes a competitive superiority for the organization when
it encounters other similar companies. If a company or an entity has more resources and has

Studies tend to proxy strategic capabilities with organisational capabilities (Bianchi & Stoian,

organizations, studies have suggested some dimensions; these dimensions are marketing
capability, market linking capability, information technology capability, and management
capability (Abazeed, 2020), and another firm's strategic capability could include innovation
capabilities (Mostafiz et al., 2021). Bianchi and Stoian (2022) posited that organizational

will enhance, develop, and offer products and services to its customers by exploiting its
capabilities and resources, which include human, physical, and organizational resources
(Hagoug & Abdalla, 2021).
Strategic capabilities have some merits and limitations that should be noted. The advantage of
strategic capabilities is demonstrated by an organization’s ability to perceive existing market
possibilities while also utilizing the necessary resources to seize these opportunities from rivals.
Furthermore, knowledge management can help improve the exploitation and exploration of
knowledge, which leads to the development of new products and services (Abazeed, 2020).
Furthermore, strategic capabilities contribut e to the development of an intensive knowledge
environment by recognizing and obtaining the appropriate knowledge from the external
environment and transferring it into the internal organizational environment for application in
the production of innovative products and services (Abazeed, 2020; Yoshikuni et al., 2024).
Strategic capabilities constitute a fundamental component of resources that provide the
framework for achieving an organization’s competitive advantage (Runtu & Ellitan, 2021). The
resource-based view and strategic capability stressed that the creation of distinctive features
enables organizations to defy competitive imitation (Abazeed, 2020). Organizational
capabilities enable a company to convert its technical expertise into results, which is a
company's capacity to run its day-to-day operations while also growing, adapting, and seeking
a competitive advantage in its industry (Olowoporoku et al., 2021).
In accordance with the aforementioned thoughts and opinions of scholars, this study defined
strategic capabilities as the set of capabilities and resources that make an organization achieve
positional excellence and competitive advantage in the industry.

Theoretical Framework
Resource-Based View Theory
The Resource -Based View (RBV) was initially developed by Wernerfelt  (1984) and later
expanded by Barney (1986, 1991), building on the foundational ideas of Penrose (1959), who
theorized the growth of the firm, and Rubin (1973, 1978), who explored firm expansion.

Freeman further refined the RBV in 2010 by integrating elements of stakeholder theory,
emphasizing that a firm’s competitive advantage stems from resources that are valuable, rare,
inimitable, and non -substitutable (Freeman et al., 2021). According to Wern erfelt (1984),
resources encompass any assets that can enhance or constrain a firm’s performance. Barney
(1991) posits that firms differ strategically due to their unique combinations of resources, which
suggests resource heterogeneity among firms. RBV ass umes that resources must be both
heterogeneous and immobile, meaning they cannot be easily transferred or replicated across
firms. Rather than acquiring new capabilities for each emerging opportunity, RBV advocates
for leveraging existing internal resource s in novel ways to exploit external opportunities.
Notably, scholars such as Rumelt, Hamel, Prahalad, and others contributed significantly to the
development and popularization of RBV throughout the 1980s and 1990s.
The RBV is founded on three assumptions: that firms aim to generate returns that are higher
than average; that resources are distributed asymmetrically among rival firms; and that
variations in resource availability result in variations in product or servi ce characteristics,
which in turn cause variations in firms' performance. The theory further assumes that people
are motivated to use all available economic resources and that firms make logical decisions
that are influenced by the economic environment (Ba rney, 1991). RBV has been seen as a
method of evaluating and determining an organization’s competitive advantage by looking at
the unique ways in which it possesses skills, capabilities, and intangibles (Ngundi & Omwenga,
2023). Studies prove that resource ownership alone is not sufficient; it involves converting
resources into profitable value s (Purwanti et al., 2022). According to Barney's, competitive
assets, resources, and resources are defined by four important characteristics, according to the
resource-based view: value, rarity, inimitableness, and non-substitutionality (VRIN) (Olubiyi,
2021). RBV , according to Barney, places emphasis on the notion of difficult -to-imitate
attributes of the firm as sources of superior performance (Purwanti et al., 2022). Applying the
VRIN framework to strategic capabilities will not just lead to competitive advantage but
superior performance for the organization.
RBV has been critiqued occasionally as it fails to take into account external market dynamics
and competitive forces, which could result in a narrow concentration on internal resources that
ignores market trends and consumer needs (Gupta, 2023). The reliability of identifying sources
of sustained competitive advantage is questioned due to the difficulty of finding resources that
fully satisfy the VRIN (Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, and Non -substitutable) criteria (Sanchez,
2008; Utami & Alamanos, 2023). Accor ding to Madhani (2010), the RBV is frequently
criticised for failing to sufficiently address the dynamic character of competitive landscapes
and the ways in which resources and capacities change over time.  Kraaijenbrink et al. (2010),
Madhani (2010), and Sanchez (2008) contend that the RBV may result in a "infinite regress"
in which the search for higher-order capabilities to explain competitive advantage never stops,
making it challenging to identify the r eal cause. When it comes to complicated corporate
environments, critics contend that the RBV may not give managers clear, actionable insights
on how to create and use vital resources (Helfat et al., 2023; Madhani, 2010).
Supporters of the RBV theory such as Chinakidzwa and Phiri (2020); Dejardin et al. (2023);
Homburg and Wielgos (2022); Homburg and Wielgos (2022); Kamar et al. (2022); Murimi and
Ombaka (2019); Ngundi and Omwenga (2023); Ngundi and Omwenga (2023); Nnodal. et al.
(2020); Purwanti et al. (2022); Rashidirad and Salimian (2020); and Susanta (2020) gave
credence to the RBV theory. With RBV , firms can configure new resources to fit the business
environment on a wide canvas (Nayak et al., 2022). RBV offers visibility for efficient resource
allocation within resource pools. Managers are then able to plan ahead and assign resources in
accordance with the project's demands, scope, and schedule. RBVs help facilitate the strategic
decision-making process. Resource -based theory, in other words, helps to give managers a
thorough assessment of their resource strengths and weaknesses. This analysis allows them to

By leveraging strengths and strategically addressing barriers, the company can align its actions

The Resource-Based View (RBV) theory is relevant to understanding the strategic capabilities
and performance of manufacturing companies.  It emphasizes how a firm's unique internal
resources and capabilities, rather than just its external market position, can lead to a competitive
advantage. By leveraging valuable, rare, inimitable, and non -substitutable (VRIN) resources
and capabilities, manufacturing firms can achieve superior performance and sustained
competitive advantage (Barney 1991; D’Oria et al., 2021; Heiko, 2024). In addition, the RBV
encourages adaptation by continually assessing and developing these resources to respond to
changing market demands so as to allow firms to adapt their strategies in an effective way
based on their core competencies, t hereby fostering a culture of innovation and resilience
(Dovbischuk, 2022; Kiefer et al., 2024). The RBV has been known to promote long -term
sustainability (Kiefer et al., 2024). This approach fosters adaptation to market changes and
creates long -term adva ntages that competitors are struggling to replicate. Thus, ensuring
sustained success and longevity in the market (Olaleye et al., 2024). The RBV preserves firms'
competitive advantage. Increased market volatility has led to considerable ad hoc project
requirements, which are often the determining factors of a company's success or failure. In
these situations, resource managers can utilize both their primary and secondary workforce
skills in order to carry out critical multispectral projects (Gupta, 2023; Kero & Bogale, 2023).
The RBV is further relevant to manufacturing companies to enables cross -functional usage of
resources. In a matrix organization, the resource strategy model enables enterprise -wide
visibility of the workforce and its sue expertise. It helps allocate appropriate resources from
different departments and forms a cross -functional team to affect the project. It lowers hiring
cycle cost s and helps to leverage a diversified workforce. In addition, employees are also
given multi-faceted projects to improve their professional portfolios (Gupta, 2023; Paliwal &
Saxena, 2023). Applying all strategic capability dimensions to an organizational input is pivotal
in making an organization achieve superior performance. The RBV frameworks, when
incorporated into the strategic capabilities dimension, should provide the proclivity for firms
to ensure these capabilities are valuable, inimitable, non-substitutable, and rare.

Research Methodology
This study used a qualitative research approach from secondary data, from where the literature
review was done. To identify current trends in this field, the study focused on peer -reviewed
articles published in prestigious and high-impact journals from 2019 to 2025.

Conclusion and Recommendations
From the literature reviews made on several articles, considering the variables and studies used,
there are indications that highlight the importance of strategic capabilities in fostering an
environment that allows for productivity within manufacturing organization. The study further
sees that strategic capabilities have significant effects on the productivity of manufacturing
companies. Based on the finding of articles reviewed, it is recommended that management of
manufacturing companies, should invest in modern technology , skills and capabilities.
Manufacturing companies should leverage strategic capabilities like  adopting lean
manufacturing principles, embracing automation a nd digitalization, optimizing supply chain
management, fostering employee engagement and continuous improvement culture, and
strategically utilizing data analytics to identify and address bottlenecks in their operations.

Suggestion for Further Studies
First, this study uses a qualitative approach by reviewing existing studies on the subject of
strategic capabilities with respect to productivity . Similar studies can be done using a
quantitative approach by using manufacturing companies as the study population and
employees as the unit of analysis. This can be either a cross-sectional or longitudinal research
design. Secondly, further study is required to be done in the Nigerian context or emerging
economies. Thirdly, further studies can add moderators or mediators to the study.
