Virtual Influencers in Marketing Research: Trends, Gaps, and Future Directions

ABSTRACT
Virtual influencers are fictional characters created by computers to interact with audiences on
social media platforms. These virtual entities are increasingly being used in marketing campaigns
by brands to reach a broader audience and create unique and engaging content. The growing
number of virtual influencers has attracted the interest of researchers, leading to an increase in
studies on their use, interaction, and  impact. This study aims to explore the literature on virtual
influencers in the academic field and uncover trends in this area to guide future researchers. In this
research, studies in Web of Science (WoS) and Scopus were analyzed using bibliometric analysis.
The studies obtained from the two databases (after removing duplicates) were combined, and a
total of 250 studies were included in the analysis. Analyses conducted using RStudio covered the
number of studies and citations over the years, the productiv ity of authors and
countries/institutions, collaborative works, utilized sources, keywords, and emerging themes. The
findings indicate a significant temporal gap in virtual influencer research.  The conceptual and
theoretical frameworks were first established by pioneering studies in 2016. Technological
developments, particularly in artificial intelligence and the metaverse, together with brands'
intensive collaboration with virtual influencers, have led to a surge in publications in 2023 and a
shift towards more empirical, data-driven research. This study is the first to discuss the concept of
digital influencer from a holistic perspective, elaborates the studies on the subject based on some
criteria, and identifies research gaps for future researchers.

Introduction
Influencer marketing has emerged as a powerful tool for brands to connect with their target
audiences, increase engagement, and influence consumer behavior. Developments in information
technology and artificial intelligence have introduced different forms of influencers (Cheung &
Leung, 2021; Mouritzen et al., 2023). These computer -generated mode ls are becoming
increasingly popular on social media platforms  (Byun & Ahn, 2023) , blurring the lines between
real and virtual personalities (Wibawa et al., 2022).  Virtual influencers are transforming social
media marketing and advertising. More brands are  using virtual influencers instead of humans in
marketing campaigns due to better control over influencer behavior, cost savings, or the desire to
stay current (Franke et al., 2023). Virtual influencers provide brands with greater flexibility and
adaptability than human influencers, while also offering enhanced logistical control and reduced
public relations risks (Audrezet et al., 2025). The increasing number of strategic partnerships with
virtual influencers has led previous studies to focus on authentici ty, persuasion, and trust.
However, this focus has largely been on the outcomes that virtual influencers achieve with their
followers. Therefore, examining the impact of virtual influencers on communication dynamics and
uncovering the underlying reasons why brands collaborate with them are of critical importance.

Advances in AI technology  and the intersection of influencer marketing and artificial
intelligence have resulted in an increased utilisation of AI -powered influencers across diverse
industry sectors (Jin & Viswanathan, 2025; Sands et al., 2022a; Thomas & Fowler, 2021; Ying,
2024). Technologies enabling the recognition of human movement and emotion have further
enhanced the realism of digital humans (Nah et al., 2022).  The integration of AI components
facilitates the virtual influencer's capacity to more effectively connect and engage with their
followers (Kietzmann et al., 2018; Nukhu et al., 2024). Recent technological advancements enable
virtual influencers to communicate synchronously via  live broadcasts and to generate content in
podcasting environments . While the impact of the emerging, technology -mediated interactive
environments created by virtual influencers on consumer perceptions is significant, research on
this topic appears to be relatively scarce. The increasing prevalence of virtual influencers across
digital platforms and brand collaborations necessitates a re -examination of concepts such as
interaction and authenticity, while simultaneously enabling virtual influencers to becom e
significant actors in media development. Therefore, systematic research is needed to understand
the interactions of virtual influencers and to conceptually frame the phenomenon.
The Virtual Influencer Market was valued at $6.9 billion in 2023 and is expe cted to reach
$154.6 billion by 2032 (SNS Insider, 2025). (SNS Insider, 2025). While economic growth in the
virtual influencer market is increasing, the literature  lacks a theoretical framework for
conceptualizing the subject from an interdisciplinary perspective. The algorithm -driven content
discovery of social media platforms, along with the fact that these platforms make influencer -
focused promotions both more co st-effective and scalable, constitute significant developments
affecting the growth of the virtual influencer market (Theodorakopoulos et al., 2025). These
developments accelerate the tendency of businesses to collaborate with influencers
(Theodorakopoulos et al., 2025), while simultaneously creating a critical area for examining the
power dynamics of virtual influencers within a theoretical framework, considering the algorithmic
mechanisms and visibility hierarchies of digital platforms . The spread of virtual influencers on a
global scale is shaped by technological infrastructure, media power and collaborations with brands.
North America leads the virtual influencer market with its digitally focused population (Straits
Research, 2024; UnivDatos, 2024). Gove rnment policies and digital culture in certain countries
have created a favorable environment for the expansion of virtual influencers (Straits Research,
2024). For example, the popularity of virtual influencers is steadily increasing in the food and
entertainment sectors in China (Grand View Research, 2024) and in the entertainment and gaming
sectors in the Asia -Pacific region (Straits Research, 2024).This popularity should not be
interpreted merely as a marketing trend but should also be considered in terms of human–computer
interaction, digital sociology, and the changing nature of posthuman identity construction
(Richters, 2020; Sonzogni, 2022). The increasing use of AI -powered digital personas challenges
established boundaries of authenticity, trust, and ethical communication in digital ecosystems.
In the literature, the concept of virtual influencers is discussed from different perspectives,
including parasocial interaction (de Brito Silva et al., 2022; Stein et al., 2022), parasocial
relationship (Block & Lovegrove, 2021), anthropomorphism (da Silva Oliveira & Chimenti, 2021),
social actors (Ham et al., 2024), perception and attitude (Sands et al., 2022b), and differences
between virtual and human influencers (Belanche et al., 2024; Hernández -Méndez et al., 2024).

Studies also address their attributes, strengths, popularity, and potential risks (Sookkaew &
Saephoo, 2021; Feng et al., 2024; Allal -Chérif et al., 2024). Virtual influencers are distinguished
from human influencers in several respects, including their origins, social connections, availability,
physical appearance, credibility, and efficiency (Belanche et al., 2024). When a virtual influencer
is viewed as a technology, users tend to feel greater comfort and intimacy (Nah et al., 2022).
However, it has been predicted that increasing realism may trigger negative reactions, resulting in
feelings of uncanniness (Mori, 1970).  Kim et al. (2024) examined the issue from the perspective
of formal realism and stated that moderate realism can increase unsettlingness, while high realism
can decrease it. Additionally, they found that this has an impact on users' intentions to follow and
purchase. Liu and Wang (2025), in their study, showed that initial interactions are important and
decisive in building trust, while repeated interactions shift the basis of trust from appearance to
perceived competence and intentions. These findings also indicate that the uncanny valley effect
is less pronounced with relational depth. However, further empirical research us ing different
contexts is needed. The study further revealed that autonomy moderates the positive effects of
anthropomorphism, as consumers respond most favorably to influencers combining high
autonomy with strong human -likeness. In this sense, followers a ppear to perceive virtual
influencers as relational partners rather than merely human -like figures. Frankee and Groeppel -
Klein (2024) showed that human -like influencers are perceived as more trustworthy, whereas
cartoon-like influencers are viewed as more novel. In addition, consumers tend to be more
forgiving of virtual influencers than human influencers in cases of service failure, exhibiting more
positive behavioral responses and lower punishment intentions (Zhao et al., 2024). Despite the
growing importance of virtual influencers, literature often lacks a holistic analytical framework,
resulting in fragmented findings and conceptual inconsistencies. Therefore, a more integrated
theoretical approach is needed to synthesize these findings.
Attitudes towards virtual influencers may vary depending on consumers' cultural backgrounds.
For instance, individuals with a collectivist cultural background are more probable to follow a
virtual influencer and purchase products sponsored by them than those with an indi vidualistic
cultural background. In addition, individualistic countries with low uncertainty avoidance have
less positive attitudes towards virtual influencers, while collectivistic countries have more positive
attitudes (Rizzo et al., 2025). In addition, the acceptance process of virtual influencers by people
is affected by various aspects of digital culture in society (Azzahra & Christin, 2024).
Virtual influencers are also discussed from a digital sociology perspective. According to this
perspective, the technological environment does not come between the parties at the moment of
interaction; it is used for the interaction itself and is part of the interaction. Additionally, the digital
space is considered both as an object or technology and as a field of study (Sonzogni, 2022). Digital
sociology evaluates four basic dimensions: the use of digital tools, the use of digital technologies
and the analysis of the meanings that emerge, the us e of digital data and the critical analysis of
digital tools (Crescenzo, 2023).  Digital sociology provides a framework for examining  the
complex interactions between virtual influencers and users within the digital space, however, the
extant literature demonstrates a limited integration of this framework, particularly with respect to
persuasion processes and attitude formation.  In this context, the notion of parasocial interaction
assumes significance. In particular, individuals have the potential to establish an emotional bond

Virtual influencers are regarded as a post-human persona. Followers of virtual influencers have
been observed to engage in post -human role-playing and commitment to a post -human identity.
Consequently, post-human identities are not contingent upon physical embodiment in order to be
considered truly posthuman.  This indicates that posthumanism can also be created by fictional
characters in the online space (Richters, 2020). When evaluated from a posthumanism perspective,
individuals will either approach virtual influencers in a paranoid and negative manner due to the
frightening feelings triggered by the almost perfect human -like images, as mentioned above, or
they wi ll engage in parasocial interaction with the virtual influencer by establishing a more
appreciative and positive interaction (Luo & Kim, 2024). The concept of posthumanism allows for
the examination of the "non-human" agency of virtual influencers and expands the scope of virtual
influencers beyond biological constraints, enabling an examination of virtual influencers through
the lens of embodied and identity-based representation.
The rise of virtual influencer marketing has gained interest with studies foc using on defining
the basic characteristics of virtual influencers (Mouritzen  et al., 2023). The increase in studies in
the literature has also led to bibliometric analysis studies examining the general state of current
studies. Muslimah & Sunengsih (2023) conducted a study on journals indexed in Google Scholar
using the keywords “virtual influencer” and “artificial influencer.” In the study, the most cited
publications, influential researchers and prominent journals were identified, and the role of
artificial intelligence-supported virtual influencer use in marketing strategies was emphasized. In
addition, it was observed that topics such as company, consumer , communication, virtual
influencer marketing, review, influence  stand out, while topics such  as uncanny valley, digital
human, virtual reality and deepfake are not sufficiently studied. This situation shows that more
research is needed in these areas in the future. Pushparaj & Kushwaha (2023) conducted a study
using the keywords “virtual influenc ers,” in the Scopus database.  An increase in publications in
this field has been observed especially after 2018, and it has been determined that the USA,
Australia and the UK have made significant contributions to this field. While the prominent themes
include virtual/social media influencers, information disclosure, source credibility, parasocial
interaction, and authenticity & engagement; in recent years, the concepts of parasocial relationship,
persuasion information, authenticity, and trustworthiness have come to the fore. However, the
study has limitations such as being limited to the Scopus database, the keywords used exclude
some relevant studies, and the impact analysis is based only on the number of citations.
Nevertheless, the development of new di gital marketing technologies such as artificial
intelligence-supported chatbots, augmented reality, Metaverse and ChatGPT shows that there are
still questions and research opportunities waiting for answers in this field, and that the subject will
remain current and will be the subject of further research in the future.  Vila-López et al. (2023)
similarly conducted their research on the WoS database using keywords related to both virtual and
human influencers. This study conducts a more comprehensive research in terms of the databases
used and keyword queries. Studies published in WoS and Scopus were examined. No initiatives
have considered these concepts together  within the same analytical scope. In the study, the most

Bibliometric studies on virtual influencers have been found to address the topic narrowly, and
as a result, they tend to be predominantly commercial in nature. These studies have largely limited
their search queries by using the general keyword 'virtual in fluencer' and have based their
evaluations on marketing and consumer behavior disciplines. Therefore, the interdisciplinary
perspective of the concept is limited, and there is a limited body of research in the literature on
virtual influencers in the conte xt of human -computer interaction, digital sociology, and media
ethics. This study significantly expands the conceptual boundaries by using broader search queries
such as humanoid, zoomorphic, avatar, and AI -based influencers , thereby enabling a broader
examination of the virtual influencer concept, and identifying theoretical gaps. Furthermore, this
research aims to identify potential research areas for future researchers. To this end, the study is
guided by the following research questions:
(1) What are the prominent themes, key authors, and research trends in research on virtual
influencers in the global citation network WoS and Scopus?
(2) What conceptual gaps and emerging directions should future studies on virtual influencers aim
to explore?
Data and Methodology
Data selection
Queries were conducted in the WoS and Scopus databases. Additionally, a holistic perspective was
provided on the subject by considering keywords such as “artificial intelligence influencer,”
“avatar influencer,” “humanoid influencer,” “non -humanoid influencer,” “zoomorphic
influencer,” “animation influencer,” “ caricature influencer,” “cartoon influencer,” “mascot
influencer,” and “anime influencer,” which were not used in previous bibliometric analyses on
virtual influencers. The keywords were chosen to represent the types of virtual influencers in the
literature. This study aims to analyze the breadth of the literature and reveal trends towards
different types of influencers. The keywords were queried in the relevant databases covering all
time periods.  A review of the literature demonstrates that virtual influe ncers are typically
categorized into two forms: human (e.g., humanoid influencers) and non-human (e.g., zoomorphic)
(Audrezet, 2025). In addition, some studies adopt the term “artificial influencer” as an alternative
to “virtual influencer.” In this study,  both “virtual influencer” and its alternative terms found in
the literature are included as keywords.
In the research, a search strategy using Boolean logic and the 'OR' operator was implemented
to reduce selection bias. The search query was: "virtual inf luencer*" OR "artificial intelligence
influencer*" OR "artificial influencer*" OR "online influencer*" OR "avatar influencer*" OR
"humanoid influencer*" OR "non -humanoid influencer*" OR "zoomorphic influencer*" OR

To guarantee the transparency and reproducibility of the data collection process, strict inclusion
and exclusion criteria were established. The inclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) studies
published in peer-reviewed journals, conference proceedings, book chapters, or available as early
access; (2) studies published in the English language; (3) documents published up to the year 2024.
The exclusion criteria involved: (1) non -academic grey literature, editorial materials, and book
reviews; (2) studies published in languages other than English (as noted in the limitations,
excluding a minor portion of non -English records such as Korean or Russian studies); and (3)
documents that, upon manual screening of titles and abstracts, did not centrally focus on the virtual
influencer phenomenon.
An analytical process was followed to create the final dataset. First, raw bibliographic data from
the databases (WoS  and Scopus) were imported into RStudio. The raw datasets from the two
databases were combined, and duplicate records were removed. The Bibliometrix package was
used at this stage. A manual validation step was applied. Exclusion criteria were also applied,
resulting in a purified dataset (a total of 250 documents). This data cleaning process ensured that
the bibliometric analysis was based on a reliable and consistent dataset.
Methodology
The data obtained was reported using bibliometric analysis.  Bibliometric analysis is a systematic
literature review method that aims to reveal patterns, trends and impacts within a specific field
(Passas, 2024). It is defined as the application of mathematical and statistical techniques to books,
articles and other communic ation media in order to quantitatively assess the productivity of
scientific output (Dervis, 2019; Donthu et al., 2021; Lim & Kumar, 2023). This method is accepted
as both a popular and rigorous approach for discovering and analyzing emerging research area s
(Donthu et al., 2021). The primary rationale for using bibliometric analysis in a study lies in the
need to quantitatively evaluate scientific output and related literature, as well as to handle large
volumes of scientific data (Ellegaard & Wallin, 2015;  Donthu et al., 2021; Passas, 2024) . This
method, which provides a quantitative assessment of written publications, has become an integral
part of research evaluation methodologies (Ellegaard & Wallin, 2015; Dervis, 2019). Researchers
use bibliometric analysis to identify trends in academic production, collaboration models, and
research components; to track popular topics and temporal developments within scientific
domains; and to explore emerging fields  (Donthu et al., 2021; Passas, 2024) . Furthermore, it is
used to assess the impact and productivity of publications, researchers, institutions, and countries,
with metrics such as citation counts, the h -index, co -authored publications, and international
collaborations being commonly considered  (Ananda & N andiyanto, 2022; Ellegaard & Wallin,
2015; Passas, 2024). Unlike traditional literature review methods, bibliometric analysis focuses on
statistical data, providing a more objective and quantitative summary of the literature and
facilitating the understand ing of large -scale scholarly output independent of subjective

interpretations (Ellegaard & Wallin, 2015; Donthu et al., 2021) . The development and increased
accessibility of databases such as Web of Science (WoS), Scopus, and Google Scholar, along with
open-source software tools such as Bibliometrix, VOSviewer, CiteSpace, and R, have made it
easier to collect, analyze, and visualize large datasets, thereby contributing to the growing
popularity of bibliometric methods . These tools rely on two main approache s: performance
analysis (examining the contributions of research components) and science mapping (exploring
the relationships among research components through techniques such as citation analysis, co -
citation analysis, bibliographic coupling, keyword anal ysis, and co-authorship analysis) (Dervis,
2019; Donthu et al., 2021; Passas, 2024) . In conclusion, studies that use bibliometric analysis
generally aim to systematically and quantitatively examine large bodies of scientific data in order
to uncover research trends, evaluate impact, and understand the structure of scholarly
communication (Donthu et al., 2021; Passas, 2024).
The Bibliometrix package in RStudio was used for the analyses. The standard process of
bibliometric analysis includes study design, data collection, data analysis, data visualization, and
interpretation (Xie et al., 2020). Accurate mapping of literature studies is considered important for
classifying and visualizing research contributions and the network of relevant contributors (Gzahli
et al., 2022). This method is designed to analyze the academic impact and characteristics of
publications in a research area (Qu et al., 2018).

is 18,8%. Among the 250 published studies, 170 are articles, 26 are early access publications, 26

Discussion and conclusion
Overview: The bibliometric analysis reveals  250 studies published between 2010 and 2024 ,
predominantly consisting of articles (170), followed by books, book chapters, and conference
proceedings. The first studies were published in 2010, and although the highest number of
publications was reached in 2023, the number of citations reached its highest level in 2016.  The
temporal gap between 2016 and 2023 stems from the asynchronous progress of the application of
technological advancements and the research that examines these advancements. Studies in 2016
established the foundational definitions, typologies, and theoretical frameworks shaping the initial
conceptualization of virtual influencers (Sokolo va & Kefi, 2020) . Initially characterized by a
predominantly theoretical, conceptual, and exploratory orientation, this early approach gradually
gave way, by 2023, to more data-intensive and empirical research as virtual influencers reached a
greater level of technological maturity. In particular, technological breakthroughs in areas such as
AI-generated content and the metaverse expanded the practical relevance of virtual influencers.
Concurrently,the increasing strategic partnerships between businesses and virtual influencers have
made the topic attractive and researchable from a marketing perspective. This has been the driving
force behind the increase in publication volume in 2023.  Therefore, while the topic was initially
approached in a more theoretical and conceptual way, it is now more empirical and data-driven.
Virtual influencers play a significant role in transforming marketing, technology, and digital
culture, with applications expected to expand beyond social media branding into autonomous AI
services, entertainment, and education. Their growing integration into digital ecosystems, such as
metaverse or live streaming platforms, challenges conventional concepts of authenticity , agency,

increasing autonomy will not only reshape marketing strategies but also generate new ethical,

This study does not approach virtual influencers solely from a commercial perspective, as has
been the focus of many previous studies, but also conceptualizes them from a more holistic
perspective by considering socio -cognitive, digital -sociological, and p ost-humanist viewpoints.
Through this research, we redefine the concept as follows: Virtual influencers are not merely
computer-generated fictional characters, but rather autonomous socio -technical actors who,
algorithmically guided and independent of biological constraints, create post-human identities and
actively reshape trust, authenticity, and cultural norms in the relationship between brands and
consumers. This definition provides an ontological foundation to overcome conceptual blind spots
concerning the uncanny valley, digital sociology, and cultural diversity.

reactions to virtual influencers. While the Uncanny Valley theory posits that human -like virtual
influencers evoke discomfort or eeriness in individuals (Mori, 1970), studies have shown that
repeated interactions with those with human -like appearances gradually diminish the importance
of appearance, shifting trust formation to perceptions of competence and conte nt quality (Liu &
Wang, 2025). This unresolved paradox reveals that temporal dynamics remain significantly under-
theorized in current human -AI relationality models. Furthermore, there is a notable silence in
literature regarding virtual influencers. Critic al concepts such as the Uncanny Valley, digital
human, and deepfake, which play a fundamental role in interpreting technological mediation and
authenticity, appear to be underrepresented in the field's thematic structure (Muslimah and
Sunengsih, 2023). Fur thermore, the ethical risks associated with authority, expertise, and AI -
mediated persuasion in research on the virtual influencers, particularly in sensitive areas such as
health communication, have not yet received full attention (Holden et al., 2025). T hese tensions,
omissions, and silences, which create conceptual gaps in literature, highlight the need for a more
critical and theoretically integrated approach to addressing virtual influencers from the perspective
of psychological, technological, and socio-ethical dimensions.
The bibliometric findings reflect not only the technological maturation of AI but also the deeper
epistemological, cultural, and political forces shaping the virtual influencer discourse. These
studies are centered around a dual logi c: i) strategic marketing and trust, which focuses on
profitability, behavioral impact, and platform -based interaction; and ii) a socio -cognitive
perspective, which prioritizes anthropomorphism and user perception. This structure reveals that
while researc h is grounded in commercial imperatives, critical concepts essential to digital
authenticity (such as the Uncanny Valley, deepfakes, and ontological agency ) remain
marginalized. Furthermore, the higher acceptance of virtual influencers in collectivist soci eties
with established anime/manga traditions suggests that user responses are culturally coded rather
than universal. The concentration of influential academic research in the US, UK, and China,
coupled with the rapid proliferation of generative artificia l intelligence and synthetic media
technologies, reveals political -economic asymmetries that prioritize technology - and market -
focused research. These emerging patterns, when considered overall, suggest that studies in

The acceptance of technological systems as relational partners in human -computer interaction
has been demonstrated in that individuals experience greater comfort and  intimacy when they
perceive virtual influencers as technological yet socially expressive entities (Nah et al., 2022; Ham
et al., 2024). This suggests that individuals develop similar bonds with virtual influencers as human
influencers. It has been noted t hat these bonds can vary depending on the context and that more
intense parasocial bonds can be established (Um, 2023; Kadirov et al., 2014). These findings align
with research demonstrating followers' expectations that virtual influencers behave as autonomous
and human-like media personalities. Finally, philosophical and sociological perspectives expand
the scope of human-computer interaction by identifying virtual influencers as actors embedded in
digital social structures. In this context, digital sociol ogy posits that technology forms part of the
interactional fabric rather than a mediation layer (Sonzogni, 2022). Post-humanist explanations of
virtual influencers conceptualize them as non -human agents capable of supporting posthuman
identity play and attachment (Richters, 2020). This allows for the expansion of virtual influencers
beyond biological constraints and the reinforcement of their role as complex objects of digital
culture. International collaboration and interdisciplinary studies on the topic h ave led to the view
of virtual influencers as socio -technical entities that illuminate the fundamental assumptions of
marketing tools and human-technology interaction.
Interactions with virtual influencers are technologically driven by rapid advances in artificial
intelligence, including natural language processing, speech and image recognition, problem -
solving, and machine learning. These advances have strengthened the individual–virtual influencer
connection by enabling virtual influencers to provide more natural, responsive, and socially
expressive interactions (Kietzmann et al., 2018; Nukhu et al., 2024). The use of virtual and AI -
powered influencers has expanded acro ss sectors thanks to the integration of AI (Jin and
Viswanathan, 2025; Sands et al., 2022a; Thomas and Fowler, 2021; Ying, 2024). Furthermore,
advances in gesture and emotion recognition have increased the level of realism of digital humans

Virtual influencers reflect the transformation in societal values regarding ethics, identity,
authenticity, and digital trust. Consumers’ increasing expectations for corporate social
responsibility, inclusivity, and transparency have led virtual influencer s to become symbolic
representatives of these values. They are designed to promote diversity, cultural values, and
environmentally responsible behaviors. This demonstrates that virtual influencers are being
positioned as tools for conveying the ethical expectations emerging within digital culture (Bo -

privacy, algorithmic bias, and regulatory oversight. Society’s normative frameworks are

distinctions between authenticity and simulation, contributing to a societal shift toward more fluid
understandings of personhood and presence (Audrezet et al., 2025).  In the realm of digital
consumption, VIs respond to —and reinforce —new expectations for personalized, emotionally
resonant, and scalable interactions.  Virtual influencers enable businesses to deliver consistent
communication messages, provide real -time feedback, and operate without temporal limitations,
thereby enabling seamless personalization and high -quality interact ion (Bo -Chiuan, 2025;
Audrezet et al., 2025; Theodorakopoulos et al., 2025).  Trust, a central value in digital

The dominance of consumer behavior and marketing in literature on virtual influencers also
limits the theoretical scope of the topic. Therefore, broader interdisciplinary frameworks (e.g.,
ethical, sociological, psychological, post -humanist, feminist techno science, or critical algorithm

contribute to a broader perspective on issues such as identity, representation, agency, and epistemic

highlights the need for culturally diverse and comparative studies (Chiu & Ho, 2023; Kim & Park,

cartoon-like, mascot, or zoomorphic figures). This provides limited insight into how algorithmic
content production shapes identity construction. While research has been conducted to uncover the
dynamics of long-term interaction with virtual influ encers, more research is still needed on how
repeated interactions influence trust formation and user experience (Cheung & Yeung, 2021; Zhou
et al., 2024; Liu & Wang, 2025).  Consequently, a more comprehensive research agenda is
required—one that integrates  interdisciplinary theories, cross -cultural comparisons, socio -
technical perspectives, and algorithm -centered analyses —to more fully explain the virtual
influencer phenomenon.
Cultural variations significantly influence the acceptance of virtual influencer s, perceptions of
authenticity, and expectations regarding form and behavior. Consequently, cross -cultural
comparisons are essential to elucidating the cultural norms virtual influencers represent across
diverse contexts. Research in this area will contribute to consumer culture theory. In addition, the
acceptance of and interaction with virtual influencers also vary across demographic groups.
Examining this issue from a demographic perspective can reveal how intimacy, identity
identification, and expectati ons of authenticity are shaped by theories such as parasocial
interaction, the uncanny valley, and anthropomorphism.
Evaluating the analysis findings on the basis of Granular Interaction Thinking Theory (GITT)
will contribute to a better understanding of the literature on virtual influencers. GITT  guides our
understanding of individuals' thoughts, feelings, and behaviors. According to this theory,
individuals filter out conflicting or irrelevant information, formulate their beliefs, and make
decisions based on reliable and relevant information. Trustworthiness is a filtering mechanism that
reduces uncertainty in individuals' decision-making processes. The presence of higher information
entropy (i.e., uncertainty) demands greater cognitive effort. In Shannon 's (1948) formulation, the
presence of more information in the mind results in higher information entropy (uncertainty and
unpredictability). Individuals are more likely to reject misleading information, but they are also
more likely to accept information that is perceived as credible. If individuals' trust is compromised,
their interest in participating may decline. When they receive relevant and practical information,
individuals internalize it, their perceptions gradually change, and their willingness to  evaluate
participation increases. It is assumed that if information that emotionally resonates with individuals
aligns with their core values (such as curiosity, excitement, or adventure), then that information is
more likely to be absorbed. Furthermore, the congruency between individuals' experiences and

personal values increases the acceptance of the information and their willingness to participate.
According to GITT, there are two basic cognitive processes. These are: i) trust assessment, which
reduces uncertainty in accordance with information reliability, and ii) cost -benefit assessment
(Nguyen et al., 2025). Considering GITT, it can be argued that researchers conduct their research
through cognitive filtering. In particular, the fact that the most cit ed researchers are based in the
West, and citing researchers perceive Western publications as more credible and useful due to the
West's dominance in the field, suggests they internalize that information and use it in their studies.
According to GITT, the human mind accepts information it perceives as trustworthy. Therefore,
trustworthiness functions as a filtering mechanism in the decision -making process. Similarly, the
concentration of studies in marketing and consumer behavior journals and their commercial focus
may lead researchers to internalize information in this field more quickly than others due to their
extensive interest and practical knowledge in this field, changing their perceptions and fostering a
desire to conduct research in this field. This situation leads to a narrow epistemological perspective
on reliable information about virtual influencers. While a narrow epistemological perspective on
the field reduces the epistemic knowledge entropy of that perspective, it also increases the
knowledge entropy of the keywords, aspects, ideas, and theories missing from the literature
discussed above. This will, in turn, allow researchers to address the identified research gaps,
increasing the amount of epistemic entropy-reducing information on virtual influencers, fostering
a more comprehensive understanding, and enriching the literature across disciplines and cultures.

Future research direction and limitations
The reviewed studies make several suggestions for future research. First, it is suggested that
research on virtual influencers should be diversified in terms of different cultures (Allal-Chérif et
al., 2024; Chiu & Ho, 2023; Kim & Park, 2024; Koles et al., 2024; Sokolova & Kefi, 2020; Stein
et al., 2022; Xie-Carson et al., 2023). In addition, it is stated that resources on the subject can be
enriched by examining their effects on different age groups (Kim & Park, 2024; Stein et al., 2022).
For experimental studies on virtual influencers, it is suggested that research can be conducted in
terms of different genders (Kim & Park, 2024); various character designs such as anime (Kim &
Quan, 2024; Zhou et al., 2024), cartoon, human -like (Kim & Park, 2024). In addit ion, it is stated
that more data collection is needed for studies such as the comparison of virtual influencers in
anime and human-like appearance (Kim & Quan, 2024; Lou et al., 2023). Researchers recommend
the use of different social media platforms (TikT ok, Youtube, Instagram) in studies on virtual
influencers (Mrad et al., 2022; Sokolova & Kefi, 2020; Stein et al., 2022; Xin et al., 2024; Zhou
et al., 2024). Studies examining the brand perception of virtual influencer use for different sectors
such as fashion, beauty, lifestyle (Akhtar et al., 2024; Allal-Chérif et al., 2024; Franke et al., 2023;
Kim & Park, 2024; Liu & Lee, 2024) can be multiplied. In addition to these sectors, researchers
have suggested that the gaming, entertainment and travel sectors would be more suitable for virtual
influencers (Akhtar et al., 2024; Allal-Chérif et al., 2024; Franke et al., 2023). Another suggestion
of the researchers was to examine the interactions with virtual influencers over longer periods of
time (Cheung & Yeung , 2021; Kim et al., 2023; Molin & Nordgren, 2019; Zhou et al., 2024).
