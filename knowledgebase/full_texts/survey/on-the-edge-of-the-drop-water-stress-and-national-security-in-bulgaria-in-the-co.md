Teodor DASKALOV

*tdaskalov@feb.uni-sofia.bg

Abstract. Water stress is increasingly recognized as a critical challenge for public governance, social
stability, and national security. While it is often addressed primarily as a technical or environmental issue,
its social, behavioral, and institutional dimension s receive comparatively less attention. This paper
examines water stress in Bulgaria as a complex socio -political risk arising from the interaction between
physical resource constraints, governance capacity, public behavior, and institutional trust. The st udy
adopts an interdisciplinary analytical approach combining a structured literature review, comparative
analysis of international water crisis cases, and an indicative empirical survey assessing public awareness
and behavioral responses to water stress. The analysis highlights how deficiencies in institutional
communication, transparency, and demand management can amplify societal impacts, even where physical
water availability is not critically constrained. Building on these findings, the paper proposes an integrated
analytical framework that structures water stress governance across three interrelated phases: prevention,
crisis management, and long -term adaptation (the PCA model). Within this framework, marketing -based
and behavioral governance instruments are conceptualized as mechanisms linking technical measures with
public behavior and institutional legitimacy, rather than as substitutes for infrastructural or regulatory
solutions. The results indicate substantial adaptive potential at the societal level, reflected in a willingness
to change behavior that remains underutilized due to fragmented institutional signals and limited public
trust. The PCA model is presented as an analytical, not causally tested, framework, offering transferable
insights for adaptive water governance where critical infrastructure management intersects with social trust
and behavioral change.

Keywords: Water stress; National security; Public governance; Behavioral change; Social marketing;
Institutional trust.

Introduction
Water stress acquires specific characteristics in the Bulgarian context. Significant losses in
the water supply network, aging infrastructure, and limited capacity for preventive planning further
exacerbate system vulnerability. Water supply restrictions and crisis situations - including the cases
in cities of Pernik (2019 -2020) and Pleven (2024 -2025) - demonstrate that the problem is not
isolated, but structural and recurrent.
Water stress, however, cannot be reduced to physical resource scarcity only. Water supply
restrictions affect institutional trust, perceptions of fairness in public policies, and citizens’
willingness to comply with imposed measures. In this way, water str ess is transformed from an
environmental and infrastructural challenge into a socio-political risk with potential consequences
for social stability and national security.
Dominant water governance policies traditionally emphasize infrastructure investments,
regulation, and emergency measures.  Although technical and regulatory instruments are necessary,
their effectiveness in crisis situations is strongly mediated by public behavior, perceived

In the present study, marketing is not employed in a typical commercial sense, nor as an
umbrella concept for all non -technical governance measures. Instead, it is approached as an
integrative governance framework that coordinates instruments of different theoretical origins -
social marketing, behavioral interventions, public communication, and mechanisms for citizen
participation - insofar as they are directed toward influencing demand, behavior, and institutional
trust in the governance of critical resources.
Building on this understanding, the article proposes the conceptual Prevention -Crisis-
Adaptation (PCA) model, which structures water stress governance into three interrelated phases:
prevention, crisis management, and long -term adaptation. The model has a conceptual-analytical
character and serves to systematize and interpret empirical observations, without claiming causal
empirical testing within the scope of the present study.
The PCA model addresses the question of how and to what extent marketing -based and
behavioral governance instruments can support adaptive water management and reduce the risks
that water stress poses to social stability and national security in Bulgaria.
The research problem addressed in this study concerns the lack of systematic integration of
marketing-based and behavioral instruments in the governance of water stress, despite their
demonstrated effectiveness in international practice. In Bulgarian public policies, such instruments
are applied in a fragmented and reactive manner and are rarely conceptualized as part of a broader
framework of national security and sustainable governance of critical infrastructure.
The aim of this study is to analyze the relationship between water stress and national
security in Bulgaria by examining the role of marketing -based and behavioral instruments in the
adaptive governance of water resources.
The main objectives of the article are: to clarify how water stress is transformed from an
environmental and infrastructural challenge into a socio -political risk for national security; to
assess the limitations of traditional governance approaches based p redominantly on technical and
regulatory measures; to analyze the potential of marketing -based and behavioral instruments as
governance mechanisms influencing consumption patterns, public trust, and institutional
resilience; to propose an integrated concep tual framework for the adaptive governance of water
stress, applicable to the Bulgarian context.

Literature review
Water stress is traditionally defined as a condition in which available water resources are
insufficient to meet the needs of households, the economy, and ecosystems within a given temporal
and spatial context (UNESCO/WWAP, 2021). The operationalization of the concept encompasses
both quantitative indicators - such as the ratio between water abstraction and available resources,
the frequency of droughts, and access to safe drinking water - as well as qualitative dimensions
related to institutional capacity and resource governance (European Environment Agency, 2023).
In international and institutional frameworks, water stress is commonly operationalized
through standardized indicators that capture the balance between water availability and
withdrawals. SDG indicator 6.4.2 defines water stress as the ratio of total fres hwater withdrawals
to available renewable freshwater resources, accounting for environmental flow requirements
(United Nations, (2021). Similarly, the Water Exploitation Index Plus (WEI+), widely used by the
European Environment Agency, measures pressure o n water resources by integrating abstraction

Conceptually, water stress differs from water scarcity, which refers to absolute or structural
shortages of water resources, and from water security, which encompasses the capacity of societies
and institutions to ensure reliable access to water of adequate quantity and quality while managing
related risks. In this study, water stress is treated as a social governance risk that links physical
resource pressure with managеment capacity, social behavior, and institutional trust, thereby
connecting scarcity dynamics to broader water security outcomes.
In contemporary scientific literature, water stress is examined as a multidimensional
problem that extends beyond physical scarcity to encompass governance-related, institutional, and
social dimensions. Foundational WOS -indexed studies frame water stress a s a systemic risk to
water security, arising from the interaction between hydrological pressure, governance
performance, and social vulnerability (Vörösmarty et al., 2010; Mekonnen & Hoekstra, 2016). At
the same time, bibliometric and systematic reviews in dicate that dominant research strands and
indicator-based frameworks —including SDG indicator 6.4.2 and the Water Exploitation Index
Plus (WEI+) —provide a robust quantitative foundation but rarely integrate behavioral,
communication, and marketing -based mechanisms for demand management (Aguirre & Paredes
Cuervo, 2023; FAO, 2024; Sondermann, 2022). This gap motivates the analytical focus and
research objective of the present study.
In the European context, intensifying droughts and extreme climatic events further
exacerbate these dynamics. Data from the European Environment Agency indicates that an
increasing proportion of EU territory is exposed to high levels of water stress, with even
traditionally resource -rich regions experiencing growing vulnerability (European Environment
Agency, 2023). In this sense, water stress is increasingly established as a structural risk rather than
a temporary deviation.
The literature on critical infrastructure governance increasingly conceptualizes water
supply systems as a key element of national security due to their potential to generate cascading
effects in the event of system failure or prolonged service restriction s (European Commission,
2022). Disruptions in water supply can lead to public health risks, economic losses, disruptions to
the normal functioning of public services, and heightened social tension (OECD, 2022;
UNESCO/WWAP, 2021).
Empirical studies demonstrate that limited governance capacity - such as aging
infrastructure, high levels of non -revenue water, lack of coordination, and delayed institutional
responses - often amplify the effects of natural stressors and transform water stress into a full-scale
crisis (OECD, 2022; World Bank Group, 2021). In this context, water security cannot be achieved
solely through supply augmentation or technical optimization if these measures are not
accompanied by effective demand management and the alignment of public expectations.
A key contribution of contemporary literature lies in the recognition that water crises
constitute not only technical failures, but also communicative and behavioral breakdowns. Water
supply restrictions imply the need for broad public compliance, which ca nnot be sustainably
achieved solely through coercive measures or administrative prohibitions (Tortajada & Biswas,
2018).
The absence of transparent and timely communication, unclear rules regarding the
allocation of restrictions, and the perceived unfairness of implemented measures often lead to a
decline in institutional trust and increased social tension, which in turn amp lifies risks to national

From this perspective, water stress may be interpreted as a trigger for governance failure
when institutional responses are delayed, opaque, or perceived as unjust. Consequently, water
governance becomes a component of the broader framework of societal and institutional resilience.
For the purposes of the present analysis, it is necessary to draw a clear distinction between
the different groups of instruments that are often subsumed under the general label of “marketing
based.” Social marketing aims at long-term change in social norms, while behavioral interventions
originate from behavioral economics and seek to influence specific decision -making processes.
Public communication focuses on legitimacy and risk management, whereas mechanisms of
participation and transparency are grounde d in the literature on public governance. Within the
scope of this article, these instruments are analyzed jointly not because of their conceptual
equivalence, but due to their shared functional role in the governance of water stress.
Against this background, a growing body of research highlights the importance of
behavioral and marketing -based instruments as complements to traditional water management
policies. Behavioral interventions, including social comparisons, consumption feedbac k, and
message framing - have demonstrated measurable effects on reducing water consumption,
particularly when supported by institutional legitimacy (Ferraro & Price, 2013).
Social marketing extends this approach by emphasizing audience segmentation, an understanding
of the motivations and barriers to behavioral change, and the formation of long-term social norms
(Kotler & Lee, 2008). In the context of water crises, marketing -based instruments function as
mechanisms for demand management and for sustaining public trust, rather than as one -way
information campaigns.
International cases from Cape Town and California clearly demonstrate that the
combination of regulatory measures, price signals, and targeted communication can lead to rapid
and sustained changes in consumer behavior (Hanak & Mount, 2015; Tortajada & Bisw as, 2018;
Rodina, 2019). These examples illustrate that marketing -based instruments constitute a key
component of adaptive governance, particularly under crisis conditions.
Despite this, in Bulgaria and more broadly across Southeast Europe, marketing -based and
behavioral instruments remain weakly integrated into national water security strategies. Analytical
and policy approaches remain largely dominated by technical and regu latory perspectives, while
instruments aimed at shaping public behavior and strengthening institutional legitimacy are only
marginally integrated.
 While standardized indicators such as SDG 6.4.2 and WEI+ are central to international
monitoring frameworks, they offer limited insight into behavioral responses, communication
dynamics, and institutional legitimacy. This study addresses this gap by complementing indicator-
based assessments with an analytical framework that explicitly integrates behavioral and
marketing-based governance instruments.
The present study addresses this gap by proposing a structured analytical model that
integrates marketing -based governance instruments into water stress governance as a national
security risk. The model embeds these instruments across the phases of prevent ion, crisis
management, and adaptation within the specific institutional and infrastructural context of
Bulgaria.

Within the scope of the present study, key concepts are employed as analytical categories rather
than as strictly quantitatively operationalized variables. “Water stress” is analytically examined
using a set of proxy indicators, including the frequency of water supply interruptions, spatial
differences in resource availability, and perceived scarcity at the household level. “National
security” is approached in terms of social stability, institutional reliability, and the governance of
critical infrastructur e, while “marketing -based instruments” are analytically operationalized
through the identification of behavioral interventions, communication strategies, and mechanisms
for public participation applied across the different phases of the Prevention -Crisis-Adaptation
model (Figure 1).
The main research hypothesis posits that technical and regulatory measures are insufficient
for the effective governance of water stress unless they are complemented by marketing-based and
behavioral instruments aimed at managing demand, public attitudes, and trust in institutions.

Data Sources
The study draws on three data groups: (1) secondary sources, including international and national
strategic and policy documents, institutional reports, and statistical data issued by the European
Commission, the European Environment Agency, the OECD, UNES CO, and the World Bank, as
well as national policy documents and official statistics from the Ministry of Environment and
Water, the National Statistical Institute, and the National Institute of Meteorology and Hydrology,
complemented by peer -reviewed acad emic literature on water security, critical infrastructure
governance, and behavioral interventions in resource management; (2) comparative case evidence
on water stress governance practices in Cape Town and California, compiled from publicly
available policy documents, institutional reports, and academic studies; and (3) primary empirical
data from an author -administered survey titled “Consumer Experience and Awareness of Water
Stress” (n = 101). The selection of institutional and academic sources is intended to minimize bias
and to support comparability across international and national contexts.

Empirical Survey
The empirical survey aims to examine respondents’ level of awareness and understanding of the
concept of water stress, their personal experience with water scarcity, and the behavioral responses
adopted in such contexts, including the persistence of these responses over time. In addition, the
survey explores perceptions of institutional actions and communication related to water stress, as
well as respondents’ willingness to modify their behavior when provided with more comprehensive
and structured information.
The sample consists of 101 respondents, and the questionnaire includes both closed-ended
and open -ended questions. The quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistical
techniques, while qualitative responses were examined through thematic anal ysis in order to
identify recurring patterns, perceptions, and attitudes.
The sample includes respondents from different age groups and regions of the country, with
a predominance of urban residents and individuals with secondary and higher levels of education.
Due to the voluntary nature of participation and the limited sample size, the findings cannot be
interpreted as representative of the national population. Instead, they provide indicative insights
into attitudes and behavioral responses within specific social groups.
The empirical component of the study has an exploratory and indicative character and does not aim
at national-level representativeness. This methodological choice is deliberate and consistent with
the overall objective of the article, which is to develop a n analytical framework and a conceptual

Methods of Analysis and Data Validation
The methodological design integrates three complementary analytical layers: a structured synthesis
of the conceptual and policy evidence, a comparative interpretation of reference cases, and a
descriptive statistical assessment of the Bulgarian context. Th is design supports an integrated
reading of water stress as both an institutional challenge and a behavioral governance problem,
while maintaining a clear distinction between conceptual inference and empirical description.
First, the literature and policy evidence was reviewed with the purpose of consolidating
working definitions and identifying recurring explanatory mechanisms relevant to the study. Rather
than reiterating the full state of the art, the review was used to extract the key constructs applied in
the paper - water stress, water security, critical infrastructure vulnerability, and marketing -based
instruments for behavioral governance - and to establish the conceptual links through which water
stress can translate into socio-economic disruption and security-relevant risks.
Second, the analysis employs a qualitative comparative interpretation of two reference
cases - Cape Town and California - focused on the governance instruments deployed under water
stress and the documented behavioral outcomes. The objective is not institu tional benchmarking,
but the identification of transferable mechanisms (e.g., demand management, trust -building,
transparency, targeted behavioral interventions) that can inform the Bulgarian context
conceptually. The qualitative content analysis proceeds through systematic thematic structuring of
evidence from documents and peer -reviewed studies, maintaining traceability of the analytical
steps without formalized coding.
Third, the empirical component applies descriptive statistical analysis to selected indicators
for Bulgaria, including the scale of water supply restrictions, their spatial distribution, and survey-
based measures of awareness, experience, and behavioral intention. The purpose is to illustrate the
magnitude and distributional profile of water stress, and to provide empirical anchors for the
conceptual argument, rather than to test causal hypotheses. Data processing and visualization were
performed using Microsoft Excel and IBM SPSS.
To support analytical robustness, the analysis relies on consistency checks across sources
and methods: convergence between institutional statistics and policy narratives, alignment between
the Bulgarian indicators and patterns observed in reference cases, and internal coherence between
closed-ended survey results and open -ended responses. This triangulated logic strengthens
interpretive robustness while acknowledging the exploratory nature of empirical evidence.

Methodological Limitations
The empirical survey has an exploratory character and does not claim statistical representativeness
at the national level. Nevertheless, it provides valuable empirical insights into consumers’ attitudes,
behaviors, and perceptions and serves as a basis for formulating governance- and communication-
related recommendations.
The methodological choices are directly aligned with the research questions of the article:
the analysis of policies and literature addresses the relationship between water stress and national
security; the comparative analysis of international cases suppo rts the identification of applicable
governance instruments; and the quantitative data and survey results provide an empirical
foundation for interpreting behavioral responses in the Bulgarian context.

Results and discussions
The discussion interprets the presented results considering the existing theoretical and governance
frameworks, without claiming quantitative testing of causal relationships.
Data on the hydrogeographic distribution of water resources reveals a pronounced spatial
mismatch between resource availability and demographic and economic concentration. Regions
with the highest relative water yield are characterized by low population density, whereas densely
populated and economically active areas are more vulnerable to water stress, regardless of overall
national resource endowment (see Table 1). This pattern underscores the structural nature of
vulnerability and limits the interpretation of water stress as a problem of physical scarcity alone.
The results of the survey “Consumer Experience and Awareness of Water Stress” (n = 101)
provide an indicative empirical basis for analyzing the social dimensions of water stress. The data
show that the concept of “water stress” is relatively recognizable: 60% of respondents report having
heard of it, and nearly half assess their level of knowledge as good or very good. At the same time,
a substantial share of participants demonstrates a fragmented understanding of the concept,
pointing to a deficit in systematic and institutionally supported communication.
Particularly revealing are the data on personal experience with water scarcity.
Approximately 80% of respondents report frequent or temporary problems with water supply,
confirming that water stress in Bulgaria is a lived reality for a significant portion of the population.
This lends the problem a clear social dimension and helps explain its potential to evolve into a
source of social tension under conditions of ineffective governance.
At the same time, the results reveal a pronounced deficit of institutional trust. More than
three quarters of respondents assess the actions of local authorities and water utilities as
insufficient, while 84% describe institutional communication as weak or  entirely lacking. These
assessments are consistent with international studies indicating that governance and
communication deficits often amplify the social effects of water stress, regardless of the actual
degree of resource scarcity.
Alongside the survey data, trends in bottled mineral water consumption in Bulgaria show a
sustained upward trajectory over the period 2018-2023, with a marked acceleration after 2021 (see
Figure 2).
The increasing consumption of bottled water can be interpreted as an indirect behavioral
indicator of perceived insecurity regarding the quality and reliability of public water supply.  Тhe
available data does not allow for a direct causal link between resource scarcity and individual
consumer decisions. However, the coincidence of more frequent water supply restrictions,
intensified public debates on water infrastructure, and the sharp increase in consumption after 2021
suggests the emergence of individual compensatory strategies.
The survey data indicates a high declared readiness for behavioral adaptation within the
examined sample, which may be interpreted as an indication of an existing potential for change in
consumer behavior. A substantial proportion of respondents report sus tained changes in their
water-saving habits, including after the subsiding of crisis periods.

in the absence of targeted, consistent, and trusted institutional signals capable of channeling this

The present analysis employs empirical results to analytically illustrate the PCA model
rather than to subject it to formal testing. The data allows for tracing how different governance and
behavioral processes are positioned across the phases of preventio n, crisis management, and
adaptation.
In the prevention phase, the limited and fragmented understanding of water stress, combined with
a declared readiness for informed behavior, highlights the role of systematic communication and
social marketing. The absence of such instruments increases the  likelihood that resource pressure
will escalate into a crisis.
The crisis management phase is characterized by high sensitivity to institutional
communication and the perceived fairness of implemented measures. The empirical results indicate
that, in the presence of deficits in transparency and timely information, eve n technically well -
founded restrictions may lead to a decline in public trust and the emergence of social tension.
In the adaptation phase, sustained behavioral changes and individual coping strategies point to
potential for long -term transformation. However, without integration into a broader institutional
framework, such adaptations remain fragmented and do not reduce systemic vulnerability.
The comparative analysis of international and Bulgarian cases demonstrates that effective water
stress governance involves distinct interventions across the phases of prevention, crisis, and
adaptation, with marketing-based governance instruments playing a key role in each of them (see
Table 2).
 Quantitative assessments of the effects of behavioral and marketing-based interventions on
water consumption are available for international cases such as Cape Town and California;
however, comparable systematic data are lacking in the Bulgarian context. F or this reason, the
present study treats these instruments as conceptually grounded hypotheses for future testing,
without assuming direct institutional policy transfer.
Accordingly, the effectiveness of marketing -based governance instruments should be
understood as conditional and context-dependent. It is strongly influenced by levels of institutional
trust, administrative capacity, and the degree of public legitimacy of policy measures, which
constitute significant constraints in the Bulgarian case.
The PCA model complements indicator -based monitoring (e.g., SDG 6.4.2, WEI+) by
specifying governance and behavioral mechanisms through which pressure translates into socio -
political risk.
The integration of empirical observations into the PCA model reveals the existence of a
structural gap between societal readiness for adaptation and institutional governance capacity. This
gap is analytically significant, аs the observed mismatch transforms water stress from a manageable
resource-related problem into a risk to social stability.
From a national security perspective, the key insight does not lie in water scarcity per se, but in the
way institutions manage expectations, communication, and trust. The limited governance capacity
functions as a risk multiplier that amplifies the effects of water stress and increases the likelihood
of social tension.
In this context, marketing -based and behavioral governance instruments emerge as a
connecting mechanism between technical solutions and actual public behavior. Their role is not to

Conclusion
The present study examines water stress as an increasingly significant factor for social stability and
national security in Bulgaria, analyzed not only as an environmental and infrastructural challenge,
but as a complex socio-political risk. By combining an analytical literature review, a comparative
analysis of international case studies, and an indicative empirical survey, the article explores the
interrelationships between physical water scarcity, limited governance capacity, public behavior,
and institutional resilience.
The analysis demonstrates that water stress in Bulgaria does not constitute an isolated or
temporary deviation, but rather a structural problem reproduced across different regions and time
periods. Empirical observations indicate that a substantial proport ion of the population has direct
experience with water supply restrictions, lending the issue a clear social dimension. In this context,
water stress functions as a risk multiplier capable of eroding institutional trust and intensifying
social tension in the presence of limited governance capacity.
The survey provides an indicative empirical basis for analyzing the behavioral and
communicative aspects of water stress. The findings reveal a declared readiness for behavioral
adaptation within the sample examined, alongside a critical assessment of inst itutional responses
and communication. These results should not be interpreted as representative of general societal
attitudes, but rather as illustrative empirical observations that support the formulation of analytical
and governance-related hypotheses.
The principal scientific contribution of the study lies in the development of the conceptual
Prevention-Crisis-Adaptation (PCA) model, which structures water stress governance into three
interrelated phases and positions marketing-based and behavioral governance instruments as a key
connecting mechanism between technical solutions, public behavior, and institutional legitimacy.
The model does not replace engineering or regulatory approaches, but complements them by
focusing on demand management, communication, and trust.
Within the framework of this study, national security is conceptualized as a function of
social stability, institutional reliability, and governance capacity to address systemic risks. In this
sense, water stress is interpreted through the lenses of societ al security, resilience, and the risk of
governance failure, whereby limited institutional effectiveness may transform a resource -related
