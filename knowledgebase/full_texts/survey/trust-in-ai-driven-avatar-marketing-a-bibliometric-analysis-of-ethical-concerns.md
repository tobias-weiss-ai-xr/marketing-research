Abstract. AI systems have fundamentally changed the way brands communicate with consumers. Avatars,
virtual influencers, and AI personas have become social actors in the brand consumer relationship, relying
on anthropomorphic design and ongoing interaction. Given th eir growing influence, trust has become a
central concept in research on consumers' responses to AI-driven marketing. This study presents how trust
is conceptualized in AI-driven marketing with a focus on avatars and virtual influencers. Our study employs
a bibliometric analysis of 2888 articles published between 2021 and 2025 from Web of Science. We have
found that avatar marketing is a fragmented research domain, and that trust plays an instrumental role in
achieving marketing outcomes. Transparency and fairness, ethical concerns discussed in the literature, hold
peripheral positions, and AI regulation appears as the most isolated concept in the cluster analysis.
Although trust occupies a central position, it is conceptualized for its functional role, not for its ethical
dimension. This is a clear literature gap that is translated into the field’s lack of a conceptual framework to
evaluate the legitimacy of AI -driven marketing practices. Our study supports a reframing of trust, from a
dependent variable of marketing efficiency to a central ethical criterion.

Keywords: artificial intelligence; trust; avatar marketing; virtual influencers; ethics; transparency; fairness.

Introduction
Nowadays, AI-driven marketing influence has gone beyond technical efficacy. Research finds that
AI systems affect customers' decisions, experiences, and, in a wider sense, social dynamics
(Puntoni et al., 2021). This growing influence cannot be reduced to the technical performance of
the system. Manipulation and reduced customer autonomy in invisible systems are real concerns,
more acute as AI operates on non-transparent infrastructure that models how options are presented
and interpreted, without user awareness (Susser et al., 2019). It is more than knowing what a
consumer decisi on is based on; it is about the space in which the choice was algorithmically
configured. Thus, the etic commitment in AI -driven marketing is not a matter of degree of
personalization. Recent studies argue that what is more important is consumers' capacity  to
recognize and understand the influence of algorithms on them. (Waqas & Qadri, 2025). A fully
personalized, but transparent, system can be more ethically embedded than one that is less
personalized but obscure in its function.

Another transformation is that AI-driven marketing is no longer an opaque platform by
design. AI systems can perform social interaction and have human -like features, beyond
personalization and algorithmic inference. They are anthropomorphized systems that
communicate, express human emotions, and mimic human cues (Du & Xie, 2020). Among these
anthropomorphized systems, avatars occupy a particular position: unlike recommendation
algorithms that operate invisibly, avatars are designed to be seen, recognized, and trusted as social
actors
The anthropomorphic dimension is of utmost importance, and among these
anthropomorphized systems, avatars have the most impactful form in social interactions. For
example, a recommendation algorithm personalizes without being seen by the users. In contr ast,
an avatar has a face, voice, expresses emotions, and social behavior. This humanized AI system
becomes an active social actor in interaction with customers (Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Puntoni
et al., 2021). From an ethical perspective, the distinction between algorithmic influence and human-
like influence emerges as more significant. Precisely for this reason, avatar marketing requires a
distinct analysis. This transformation calls into question the potential influence of AI entities on
human decisions. We cannot help but ask what criteria we should use to assess their legitimate
influence on consumers. Do we consider the same criteria as for human influencers, for example,
transparency towards paid partnerships, real endorsements for products used as aut henticity calls
out, or consideration for consumers' autonomy by avoiding exploitations of vulnerabilities? What
role does trust have in the interaction with AI systems?
As AI systems become more socially integrated, trust has emerged as a key concept in
understanding consumer responses to AI-enabled marketing systems. Existing research shows that
trust plays an important role in consumer acceptance, engagement, and adoption of AI technologies
(Glikson & Woolley, 2020), while experiential perspectives show  trust as a relational and
psychological response to socially integrated AI systems (Puntoni et al., 2021).
By reviewing the business ethics literature, we find that trust is a necessary condition for
consumers to evaluate fairness, transparency, and acco untability in their interactions with AI
systems (Schultz & Seele, 2023; Zwick & Dholakia, 2004, as cited in Waqas & Qadri, 2025).
Transparency and fairness are presented as factors that reduce the unexplainability of AI systems,
but elements that support users' understanding of the decision -making algorithmic mechanisms
(Shin, 2021). However, their role is external; they don’t define trust, they act as enablers of trust
(Saveljeva & Volkova, 2025). Thus, ethics enters into the discussion as an antecedent of consumer
trust, not as a constitutive element of trust.
This functional perspective of trust is even more pronounced in avatar marketing research.
Studies on AI-driven avatars and virtual influencers demonstrate that, on the one hand, trust serves
as a link among anthropomorphic design traits, social presence, and emotional engagement, and,
on the other hand, as a behavioral outcome such as buying intention and loyalty (Gerlich, 2023;
Keng et al., 2025). The ethical risks associated with AI -driven marketi ng recognize trust as
important, but as a persuasion tool, not as an assessment criterion.
Despite discussions of ethical risks in AI -driven marketing, trust is conceptualized in
relation to these consumer responses. The aim of this paper is to clarify d ominant patterns in the
discussion of trust in AI-driven marketing research and to examine how trust is conceptualized in
the emerging literature on avatar -based marketing. Using a bibliometric approach, this study
reviews existing research to understand how trust is conceptualized and theorized, and to identify
gaps in the study of trust in AI-mediated marketing interactions.

Literature review
2.1 AI-driven marketing: ethical concerns and the critical role of trust
AI in marketing is undergoing the transition from being used only as a background tool to being used
as a social actor. Moreover, studies demonstrate that the presupposition that it is neutral is no longer
valid. AI systems can elicit cognitive, emotional, and social reactions from consumers through their
anthropomorphized features. Moreover, because they are socially integrated, they can mirror and
reinforce existing stereotypes, gender biases, and discriminatory norms. (Puntoni et al., 2021).
Therefore, we are facing a clear paradox in the wa y trust is discussed in the AI marketing
research. The literature recognizes that AI systems can manipulate and exploit consumers'
vulnerabilities, but trust remains a variable to be optimized and not an end goal. The question is:
how did this happen?
The answer is partially related to the manner in which AI marketing was theorized from the
start. As AI overcomes the position of invisible infrastructure and enables integrated social roles
such as anthropomorphized avatars, virtual influencers, and emotiona lly responsive agents,
researchers adopted the technology adoption and persuasion frameworks from literature in which
trust was already defined as a mediator between system characteristics and behavior outcomes
(Shin, 2021; Glikson & Woolley, 2020). Transp arency and fairness entered these frames not as
ethical demands, but as antecedents, mainly conditions that, while being present, increase the
probability of obtaining trust (Saveljeva & Volkova, 2025). The ethical perspective was there, but
it was subordinated to the logic of performance from the beginning.
Transparency is commonly described in literature as a condition that improves consumers’
understanding of AI systems and supports perceived control, as a result, facilitating acceptance and
reducing resistance to technology (Shin, 2021). Fairness, as well, depends less on the level of
personalization than on the consumer's perception of whether the practice is acceptable and not
intrusive (Waqas & Qadri, 2025). Trust is understood as a mechanism that li nks heuristic
evaluations of system characteristics to subsequent attitudes and responses, in relation to system
opacity and increased perceived control (Shin, 2021), as well as to relational cues generated by
anthropomorphism and social presence (Glikson & Woolley, 2020). The resulting implications are
important: ethics becomes a control variable, not a criterion for evaluating the legitimacy of
marketing practice.
When ethical conduct is assumed as an aspect to increase trust, that in turn can generate
engagement and adoption (Ali, 2025; Yadav et al., 2024), the implicit idea is that ethics matters if
it is instrumental and not because it is right. Experimental evidence demonstrates that although
trust is a strong behavioral predictor, optimizations on pe rsonalization alone do not translate
recommendation cues into buying intentions, especially in a high -risk context (Ageman &
Hollmann, 2025). A predictor for adoption is not the same as a basis for assessing legitimacy. As
AI systems increasingly simulate more relational experiences using personal and emotional data
(Bozdağ, 2025), this conceptual confusion increases. Ethics and trust coexist in the same studies,
but they fail to be conceptually intertwined; this separation reveals a central conceptual gap in
literature.
Studies on the diversity of perspectives on trust are aligned with this observation.
Researchers are calling out the limited analysis on current concepts of trust, including the
distinctions between its affective, rational, and normative dimensions (Sheikh & Hoeyer, 2020).
Additionally, we can find in the literature studies that justify the necessity of ethical and normative
frameworks in domains where essential values have been neglected (Ursin et al., 2024).
 To conclude, the literatur e shows that ethics is more of an external context, and trust is a

2.2 Trust in AI-Driven Avatar Marketing Research
Avatars are anthropomorphized digital representations with different degrees of realism and
autonomy (Burden & Savin -Baden, 2019). This may seem like a clear and simple definition, but
practice shows a large range of entities. CGI digital avatar managed by an agency, a virtual
influencer and AI persona that generates automatic responses, and a chatbot with a human face, or
a client service agent (AI -enabled digital agents) are all discussed under the same conceptual
umbrella of avatar marketing (Appel et al., 2020; Moustakas et al., 2020; Miao et al., 2023; Qu,
2025). Terms such as digital avatar, virtual influencer, AI persona, or digital humas are often used
interchangeably without systematic differentiation. This terminology ambiguity is not only
inconvenient, but it also hides an important ethical aspect: who or what controls the interaction. A
consumer who interacts with an AI avatar may have no way to know whether the interaction is
genuine, a persuasion strateg y, or something in between (Susser et al., 2019). This problem
becomes even more complex considering the current technological context. The fine line between
avatars controlled by humans and those controlled by software, identified by Miao et al. (2021) as
a conceptual clarity problem, becomes more blurred as generative AI technology becomes widely
accessible. A CGI persona, such as Lil Miquela, the famous Instagram virtual influencer, can be
progressively augmented with autonomous AI capabilities without a  transparent transition for her
followers. This adds a temporal dimension to the already identified opacity from the literature.
Review studies show that avatar marketing has emerged as a distinct and rapidly developing
research area at the intersection of marketing, technology, and consumer behavior (De Silva & de
Campos, 2024). According to its definition, a brand interacts with consumers through these entities,
designed to foster interaction through anthropomorphic appearance, social presence, and
communicative behaviors (Miao et al., 2023).
Studies confirm that although anthropomorphism is used to improve avatar effectiveness,
a higher level of realism in the avatar does not increase trust; realist avatars can elicit the uncanny
valley effect, creating discomfort that can reduce their persuasive impact (Qu, 2025). Moreover,
the distinction between the trust in avatars and the trust in the product is important because it
underlines that the avatars' features, such as anthropomorphism and interaction quality, can create
trust and influence purchase intentions, while the trust in the product is built through brand
awareness and perceived quality (Keng et al., 2025).
Another critical distinction is the one between cognitive trust and affective trust. While the
first one is determined by rational evaluations of competence and transparency, the second is
developed through emotional engagement and sustains brand loyalty in the long term, with both
dimensions operating as operational conditions of customer–brand relationship development (Guo
et al., 2023; Glikson & Woolley, 2020). Importantly, anthropomorphic cues can initially increase
trust, but expectations may rapidly erode when they are not met with the system’s actual
capabilities and performance (Glikson & Woolley, 2020).
Trust in avatar marketing is a persuasion and relationship -building tool. It can be that the
most significant gap in literature is not missing data, but asking the right question: How does the
unclear degree of autonomy affect the conditio ns in which trust is conceptualized? If you don’t
know how autonomous an avatar can be, can we even begin to discuss trust in an ethical way? A
more detailed perspective on how trust is conceptualized can be found in the literature on virtual
influencers.

2.3 Trust in AI-Driven Virtual Influencer Research
Research on virtual influencers starts from a tension identified by literature but not resolved:
transparency, usually considered an ethical value, can reduce the campaign efficiency. Empirical
studies s how that behind -the-scenes disclosures about virtual influencers, which increase
transparency, can weaken the influencer effect and reduce consumers' trust in their endorsement
(Chen et al., 2025). In other words, we are confronted with an ethical dilemma:  if transparency
reduces trust, what can motivate brands to be transparent?
Empirical evidence further shows that virtual influencers are often perceived as more
trustworthy than human influencers, with trust being a driver of acceptance and purchase intention
(Gerlich, 2023).
Trustworthiness is seen as a part of source credibility that influences how persuasive
endorsement is. It refers to consumers’ perceptions of an influencer’s honesty, sincerity, and
authenticity, and it is judged by using human -like, anthropomorphic cues (Özdemir et al., 2023).
However, virtual influencers are more vulnerable to evaluation than humans because presenting them
as autonomous agents with real-life experiences can conflict with consumers’ awareness that they
exist only in digital form, thereby reducing perceived authenticity and sincerity (Özdemir et al., 2023).
At the same time, findings demonstrate that anthropomorphism fosters trust and brand love.
The more a virtual influencer looks and behaves human -like, the more em otional the consumer
response, and it finds it hard to critically evaluate the artificial nature of the interaction.
Anthropomorphic design enhances emotional engagement, which, in turn, strengthens trust,
particularly among individuals with higher opinion leadership (Rehman et al., 2025).
AI influencers, understood as AI -driven virtual influencers, are presented as an extension
of AI -driven marketing, in which artificial personas act as social actors capable of generating
parasocial relationships and soci al presence and enhancing perceived credibility and user
engagement (Omeish et al., 2025). Moreover, empirical research on virtual streamers, as a specific
category of virtual influencers, demonstrates that anthropomorphic cues enhance cognitive trust,
which in turn increases purchase intention. (Li & Huang, 2024).
In summary, research on AI-driven virtual influencers offers a more explicit perspective of
trust compared to the avatar marketing literature. Across studies, trust is mainly conceptualized as
a component of source credibility and as a psychological mechanism linking anthropomorphic
cues, authenticity, and parasocial interaction to persuasion, engagement, and purchase intention. A
conceptual gap is still present in literature, although trust plays a central role in influencing
behavior, ethical considerations remain outcome-oriented.

Methodology
The aim of this paper is to clarify dominant patterns in how trust is discussed in AI -driven
marketing research and to identify how trust is addressed in avatar -based marketing, including
research on virtual influencers, as part of the avatar marketing research. Our analysis is guided by
research questions and objectives; no hypotheses were formulated, considering the exploratory and
bibliometric nature of the study.
 RQ1: How is trust addressed in AI-driven marketing research?

 O2: Determining the ways trust is discussed in avatar-based marketing
 RQ3: How is trust framed in research on virtual influencers?

 No existing bibliometric studies explicitly map trust in avatar marketing as a theme. Some
analysis of the evolution of avatar marketing reveals that trust ideas appear within larger themes
such as anthropomorphism, social presence, and engagement. Here, trust is treated as a relational
outcome. (Kusumawati et al., 2024; Sinha & Sarkar, 2025). Pushparaj & Kushwaha (2023) analyze
virtual influencer marketing and identify trust as an element of credibility and authenticity, as well
as engagement.
 At a larger scale, studies about AI biases in marketing position trust as an indirect result of
algorithmic fairness (Bacalhau et al., 2025). Complementing this perspective, bibliometric and co-
citation analyses of AI-driven marketing show that trust is examined within relationship clusters,
where its function is to  support long -term relationships, organizational performance, and risk
reduction, and not explicitly theorized (Verma et al., 2021).
 The Web of Science Core Collection was selected for this study, and we used VosViewer
for thematic cluster analysis. The search string combined the terms artificial intelligence (AI) and
virtual, digital, and avatar representations with the marketing term. These terms were further
combined with trust -related and ethical concepts, including trust, credibility, authenticity,
transparency, fairness, manipulation, and consumer and ethical concepts, including trust,
credibility, authenticity, transparency, fairness, manipulation, and consumer vulnerability.
 We have excluded from the search technical terms such as robotics, robot -based systems,
computer vision, deep learning, neural networks, machine learning models, and algorithm design,
based on the rationale of maintaining the data set marketing literature-oriented (Appendix A.). We
acknowledge that this decision may limit the results, as studies on trustworthy AI could have been
excluded. Even so, the inclusion of the terms would have changed the analysis focus from
consumer behavior and brand consumer relationships to system architecture, a change of paradigm
inconsistent with our study objectives. We have refined the search by limiting the time period to
publications published between 2021 and 2025, written in English. This period reflects a growing
academic interest in AI-driven marketing due to milestone technological developments such as the
increased presence of virtual influencers on Instagram and TikTok in 2021, the launch of ChatGPT
in 2022, and the proliferation of LLM -based systems. The search resulted in 2888 articles, and it
was conducted in January 2026.

Results and discussions
The results of the work are presented on the three specific objectives that were set.
O1: Identifying how trust is approached in research on artificial intelligence in marketing.
Based on the network map (Figure 1), trust is the central concept of the analysis that
connects artificial intelligence, virtual influencer, consumer psychology, personalization, and

Trust and engagement form the core cluster on the map, which connects the other clusters
but remains separate from them. This may reflect that trust is not a systematic problem but rather
is portrayed as a mechanism that connects the research area of artificial intelligence, virtual

The p eripherality of the ethical concepts on the map is not neutral. If fairness and
transparency are at the margins of the network, while adoption and engagement occupy a central
position, then the current academic landscape is prioritizing market penetration over consumer
protection. Questions regarding how to increase adoption and engagement are getting elaborate
answers, while questions about whether a marketing practice is legit do not have a clear theoretical
framework. This is by far the most significant finding of our study.

Conclusion
This study aims to respond to how trust is conceptualized in AI-driven marketing research, with a
specific focus on avatar marketing and virtual influencers. The bibliometric analysis confirmed that
the answer is problematic. Trust occupies a central, but instrumental role across literature.
Avatar marketing is not yet a consolidated research area, and virtual influencers dominate
the marketing research. Trust in relation to virtual influencers is mainly analyzed as a relational
mechanism without any ethical evaluation.
The peripheral position of ethics and the isolation of AI regulation in the cluster analysis
confirm that the field lacks conceptual frameworks to evaluate the legitimacy of marketing
practices. As noted in the literature review, researchers acknowledge the lack of normative
frameworks in domains with high ethical stakes (Ursin et al., 2024).
Transparency and fairness, although present in the analysis, do not shape trust as external
factors; they do not define it, leaving trust as a mechanism for managing AI anxiety and increasing
market performance in contexts characterized by algorithmic opacity. This finding is in line with
the current literature that confirms that researchers capture the functional dimension of and neglect
its affective and normative aspects (Sheikh & Hoeyer, 2020). Moreover,
Thus, despite attention being given to ethical risks, trust in AI -driven and avatar -based
marketing is a tool that helps consumers accept AI systems and improves marketing outcomes, not
a criterion of legitimacy.  Trust’s ethical perspective goes beyond effectiveness and performance;
it is the central dimension through which AI -driven marketing practices can be evaluated as
legitimate, transparent, and respectful of consumer autonomy.
From a managerial perspective, managers should unders tand that transparency and
authenticity are complementary objectives. A brand that builds consumer trust through
anthropomorphic design and the social presence of avatars can achieve short -term results but can
be vulnerable in the long run, especially as c onsumers become more aware of the artificiality of
the interactions. Transparency of AI avatars should be a part of their AI-mediated identity, not just
a mandatory disclosure practice. A transparent avatar, with an artificial image that is honest and
constantly communicates, can build long -term trust with customers compared to one that is more
human-like and makes no disclosures. For example, the Brazilian avatar, Lu do Magalu, launched
