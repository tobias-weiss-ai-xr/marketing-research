Abstract
As AI technologies rise to prominence in consumers’ lives, marketing scholars and practitioners face an urgent need to
understand how different consumer segments perceive, trust, and adopt these tools. However, current literature often treats
consumer AI sentiment as monolithic, and practical guidance for addressing diverse consumer concerns remains sparse.
To address this gap, we introduce the RISE framework—organized around Relational meaning differences, In-context
segmentation, Skepticism-usage paradox, and Equitable AI integration—as a diagnostic lens for understanding AI adoption
heterogeneity. We offer RISE not as a grand theory but as pre-theoretic diagnostic scaffolding: each dimension classifies
the type of heterogeneity problem researchers or managers face, determining which mechanisms to investigate and which
strategic levers to pull. Drawing on illustrative evidence from a U.S. census-representative survey ( n = 2,144), we docu -
ment patterns that motivate each framework dimension and articulate twelve formal propositions (P1–P12) that provide
specific, testable entry points for future research. We develop a structured research agenda comprising twelve priority
research questions with specific methodological recommendations and boundary conditions. We also include a manage -
rial diagnostic appendix with 25 strategic questions that practitioners can use to assess their customer base on each RISE
dimension. By mapping these heterogeneous patterns, our research provides a nuanced perspective on AI acceptance and
offers implications for both marketing theory and practice, including guidance on building consumer trust and tailoring
AI solutions to diverse needs across consumer segments.

1.1 The Diagnostic Challenge
The dominant approach to understanding AI adoption—
across marketing, information systems, and management—
asks: What drives AI adoption? This question assumes a
single adoption process with a common set of drivers. But
AI does not have an adoption problem. The market has
answered the adoption question: yes, rapidly and over -
whelmingly. What remains unresolved—and what matters
enormously—is what happens inside that adoption. The
consumers who “use AI” are not one segment. They are
multiple segments with fundamentally different relation -
ships to the technology.
Consider three puzzles that existing frameworks nei -
ther predict nor explain. First, the anxious adopter: a
2025 workplace survey found that 78% of workers use
unauthorized AI tools, yet 49% actively hide their AI use
from colleagues and 45% have pretended to understand
AI in meetings [ 42]. Among Gen Z workers, 62% have
hidden their use. This is not enthusiastic adoption—it is
compelled, anxious, covert use. Second, the relational
meaning gap: more than 70% of U.S. teenagers have
tried AI companions, and one-third find them as satisfy -
ing as real friendships [ 8]. Meanwhile, women in our sur -
vey are significantly more worried that AI will damage
human connections (M = 4.87) while men are more likely
to view AI as a companion (M = 4.14). Two consumers
can both “use AI” yet experience fundamentally different
relationships with it. Third, the counterintuitive equity
pattern: African American (M = 1.85 h/day) and Hispanic
(M = 1.91 h/day) respondents report significantly higher
daily AI usage than White Americans (M = 1.37 h/day)—
despite lower average household income. Parents with
children under 18 are nearly twice as likely to use AI as
non-parents [ 26].
These puzzles share a common diagnostic structure:
the same surface-level observation can arise from funda -
mentally different underlying dynamics, and the correct
response depends entirely on which dynamic is operat -
ing. A researcher observes that women use AI signifi -
cantly less than men. The instinct trained by TAM [ 10]
would look for differences in perceived usefulness. The
instinct trained by diffusion theory would look for dif -
ferences in innovativeness. Both treat the gender gap as
a magnitude problem. But our data suggest something
different: women are more likely to interpret AI as a
relational threat—not that they find it less useful. This is
not a difference in how much they value AI; it is a differ -
ence in what AI means to them. The diagnosis determines
the research design. The wrong diagnosis produces the
wrong study.
1.2 RISE as Pre-theoretic Diagnostic Scaffolding
This paper is conceptual in orientation. Our primary
contribution is the RISE framework—a diagnostic lens
organized around four dimensions of consumer AI het -
erogeneity: Relational meaning differences, In-context
segmentation, Skepticism-usage paradox, and Equitable
AI integration. We ground this framework in illustra -
tive evidence from a large-scale U.S. survey, not to offer
definitive hypothesis tests, but to demonstrate the plau -
sibility and diagnostic value of each dimension. We then
articulate a structured research agenda—complete with
twelve formal propositions, specific research questions,
methodological suggestions, and boundary conditions—
designed to guide future scholarship on consumer AI
adoption.
We offer RISE not as a grand theory of AI adoption
but as something the field arguably needs more urgently:
pre-theoretic diagnostic scaffolding. The periodic table
did not explain why elements behave as they do—that
required quantum theory, which came later. But by orga -
nizing elements into a coherent classification, it made
quantum theory possible by revealing the patterns that
needed explaining. Similarly, RISE classifies the types
of consumer AI heterogeneity so that theory-building can
proceed cumulatively rather than in scattered fragments.
Grand theories are the destination. We believe RISE is a
map that helps the field get there faster.
The paper proceeds as follows. We first introduce the
RISE framework as an organizing theoretical lens. We
then present illustrative evidence from a national survey,
mapping observed patterns to each framework dimension.
Finally, we develop a structured research agenda and dis -
cuss implications for marketing theory and practice.

2.2 I — In-Context Segmentation
Diagnostic question Is this a context problem? Does the
same consumer accept AI in some domains but resist it
in others?
AI attitudes are domain-specific rather than monolithic.
Consumers may embrace AI enthusiastically in one con -
text (e.g., productivity, entertainment) while remaining
skeptical or resistant in another (e.g., healthcare, educa -
tion, creative work). This pattern aligns with research on
attitudinal ambivalence [ 28, 34] and risk-benefit calcu -
lus models of technology evaluation. Consumer research
has long emphasized that evaluations of consequential
innovations are rarely one-dimensional—people can feel
torn, simultaneously attracted to an innovation’s prom -
ised benefits and repelled by its perceived threats [ 35].
We propose that “AI acceptance” is better modeled as
a portfolio of context-specific evaluations, each shaped
by the particular bundle of benefits and risks consumers
perceive as salient in that domain. Effective segmentation
and deployment therefore require context-level analysis,
not global sentiment scores. Drawing on this and related
literature, we propose the following:
P4: Consumer AI acceptance varies more across use
contexts than across individuals for a given context.
Domain-level variance in AI attitudes exceeds per -
son-level variance.
P5: Perceived stakes of a decision context moderate AI
acceptance, with consumers demanding greater trans -
parency and human oversight in high-stakes contexts.

2.5 How the Four Dimensions Relate
A natural question is whether the four RISE dimensions
are intended as competing explanations or as comple -
mentary lenses. We view them as complementary diag -
nostic lenses. Although they may appear to operate at
different levels, they are better understood as different
analytical focal points applied to the same empirical
pattern. R concerns the relational meaning consumers
assign to AI. I concerns how acceptance varies across use
contexts. S concerns whether observed behavior reflects
genuine endorsement or reluctant, necessity-driven use.
E concerns how structural position shapes access, inten -
sity, breadth, and downstream benefit. Thus, RISE is not
a single continuum and not a stage model. It is a diag -
nostic architecture for identifying the dominant form of
heterogeneity present in a given case.
Importantly, the four dimensions are not mutu -
ally exclusive. The same observable phenomenon can
be generated by more than one RISE dimension at the
same time. A consumer who uses AI despite believing it
may weaken human connection reflects an S-dimension
P6: Two-sided AI communications—acknowledging
both benefits and limitations—generate greater con -
sumer trust than one-sided communications, particu -
larly in high-stakes domains.
2.3 S — Skepticism-usage Paradox
Diagnostic question Is this a commitment problem? Are
consumers engaged because they want to be, or because
they feel they must be?
A long tradition in consumer psychology warns against
equating attitudes with behavior. The theory of planned
behavior, for example, treats attitudes as one input into
action rather than a deterministic cause, leaving room
for constraints, norms, and perceived control to shape
what people actually do [ 1, 31]. We propose that AI
represents a particularly stark case of attitude-behavior
divergence: consumers may use AI frequently and inten -
sively despite holding anxious, skeptical, or negative
attitudes. This “anxious adopter” pattern may arise from
perceived necessity, fear of falling behind, workplace or
social pressure, or the high costs of non-adoption in an
AI-saturated environment. If so, usage statistics alone
may mask substantial latent resistance—a segmentation-
relevant insight for firms seeking to convert reluctant
users into genuine advocates. The implication is that
businesses and solution designers must not mistake usage
for wholehearted acceptance; just because a segment of
customers is using an AI tool does not mean they have
overcome their concerns. With this dimension and the
relevant literature as a foundation, we present the follow -
ing propositions:
P7: AI anxiety is positively associated with AI usage
intensity, mediated by perceived necessity and fear of
missing out (FOMO).
P8: The relationship between AI use and AI atti -
tudes is directionally asymmetric: usage experience
shapes subsequent attitudes more than prior atti -
tudes shape subsequent usage (requires longitudinal
confirmation).
P9: Anxious adopters exhibit lower brand loyalty and
higher switching intention than enthusiastic adopters
with comparable usage levels.

3.1 Data Context
To better understand the contemporary landscape of U.S.
consumers’ attitudes toward—and use of—AI tools, we
conducted a large-scale national online survey administered
by Dynata using a U.S. Census–representative sample.
Dynata set the survey’s completion incentive using a pre -
test, which estimated that the average U.S. adult would need
about 16 min to finish it. We treat this survey as a source
of illustrative evidence that grounds our conceptual frame -
work. Specifically, to reduce variation in how respondents
interpreted the term “AI,” we stated that, throughout the sur-
vey, “AI” refers to generative artificial intelligence (GenAI).
We provided examples such as ChatGPT, Google Gemini,
Anthropic’s Claude, and similar chatbot-based AI systems so
that participants would anchor their responses to a common
class of AI technologies. In total, 2,585 respondents began
the survey; 2,144 completed the full survey (120 items).
Participants ranged in age from 18 to 98 years (M = 47.05,
SD = 17.65). With respect to gender, 50.61% identified as
female, 48.93% as male, and 0.47% preferred not to answer.
Regarding ethnicity, 73.32% identified as White, 11.75%
as African American, 4.48% as Asian American, 7.37% as
Hispanic, and 3.08% as another ethnicity. Annual household
income was distributed as follows: 12.22% reported less
than $25,000; 19.03% $25,000–$49,999; 16.56% $50,000–
$74,999; 15.25% $75,000–$99,999; 18.56% $100,000–
$149,999; 8.35% $150,000–$199,999; 7.88% $200,000 or
more; and 2.15% preferred not to answer. Participants also
reported their residential area as urban (38.48%), suburban
(44.82%), or rural (16.70%). On average, survey comple -
tion time was 935.61 s (SD = 45.17). In what follows, we
report selected patterns from this dataset, organized around
the four RISE framework dimensions.
3.2 Evidence for Relational Meaning Differences (R):
Gender and the Interpretation of AI
The R dimension of the RISE framework proposes that con-
sumers differ in relational meaning—whether they view AI
as a threat to human connection or as a relational supple -
ment. Gender provides a diagnostic test case, given estab -
lished theory on communal versus agentic orientations [16].
Gender is a foundational social identity that shapes goals,
risk perceptions, and relationship norms. In marketing, it
has long served as a powerful segmentation base precisely
because it predicts not only what people do, but also why
they do it and what meanings they attach to products and
technologies [2, 43]. Recent work in the AI context simi -
larly suggests that women tend to report higher AI anxiety
and less favorable attitudes, whereas men report more posi-
tive perceptions and higher usage [ 24, 37]. Our data are
pattern at the level of attitude-behavior divergence, but
may simultaneously reflect an R-dimension pattern if the
underlying concern is relational threat. A lower-income
consumer from a historically underrepresented group
who uses AI heavily may reflect an E-dimension pat -
tern if structural constraints make AI especially valuable,
and an S-dimension pattern if that use is driven more by
necessity than by enthusiasm. Likewise, a consumer who
accepts AI for productivity but rejects it for healthcare
may reflect the I dimension because acceptance varies
across contexts, while also reflecting the R dimension if
healthcare is resisted specifically because it is understood
as a domain requiring human care. In such cases, forc -
ing a single label would obscure rather than clarify the
mechanism.
For that reason, the goal of RISE is not to identify which
dimension fits the surface description of a phenomenon
most easily. The goal is to identify the fundamental cause
of the observed heterogeneity, and then to consider whether
other dimensions are operating as secondary manifestations
or interacting conditions. As a practical rule, R is primary
when the central difference lies in what AI means to con -
sumers; I is primary when the main variation lies in where
AI is accepted or rejected; S is primary when the key issue
is the gap between attitude and behavior; and E is primary
when the pattern is fundamentally structured by differences
in resources, constraints, or downstream returns across
groups. Researchers and managers may therefore arrive at a
primary diagnosis and one or more secondary diagnoses in
the same case. This is not a classification failure. It is pre -
cisely what one should expect when a complex technology
enters diverse consumer lives.
The RISE framework is not intended as a formal theory
with testable causal mechanisms; rather, it is an organiz -
ing lens—a way of seeing consumer AI heterogeneity that
highlights dimensions often overlooked in both academic
research and industry practice. In the next section, we pres-
ent illustrative evidence from a national survey that grounds
each dimension in empirical patterns.

Mixed-valence, Domain-specific Beliefs
The I dimension of the RISE framework proposes that AI
attitudes are domain-specific portfolios rather than a single
global sentiment. Our survey data reveal patterns consistent
with this proposition.

simply sort into “pro-AI” versus “anti-AI.” Instead, the
same sample expresses meaningfully above-midpoint agree-
ment with both a threat-oriented framing and a productivity-
oriented framing. Consumers view AI as a shadow posing
serious threats to humanity, freedom, and human autonomy

.001; Cohen’ s d = 0.54). Yet they simultaneously view AI
consistent with that broad pattern—but they also surface a

appears in both adoption and interpretation.

4.2 Research Agenda for In-context Segmentation
(I)
The I dimension challenges the assumption that consum -
ers hold a single, global attitude toward AI. Our illustrative
evidence reveals that the same consumers can simultane -
ously embrace AI in some contexts while resisting it in oth-
ers. This pattern of mixed-valence, domain-specific beliefs
aligns with classic research on attitudinal ambivalence [ 28,
34] and suggests that “AI acceptance” is better modeled as a
portfolio of context-specific evaluations rather than a stable,
unitary sentiment. For marketers and AI developers, this
has immediate practical implications: the relevant question
is not “Does this consumer like AI?” but rather “In which
contexts does this consumer welcome AI, and in which con-
texts do they resist it?”
The theoretical logic underlying in-context segmentation
draws on risk-benefit calculus models of technology evalu -
ation. Consumers evaluate AI not in the abstract but in rela-
tion to specific use cases, each of which presents a distinct
bundle of perceived benefits (e.g., time savings, accuracy,
convenience) and perceived risks (e.g., privacy loss, job
displacement, reduced human contact). A consumer might
enthusiastically use AI for generating travel itineraries—
where the benefits are clear and the stakes are low—while
adamantly refusing AI involvement in medical diagnosis,
where errors could be catastrophic and the desire for human
judgment is strong. This context-specificity means that
aggregate measures of “AI attitude” may obscure more than
they reveal.
Priority research questions for this dimension include
the following. First, which contextual factors most strongly
moderate AI acceptance? Our data suggest that domain mat-
ters (healthcare vs. entertainment, education vs. customer
service), but other factors—perceived stakes, reversibility
of outcomes, consumer expertise, and the availability of
human alternatives—may also play important roles. Sys -
tematic research could identify which contextual cues most
powerfully shift consumers from acceptance to resistance
(or vice versa). Second, how should firms tailor messaging
and design when the same consumer embraces AI in one
domain but resists it in another? Cross-selling AI services
across domains may backfire if consumers perceive the AI
as overstepping its appropriate boundaries. Research on
mental accounting and category boundaries could inform
strategies for extending AI acceptance from one domain
to another. Third, do two-sided messages—acknowledging
both benefits and risks—outperform one-sided messages in
building context-specific trust? Classic persuasion research
suggests that two-sided messages can enhance credibility
[9, 39], but whether this generalizes to AI communication
remains untested.

minority groups show higher AI intensity despite lower
overall access and favorability? Qualitative research
exploring the motivations, goals, and barriers experienced
by these groups could surface explanations that quantita -
tive data cannot reveal. Second, does AI use narrow or
widen socioeconomic disparities in outcomes over time?
Longitudinal data linking AI usage patterns to downstream
outcomes—income growth, educational attainment, career
advancement—could reveal whether AI is functioning as
an equalizer or an amplifier of existing inequalities. Third,
how should firms design AI products and onboarding to
serve under-resourced segments effectively? If lower-
income users engage intensively but narrowly (using AI
for few purposes), interventions that expand usage breadth
could unlock additional benefits; if they lack digital liter -
acy to use AI effectively, training programs might be more
valuable than feature enhancements.
Methodologically, research on equitable AI integration
calls for matched longitudinal data that can link AI usage
patterns to life outcomes while controlling for confounding
variables. Field experiments testing onboarding and train -
ing interventions could reveal which approaches most effec-
tively expand AI benefits to under-resourced populations.
Cross-national comparisons could reveal whether the pat -
terns we observe in the U.S. generalize to other contexts
or reflect country-specific factors (e.g., inequality levels,
educational systems, cultural attitudes toward technology).
Qualitative research—including ethnographic observation
and in-depth interviews—could provide rich understanding
of how different communities make sense of AI and inte -
grate it into their lives.
Boundary conditions for this dimension include the type
of AI tool, institutional context, and policy environment.
The intensity-without-breadth pattern may be specific to
free, general-purpose tools; access to premium or special -
ized AI (e.g., enterprise software, professional-grade tools)
may follow traditional diffusion patterns more closely. The
pattern may shift as AI tools become more embedded in
institutional contexts like schools and workplaces, where
access is determined by organizational decisions rather than
trust-building strategies (transparency, control, human over-
sight) convert anxious users into genuine advocates.
Boundary conditions include the essentiality of AI for
the consumer’s goals, the availability of alternatives, and
individual differences in tolerance for ambiguity. The
paradox may be stronger for “essential” AI—workplace
productivity tools that directly affect job performance—
than for discretionary AI like entertainment or creative
applications. When viable non-AI alternatives exist,
anxious consumers may be more likely to defect, reduc -
ing the observed paradox. Individual differences in need
for cognition, tolerance of ambiguity, and comfort with
uncertainty may moderate whether anxiety translates into
approach or avoidance behavior.
4.4 Research Agenda for Equitable AI Integration
(E)
The E dimension addresses a question with profound societal
implications: How do the benefits and costs of AI adoption
distribute across socioeconomic and demographic groups?
Classic diffusion theory predicts that higher-income, higher-
education consumers adopt innovations earlier and more
broadly [3, 36]. Our illustrative evidence partially confirms
this prediction—income is associated with more favorable
attitudes and broader AI engagement—but also surfaces a
counterintuitive pattern: African American and Hispanic
respondents report higher daily usage intensity despite
lower average income and less favorable overall attitudes.
This finding challenges simplistic digital divide narratives
and suggests that AI adoption heterogeneity is multidimen-
sional, requiring analysts to distinguish at least three dimen-
sions often conflated under “adoption”: access (whether
someone uses AI at all), intensity (how much they use it),
and breadth (across how many contexts).
The theoretical foundations for this dimension draw
on digital divide scholarship, which has long argued that
inequalities in technology access are only the first layer of
a more complex stratification [14, 20]. Even when access is
equalized, differences in digital skills, autonomy of use, and
diversity of applications can perpetuate or even amplify pre-
existing social inequalities. Applied to AI, this multi-level
framework suggests that achieving equitable AI integration
requires more than distributing access—it requires support-
ing diverse use-cases, building competencies, and ensuring
that AI-derived benefits flow to all segments, not just those
who are already advantaged.

5.4 Designing for Equity
The equitable AI integration findings challenge firms to
think beyond access metrics to consider intensity, breadth,
and outcome equity. If lower-income and minority consum-
ers are using AI intensively but narrowly—perhaps relying
on a single free tool for limited purposes while higher-
income consumers leverage a portfolio of AI tools across
diverse life domains—then “democratizing AI access” has
not truly been achieved. Equity requires not just that all seg-
ments can use AI, but that all segments can derive compa -
rable benefits from AI use.
Several design principles follow from this perspective.
First, onboarding matters: lower-income or less tech-savvy
users may benefit from guided introductions that expand
their sense of what AI can do, rather than assuming users
will discover capabilities on their own. Khan Academy’s
Khanmigo AI tutor exemplifies this approach, actively sug-
gesting ways students can use AI assistance they might
not have thought to request. Second, language and cultural
inclusivity matters: AI tools trained predominantly on Eng-
lish-language, Western-oriented data may serve some pop -
ulations better than others, and firms committed to equity
should audit and address these disparities. Third, pricing
and access models matter: freemium models that reserve the
most valuable features for paying customers may inadver -
tently widen rather than narrow outcome disparities.
For firms with equity commitments, tracking AI-derived
outcomes across demographic segments—not just usage
rates—provides a more meaningful measure of whether AI
is serving all customers well. If AI tools improve productiv-
ity for all users but improve it much more for some users
than others, the net effect may be to widen rather than nar -
row pre-existing inequalities. Firms genuinely committed
to equitable AI integration should monitor these differential
outcomes and adjust their products accordingly.

accountability, and the ability to opt out or escalate to human
decision-makers. Amazon’s healthcare arm, One Medical,
provides a useful model: AI is positioned as enhancing the
human care team rather than replacing it, and patients can
always request to speak with a human provider.
5.3 Building Trust with Anxious Adopters
The skepticism-usage paradox—wherein anxious consum -
ers use AI heavily despite their reservations—carries a cru-
cial warning for firms interpreting their engagement metrics.
High daily active users, lengthy session times, and frequent
return visits may look like success, but if many of those
users are anxious adopters using AI only because they feel
they must, the firm’s position is more precarious than met -
rics suggest. When alternatives become available—whether
competing AI products that inspire more trust, or human-
powered services that become economically viable—anx -
ious adopters may be the first to defect.
To convert anxious adopters into genuine advocates, firms
should invest in trust-building that goes beyond marketing
messages [ 5, 29, 30]. Transparency is essential: clearly
explaining what the AI can and cannot do, how it reaches
its outputs, and what data it uses helps users feel informed
rather than mystified. Control is equally important: allow -
ing users to adjust AI behavior, correct errors, and opt out
of specific features respects user autonomy and reduces the
sense of being subjected to an inscrutable system. Evidence
of benefit helps too: showing users concrete examples of
how AI has helped them (time saved, errors caught, goals
achieved) provides experiential justification for continued
use that can gradually soften skepticism.

contribution of this paper is to reframe AI adoption as a seg-

do we get more people to use AI?” firms and researchers
should ask “How do different consumer segments experi -

distinct meanings, contexts, and concerns?”

the survey relies on self-reported measures of AI usage and
attitudes, which may be subject to recall bias or social desir-
ability effects. Future research using behavioral trace data
(actual usage logs, time-stamped interactions) could pro -
vide more objective measures. Third, our sample is limited
to U.S. consumers; whether the RISE framework general -

question [40]. AI adoption patterns in China, Europe, or the
Global South may reveal different dimensions of heteroge -
neity or different segment profiles.
