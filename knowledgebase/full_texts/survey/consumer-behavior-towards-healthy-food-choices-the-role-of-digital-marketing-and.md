Introduction
iet-related non -communicable diseases remain among
the leading global public health challenges, and
consumer food choice sits at the intersection of individual
psychology, marketing influence, and information access [64].
Historically, food marketing research focused on mass -media
advertising and in -store point-of-purchase influence [12, 40].
The proliferation of social media, influencer culture,
algorithmically targeted digital advertising, and nutrition -
tracking mobile applications has transformed the information
ecosystem within which food decisions are made [42, 38] .
Consumers today may encounter a recipe video, a calorie -
tracking app notification, a peer’s meal photograph, and a
targeted advertisement for a snack product within minutes of
one another, each exerting a distinct pull on food -related
attitudes and behavior.

Simultaneously, nutritional awareness — encompassing
knowledge of nutrients, the ability to interpret food labels, and
general health literacy — has become a widely studied
determinant of dietary quality [26, 42, 63] . Yet nutritional
awareness does not ope rate in isolation from the marketing
environment. It interacts with, and is itself shaped by, digital
information sources, including the very platforms used for
food marketing [73, 11] . Two literatures have therefore
developed largely in parallel: a public  health literature
documenting the reach and effects of digital food marketing,
and a consumer research literature documenting how
nutrition knowledge and label literacy influence purchase
decisions. Each address one side of the same decision, and
neither on its own explains why highly informed consumers
still respond to persuasive food marketing, or why nutrition

Consumer decisions about food are increasingly shaped not only by taste, price, and habit, but by a dense information
environment of digital marketing and nutrition communication. This paper presents an integrative conceptual review of
social science and c onsumer behavior research on healthy food choice, with particular attention to two forces reshaping
that choice: digital marketing (social media advertising, influencer endorsement, algorithmically targeted content, and
mobile applications) and nutritional awareness (consumers’ knowledge of, and attentiveness to, nutritional information).
Literature was identified through a structured search of Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed, and Google Scholar covering
2010–2025, screened against explicit inclusion criteri a, and synthesized thematically. Drawing on the Theory of Planned
Behavior, the Health Belief Model, and the Elaboration Likelihood Model, the paper develops an integrative framework in

reciprocal channel that both markets food and supplies the nutrition knowledge assumed to protect against marketing.

components, influencer disclosure enforcement, and platform-level restrictions on targeted food advertising are identified
as the highest -priority interventions. Implications for health communicators, marketers of healthier products, and
policymakers are discussed, alongside a research agenda for empirical validation.

Review Methodology
❖ Type of review and rationale
This paper is an integrative conceptual review. Following
Whittemore and Knafl (75), Torraco (67), and Snyder (61), an
integrative review combines theoretical and empirical
literature of diverse methodological types in order to develop
new conceptual understanding, rather than to estimate a
pooled effect. This design was selected deliberately for three
reasons. First , the relevant evidence is methodologically
heterogeneous — randomized experiments on influencer
marketing, cross -sectional consumer surveys on label use,
content analyses of platform advertising, and app -based
intervention trials — and cannot be meaningfu lly pooled
statistically. Second, the review’s central objective is theory
development: the specification of an interaction between two
constructs that existing frameworks treat separately. Third,
the constructs of interest are operationalized so inconsistently
across studies  that a quantitative synthesis would aggregate
measures that are not comparable.
Consequently, this review does not follow the PRISMA 2020
statement [49], which is designed for systematic reviews and
meta-analyses of effect estimates, nor PRISMA-ScR for scoping
reviews [68]. The search strategy and screening criteria are
nevertheless reported transparently below so that the
evidence base underlying the model is auditable and the
review is reproducible in its essentials. Readers should
interpret the synthesis as conceptual and interpretive rather
than exhaustive; this is stated as a limitation.
❖ Search strategy
A structured search of Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed, and
Google Scholar was conducted, supplemented by targeted
searching of the World Health Organization publications
repository for grey literature on digital food marketing. Search
strings combined terms from three blocks using Boolean
operators:
Marketing block:  “digital marketing” OR “social media
advertising” OR “influencer mark eting” OR “online food
marketing” OR “targeted advertising” OR “advergame” OR
“user-generated content”
Awareness block:  “nutritional awareness” OR “nutrition
knowledge” OR “nutrition literacy” OR “food literacy” OR
“health literacy” OR “food label” OR “front-of-pack”
Outcome block: “food choice” OR “ consumer behavior” OR
“dietary intake” OR “purchase intention” OR “healthy  eating”
OR “diet quality”
Theory-specific searches were run separately for “theory of
planned behavior,” “health belief model,” “elaboration
likelihood model,” and “persuasion knowledge” in
combination with food and nutrition terms. The search
covered pee r-reviewed journal articles, book chapters, and
institutional reports published between January 2010 and
2025, with the exception of foundational theoretical sources ,
for example [2, 51, 56], which were included regardless of date
because the frameworks themselves are the object of analysis.
Reference lists of identified reviews were screened for
additional relevant sources (backward snowballing), and
forward citation searching was used for the most frequently
cited empirical studies.

❖ Screening and synthesis
Records were screened first by title and abstract against the
criteria in Table 1, then by full text. Retained sources were
charted into a matrix recording: theoretical framework
invoked, marketing mechanism examined, operationalization
of nutritional awar eness, study design, population, and
reported direction of effect. Synthesis then proceeded
thematically in three stages, consistent with integrative review
practice [75]. In the first stage, digital marketing mechanisms
were grouped into four channels (Digital Marketing and Food
Choice: Mechanisms of Influence). In the second, evidence on
nutritional awareness was grouped by the mechanism through
which it was argued to operate ( The Moderating Role of
Nutritional Awareness). In the third, points of contradi ction
between the two bodies of evidence — in particular, cases in
which nutritional knowledge failed to protect against
marketing influence — were treated as the analytic focus,
since it is these inconsistencies that the proposed model seeks
to explain. As a single-author conceptual review, the screening
and charting were not duplicated by a second reviewer; this is
acknowledged in Limitations.
Theoretical Frameworks and Their Integration
Four literatures inform the model developed in The
Integrative Conce ptual Model . They are summarized briefly
below, but the substantive contribution of this section lies in
Integration: why no single framework suffices, which specifies
how they are combined. Each framework is individually well
established and individually insufficient for the problem at
hand.
❖ Theory of Planned behavior
Ajzen’s [2,3] Theory of Planned behavior (TPB) holds that
behavioral intention — the most proximal predictor of
behavior — is shaped by attitudes toward the behavior,
subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control (PBC).
Meta-analytic evidence supports the model’s predictive utility
across health behaviors, with the three antecedents typically
accounting for a substantial share of variance in intention,
though a persistent intention – behavior gap remains [5].
Applied to food choice, digital marketing can shift attitudes
(portraying a product as desirable or healthy), shape
subjective norms (through visible peer engagement and
influencer endorsement), and affect PBC (through
convenience-oriented messaging emphasizing the ease of
healthy meal preparation, or conversely the effort it requires).
The TPB thus supplies the mediating architecture of the
present model: it specifies through what psychological
channels marketing exposure reaches behavior.
❖ Health Belief Model
The Health Belief Model (HBM), originating in public health
research associated with Rosenstock [56] and elaborated by
Janz and Becker [36], explains health behavior as a function of
perceived susceptibility to health threats, perceived severity of
consequences, perceived benefits of action, and perceived
barriers, moderated by cues to action and self -efficacy.
Nutritional awareness campaigns and digital health content
frequently operate through this model, using informational
cues — statistics on diet-related disease risk, for instance — to
activate perceived susceptibility and severity. Meta -analytic
evidence indicates that perceived benefits and perceived
barriers are the strongest HBM predictors, while susceptibility
and severi ty are comparatively weak [14]. This asymmetry
matters: it implies that threat -based nutrition messaging, in
the absence of corresponding efficacy and barrier -reduction
support, is a weak lever and may produce defensive avoidance
rather than behavior change. The HBM contributes to the
present model the recognition that nutritional awareness
carries an affective and motivational component, not merely a
cognitive one.
❖ Elaboration Likelihood Model
Petty and Cacioppo’s [51] Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM)
distinguishes central -route persuasion, in which attitude
change results from careful evaluation of message content
such as scrutinizing a nutritional claim, from peripheral-route
persuasion, in which attitude change results from superficial
cues such as source attractiveness, emotional appeal, or social
proof. The route taken depends on the recipient’s motivation
and ability to elaborate. This distinction is directly relevant to
digital marketing: short -form video, autoplay feeds, and
infinite scroll a re structurally suited to peripheral -route cues
and structurally hostile to deliberation, which helps explain
how nutritionally inferior products can be marketed
effectively despite minimal informational content. The related
Persuasion Knowledge Model [27] adds that consumers deploy
stored knowledge about persuasive intent when they

and influencer formats are designed to suppress. The ELM
supplies the model’s second moderator: it specifies the
conditions under which nutritional awareness can be brought
to bear at all.
❖ Nutrition literacy and food label research
A distinct stream of research examines how consumers
process nutrition labels and front -of-pack claims [20, 28, 29,
13]. This literature con sistently demonstrates that possessing
nutritional knowledge does not guarantee its application at
the point of decision: label use is contingent on motivation,
time pressure, numeracy, and label design, and self -reported
label use substantially exceeds ob served use [29]. Systematic
review and meta -analytic evidence indicate that labelling
interventions produce real but modest shifts in choice, with
interpretive front -of-pack formats outperforming purely
numerical ones [15, 32]. Parallel conceptual work distinguishes
nutrition literacy from the broader construct of food literacy,
which includes planning, selection, preparation, and
consumption competencies [71, 69] . The gap between
knowledge and applied literacy is central to understanding
why nutritional aw areness does not uniformly translate into
healthier purchasing, even among informed consumers.
❖ Integration: why no single framework suffices
These frameworks are typically applied in isolation, and each
fails at a different point in the phenomenon of inter est. The
TPB specifies the psychological pathways from exposure to
behavior but is silent on the processing mode through which
a persuasive message enters those pathways; it treats attitudes
as inputs without theorizing how they were formed. The ELM
specifies processing mode but stops at attitude change and
does not model norms or perceived control, both of which are
central to food choice in social digital environments. The HBM
captures the motivational activation on which nutrition
messaging depends but h as little to say about commercial
persuasion and no account of social influence. The nutrition
literacy literature documents the knowledge –action gap with
precision but generally treats marketing as background noise
rather than as an active countervailing force.
The integration proposed here assigns each framework a
distinct structural role rather than layering them additively.
The TPB provides the mediating mechanism (attitudes,
norms, PBC), the ELM provides the processing -mode
moderator that determines wh ich route marketing takes, the
nutrition literacy literature provides the substantive content
of the awareness moderator and its measurement, and the
HBM specifies the motivational conditions under which
awareness translates into intention. The critical th eoretical
move is to place nutritional awareness and elaboration
likelihood in a conjoint moderating position: awareness
supplies the capacity to evaluate marketing claims, while
elaboration likelihood determines whether that capacity is
deployed. This pre dicts — and explains — the otherwise
puzzling finding that nutritional knowledge is only weakly
protective in naturalistic settings, since the digital
environments in which most food marketing now occurs
systematically suppress elaboration.

Digital Marketing and Food Choice: Mechanisms of
Influence
Digital food marketing is not a single treatment but a family
of mechanisms with different theoretical signatures and
different evidentiary strength. Four established channels and
one emerging cluster are distinguished below.
❖ Social media advertising and algorithmic
targeting
Social media platforms enable granular targeting of food
advertisements using demographic, behavioral, and
psychographic data, and content analyses have documented
extensive promotion of energy-dense, nutrient-poor products
through these channels [26, 57] . Reviews of food marketing
exposure — much of it originating in studies of adolescent and
young-adult populations — associate frequent exposure to
such advertising with increased snacking and strong er
preference for advertised products [11, 54], and meta -analytic
evidence indicates small but consistent effects on intake and
choice [10, 58] . Experimental work indicates that sustained
exposure to online food advertising increases energy intake
relative to non-food advertising controls [47]. Mechanistically,

these effects appear to operate largely through peripheral,
low-elaboration processing consistent with the ELM, and are
amplified by the personalization of delivery: targeting means
the cue arrives pre-matched to the recipient’s susceptibilities.
Importantly, exposure is not distributed evenly — socio-
economically disadvantaged and minority -ethnic groups face
disproportionate exposure, with equity consequences [6].
❖ Influencer and peer-driven marketing
Influencer marketing leverages parasocial relationships and
perceived source credibility to shape food attitudes, often
blurring the boundary between organic content and paid
promotion [21, 35]. Randomized experimental evidence shows
that exposure to infl uencer marketing of unhealthy snacks
increases children’s immediate intake, and — significantly for
policy — that a standard advertising disclosure does not
eliminate the effect and may not reliably trigger persuasion
knowledge in younger audiences [17, 18, 60]. Content analyses
further document unhealthy product placement in child -
influencer content at scale [4]. Because influencer content
frequently appears within lifestyle or wellness framing, it can
be persuasive for healthy and unhealthy products alike ,
illustrating that digital marketing techniques are valence -
neutral tools whose well -being impact depends on the
nutritional quality of what is promoted and the transparency
of the promotional relationship. Disclosure research indicates
that when sponsorship is recognized, persuasion knowledge is
activated and can reduce persuasive impact — but recognition
is precisely what native formats are designed to impede [27, 8].
❖ Health and nutrition applications
Conversely, mobile applications for calorie tracking,  recipe
recommendation, and grocery planning represent a form of
digital information delivery explicitly oriented toward
facilitating healthy choice. Such tools can strengthen
perceived behavioral control by reducing the practical burden
of healthy meal planning, and can embed recognized behavior
change techniques including self -monitoring, goal setting,
and feedback [45]. Systematic reviews and meta -analyses
report small -to-moderate positive effects of app -based
interventions on nutrition behaviors and related outcomes,
with effects strongest for self -monitoring-intensive designs
and weakest where engagement decays [23, 72]. Two caveats
are substantial. First, self -selection into app use confounds
observational estimates: those who download nutritio n apps
differ systematically from those who do not. Second, sustained
engagement is low, and effect estimates from trials with
intensive support may not generalize to organic consumer use.
Possible unintended consequences , considers a further
concern regarding possible harms in vulnerable subgroups.
❖ User-generated content and social proof
Beyond brand -originated content, peer -generated posts —
meal photography, reviews, and recommendation threads —
function as informal digital marketing that shapes norms
about what constitutes a desirable or normal diet. This
channel operates largely through the subjective -norm
component of the TPB and can reinforce either health -
conscious or indulgence-oriented eating norms, depending on
the composition of a consumer’s soci al network and the
platform algorithm curating it [60]. Because the content is not
commercially labelled, persuasion knowledge is rarely
activated, and because algorithmic curation is responsive to
prior engagement, exposure tends to become self-reinforcing.
The evidence base here is thinner and more observational than
for paid advertising, and effect estimates should be treated as
provisional.
❖ Emerging mechanisms
Three developments postdate much of the reviewed evidence
and warrant explicit flagging. First, generative AI now permits
low-cost production of personalized persuasive food content,
including synthetic endorsement, at volumes that outstrip the
disclosure and monitoring regimes designed for human
influencers. Second, gamified formats — advergames and in-
app rewards — have demonstrable persuasive effects on
children’s food preferences and consumption [25, 24] , and
their mechanics migrate readily into delivery and loyalty
applications. Third, quick -commerce and delivery platforms
fuse marketing and purchase into a single interface, collapsing
the interval between exposure and transaction in which
deliberation might otherwise occur. Each of these
developments should, on the logic of the model developed
here, further depress elaboration likelihood and th erefore
weaken the protective value of nutritional awareness — a
prediction that is currently untested.

The Moderating Role of Nutritional Awareness
Nutritional awareness is proposed here as a ke y moderator of
digital marketing’s effect on food choice, rather than as a
direct determinant of dietary quality. The distinction is
consequential: a determinant framing predicts that raising
knowledge raises diet quality, whereas a moderator framing
predicts that raising knowledge changes how strongly
marketing acts — an effect that can be large or negligible
depending on conditions. The moderating role is argued to
operate through at least three mechanisms.
❖ Three moderating mechanisms
Critical evaluation capacity. Consumers with higher
nutrition literacy are more likely to engage in central -route
processing of marketing claims, scrutinizing health claims
rather than accepting peripheral cues at face value [51, 28] .
Capacity, however, is not deployment: the  ELM specifies that
ability without motivation does not produce central
processing.
Label and claim verification.  Nutritionally aware
consumers are more likely to cross-reference marketing claims
against nutrition labels, reducing susceptibility to ‘health halo’
effects in which a product is perceived as healthier than it is
because of an isolated positive claim — ‘low fat’ on a high -
sugar product, for example [16, 53]. Interpretive front-of-pack
formats appear to lower the literacy threshold required for this
verification [15].
Resistance to peripheral persuasion.  Greater nutrition
knowledge, particularly when coupled with persuasion
knowledge, is associated with reduced susceptibility to
emotionally or socially driven marketing appeals [27, 8]. This
effect is attenuated — in some studies to non -significance —
under time pressure, low involvement, cognitive load, or
fatigue [44].
The relationship is not unidirectional.  Digital platforms
are themselves increasingly a primary source through which
consumers acq uire nutritional knowledge, including from
commercially interested actors, meaning that marketing and
awareness co-evolve within the same environment rather than
operating as independent forces [73]. This reciprocal path is
represented explicitly in the mo del in The Integrative
Conceptual Model  and, to the author’s knowledge, is not
incorporated in existing frameworks in this domain.
❖ Measurement and operationalization problems
A central obstacle to synthesizing this literature — and a
reason the present rev iew is conceptual rather than meta -
analytic — is that ‘nutritional awareness’ denotes at least five
distinguishable constructs across studies, measured with
instruments of markedly different provenance and
psychometric quality:

                                                                                                                                                               17

• Declarative nutrition knowle dge, typically assessed
with adapted versions of the General Nutrition
Knowledge Questionnaire [50, 39] , which has
established reliability but measures recall rather than
application.
• Label literacy and numeracy, assessed via
comprehension tasks on real or simulated labels [29,
13] Campos et al., 2011), which predicts label use
better than declarative knowledge but is rarely
measured alongside it.
• Functional health literacy, assessed with general
instruments not specific to food [48], which travels
well across health domains but is insensitive to
nutrition-specific competencies.
• Food literacy, a broader competency construct
including planning, selection, and preparation [71,
69], conceptually richer but with less measurement
consensus.
• Self-reported nutri tional attentiveness or
involvement, frequently single -item and vulnerable
to social desirability, yet common in consumer
survey work.
Two consequences follow. First, apparent inconsistency in
findings on whether nutritional awareness protects against
marketing may partly reflect construct heterogeneity rather
than genuine effect heterogeneity: studies reporting null
protective effects disproportionately use declarative
knowledge measures, whereas those reporting protective
effects more often use applied li teracy or involvement
measures. Second, common -method variance is a serious
threat, since awareness, marketing exposure, and dietary
outcomes are frequently all self -reported in the same
instrument [52, 63] . Empirical tests of the present model
should therefore report which construct is measured, justify
the instrument, and where possible include both a declarative
and an applied measure so that their divergent moderating
roles can be estimated separately.
❖ Boundary conditions of the protective effect
The re viewed evidence suggests the protective effect of
nutritional awareness is bounded in at least four ways. It
weakens sharply under conditions that suppress elaboration
— time pressure, decision fatigue, and interfaces engineered
for speed. It is largely ab sent in young children, whose
developmental capacity for advertising skepticism is limited,
making informational safeguards a poor substitute for
exposure restriction [18, 41] . It is weaker where structural
barriers dominate: knowledge cannot be enacted wh ere
affordable healthy options are unavailable [62, 1]. And it does
not extend well to formats that are not recognized as
marketing at all, including peer content and native placement.
These boundaries are not peripheral caveats; they are the
substantive content of the model’s moderating claims and the
source of its testable predictions.
The Integrative Conceptual Model
This section presents the paper’s core contribution: a
conceptual model of healthy food choice in digitally mediated
environments, together  with four testable propositions, an
account of how the model advances existing frameworks, and
a specification of its boundary conditions.
❖ Model logic
The model holds that healthy food choice behavior results
from the interaction of digital marketing expo sure and
nutritional awareness, mediated by attitudes, subjective
norms, and perceived behavioral control as specified in the
TPB, and moderated by elaboration likelihood as specified in
the ELM. High nutritional awareness is hypothesized to shift
consumers toward central-route processing of food marketing,
strengthening the link between accurate nutritional
information and healthy choice, while low nutritional
awareness leaves consumers more susceptible to peripheral
marketing cues regardless of a product’ s actual nutritional
quality. Critically, the two moderators are conjoint rather than
substitutable: awareness supplies evaluative capacity,
elaboration likelihood determines whether that capacity is
engaged, and neither alone is sufficient. Digital market ing, in
this model, is not treated as uniformly harmful or beneficial,
but as a channel whose net effect on well -being depends on
the nutritional content of what is promoted, the processing
mode the format induces, and the literacy of the audience it
reaches. A reciprocal path from the digital environment to
nutritional awareness acknowledges that the same platforms
supply both the marketing and much of the nutrition
information assumed to counteract it.

❖ Propositions
The following propositions render the model empirically
falsifiable. They are stated at the level of directional
prediction; operational guidance for testing them appears in
Methodological Considerations for Future Research.

18

https://doi.org/10.63954/3xzrsg73

P1. Exposure to digital food marketing is positively associated
with (a) attitudes toward marketed products, primarily via
peripheral-route processing; (b) subjective norms regardin g
consumption of those products, via visible social proof; and (c)
perceived behavioral control over acquiring them, via
convenience framing. Where marketed products are energy -
dense and nutrient -poor, these paths jointly predict reduced
healthy food choice.
P2. Nutritional awareness moderates the effect of digital
marketing on food choice, such that (a) higher awareness
reduces susceptibility to peripheral marketing cues; (b) higher
awareness strengthens central-route processing of nutritional
claims; and (c) higher awareness attenuates the health -halo
effect. The moderating effect is predicted to be stronger for
applied label-literacy measures than for declarative knowledge
measures.
P3. The interaction of marketing exposure and nutritional
awareness on food choice is mediated by attitudes, subjective
norms, and perceived behavioral control, as specified by the
TPB. That is, the model predicts moderated mediation rather
than a direct interaction on behavior.
P4. Elaboration likelihood moderates the marketin g–
awareness interaction, such that (a) under high elaboration,
nutritional awareness is more protective; and (b) under low
elaboration, peripheral marketing cues dominate largely
regardless of awareness. This is a three -way interaction and
constitutes the model’s strongest and most distinctive
prediction.
P5. Digital marketing exposure and nutritional awareness are
reciprocally related over time: platform use that increases
marketing exposure also increases exposure to nutrition
information of variable quality, so cross-sectional estimates of
the moderating effect will be biased unless this endogeneity is
modelled.
❖ How the model advances existing frameworks
Three advances are claimed. First, existing applications of the
TPB to food choice typically enter nutrition knowledge as an
additional direct predictor of intention; the present model
instead specifies it as a moderator of the marketing –mediator
paths, which accounts for the repeated finding that knowledge
predicts intention better than behavior. Second, existing ELM
applications in food marketing treat processing route as an
outcome of message design; the present model treats
elaboration likelihood as a m oderator of a moderation,
thereby specifying the conditions under which awareness -
based interventions should and should not be expected to
work. This yields the concrete, falsifiable prediction that
awareness-based interventions will fail in low -elaboration
environments even where the awareness gain itself is real — a
prediction that distinguishes the model from the general claim
that education ‘sometimes’ works. Third, no existing
framework in this literature, to the author’s knowledge,
models the reciproc al supply of nutrition information by the
marketing channel itself, which has direct implications for
research design (P5) as well as for the interpretation of
platform-based health promotion.
❖ Boundary conditions
The model is specified for adult and adoles cent consumers
with the developmental capacity for advertising skepticism; it
is not intended to apply to young children, for whom exposure
restriction rather than literacy is the appropriate lever. It
assumes a choice environment in which healthier alternatives
are physically and economically available, since neither
awareness nor elaboration can be enacted where structural
access is absent. It assumes marketing is at least potentially
recognizable as such; fully covert formats are expected to
bypass the m oderators entirely. Finally, it is specified at the
level of discrete choice episodes rather than long -run dietary
patterns, and does not model habit formation, which is likely
to attenuate all deliberative paths over repeated exposure.
Methodological Considerations for Future Research
❖ Research designs
• Longitudinal panel designs tracking nutrition
literacy, digital marketing exposure, and dietary
outcomes at three or more waves, to address the
causal ambiguity of cross -sectional survey research
and to estimate the reciprocal path in P5 using cross-
lagged panel or random -intercept cross -lagged
models.
• Factorial experiments to test P4 directly. A 2
(marketing cue type: central-route informational vs.
peripheral-route affective) × 2 (nutrition literacy:
measured or primed high vs. low) × 2 (elaboration
opportunity: unconstrained vs. time -pressured)
design isolates the three -way interaction that
constitutes the model’s distinctive prediction.
Elaboration can be manipulated via response
deadlines, concurrent cognitive load, or scroll-paced
presentation.
• Ecological momentary assessment pairing in -the-
moment exposure prompts with immediate choice
reports, reducing the recall error that attends
retrospective dietary measures.
• Platform-level behavioral data — ad-exposure logs,
screen-capture sampling, app -usage analytics —
triangulated with purchase records (loyalty -card or
receipt data) rather than self-reported intake, where
data-access and ethical approvals permit.
• Field experiments with retailers or delivery
platforms, in which interpretive labelling or friction-
adding interface changes are randomized, providing
external validity that laboratory designs cannot.
❖ Measurement
• Report which awareness construct is measured

and use instruments with published reliability and
validity evidence: the revised General Nutrition
Knowledge Questionnaire for declarative knowledge
[39], objective label-comprehension tasks for applied

• Include both a declarative and an applied awareness

❖ Sampling and statistical power
The model’s central predictions are  interaction effects, which
require substantially larger samples than main-effect tests. As
a working guide, detecting a small -to-moderate two -way
interaction in a moderated mediation model typically requires
several hundred participants, while the three -way interaction
in P4 will generally require samples in the high hundreds to
low thousands for adequate power; a priori power analysis
using Monte Carlo simulation for the specific indirect -effect
structure is preferable to rules of thumb. Samples should be
stratified by socio -economic position given the documented
differential exposure to unhealthy food advertising [6], and
adolescent samples should be analyzed separately rather than
pooled with adults.
❖ Analytic approaches
• Structural equation modelling with latent constructs
for the TPB mediators, permitting simultaneous
estimation of the mediation paths while correcting
for measurement error.
• Moderated mediation (conditional process) analysis
with bias -corrected bootstrap confidence intervals
for conditiona l indirect effects, rather than
sequential regression with mean -split moderators
[33].
• Multi-group or multilevel models where consumers
are nested within platforms, retailers, or countries,
since exposure is clustered by design.
• Pre-registration of the int eraction hypotheses and
the analysis plan, given the known flexibility of
interaction testing and the elevated risk of false
positives in exploratory moderator search.

❖ Cross-cultural scope and vulnerable populations
Food norms, advertising regulation, and  nutrition education
systems vary substantially across countries and shape both
marketing exposure and literacy levels, yet the reviewed
evidence originates disproportionately from high -income
Western settings. Replication in low - and middle -income
contexts — where digital penetration is rising rapidly,
regulatory capacity is often weaker, and the nutrition
transition is ongoing — is a priority, and comparative designs
exploiting regulatory variation (for example the Chilean
marketing and labelling reforms;  Taillie et al., [65]) offer
natural-experiment leverage. Attention to vulnerable
populations is equally important: children and adolescents
exhibit lower resistance to peripheral -route persuasion and
are frequent targets of digital food marketing [10, 41] , and
socio-economically disadvantaged groups face both higher
exposure and greater structural barriers to enacting healthier
choices.
Discussion
❖ Relation to previous reviews
The conclusions here converge with, and depart from,
previous reviews in specific ways. On exposure and effect, this
review concurs with meta -analytic syntheses that unhealthy
food marketing exerts small but consistent effects on
children’s and adolescents’ intake and preferences [58, 10, 54],
and with WHO assessments that digital chan nels have
expanded both the reach and the persuasive power of that
marketing while outpacing regulation [77, 78]. On labelling, it
concurs with syntheses finding real but modest effects, larger
for interpretive formats [15]. The departure is one of framing
rather than of finding: the public health reviews largely treat
consumer literacy as a weak or supplementary lever and
conclude in favor of exposure restriction, while the consumer
research literature treats literacy as a determinant to be raised.
The present model reconciles these positions by specifying the
condition — elaboration likelihood — under which each
conclusion holds. On this account the public health reviews
are right about the environments in which most digital food
marketing occurs, and the consumer research literature is
right about the environments in which deliberation is feasible;
the disagreement is about the modal case, not about the
mechanism.
❖ Contradictory and null evidence
Critical engagement requires acknowledging evidence that
does not fit. Several strands qualify the model. First, a number
of studies find nutrition knowledge to be a weak or non -
significant predictor of dietary quality once socio -economic
position and food access are controlled, which is consistent
with the moderator framing but also raises the possibility that
awareness effects are partly confounded by unmeasured
resources. Second, effect sizes in marketing -exposure meta -
analyses are small, and are drawn substantially from short -
term laboratory measures of intake wh ose relationship to
habitual diet is unestablished; publication and small -study
effects have been raised as concerns in this literature. Third,
evidence on app -based interventions is heterogeneous, with
several trials reporting null effects on dietary outc omes and
attrition frequently exceeding half the sample, so the positive
PBC path in the model should be regarded as the weakest -
supported. Fourth, disclosure research yields mixed results:

recognition of sponsorship sometimes reduces persuasion and
sometimes increases engagement, and in child samples
disclosure has failed to attenuate intake effects at all [18]. Fifth,
the intention–behavior gap documented in TPB meta-analyses
[5] means that a model resting on intention as the proximal
mediator will systematically over-predict behavior, and habit
and automaticity may be doing work the model assigns to
deliberation [44]. These are not marginal caveats: taken
together they imply the model’s central three-way interaction
may prove smaller and more context -dependent than its
theoretical prominence suggests.
❖ Why nutrition education campaigns often fail
The model offers a mechanistic account of a well-documented
disappointment. Mass -media health campaigns produce
measurable but small behavioral effects, generally requiring
sustained exposure, supportive environments, and
complementary structural measures [74]. Four failure
mechanisms follow from the framework developed here. First,
asymmetry of resources and repetition: commercial food
marketing outspends nutrition promotion by orders of
magnitude and reaches consumers with far greater frequency
and precision. Second, route mismatch: nutrition education is
characteristically delivered as central -route informational
content into environments engin eered for peripheral
processing, so it is not competing on the same channel. Third,
motivational insufficiency: consistent with meta -analytic
HBM findings, campaigns that raise perceived susceptibility
without lowering perceived barriers or raising self -efficacy
generate awareness without action [14]. Fourth, differential
uptake: information -based interventions are preferentially
adopted by higher-literacy, higher-resource groups, which can
widen dietary inequalities even where average effects are
positive [1]. The practical corollary is that the failure is not
primarily one of message quality but of channel, motivation,
and structural context.
❖ Implementation challenges
The recommendations in Section 9 face substantial barriers,
and it would be misleading to present them as straightforward.
Media literacy components require curriculum time and
trained educators, and meta -analytic evidence indicates that
media literacy interventions shift knowledge and attitudes
reliably but behavior less so, with effects that decay without
reinforcement [37]. Influencer disclosure regimes depend on
the detectability of undisclosed sponsorship across millions of
accounts and on enforcement capacity that most regulators
lack; disclosure also assumes an  audience able to act on it,
which the child evidence contradicts. Platform -level
advertising restrictions confront jurisdictional limits,
definitional disputes over which products count as unhealthy,
and the ready migration of marketing to formats and
territories outside the restriction’s scope. Algorithmic
transparency requirements encounter commercial
confidentiality claims and the practical difficulty of auditing
systems that are personalized by construction. Structural
measures such as reformulation in centives and pricing policy
have the strongest evidence but the greatest political cost [31,
55]. Recognizing these constraints is a precondition for
prioritization rather than an argument against action.
❖ Possible unintended consequences
Raising nutritional awareness is not costless, and the literature
identifies several potential adverse consequences that
intervention designers should anticipate. Intensive self -
monitoring through calorie -tracking applications has been
associated in observational research w ith heightened dietary
preoccupation and, in some samples, with disordered eating
symptomatology, with the direction of causation unresolved
and self -selection a serious confound [59, 43] . Strongly
moralized nutrition messaging risks stigma and, for
individuals with existing vulnerability, may reinforce rigid
rule-based eating. Information -based approaches can also
individualize responsibility for a problem that is substantially
environmental, providing rhetorical cover for inaction on
structural determinants, and — as noted above — may widen
inequalities through differential uptake. Practical mitigation
includes designing literacy interventions around flexible
rather than restrictive rules, avoiding fear -based framing
without efficacy support, monitoring ad verse outcomes as a
matter of course in nutrition -app trials, and pairing
individual-level measures with environmental ones. The
purpose of raising these possibilities is not to argue against
nutrition education but to specify what responsible
implementation entails.
Practical and Policy Implications
❖ For health communicators
Nutrition education campaigns are unlikely to succeed if
delivered purely as informational content without addressing
the peripheral -route persuasive techniques used in
commercial food marketing. Concretely, this suggests:
embedding advertising -literacy modules that teach
recognition of native advertising, influencer sponsorship, and
health-halo claims alongside nutrient content; delivering
content in the formats and on the platforms whe re food
marketing occurs, in short -form video rather than long -form
text; pairing threat information with explicit barrier-reduction
and efficacy content, consistent with HBM evidence; and
using interpretive rather than numerical presentation to lower
the literacy threshold. Evaluation should be specified in
advance and should include behavioral or purchase endpoints,
retention measures at three and six months given documented
decay of media-literacy effects, and stratified analysis by socio-
economic position to detect inequitable uptake.
❖ For marketers of healthier products
For marketers of healthier food products, the findings suggest
that pairing credible, verifiable nutritional claims with the

                                                                                                                                                               21

same peripheral cues — social proof, relatable messaging,
aspirational framing — used by less healthy competitors may
improve competitiveness without misleading consumers.
Specific strategies indicated by the reviewed evidence include
leading with taste and identity appeals rather than health
warnings, since health -framed positioning can depress
expected palatability; using credible micro -influencers with
transparent disclosure, which sustains persuasion while
preserving trust; reducing friction at the point of purchase,
which acts on perceived  behavioral control rat her than
attitudes; and avoiding isolated single -nutrient claims, which
invite health -halo effects and, once recognized, damage
credibility among the most nutritionally literate consumers.
❖ For policymakers
The review supports continued attention to the reg ulation of
health claims and to disclosure requirements for influencer -
sponsored food content, given the demonstrated susceptibility
of low -literacy consumers to health -halo and peripheral
marketing effects. Specific regulatory approaches indicated by
the evidence include: restrictions on the targeting of food
advertising to children and adolescents on digital platforms,
aligned with current WHO guidance [78]; standardized,
machine-readable, and prominently placed sponsorship
disclosure, coupled with enforc ement resourcing rather than
voluntary codes; advertising-content restrictions defined by a
validated nutrient profiling model to avoid definitional
disputes; algorithmic transparency and researcher data-access
obligations, without which independent assess ment of
exposure is impossible; mandatory interpretive front -of-pack
labelling, for which the evidence base is comparatively strong;
and evaluation clauses attached to new measures so that
natural-experiment evidence accumulates.

Limitations
The limitations of this paper are substantial and fall into two
groups: limitations of the review itself, and limitations of the
evidence base it synthesizes.
❖ Limitations of this review
Non-systematic synthesis. Although the search strategy and
inclusion criteria are reported, this is an integrative
conceptual review rather than a systematic review. It does not
claim exhaustive coverage, no formal risk -of-bias appraisal
was conducted, and no pooled effect estimates are offered.
Single-reviewer screening.  Screening and charting were
performed by one author without duplicate independent
assessment, so selection bias cannot be excluded and inter -
rater reliability cannot be reported.
Language and database restriction.  The restriction to
English-language sources and to four databases will have
under-represented research from non-Anglophone and lower-
income settings — precisely the contexts the paper identifies
as under-studied.
Untested model. The integrative model and its propositions
are derived analytically and have not been empirically
validated. They require testing across product categories,
platforms, and populations before any confidence in the
specified interactions is warranted.
❖ Limitations of the evidence base
Construct operationalization. As set out in Measurement
and operationalization problems, ‘digital marketing exposure’
and ‘nutritional awareness’ are measured heterogeneously
across studies — exposure via self-report, content analysis, or
platform logs; awareness via at least five distinct instruments
— with correspondingly variable validity and reliability, which
limits comparability and makes apparent inconsistencies
difficult to interpret.
Causality. Most non-experimental evidence is cross-sectional
and correlational, while most experimental evidence measures
short-term intake in laboratory settings. Neither design
supports inference about effects on habitual diet, and the

22

https://doi.org/10.63954/3xzrsg73

reciprocal relationship posited in P5 means c ross-sectional
moderator estimates are likely biased.
Contextual specificity.  The evidence is drawn
predominantly from Western, high -income countries with
particular regulatory regimes and food environments, limiting
generalizability to the settings where digital penetration is
growing fastest.
Rapid technological change.  The digital marketing
landscape evolves faster than the publication cycle. Generative
AI content, increasingly personalized algorithmic targeting,
and quick -commerce interfaces may introduce persuasive
mechanisms not captured by frameworks developed before
these technologies, so parts of the reviewed evidence may
already describe a superseded environment.
Publication and small -study bias.  Positive findings are
likely over-represented in both the marketing -effects and the
app-intervention literatures, and several relevant meta -
analyses rest on small trials.
Self-report bias. Most dietary intake and marketing exposure
measures ar e self -reported and subject to recall error and
social desirability, with well -documented systematic under -
reporting of energy intake [63]; common-method variance is a
further threat where all constructs share a single instrument
[52].
Conclusion
Digital m arketing and nutritional awareness jointly shape
consumer behavior toward healthy food choices, often in
interacting and sometimes counteracting ways. Rather than
treating digital marketing as uniformly detrimental to healthy
eating, this review argues that its effects are contingent on
message content, platform -driven p rocessing mode, and the
nutritional literacy of the audience it reaches. The integrative
model developed here makes that contingency explicit:
nutritional awareness supplies the capacity to evaluate
marketing claims, elaboration likelihood determines wheth er
that capacity is deployed, and the psychological pathways
specified by the Theory of Planned behavior carry the resulting
effects through  choice. The model’s most consequential
implication is also its most testable: interventions that raise
nutritional awareness should be expected to fail in
environments engineered to suppress deliberation, however
successfully they raise knowledge. If that prediction holds, the
policy inference is not that consumer literacy is worthless but
that it must be paired with m easures that either restore the
opportunity to deliberate or restrict exposure where
deliberation is not possible — and, for young children, with
exposure restriction alone. A social science approach
integrating consumer psychology, marketing science, and
public health nutrition offers the most complete account of
this dynamic, and provides a foundation for interventions —
educational, regulatory, and commercial — that support
healthier food choice in an increasingly algorithmically
mediated food environmen t. The empirical validation of the
propositions advanced here is the necessary next step.
