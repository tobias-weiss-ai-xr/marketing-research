Abstract
Acne vulgaris requires sustained treatment, yet adherence may weaken before meaningful improvement
occurs, particularly when topical therapy causes xerosis, peeling, erythema, or stinging. Digital acne
content can expand access to education and patient perspectives, but it also places clinical advice beside
commercial claims, transformation narratives, and complex skincare routines without a consistent hierarchy
of evidence. This narrative review examined evidence relevant to how psychodermatological vulnerability
and digital skincare exposure may contribute to cosmetic self-experimentation, cutaneous irritation, and
treatment non-adherence, while distinguishing observed associations from untested mechanistic links. This
digital exposure includes exposure to online acne/skincare recommendations, social media advice,
influencer content, transformation narratives, and algorithmically reinforced skincare-routine content.
PubMed, Embase, and Google Scholar were searched through July 22, 2026, for English-language literature
published since 2016. Across the reviewed literature, acne-related stigma, depressive symptoms, body
dysmorphic symptoms, and treatment expectations were associated with psychological burden, treatment
satisfaction, and intention to adhere. Separate studies also described patient uptake of online acne
recommendations, clinician-observed dermatoses associated with multi-step cosmetic routines, and
associations between cutaneous adverse effects and poorer medication adherence. Together, these findings
support a conceptual model in which psychodermatological distress, delayed improvement, online product
exposure, cutaneous symptoms, and adherence difficulties may interact, but the available evidence does not
establish this sequence causally or longitudinally. No longitudinal study measured digital exposure, product
additions, objective barrier change, symptom attribution, and medication persistence in the same patients.
Acne care should therefore emphasize realistic treatment timelines, application demonstration, regimen
simplification, early follow-up, and detailed review of prescribed and non-prescribed products. These
findings do not justify treating all digital content or non-prescription skincare as harmful. Clinician-
produced content may support patient education, and selected dermocosmetics may have a role as adjuncts
to structured acne care.

Introduction And Background
Acne vulgaris is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the pilosebaceous unit and one of the most prevalent
skin diseases globally, affecting 9.4% of the global population in 2010 and ranking as the eighth most
prevalent disease worldwide
[1]
. Evidence-based care is guided by lesion morphology, severity, scarring risk,
tolerability, patient preference, and psychosocial burden, with strong clinical recommendations supporting
benzoyl peroxide, topical retinoids, topical antibiotics, oral doxycycline, and oral isotretinoin for severe,
scarring, treatment-resistant, or psychosocially burdensome disease
[1]
. This therapeutic standard places
acne treatment within a longitudinal disease-control model: reduction in inflammatory and
noninflammatory lesion count, prevention of post-inflammatory dyspigmentation and scarring, and
maintenance of remission through sustained exposure to appropriate pharmacologic agents. It is not a
cosmetic trial-and-error process. Effective acne care requires time.
Therapeutic adherence remains fragile during the earliest phase of treatment. Acne management involves
numerous topical and systemic options, long-term disease control, and active patient cooperation, yet
undertreatment and non-adherence persist despite available therapies
[2]
. The first failure point occurs
before many regimens have had adequate biological time to work. Topical treatments are first-line for mild-
to-moderate acne, but persistence is limited when patients experience xerosis, peeling, erythema, stinging,
or irritation without immediate visible reduction in lesion burden. Counseling must account for the
expected six-to-eight-week interval before meaningful improvement is typically observed; without that
anticipatory guidance, early adverse effects can be misread as therapeutic failure
[3]
. The adherence problem
begins inside this mismatch between pharmacologic time and cosmetic urgency.

Treatment friction may occur within a measurable psychosocial context. Acne carries psychodermatological
consequences that extend beyond visible lesions, with appearance comparison, stigma, avoidance behavior,
and media-shaped beauty ideals contributing to patient distress
[4]
. Quantitative data from a cross-sectional
cohort of 150 patients with acne vulgaris link acne severity with depression, anxiety, and stress, while
regression modeling demonstrates negative effects of acne and psychological distress on self-esteem and
quality of life
[5]
. The patient waiting for lesion resolution may be managing embarrassment, social
vigilance, diminished confidence, and a need to regain control over a highly visible disease. The behavioral
relevance of digital exposure is already observable: among 358 adult female acne patients, 20.9% reported
willingness to alter physician-prescribed treatment based on social media advice
[6]
.
This review examines how psychodermatological vulnerability and digital skincare marketing interact to
shape cosmetic self-experimentation, cutaneous irritation, and treatment adherence in acne vulgaris, and
evaluates which clinician-led responses are supported by the current evidence. The literature does not
establish a single longitudinal causal chain. It does, however, support a hypothesized conceptual pathway:
appearance-related distress may narrow tolerance for delayed improvement; digital content can supply
highly visible product-centered alternatives; multi-product exposure may increase irritant and allergen
burden; and treatment-associated or product-induced symptoms may contribute to premature
discontinuation (Figure

Records were screened in a stepwise manner: duplicate or clearly irrelevant records were removed, titles and
abstracts were screened for relevance to acne management and adherence, psychodermatological
vulnerability, digital content quality, consumer-product exposure, or clinician response, and full texts were
reviewed for direct relevance to treatment adherence, digital product use, skin barrier injury, cutaneous
adverse effects, or clinician-led adherence interventions. Reference lists of selected publications were
manually searched for additional evidence. Included patient, clinical, and consumer-behavior studies
represented multiple international settings, including Saudi Arabia, Thailand, Turkey, the Netherlands, and
Sri Lanka.
Non-English literature was excluded because formal translation was not available. Single-patient case
reports and small case series were excluded because this review focused on population-level behavior,
digital content environments, clinician-observed trends, and adherence patterns rather than rare individual
adverse events. Individual social media posts, brand websites, industry white papers, promotional skincare
materials, and non-peer-reviewed marketing reports were not used as principal evidence. Regulatory
documents were not used as principal sources because the review focused on patient behavior, product
exposure, and treatment adherence rather than legal classification of over-the-counter products.
This review was conducted as a narrative synthesis rather than a registered systematic or scoping review
because the research question integrated heterogeneous evidence from psychodermatology, digital media
analysis, consumer marketing, dermatologic adverse effects, and medication adherence. The aim was to
synthesize converging mechanisms and practice implications rather than estimate pooled effect sizes or
answer a single intervention-outcome question. Formal duplicate screening, quantitative meta-analysis,
and risk-of-bias scoring were not performed. To reduce interpretive bias, eligibility categories were defined
before final synthesis, reference lists were manually searched, borderline inclusions were rechecked against
the review question, and principal sources were evaluated according to study design, sample size, directness
to acne treatment behavior, and whether exposure and outcome were measured within the same population.

Psychodermatological vulnerability and treatment expectations
Psychosocial burden is consistent across independent acne cohorts, but its components are not
interchangeable. In a cohort of 150 patients, acne severity and psychological distress were associated with
reduced self-esteem and poorer quality of life
[5]
. In a separate cohort of 210 patients aged 14 to 40 years,
global acne severity correlated modestly with dermatology-specific quality-of-life impairment (r=0.196,
p=0.004) and stigmatization (r=0.156, p=0.024), whereas stigmatization showed stronger associations with
depression (r=0.461, p<0.001), social anxiety (r=0.336, p<0.001), and quality-of-life impairment (r=0.472,
p<0.001)
[7]
. The difference in association strength matters for patient care: objective severity was only
modestly related to stigma, while perceived stigma was more closely related to psychological morbidity and
impaired daily functioning. Because both studies were cross-sectional, the direction of these relationships
remains uncertain, but their convergence shows that treatment decisions occur within a measurable
psychosocial burden rather than neutral consumer conditions.

Digital content quality, commercial persuasion, and modeled skincare
routines
Short-form acne media separate evidentiary quality from visibility. In a cross-platform analysis of TikTok,
Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts, dermatologist- and clinic-produced videos scored more highly on
DISCERN and Global Quality Scale measures, whereas layperson posts generated significantly greater
engagement than posts by dermatologists
[10]
. On TikTok, only 38 of the 100 most-liked #acne videos were
physician-created; 29 were produced by non-physicians and 27 by private companies, while DISCERN scores
remained low across creator categories
[11]
. A separate thematic analysis of 150 leading #acne videos found
that medically trained clinicians produced only 11% of the content; acne education appeared alongside
pimple-popping, transformation narratives, acne positivity, and unfiltered “acne reality” footage
[12]
.
Qualitative misinformation research identified exaggerated dietary causation, unconventional cures,
distrust of conventional therapies, and product-affiliated claims of rapid resolution after standard
treatment failure
[13]
.
None of these studies claimed that inaccurate content reliably outperformed accurate content, or that
viewers acted on every claim they saw. The problem they revealed was subtler: creator authority,
information quality, emotional salience, commercial affiliation, and audience engagement were not reliably
aligned
[10-13]
. Clinical advice, personal testimony, transformation imagery, product advertising, and
treatment distrust can therefore appear within the same feed without an evidentiary hierarchy visible to the
patient. This mixed environment does not negate the educational value of clinician-produced digital
content; rather, it makes source identification, evidence quality, and commercial transparency relevant.
Commercial cosmeceutical messaging can add another source of ambiguity when product claims borrow
medical language without corresponding clinical supervision. Cosmeceuticals occupy an intermediate
market position between cosmetics and pharmaceuticals and are promoted through the language of tissue
repair, molecular renewal, barrier restoration, and regeneration
[14]
. “Epistemic consumption” describes
how scientific vocabulary and expert-coded claims can make product selection appear rational, responsible,
and medically informed
[14]
. For patients already experiencing stigma, appearance monitoring, and limited
tolerance for delayed improvement, this language may recast purchasing as self-directed treatment. Because
the marketing evidence is not acne-specific, this connection should be interpreted as a plausible persuasion
mechanism rather than a demonstrated treatment pathway.
Recent dermatologic commentary has proposed “cosmeticorexia” as a descriptive term for excessive or age-
inappropriate cosmetic use, although it is not a formal diagnosis
[15]
. Direct content evidence shows that
the modeled behavior can involve substantial product and ingredient exposure. In an analysis of 100 TikTok
videos featuring skincare routines by creators aged 18 years or younger, routines contained a mean of six
products, cost an average of $168, and received a mean of 1.1 million views; only 26.2% of daytime routines
included sunscreen. Among the 25 most-viewed videos, routines contained a mean of 11 and a maximum of
21 potentially irritating active ingredients, while 76% included at least one potential contact allergen.
Experimental engagement by accounts registered as 13-year-old users increased subsequent exposure to
similar routine content
[16]
.
These findings extend the concern beyond the accuracy of individual claims: short-form content repeatedly
models complex regimens, high active-ingredient exposure, incomplete photoprotection, and continued
exposure to similar content after engagement
[16]
. Because Hales et al. analyzed pediatric skincare videos
rather than acne patients, the study demonstrates the routines being modeled, not whether patients
receiving acne pharmacotherapy adopt them.

Digital exposure becomes consequential when online recommendations enter treatment behavior. In a
cross-sectional survey of 358 adult female acne patients, 88.4% of participants who sought acne information
through social media or the internet followed at least one treatment recommendation from those platforms;
20.9% reported willingness to alter physician-prescribed acne treatment based on social media
recommendations. Online recommendations therefore entered the treatment sequence as a source of action,
including self-medication, home remedies, oral supplements, dietary modification, and over-the-counter
product use. In the same cohort, only 0.8% reported significant improvement after following online acne
advice, while 12.8% reported worsening
[6]
. he study demonstrated behavioral uptake of online advice but
left unaddressed which specific exposures caused the reported worsening and how patients attributed those
symptoms.
The cutaneous consequences appeared in routine dermatology practice. A national survey of 168 Dutch
dermatologists and dermatology residents reported that 91.6% expressed concern about the popularity of
multi-step topical cosmetic regimens, and 88.1% regularly encountered skincare-related dermatoses, most
commonly irritant eczema, perioral dermatitis, allergic contact dermatitis, and acne exogenica
[17]
.
Frequently implicated product categories included retinoids, exfoliating acids, fragrances, and products
marketed as natural. The combination of frequently implicated product categories and reported diagnoses
was consistent with cumulative irritant and allergen exposure from complex topical routines
[17]
. As a
clinician survey, it documented complication patterns encountered in practice rather than verified patient-
level exposure histories or causation.
Cutaneous adverse effects are associated with non-adherence. In a cross-sectional cohort of 198 patients
with dermatologist-confirmed acne vulgaris, non-adherence to anti-acne medication was 50.5% and was
significantly associated with treatment-related adverse effects, particularly skin dryness, as well as moderate
acne severity and topical therapy
[18]
. Dryness affected 37.9% of patients with reported side effects, while
skin stinging affected 14.1%; non-adherence was significantly higher among patients receiving topical
therapy alone and among those experiencing skin dryness or skin stinging
[18]
. Because social-media
exposure and non-prescribed skincare use were not measured in this cohort, this finding established an
association between cutaneous adverse effects and non-adherence rather than the origin of those adverse
effects.
Three studies measured adjacent stages rather than one continuous sequence. Ertekin et al. documented
uptake of online treatment recommendations
[6]
; Poelhekken and van der Bent documented dermatoses
encountered in association with multi-step cosmetic routines
[17]
; and Aleissa et al. linked dryness and
stinging with medication non-adherence
[18]
. Together, these studies support a hypothesis-generating
model in which digital advice, additional topical exposure, cutaneous symptoms, and reduced treatment
persistence are linked as adjacent but separately studied stages; no single study followed the same patients
through every stage. The unresolved step was symptom attribution: whether irritation arising during
combined prescription and cosmetic use was blamed on the prescribed regimen and prompted premature
discontinuation.
Adherence difficulties recur across geographically distinct acne populations, although the reported rates are
not directly comparable because the studies used different populations and adherence measures. In the
Saudi cohort, non-adherence was 50.5%
[18]
. Among 2,349 patients across 18 Turkish dermatology centers,
48% reported consistent medication use; leading barriers were forgetfulness, dislike of treatment, and
reluctance toward long-term therapy
[19]
. In Thailand, adherence among 1,237 participants was 33.71%,
with cost, adverse effects, lack of noticeable improvement, convenience, and aesthetics influencing
persistence or treatment selection
[20]
. The recurring pattern was not a uniform prevalence estimate but a
shared set of treatment frictions: delayed visible benefit, adverse effects, inconvenience, expense, and
resistance to prolonged therapy. These are the same points of vulnerability at which transformation-
oriented and rapid-result digital messaging may become especially persuasive
[6,10-13]
.
Clinician responses: expectation management, exposure history, and
supportive skincare
High physician trust did not eliminate online information-seeking. In a cohort of 100 acne patients, 97%
reported that they would trust dermatologist guidance over influencer guidance when the two conflicted,
while 67% continued to seek acne information from influencers, most commonly regarding cosmetic
products
[21]
. These findings suggest that distrust is not the sole explanation for digital exposure. In a
separate survey of 384 Facebook skincare purchasers in Sri Lanka, source credibility, electronic word-of-
mouth credibility, and trust in online communities were positively associated with purchase intention
[22]
.
Together, these studies show that professional trust and online influence can coexist: patients may defer to
dermatologists in a stated conflict while continuing to encounter credible-seeming community or
commercial information
[21,22]
. Expressed trust therefore cannot be assumed to eliminate online influence
on information seeking and product evaluation.

distinguish persistent acne from treatment irritation, product-induced dermatitis, and incomplete
medication use before the original regimen is intensified or abandoned.
Expectation management requires procedural specificity. Reported adherence barriers included limited
disease knowledge, confusion about application, weak patient-physician communication, fear or experience
of adverse effects, cost, lack of perceived efficacy, regimen complexity, lifestyle burden, forgetfulness,
inconvenience, and psychiatric comorbidity
[23]
. Several of these barriers can be addressed within the
consultation. Clinicians can provide a written regimen map identifying which product is therapeutic, which
is supportive, how much should be applied, which early effects are expected, which symptoms require
stopping therapy, and when clinical response should be assessed. Physical demonstration of topical
medication application produced 15% higher mean adherence over six weeks compared with no
demonstration, and adherence rose around scheduled follow-up visits, making early follow-up a behavioral
intervention rather than a passive monitoring step
[23]
. Early reassessment was most useful before irritation
or lack of visible improvement prompted unsupervised treatment changes
[3,23]
. When in-person
reassessment is impractical, structured digital check-ins could be evaluated as a means of detecting
irritation, perceived inefficacy, and newly added topical products before discontinuation.
Supportive skincare should be distinguished from unsupervised product accumulation. Expert consensus
recommends acne-appropriate cleansers, moisturizers, and sunscreens to support barrier function, reduce
treatment-related irritation, and address post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation when clinically relevant
[24]
. Selected dermocosmetics may improve tolerability and clinical outcomes when assigned a defined role
within medical therapy
[24]
. This evidence does not support unrestricted over-the-counter experimentation.
A minimal cleanser-moisturizer-sunscreen routine may preserve a tangible element of daily self-care while
reducing duplicate actives and cumulative irritant exposure; that psychological benefit remains an inference
rather than a tested adherence outcome.
These recommendations do not carry equal evidentiary weight. Application demonstration and scheduled
follow-up had the closest direct evidence of adherence
[23]
. Regimen simplification, written instructions,
and anticipatory counseling were also supported by established adherence barriers, including confusion
about application, adverse effects, poor communication, lack of perceived efficacy, inconvenience, and
regimen complexity
[23]
. In contrast, documenting the digital source of product additions and using
structured digital check-ins are proposed digital-era strategies rather than interventions with demonstrated
efficacy. These strategies are evidence-informed extensions derived from treatment-modification,
complication, and influencer-use data
[6,17,21]
, while dermocosmetic integration is supported mainly by
expert consensus
[24]
.
Limitations of the evidence base
The evidence base is limited primarily by cross-sectional design, self-report, and fragmentation across study
types. Patient studies measured digital exposure, treatment modification, adverse effects, or medication use
at a single time point; content analyses measured available information rather than patient behavior;
clinician surveys captured encountered patterns rather than verified exposure histories; and adherence
cohorts could not distinguish symptoms caused by prescribed therapy from those caused by non-prescribed
products. Several mechanistic and therapeutic arguments also relied on non-acne marketing research,
narrative reviews, editorial commentary, and expert consensus. Accordingly, findings from non-acne
skincare, consumer-behavior, or general digital-media studies were used only as contextual or mechanistic
support and were not treated as direct evidence of behavior, cutaneous outcomes, or adherence among acne
patients. Populations and adherence measures were heterogeneous, and the central gap identified above, a
single cohort followed from digital exposure through symptom attribution to prescription persistence, has
not yet been studied. Additional limitations include restriction to English-language literature, absence of
formal duplicate screening or risk-of-bias assessment, and substantial heterogeneity in populations,
exposures, outcomes, and study designs, which limits direct comparison across studies. These limitations
prevent causal inference but define a specific prospective research agenda.
Conclusions
Acne care increasingly occurs in a digital environment where patients may encounter rapid-result product
narratives before prescribed therapy has had adequate time to work. The evidence reviewed here supports a
plausible, hypothesis-generating model rather than a demonstrated longitudinal sequence. Individual
components of the proposed pathway have been observed separately: psychodermatological distress and
expectation mismatch are associated with acne burden and treatment-related outcomes; digital content can
present product-centered alternatives and model complex routines; and cutaneous adverse effects are
associated with poorer medication persistence. However, the longitudinal relationship among these

Dermatologic practice need not wait for the entire pathway to be proven. Dermatologists can define realistic
treatment timelines, demonstrate application, simplify regimens, review the chronology of prescribed and
non-prescribed exposures, and integrate selected supportive products. These actions address documented
adherence barriers while preserving the distinction between interventions with direct adherence evidence
and digital-era extensions that still require testing. The objective is not to discourage online acne education
or non-prescription skincare, but to prevent unsupervised product accumulation from replacing structured,
tolerable treatment. The priority for future research is a prospective acne cohort that measures digital
exposure, product additions, barrier change, symptom attribution, and prescription persistence together.
