Abstract
Purpose: Disruption, long treated in marketing scholarship as an episodic departure from
equilibrium, has become a permanent feature of the competitive landscape. This article
lays the conceptual foundations for a marketing theory of persistent disruption, arguing
that firms no longer experience disruption as isolated events with a clear before, during,
and after, but instead operate continuously within overlapping, interacting disruptive
forces- technological, geopolitical, ecological, and institutional.
Design/methodology/approach: We first reconceptualize disruption itself, proposing
that the conventional exogenous-endogenous binary be extended into a feedback system
in which a firm's response to one disruption becomes a source of disruption for others,
with marketing positioned at the critical interface of this loop. Building on this, we
develop a triadic framework- sensing, seizing, and shaping- to characterize marketing's
role under persistent disruption, showing that these capabilities are disrupted
simultaneously rather than sequentially, and introduce the Disruption-Marketing
Response Matrix, a typology crossing disruption origin (endogenous/exogenous) with
response domain (upstream/downstream) to identify underdeveloped theoretical territory.
Drawing on the nine papers in this Special Issue, we also develop an integrated five-stage
marketing reinvention framework spanning disruption recognition, stakeholder
engagement, and agile operating models
Findings: We argue that emerging markets, with their institutional volatility and resource
constraints, constitute an underutilized theoretical laboratory for understanding marketing
under sustained disruption, and that new-age technologies (AI, generative AI, machine
learning, extended reality, IoT, robotics, and blockchain) should be understood not as
discretionary tools but as infrastructure that reconstitutes the marketing environment
itself. Drawing on the nine contributions in this Special Issue, we distill seven theoretical
implications spanning the persistence of disruption, the feedback-based collapse of the
exogenous-endogenous distinction, the simultaneous disruption of dynamic marketing
capabilities, the diagnostic value of the Response Matrix, emerging markets as theory-
generating contexts, technology as infrastructure, and marketing's expanding role in
societal value creation.
Practical implications
The article presents a Disruption–Response Action Matrix that helps managers diagnose
disruption types, assess sensing, seizing, and shaping capabilities, prioritize capability
investments, and guide marketing reinvention under persistent disruption.

disruption, but rather how to build the capabilities needed to function within it as a

1. Introduction
The (over)use of the term “disruption” in everyday parlance is unmistakable. These days,
disruption has come to describe any change happening around us. The change can manifest
through a person (e.g., a startup founder), a thing (e.g., a new technology), a phenomenon
(e.g., social/cultural trends), or a new way of doing things (e.g., a new business model),
among others. This is a newer understanding of disruption. In the earlier decades,
disruption was often seen as the occasional, industry-specific disturbance caused by a new
entrant or a breakthrough technology. This changed understanding of disruption is now
firmly entrenched in management vocabulary and actively impacting the strategic thinking
of organizations. Another aspect that has changed about disruption is that firms are not just
managing disruption and returning to “normalcy”. They are now operating inside it, all the
time. In a way, it can be surmised that disruption itself has evolved, along with our
understanding of it. So, the question facing academia and industry is not how to recover
from disruption, but rather how to build the capabilities needed to function within it as a
permanent condition.
This shift in how disruption is handled deserves careful conceptual attention.
Recent academic research has approached disruption from multiple perspectives. For
instance, from a supply chain viewpoint, disruption is viewed as “…unplanned and
unanticipated events that disrupt the normal flow of goods and materials within a supply
chain” (Craighead et al., 2007, p. 132). Examples of recent disruptions from an operational
lens include the Red Sea shipping crisis that started in late 2023 and the ongoing Strait of
Hormuz crisis. From a human resources perspective, disruption has been discussed in

impacted the digital advertising industry, and the Corporate Sustainability Reporting
Directive (CSRD) in Europe that mandates standardized environmental, social, and
governance (ESG) reporting. These are but three perspectives that academic research has
studied disruption. The larger point is that disruption needs to be better understood and
studied from a multi-pronged lens.
The above-mentioned discussion represents a nuanced departure from the
traditional treatment of disruption in marketing and management research that viewed
disruption as episodic (i.e., an event with a clear before, during, and after) (see Porter &
Siggelkow, 2008; Tushman & Anderson, 1986). In such a context, when strategy is
disrupted, firms respond, and organizational control is restored. While such a way of
conceptualizing disruption management produced useful theory, it largely reflects an
environment where disruptions arrive singularly and at intervals long enough to allow
recovery and recalibration. That world no longer exists.
Instead, the current world reflects a state of persistent, overlapping disruption,
wherein multiple forces (e.g., technology, geopolitics, ecology, epidemiology, etc.) operate
and interact, creating an amplified version of the individual effects. Consider the COVID-
19 pandemic. It did not merely disrupt supply chains. It accelerated the shift to digital
commerce, restructured where people worked from, and triggered inflationary pressures
that subsequently led to other geopolitical tensions. These were not discrete events. They
arrived together (or in quick succession), and their interactions created a disruptive
environment, markedly different from anything prior frameworks were designed to
address.

The consequences of such changes for marketing are immense. Firms had to
contend with multiple shocks concurrently. For instance, consumers started to closely
examine the sourcing practices of companies during the pandemic. For companies, this
meant that supply chain visibility was not merely an operations issue, but also a branding
issue. Similarly, sustainability initiatives were no longer restricted to corporate reputation
but also became a regulatory issue. Here, the Chief Marketing Officer’s responsibility
expanded further to include responsibilities that had previously belonged to other functions
entirely. These changes apply to firms of all sizes and geographic presence, as
technological disruption, infrastructure volatility, currency instability, and shifting
regulatory environments impacted everyone, not only the major corporations.
Considering the reframed view of disruption, three notable aspects emerge that are
of particular concern to this Special Issue. First, when disruption defines the default state
of business rather than a special situation, traditional marketing tools such as annual brand
tracking and forward-looking strategic plans (e.g., 3-year plans) become less relevant and
reliable. This is because the old models assume a steady environment, which cannot be
ensured now. Companies that were successful in navigating stressful times have done so
by focusing on sensing changes quickly, seizing opportunities, and responding rapidly,
rather than making long-term projections. Here, the development of detailed forward-
looking plans gave way to fast recalibration methods. This is why understanding these
adaptability skills and how marketing helps create them is key.
Second, disruption in marketing now comes from many angles. In the past, demand-
side disruption was the most apparent and common (e.g., shifting consumer tastes, new
consumer groups, or brands becoming outdated, etc.). But nowadays, firms also have to
contend with supply-side disruption (e.g., regulatory changes, infrastructure changes,
changes in the technology landscape, newer business models, etc.). For instance, Google’s
constant changes in its search algorithm influence how businesses bring in new customers.
Similarly, Meta had to alter its ad targeting following Apple’s privacy updates. Such
changes force brands to quickly revamp their whole marketing strategy and digital plans.
The current theories do not comprehensively address this issue.
Third, and perhaps the most critical aspect for marketing theory, persistent
disruption does not hit every part of the marketing function equally or at the same speed.

concurrent disruptions has created an environment that existing marketing theory does not
fully cover. We see this as a call to develop new theoretical constructs that take the
permanence of disruption as their starting point rather than their exception.

theory of persistent disruption. This article is organized as follows. Section 2 rethinks

under disruption. The section also presents a typology of disruption forms against

under persistent disruption. Section 5 presents the overview of the article and integrated

Schmidt & Druehl, 2008), research on technological discontinuities  (e.g., Anderson &
Tushman, 2018; Ehrnberg, 1995; Tushman & Anderson, 1986), and the industrial
organization research stream on competitive shocks as exogenous events (e.g., Porter,

typically classified along a single axis, wherein it originates either outside the firm (e.g.,
environmental shocks beyond the firm’s control) or inside the firm (e.g., a consequence of
the firm’s innovation and strategic choices).
While exogenous vs. endogenous classification is useful for identifying the origin
of a disruption, it is silent on how exogenous and endogenous disruptions interact, amplify
each other, and generate feedback. It is this interaction effect that demands theoretical
attention. For instance, Uber’s ridesharing solution did not merely disrupt the taxi industry.
It sparked a series of endogenous responses from key industry partners (e.g., automotive
firms, public transit authorities, and logistics companies) that subsequently created new
exogenous pressures on Uber itself. Similarly, the development of Amazon’s fulfilment
and cloud infrastructure, which began as an internal capability-building exercise,
subsequently transformed how companies in other industries managed their cost
competitiveness. Therefore, the exogenous and the endogenous can be seen as points in a
feedback loop, rather than as two ends of a rigid spectrum.
The above-mentioned looped feedback system presents three important
implications. First, it means that disruption is not self-contained. That is, a firm’s response
to an exogenous shock can itself become a source of disruption for others. For instance,
consider Moderna, Pfizer, and BioNTech’s development of mRNA vaccine technology
during the pandemic. What began as a response to an exogenous biological shock produced
an endogenous technological innovation that is now disrupting the larger pharmaceutical
industry’s approach to vaccine and drug development. For the marketing function, this calls
for navigating not only the original disruption but also the impact of the firm’s response on
the competitive landscape.
Second, the speed of disruption is not constant. While exogenous shocks tend to
arrive suddenly, warranting a rapid response, endogenous disruptions tend to unfold more
gradually. However, when the two forces overlap (as they have since the pandemic), firms
are forced to respond rapidly to external shocks while simultaneously managing the slower-
moving consequences of their own initiatives. For instance, consider Reliance Industries’
launch of Jio in the Indian telecommunications market. The deployment of a low-cost
greenfield 4G network (i.e., an endogenous disruption) triggered rapid exogenous

Third, and of particular relevance to this Special Issue, marketing sits at the
intersection of this feedback loop. That is, the marketing function is directly exposed to
both sides of the exogenous/endogenous dynamic. Marketing powers both exogenous
disruptions (e.g., market intelligence, consumer sensing, and competitive monitoring) and
endogenous disruptions (e.g., new product development, brand strategy, pricing
architecture, and channel design). Marketing is, therefore, not merely a function that
responds to disruption, but rather it participates in producing it. Scholars have presented
this observation in earlier studies through the concepts of market-driven vs. market-driving
strategies (Jaworski et al., 2000), adaptive marketing capabilities (Day, 2011), vigilant
organizations (Day & Schoemaker, 2016), and developing a marketing organization
(Moorman & Day, 2016), among others. Building on this knowledge base, the current
nature of a disruptive environment adds urgency and scale. That is, the feedback loop
between exogenous and endogenous disruption is now operating faster, wider, and deeper
across domains simultaneously than any of these frameworks were designed to
accommodate.
Considering the above implications, we propose that rather than treating exogenous
and endogenous disruption as binary explanatory categories, they can be treated as
interactive forces in a dynamic system, with the marketing function serving the roles of
sensing, seizing, and shaping opportunities. It also requires us to look beyond the
established markets and incumbent firm contexts that have dominated disruption research.
The feedback loop between exogenous and endogenous disruption plays out differently in
emerging markets where institutional volatility, infrastructure constraints, and resource
scarcity create both more frequent shocks and stronger incentives for disruptive innovation.

can drive future research. The three roles are briefly described here:
Sensing. This is where the marketing function performs an observational role by
systematically detecting and interpreting signals from the market environment. This
includes shifts in consumer behavior, competitive moves, regulatory changes,
technological developments, and macroeconomic trends, among others. Firms that have
mastered the sensing role do not merely collect data. They build internal processes that
convert weak signals into actionable intelligence before competitors pick up on those
signals. For instance, Zara’s near-real-time feedback loop between store staff, designers,
and production facilities allows them to identify emerging fashion trends and translate them
into new product lines within a short time. In this regard, sensing extends beyond a research
function and serves as a strategic capability.
Seizing. This is where the marketing function performs a responsive role based on
the detected and interpreted environmental signals. This involves repositioning its
offerings, reallocating resources, reconfiguring its channel architecture, or redesigning its
customer engagement model, among others. In this stage, the firm recognizes that the
unevenness of speed between upstream and downstream marketing becomes most
consequential. That is, while downstream responses (e.g., adjusting communication,
repricing, activating new channels, etc.) can be executed relatively quickly, upstream
responses (e.g., reformulating products, restructuring supplier relationships, reorienting
R&D, etc.) require longer timelines and multifunctional coordination that are beyond
marketing’s control. For instance, during the pandemic, LVMH redeployed its perfume
production facilities to manufacture hand sanitizer within 72 hours. This is a seizing
response that, though downstream in its public-facing impact, required immediate
upstream operational reconfiguration (Newburger, 2020). Here, the marketing function not
only communicated the quick change publicly but also managed the implications of a
luxury brand’s actions under emergency conditions.

actively reconfiguring the markets. It does so by creating demand where none previously

Importantly, during disruptions, the sensing, seizing, and shaping functions are
impacted simultaneously. During disruptions, the sensing mechanism is likely to produce
more “noise” than meaningful signals as the environment is changing faster than the
feedback cycles can handle. Similarly, the seizing capabilities that allowed rapid response
to one type of shock may be poorly suited to a different type arriving concurrently during
disruptions. Likewise, during disruptions, the shaping strategies may misfire as the
landscape itself is being reshaped by external forces beyond the firm’s control. Now, the
simultaneous disruption of all three marketing roles is what makes persistent disruption
remarkably different from episodic disruption. This change lies at the core of the
development of a new marketing theory to address this.

4. The Curious Case of Emerging Markets
A large part of the disruption literature is from the perspective of developed and
institutionally stable economies. While a part of the reason for such coverage has been
stable contexts (e.g., reliable infrastructure, established capital markets, mature regulatory
frameworks, etc.), the geographic concentration of academic outlets (i.e., journals,
researchers, doctoral programs, etc.) in such stable contexts has also influenced this
outcome.
Here, it is important to note that what is typically thought of as disruptions that
developed-market firms are experiencing (e.g., volatile macroeconomic environments,
unreliable physical and digital infrastructure, rapid regulatory change, compressed
consumer purchasing power, etc.) are conditions that firms operating in the emerging
markets have long navigated (Varadarajan et al., 2022). In this regard, emerging market
firms have been managing persistent disruption, in many instances even without the
theoretical guidance from academic research, for decades (e.g., Hoskisson et al., 2000;
Khanna et al., 2005; Prahalad & Hammond, 2002).

5. The Role of New-Age Technologies
Every major wave of disruption has featured technology. This has prompted marketing
scholars to ask how new technologies/tools alter the practice of marketing. Consider some
recent prominent disruptions in marketing, such as television advertising, database
marketing, the internet, social media, and the recent emergence of NATs. Each of these
disruptions has generated a substantial body of research examining how marketers could
deploy these technologies more effectively. The operating lens of developing this body of
knowledge has been to discern how the marketing function can pick up a technology, learn
to use it, and apply it within an otherwise stable, competitive, and institutional
environment. We believe that this perspective is no longer relevant. Further, holding on to
this perspective, in our opinion, can lead to theoretical blind spots for the marketing
discipline.

7. Theoretical Implications
This section develops the theoretical implications arising from the conceptual arguments
and empirical contributions assembled in this Special Issue. We organize these
implications into six interconnected domains, each corresponding to a substantive advance
that the collected work, taken together, adds to marketing theory. Rather than summarizing
individual papers in isolation, the goal is to extract the durable theoretical claims that cut
across the contributions and to articulate their significance for the broader discipline. The
implications are presented in a sequence that moves from the most foundational
reconceptualization of disruption itself, through to the mid-range theoretical advances
concerning marketing capabilities and organizational responses, and finally to the macro-
level questions about marketing's relationship with technology and societal transformation.
Theoretical Implication 1: From Episodic to Persistent Disruption as a Theoretical
Starting Point
The Insufficiency of the Episodic Paradigm
The most foundational theoretical implication of this Special Issue concerns the ontological
status of disruption in marketing theory. Received treatments of disruption in the marketing
and management literatures, including disruptive innovation theory (Christensen, 1997;
Christensen and Raynor, 2003), research on technological discontinuities (Anderson &
Tushman, 2018; Tushman & Anderson, 1986), and industrial organization perspectives on
competitive shocks (Porter, 1985), share a common structural assumption; i.e., disruption
is an episodic occurrence that arrives against a backdrop of relative environmental stability.
Embedded in this assumption is the proposition that disruption has a temporal boundary, a
before, a during, and an after. And that the analytical task for theory is to explain what
happens within those boundaries and how equilibrium is subsequently restored.
This Special Issue, taken collectively, provides theoretical grounds for abandoning this
assumption. The empirical and conceptual contributions herein, when read together,
suggest that episodic framing is no longer a useful organizing assumption for marketing
theory, not because of incremental empirical falsification, but because the structural
conditions it presupposes have themselves changed. Firms in this issue's studies operate

Towards a Theory of Persistent Disruption

towards the second vocabulary, and in doing so they advance a distinct theoretical program.
Which is the development of a marketing theory of persistent disruption in which
environmental instability is the baseline, not the exception.

dynamic, longitudinal, or multi-wave designs, or that theorize feedback mechanisms

reconceptualization in a way that cross-sectional designs in stable contexts are not.

The theoretical significance of treating disruption as a feedback system rather than a binary
classification is substantial. It relocates marketing from a passive responder to exogenous
events to an active participant in feedback dynamics. Marketing powers both sides of the
loop: market intelligence, consumer sensing, and competitive monitoring feed into the
exogenous detection function, while new product development, brand architecture, pricing
strategy, and channel design constitute endogenous disruptive capabilities. This dual
positioning means that marketing theory must account for the firm’s role as both a subject
of disruption and a creator of it.
The papers in Cluster 4 and Cluster 5 of this Special Issue are particularly instructive here.
The finding that disruptive intent alone does not improve firm value but that disruptive
intent interacting with market-based asset-building and leveraging capabilities does
(Padigar et al., 2026), is consistent with this theoretical framing. It suggests that
endogenous disruption capacity is not sufficient on its own; it must be coupled with the
firm's ability to convert disruption into market-anchored value. This is a theoretically
important constraint on the widespread managerial discourse that treats disruption as
inherently value-creating.

Theoretical Implication 3: The Sensing-Seizing-Shaping Triad as a Marketing-
Specific Dynamic Capability Framework
Extending Dynamic Capabilities into the Marketing Domain
The dynamic capabilities framework (Teece, 2007), and its marketing antecedents in Day
(2011) adaptive marketing capabilities and Vorhies and Morgan (2005) marketing
capability configurations, provides the primary theoretical scaffold for understanding how
firms build and deploy marketing-relevant capabilities under disruption. This special
issue’s contributions extend this scaffold in two theoretically significant directions.
First, the contributions collectively demonstrate that the sensing, seizing, and shaping
functions are not sequential stages in a linear process but are simultaneously disrupted
under conditions of persistent disruption. This is a theoretically important distinction. The
original dynamic capabilities framework, while acknowledging the interrelation among its
components, was largely developed in contexts where one capacity could be prioritized
over another at different phases of a strategic cycle. Under persistent disruption, the
simultaneous disruption of all three functions creates a qualitatively different
organizational challenge - one that requires theoretical treatment as a co-disruption
problem rather than a sequencing problem.
The Asymmetric Speed of Upstream and Downstream Response
Second, and more specifically, the contributions highlight a theoretically underspecified
asymmetry between upstream and downstream marketing responses. The seizing function,
i.e., the firm's capacity to mobilize and reconfigure resources in response to sensed
opportunities, operates at different speeds and under different constraints depending on
whether the response domain is upstream (product development, supplier relationships,
R&D reorientation) or downstream (customer communication, channel activation, pricing
adjustment). Downstream responses can be executed relatively rapidly; upstream responses
require longer timelines and cross-functional coordination that exceed marketing's direct
sphere of control.
The theoretical implication is that seizing capacity cannot be treated as a homogeneous
construct in disruption contexts. A firm with strong downstream seizing capabilities but

but do not fully theorize it. Shaping is distinct from seizing in that it involves not the
reconfiguration of the firm’s own capabilities in response to environmental signals, but the
deliberate reshaping of the environment itself- demand creation, category redefinition,
consumption norm establishment. Theory development in this space requires engagement
with market-driving (as opposed to market-driven) strategy (Jaworski et al., 2000),
institutional theory, and the emerging literature on market formation and market practices.
Theoretical Implication 4: The Disruption-Marketing Response Matrix as a
Diagnostic and Theory-Building Tool
Theoretical Value of the 2x2 Typology
The Disruption-Marketing Response Matrix introduced in this Special Issue- organized
along the axes of disruption origin (endogenous vs. exogenous) and marketing response
domain (upstream vs. downstream) represents a theoretical contribution in its own right,
beyond its practical diagnostic utility. Typologies in marketing theory have traditionally
served one of two functions: they either classify existing phenomena to impose conceptual
order, or they identify underexplored theoretical territory by revealing cells in the
classification space that lack adequate theoretical development. The Disruption-Marketing
Response Matrix serves both functions.

Cross-Quadrant Theory Building as an Imperative
The more significant theoretical implication, however, is that firms facing persistent
disruption do not occupy a single quadrant. They operate across all four simultaneously,
with different timelines, different capability requirements, and different theoretical and
practical resources available to them. This cross-quadrant complexity is precisely the
territory that existing marketing theory is least equipped to address. The theoretical
imperative is therefore not to deepen individual quadrants further but to develop integrative
theoretical accounts of how firms manage multiple concurrent disruption-response
configurations.
The papers in this special issue offer partial instantiation of such accounts. The CMO-role
study (Sharma et al., 2026), by examining how chief marketing officers build
organizational resilience across multiple disruption types simultaneously, operates
implicitly across all four quadrants. The finding that dynamic marketing capabilities,
specifically sensing, seizing, and reconfiguration, must be deliberately strengthened and
that their development is enabled by board support, strategic clarity, and cross-functional
collaboration, suggests that the capability requirements for cross-quadrant navigation are
both higher-order and harder to develop than single-quadrant capabilities. This is a
theoretically productive finding that deserves systematic follow-up.

Theoretical Implication 5: Emerging Markets as a Theoretical Laboratory, Not an
Empirical Footnote
Recentering Emerging Market Theory in Disruption Research
A fifth theoretical implication concerns the status of emerging market contexts within
marketing theory. The historical tendency to treat emerging markets as contexts to which
developed-market theories are applied and tested, rather than as contexts from which
theoretical insights are generated, has created a systematic blind spot in disruption research.
The capabilities and strategies that firms in institutionally volatile, infrastructure-
constrained, and resource-scarce environments have developed to function under persistent
disruption with innovation, such as, frugal innovation, jugaad, grassroots innovation,
reverse innovation, and strategies built around institutional voids, represent a rich
theoretical resource that developed-market disruption theory has substantially
underutilized.
The theoretical implication is bidirectional. On one hand, emerging market research
streams have generated constructs (institutional void navigation, constraint-based
innovation, grassroots market creation) that are directly applicable to the disruption
challenge now facing firms in developed markets, where persistent disruption is creating
conditions, such as, volatility, institutional uncertainty, infrastructure fragility, that
structurally resemble what emerging market firms have long managed. On the other hand,
the disruption frameworks developed in the developed-market context, i.e., the sensing-
seizing-shaping triad, the exogenous-endogenous feedback loop, the Disruption-Marketing
Response Matrix, provide theoretical scaffolding that can organize and systematize the
insights from emerging market research in ways that make them generalizable.

Theoretical Implication 6: New-Age Technologies as Marketing Infrastructure, not
as Marketing Tools
The Infrastructural Turn in Technology-Marketing Theory
The sixth and perhaps most consequential theoretical implication concerns the relationship
between new-age technologies (NATs)- artificial intelligence, generative AI, machine
learning, augmented and virtual reality, the Internet of Things, robotics, drones, and
blockchain- and marketing functions. The dominant theoretical lens through which
marketing has approached new technologies is an adoption and diffusion lens: theories of
technology acceptance (Davis, 1989), diffusion of innovations (Rogers, 1983), and their
many extensions ask how individuals and organizations come to adopt new technologies,
and how the characteristics of the technology and the adopter interact to determine adoption
speed, extent, and outcomes.
This adoption-and-diffusion lens treats technology as a tool, as an artefact that is adopted
or not adopted, deployed or not deployed, by a marketing function that remains structurally
stable regardless of whether the technology is in use. The theoretical implication of this
framing is that the relevant theoretical question is whether and how marketing uses the
technology. This special issue, particularly through its treatment of AI-based stakeholder
engagement (N. Tehrani & Roy, 2025), augmented reality-enabled sustainability marketing
(Nadeem et al., 2025), extended reality and firm value (Bahmani et al., 2026), and drone
delivery in extreme contexts (Mahmoud & Mahroof, 2025), collectively challenges this
framing.

Infrastructure Reconstitutes the Marketing Environment
The theoretical claim advanced, implicitly and sometimes explicitly, across these
contributions is that NATs are not merely more powerful instances of existing marketing
tools but constitute a new marketing infrastructure. One that reorganizes the environment
within which all marketing activity takes place, regardless of any individual firm's adoption
decision. Infrastructure, unlike a tool, cannot be set aside: firms do not choose whether to
operate in an AI-mediated information environment any more than they choose whether to
operate in a monetized economy. The choice is not adoption versus non-adoption but how
to operate within an already-transformed environment.
The theoretical implications of this infrastructural view are substantial and far-reaching.
Technology acceptance and diffusion models are premised on the existence of a pre-
technological baseline- a situation before the technology that provides the comparison case.
When the technology is infrastructural, this baseline ceases to exist, and the theoretical
framework built upon it loses its anchoring assumption. What marketing theory requires
instead is a framework in which the unit of analysis shifts from technology adoption to
technological environment reconfiguration: theories that explain how marketing-relevant
environments are constituted and reconstituted by infrastructural technological change, and
how firms develop the capabilities to operate effectively within successively transformed
environments.
The study by Bahmani et al. (2026) on extended reality experiences and firm value provides
a theoretically instructive case in point. The finding that extended reality experience
announcements on average reduce firm value, but that this negative effect is reversed for
firms adopting augmented reality over virtual reality.  Concentrating on the purchase phase
of the customer journey, and announcing in more recent years, suggests that markets are
learning to differentiate between extended reality deployments that are infrastructurally
aligned and those that are not. This is an empirical pattern that adoption-and-diffusion
theory, focused on the adoption decision itself, is structurally unable to explain. An
infrastructural theory of marketing technology, focused on how technologies articulate
with the broader marketing environment, is better positioned to generate such predictions.

The AI-Stakeholder Engagement Interface as Theoretical Frontier
The finding that AI-based stakeholder engagement enhances marketing agility, and that
this relationship is positively moderated by technological turbulence (N. Tehrani and Roy,
2025), advances a theoretically important claim about the mechanism through which NATs
generate marketing value under disruption. The mechanism is not direct value creation
through AI deployment per se, but indirect value creation through AI-enhanced
organizational agility. The capacity to detect, interpret, and respond to environmental
signals more rapidly and accurately than would be possible without AI-mediated sensing
capabilities. This positions AI not as a marketing tool among others but as an agility-
enhancing infrastructure that reshapes the firm's capacity to sense the environment,
connecting directly to the sensing function in the sensing-seizing-shaping triad.
Similarly, the finding that augmented reality-enabled sustainability marketing influences
consumer engagement, sustainable consumption intention, and psychological and social
wellbeing (Nadeem et al., 2025) advances the theoretical claim that the value-creating
potential of NATs in marketing extends beyond economic outcomes to social and hedonic
outcomes. This connects to the broader transformative marketing framework (Mick et al.,
2012) and suggests that the infrastructural view of NATs must incorporate not only
economic value creation but transformative social value creation as a theoretical objective.

Synthesis: Towards a Unified Theoretical Program
The seven theoretical implications developed above are not independent. They constitute
a coherent, if not yet fully integrated, theoretical program for the study of marketing under
persistent disruption. The program begins with the ontological claim that disruption is a
permanent condition rather than a temporary perturbation (TI 1) and proceeds through the
structural claim that disruption is a feedback system that collapses the exogenous-
endogenous binary (TI 2). It then develops the capability-theoretical claims that
marketing's sensing-seizing-shaping triad operates simultaneously and asymmetrically
under persistent disruption (TI 3), and that cross-quadrant capability configurations are
theoretically necessary and currently underdeveloped (TI 4). The program extends to
contextual claims about emerging markets as theoretical laboratories rather than empirical
annexes (TI 5), and to the technological claim that NATs constitute marketing
infrastructure rather than marketing tools (TI 6). Finally, it integrates these claims through
the normative theoretical claim that marketing value creation must be theorized at
economic, relational, and societal levels simultaneously (TI 7).
Taken together, these implications amount to a program for reconstituting marketing theory
on foundations that take environmental instability, feedback dynamics, multi-level value
creation, and infrastructural technological change as their starting points rather than their
limiting cases. This program is necessarily incomplete, the special issue represents a
beginning rather than an endpoint, but the trajectory it establishes is, we believe, both
theoretically justified and empirically grounded in the contributions assembled here.

8.1. Treating Disruption as a Permanent Operating Condition Not as a Crisis to
Manage
Managers must relinquish the assumption that disruption is a temporary state from which
the organization will eventually recover. The evidence across this special issue is
unambiguous: technological turbulence, geopolitical shock, institutional change, and
societal disruption now arrive concurrently and continuously. The practical consequence
is that annual brand plans, three-year strategic roadmaps, and fixed budgeting cycles are
structurally misaligned with the operating environment. Firms that navigated disruption
effectively in these studies, whether through CMO-led dynamic capability development
(Sharma et al., 2026) or AI-enabled marketing agility (N. Tehrani & Roy, 2025), did so by
institutionalizing fast-cycle sensing and recalibration rather than detailed forward planning.
Managers should replace planning-cycle logic with rolling, signal-driven recalibration
processes and invest in organizational structures that can respond within days, not quarters.
8.2. Investing in Sensing Infrastructure Before Disruption Arrives
Across all five disruption clusters, the firms that responded most effectively were those
with pre-built sensing capabilities. Machine learning-based analysis of consumer reviews
for food safety risk detection (Jia et al., 2025), AI-enabled stakeholder engagement
platforms (N. Tehrani and Roy, 2025), and CMO-maintained pulse dashboards during
pandemic onset (Sharma et al., 2026) all illustrate the same managerial principle: sensing
capacity is not deployable on demand. It must be built, tested, and embedded in
organizational routines before disruption materializes. Managers should audit their current
signal-detection infrastructure, such as, social listening tools, channel health monitoring,
regulatory tracking, and demand-side analytics- and treat these not as research support
functions but as core strategic assets. The cost of under-investment in sensing is
asymmetric: it is far higher during a disruption than before it.

8.4. Differentiating Upstream and Downstream Response Timelines
One of the most practically significant insights from this special issue concerns the
asymmetric speed of upstream and downstream marketing response. Downstream actions:
communication adjustment, channel repricing, promotional redeployment, can be executed
within days. Upstream actions: supplier restructuring, product reformulation, R&D
reorientation, require weeks or months and demand cross-functional coordination beyond
marketing’s direct authority. Managers who conflate these timelines will either move too
slowly on downstream responses (waiting for upstream clarity that is not coming) or
commit to upstream changes prematurely based on downstream signals that have not yet
stabilized. The practical suggestion could be to maintain two parallel response tracks: a
fast-cycle downstream track managed within the marketing function, and a slower, cross-
functional upstream track with formal governance checkpoints. The LVMH hand-sanitizer
redeployment (upstream reconfiguration within 72 hours) and PepsiCo's direct-to-
consumer launch within 30 days of pandemic lockdowns represent the rare cases where
both tracks ran concurrently with explicit executive coordination.

consequences. These technologies are marketing infrastructure: they reshape the
competitive environment, consumer expectations, and channel economics for all
participants, regardless of individual adoption decisions. The evidence from this Special

9. Future Research Directions
The contributions assembled in this Special Issue resolve several long-standing questions
about how marketing operates under disruption, while opening others that the field has not
yet had reason to ask. Three broad gaps stand out across the collected work, each pointing
toward a distinct stream of future inquiry.
The first concerns research design. Almost every empirical study in this issue, by necessity,
examines a single disruption in isolation - a pandemic, a regulatory change, a market entry.
This is a defensible choice for any individual study, but it sits uneasily alongside the issue's
broader claim that disruption is now rarely singular: firms increasingly contend with two
or three overlapping disruptions at once, and the dominant cross-sectional and single-case
designs are not well equipped to capture that overlap. Longitudinal research that follows
firms through multiple, concurrent disruptions would address a gap that existing methods,
however rigorously applied, cannot close on their own.
The second concerns measurement. Several constructs developed in this issue are
conceptually well specified but have no corresponding empirical instrument. Market-
shaping capability is the clearest example: the contributions describe what shaping looks
like in practice, yet no validated scale exists to measure it, which limits the construct to a
descriptive rather than testable role in future research. Constructs proposed without a path
to measurement risk remaining metaphors rather than becoming cumulative knowledge.
The third concerns the field's choice of theoretical and methodological resources. The most
generative insights into operating under sustained volatility in this issue draw on
institutional theory and emerging-market innovation research- traditions that mainstream
marketing strategy scholarship engages with less often than might be warranted.

 10. Conclusion
This special issue set out to interrogate a deceptively simple premise: that disruption, long
treated in marketing scholarship as an episodic departure from equilibrium, has become a
permanent feature of the competitive landscape. The nine contributions assembled here-
spanning digital and technological disruption, societal disruption, institutional disruption,
internal strategic disruption, and external shocks- converge on a more demanding
conclusion than the premise alone would suggest. Disruption is not simply more frequent
than it once was; it is structurally different in kind. It is concurrent rather than sequential,
interactive rather than isolated, and infrastructural rather than incidental. Firms no longer
wait for disruption to pass before returning to a settled strategic posture, because no such
settled posture is presently available to return to.
Theoretically, this special issue advances marketing's understanding of disruption along
six interconnected dimensions: the reconceptualization of disruption as a persistent rather
than episodic condition; the collapse of the exogenous-endogenous binary into a feedback
system in which marketing operates at the critical interface; the demonstration that the
sensing-seizing-shaping triad of dynamic marketing capabilities is simultaneously, rather
than sequentially, disrupted under persistent conditions; the development of the
Disruption-Marketing Response Matrix as a tool for identifying underexplored cross-
quadrant capability requirements; the recasting of emerging markets as a theoretical
laboratory whose constraint-based innovation traditions are newly relevant to developed-
market firms; and the argument that new-age technologies function as marketing

disruption as the baseline condition for strategy. The managerial actions and the
Disruption-Response Action Matrix presented earlier in this Introduction translate these
theoretical claims into a practical diagnostic that managers can apply directly to their own
organizational context, regardless of which combination of disruption types they currently
face.
We close by returning to the observation with which this Introduction began. Marketing,
as a discipline, has always concerned itself with change- in consumer preference, in
competitive positioning, in technology, in channels. What distinguishes the present
moment, and what this Special Issue collectively documents, is not the presence of change
but its persistence, its interactivity, and its infrastructural depth. The task facing marketing
scholarship is no longer to explain how firms recover from disruption and return to
stability, but to explain how firms create and sustain value when stability itself is no longer
the relevant baseline. We hope that the theoretical contributions, managerial implications,
and research agenda offered in this Special Issue provide a meaningful foundation for that
task, and we look forward to the scholarship that will, in time, supersede it.

Table 4. Future Research Directions Arising from This Special Issue
No. Theme Research Question Rationale
RQ1 Persistent
disruption
What cues do managers rely on, in real time
and with incomplete information, to judge
whether a given disruption is transient or
marks a lasting change in competitive
conditions?
This issue argues that disruption is
increasingly the rule rather than the
exception, but stops short of explaining
how that judgement is actually formed
under uncertainty. The cost of misjudging it
runs in both directions: overreacting to a
passing shock wastes resources, while
treating a structural shift as temporary
leaves a firm exposed.
RQ2 Feedback dynamics How far, and through how many
intermediate actors, can a firm's
endogenous response to disruption be
traced as it becomes a new exogenous
pressure on competitors, suppliers, or
regulators?
The contributions collectively move
disruption away from a one-directional,
externally imposed event and toward an
interactive system, yet none traces the
cascade beyond its first link. Methods such
as dynamic network analysis or agent-based
simulation are well suited to extending this
work empirically.
RQ3 Capability
asymmetry
Is the observed gap between fast
downstream marketing responses (pricing,
communication, channel) and slower
upstream responses (sourcing, product
development, R&D) a structural constraint,
or a capability deficit that deliberate
investment can narrow?
Several contributions note this asymmetry
without resolving its underlying cause.
Whether it reflects an inherent limit on
marketing's authority or simply
underdeveloped cross-functional capability
has direct implications for how firms
allocate investment under disruption.
RQ4 Market shaping What capabilities distinguish firms that
actively create new markets and
consumption norms under disruption from
firms that primarily sense and seize
opportunities within markets that already
exist?
Of the three dynamic marketing capabilities
addressed in this issue, shaping remains the
least developed both conceptually and
empirically. No contribution offers a
measure of it, leaving this the most
consequential gap for advancing a complete
capability-based account of disruption.
RQ5 Disruptive intent
and firm value
Does the positive interaction between
disruptive strategic intent and market-based
asset capabilities, reported by Padigar et al.
(2026), hold across industries at different
stages of maturity and under varying levels
of competitive intensity?
The finding is well supported within its own
empirical setting, but its boundary
conditions are untested. Establishing
whether the effect generalizes, attenuates,
or reverses across industry contexts would
materially strengthen its practical and
theoretical reach.

RQ7 MNE governance What degree and form of headquarters
control allows multinational subsidiaries to
respond to local institutional pressure
without compromising strategic coherence
across the organization?
Roh et al. (2026) find that negative effect of
MNEs headquarter control over specific
functions of subsidiaries and headquarter
use of subsidiary control mechanism on the
adoption of disruptive innovation. The
finding identifies a governance tension but
does not specify how much control should
be relaxed, or in what form, leaving an open
and practically consequential question for
MNE strategy.
RQ8 New-age
technology
deployment
What firm-level decision criteria, applied
before a technology rollout, would reliably
distinguish deployments likely to create
value from those likely to be discounted by
the market as symbolic?
Bahmani et al. (2026) show that extended
reality announcements reduce firm value on
average, with the effect reversing only for
AR deployments concentrated at the
purchase stage and announced more
recently. The market appears able to
distinguish substantive deployment from
symbolic gesture after the fact; firms
evidently cannot always do so beforehand.
RQ9 AI and marketing
agility
Does the positive relationship between AI-
based stakeholder engagement and
marketing agility reported by N. Tehrani
and Roy (2025) hold uniformly across
stakeholder types, or is it concentrated in
particular relationships such as customers
or employees?
Stakeholder engagement is treated as a
single construct spanning several distinct
relationships. Disaggregating by
stakeholder type would clarify which
engagement channels are responsible for
the agility gains observed, and under what
disruption conditions.
RQ10 Multi-level value
creation
When economic, relational, and societal
value-creation objectives come into conflict
under disruption, which objective do firms
prioritize first, and does that sequencing
predict subsequent performance?
This issue argues persuasively that
marketing creates value at three levels
simultaneously under disruption, but none
of the included contributions models how
firms manage trade-offs among these levels
when they compete for the same resources
or attention.
