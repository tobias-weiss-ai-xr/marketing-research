Abstract
The rapid evolution of digital technologies, particularly the emergence of the metaverse, presents
transformative opportunities for e-tourism marketing in emerging economies. This study examines the
role of the metaverse in shaping the future of e-tourism marketing in Zimbabwe, a country where tourism
contributes approximately 7.3% to the Gross Domestic Product. Adopting an interpretivism research
philosophy, the study employed a qualitative research approach, collecting data from hotel managers,
lodge managers and tourism professionals across Victoria Falls, Hwange and Masvingo, using semi-
structured interviews and focus group discussions with domestic and international tourists. Thematic
analysis following Braun and Clarke's (2006) framework revealed four primary themes: infrastructural
deﬁcits as critical barriers; digital literacy and skills gaps in the tourism workforce; perceived potential
and the risk of diluting cultural authenticity through digital twins; and economic viability concerns
surrounding new revenue streams and disintermediation. The ﬁndings indicate that while stakeholders
recognise the metaverse's potential to enhance destination marketing through virtual tours, digital twins
and blockchain-enabled transactions, Zimbabwe's tourism industry remains inadequately prepared for
adoption due to erratic power supply, high data costs, limited digital skills and inadequate policy
frameworks. The study proposes a phased framework encompassing planning, development and
implementation stages for integrating metaverse technologies into Zimbabwe's e-tourism marketing
strategies. Recommendations include investment in digital infrastructure, capacity building for tourism
professionals, development of supportive regulatory frameworks and culturally sensitive content
creation. This research contributes to the growing discourse on digital transformation in African tourism
and provides actionable insights for policymakers, tourism authorities and industry practitioners seeking
to leverage immersive technologies for sustainable tourism development.
Introduction and Background
The rapid evolution of digital technologies has revolutionised various industries, including tourism. One
of the most transformative innovations in recent years is the metaverse a collective virtual shared space
that blends augmented reality (AR), virtual reality (VR) and the internet to create immersive digital
experiences (Mystakidis, 2022). As global tourism recovers from the disruptions caused by the COVID-19
pandemic, the metaverse presents an unprecedented opportunity for e-tourism marketing, particularly in
emerging economies like Zimbabwe. Zimbabwe’s tourism sector, renowned for its rich cultural heritage
and natural attractions such as Victoria Falls, Hwange National Park and Great Zimbabwe, has
increasingly embraced digital marketing strategies to attract international visitors (Zimbabwe Tourism
Authority [ZTA], 2023). However, the adoption of metaverse-based tourism marketing remains in its
infancy.
In Zimbabwe, where tourism is a critical economic sector contributing approximately 7.3% to the GDP
(World Travel & Tourism Council [WTTC], 2023), the integration of the metaverse presents new
opportunities for immersive and interactive marketing strategies. E-tourism, deﬁned as the digitization of
all processes and value chains in the tourism industry (Buhalis & Sinarta, 2019), is increasingly

leveraging virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR) and blockchain technologies to enhance customer
engagement. The metaverse, with its immersive 3D environments, offers Zimbabwean tourism
marketers a platform to showcase attractions such as Victoria Falls, Great Zimbabwe and wildlife safaris
in innovative ways. This research explores how the metaverse can reshape e-tourism marketing in
Zimbabwe by enhancing customer engagement, offering virtual travel experiences and creating new
revenue streams for tourism stakeholders.
The concept of the metaverse, ﬁrst coined by Neal Stephenson in his 1992 science ﬁction novel Snow
Crash, has evolved into a tangible digital ecosystem where users interact via avatars in real-time
(Dionisio et al., 2013). Tech giants like Meta (formerly Facebook), Microsoft and Google have heavily
invested in metaverse development, signaling its potential to redeﬁne digital interactions (Chang & Lee,
2020). In tourism, the metaverse enables virtual tours, digital twin destinations and NFT-based travel
souvenirs, offering a new dimension to destination marketing (Buhalis & Sinarta, 2019).
E-tourism, which refers to the digitization of tourism services and marketing, has grown signiﬁcantly in
Zimbabwe, with platforms like Airbnb, Booking.com and local travel agencies leveraging social media
and online booking systems (Buhalis & Law, 2008). However, challenges such as limited digital
infrastructure, low internet penetration and resistance to technological adoption hinder the full potential
of e-tourism (Chikuta et al., 2023). The metaverse could mitigate some of these challenges by providing
low-cost virtual tourism experiences, reducing physical travel barriers and attracting tech-savvy tourists.
Studies suggest that virtual reality tourism increases destination awareness and inﬂuences travel
decisions(Tussyadiah & Park, 2018). For Zimbabwe, integrating the metaverse into tourism marketing
could enhance global visibility, especially among younger travelers who prefer immersive digital
experiences. However, the feasibility of such adoption depends on government policies, private sector
investment and digital literacy improvements (Digital Economy for Zimbabwe, 2021). This study examines
the opportunities and challenges of metaverse-driven e-tourism marketing in Zimbabwe, contributing to
the broader discourse on digital transformation in African tourism.
E-Tourism in Zimbabwe
Zimbabwe’s tourism sector relies heavily on international visitors, with major attractions including
UNESCO World Heritage Sites like Victoria Falls and Mana Pools (Zimbabwe Tourism Authority [ZTA],
2022). However, the sector faces challenges such as limited global visibility and infrastructural
constraints. E-tourism marketing, through social media and online travel agencies (OTAs), has been
pivotal in promoting Zimbabwe as a destination (Buhalis & Law, 2008). The metaverse could further
amplify these efforts by providing virtual tours, gamiﬁed experiences and digital twin simulations of
tourist sites.
Potential beneﬁts of the metaverse for Zimbabwean tourism include enhanced destination marketing,
that is, virtual previews of attractions that can increase tourist interest and bookings (Guttentag, 2010).

To explore the potential applications of the metaverse in enhancing e-tourism marketing strategies.
To assess the readiness of Zimbabwe's tourism industry to adopt metaverse technologies.
To propose a framework for integrating the metaverse into Zimbabwe's e-tourism marketing

Literature Review
Theoretical Framework: The Extended UTAUT2 Model for
Metaverse Adoption in E-Tourism
This study is grounded in the Uniﬁed Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology 2 (UTAUT2), originally
developed by Venkatesh, Thong, and Xu (2012) as an extension of the foundational UTAUT model
(Venkatesh et al., 2003). The UTAUT2 framework provides a comprehensive theoretical lens for
examining technology adoption in organisational and consumer contexts, making it particularly suitable
for investigating metaverse integration in Zimbabwe's e-tourism marketing.
The choice of UTAUT2 is justiﬁed by several considerations. First, the model has been extensively
validated in tourism and hospitality research, demonstrating robust explanatory power for technology
adoption behaviors (Kumar & Kaushik, 2021; Kim & Lee, 2020). Second, UTAUT2 incorporates hedonic
motivation and price value constructs, which are critical for understanding tourism stakeholders'
adoption decisions where experiential value and cost implications are paramount (Gupta & Arora, 2017).
Third, the framework's ﬂexibility allows for contextual extension, enabling this study to incorporate
Zimbabwe's unique infrastructural and cultural dynamics.
The UTAUT2 model comprises seven core constructs that explain behavioral intention and use
behaviour: performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social inﬂuence, facilitating conditions, hedonic
Page 4/24

motivation, price value and habit (Venkatesh et al., 2012). In the context of this study, these constructs
are operationalised as follows:
Performance expectancy refers to the degree to which tourism stakeholders believe that adopting
metaverse technologies will enhance their marketing effectiveness and destination promotion. This
aligns with Objective 2 (exploring potential applications of the metaverse) and the study's ﬁndings on
perceived potential for immersive storytelling and virtual tours. Tour operators and hotel managers who
perceive the metaverse as capable of increasing tourist engagement and bookings are more likely to
adopt these technologies (Deng et al., 2024; Tussyadiah & Park, 2018).
Effort expectancy captures the perceived ease of using metaverse platforms and tools. Given the digital
literacy gaps identiﬁed in Zimbabwe's tourism workforce (Chikuta et al., 2023; Marketers Association of
Zimbabwe, 2025), this construct is particularly relevant to Objective 3 (assessing industry readiness).
The literature suggests that complex technological interfaces requiring specialised skills may deter
adoption, especially among small and medium tourism enterprises (Mupﬁga, 2015).
Social inﬂuence reﬂects the extent to which tourism professionals perceive that important other
(government, ZTA, competitors, tourists) believe they should adopt metaverse technologies. In the
Zimbabwean context, the Zimbabwe Tourism Authority's "Tourism Innovation Challenge" and
promotional campaigns represent social inﬂuence mechanisms encouraging digital transformation
(ZTA, 2025). This construct is pertinent to understanding the diffusion of metaverse adoption across the
industry.
Facilitating conditions encompass the technical and organisational infrastructure supporting metaverse
adoption. This directly addresses Objective 1 (examining current e-tourism limitations), as the literature
and empirical ﬁndings consistently identify infrastructural deﬁcits, erratic power supply, high data costs,
limited internet connectivity, as critical barriers (Mkwizu, 2025; Digital Economy for Zimbabwe, 2021).
Without adequate facilitating conditions, even willing adopters cannot implement metaverse solutions
effectively.
Hedonic motivation refers to the enjoyment and pleasure derived from using metaverse technologies.
For tourists, this translates to the immersive and entertaining nature of virtual experiences (Prabawati et
al., 2024; Dwivedi et al., 2022). For tourism marketers, hedonic motivation may manifest in the creative
satisfaction of developing innovative digital content. This construct helps explain why certain
stakeholders might adopt the metaverse despite infrastructural challenges, driven by the intrinsic appeal
of the technology.
Price value captures the cognitive trade-off between perceived beneﬁts and costs of metaverse
adoption. The high costs of VR headsets, data and specialised software (Ministry of ICT, 2025) are
signiﬁcant considerations for Zimbabwean tourism enterprises, particularly given the country's
economic constraints (Dengedza et al., 2025). This construct is essential for understanding the

economic viability concerns explored in Objective 2 and Objective 4 (proposing an integration
framework).
Habit reﬂects the extent to which tourism professionals have developed automatic routines in their
marketing practices. While habit may currently favor traditional marketing approaches in Zimbabwe, the
post-pandemic digital acceleration has begun shifting behavioral patterns, as noted in the World Travel
and Tourism Council (2023) reports.
To examine the current state of e-tourism marketing in Zimbabwe and identify its limitations.
The facilitating conditions construct directly informs this objective by directing attention to existing
infrastructure and support systems. The habit construct helps explain why many tourism enterprises
continue using traditional marketing approaches despite digital alternatives. This framework guides the
investigation of how limited internet connectivity, power supply issues and inadequate technical support
(Mupﬁga, 2015; Chikuta et al., 2023) constrain current e-tourism efforts. Furthermore, the price value
construct illuminates how economic barriers perpetuate these limitations.
E-tourism marketing adoption in Zimbabwe has gained momentum in the recent years due to the digital
shift in technology. Its implementation remains signiﬁcantly low however, despite its adoption due to
many challenges that include economic, infrastructural as well as socio cultural challenges. The
Zimbabwean tourism sector has embraced the use of digital tools such as mobile applications, social
media and websites to promote tourist destinations like Victoria Falls, Hwange National Parks and the
Great Zimbabwe. The Zimbabwe Tourism Authority (2022) launched campaigns to rebrand the country
as a world of wonders. They used social media and websites to endorse these and according to Chikuta
et al., (2023), platforms like these are the widely used nowadays by hotels and tourists operators to
target tourists. However, despite all these, (Mupﬁga, 2015) in their study, found out that only 45 percent
of tourism enterprises in Zimbabwe have functional websites and most rely on third party platforms like
booking.com which limits local control over marketing.
The key limitations can be attributed to infrastructural deﬁcits in Zimbabwe. According to Mupﬁga and
Chikuta (2021), Zimbabwe is characterised by poor internet, erratic power supply, both in urban and rural
areas which hinder digital engagement. Other challenges were noted by (Dengedza et al., 2025) who
mentions that Zimbabwe economic environment is a barrier to e-tourism in Zimbabwe as there is hyper-
inﬂation and shortages of foreign currency to invest in digital tools. Dengedza et al., (2025) links these
shortages to Zimbabwean economic instability which hinders technological advancements and growth.
In addition, Chikuta et al., (2023) identiﬁes digital literacy gaps as one of the challenges of e-tourism in
Zimbabwe. They argued that many tourist centres and their workers lack digital skills to manage online
campaigns effectively which in-turn leads to poorly optimised content as well as outdated content on
organisation websites. Even though some organisations are trying, (Moyo et al., 2025) is of the view that
even though ZTA promotes e-tourism, there is still lack of cohesive policies to support innovation and
public-private partnerships. This therefore has come as a great hindrance to e-tourism adoption in the

Zimbabwean Tourism Sector. He also identiﬁed cultural narratives as challenges to e-tourism in
Zimbabwe as he argued that digital campaigns often fail to integrate the local cultural heritage, resulting
in generic content that fails to completely differentiate Zimbabwe’s tourism offerings. Zimbabwe’s e-
tourism presents potential for growth but it still remains hampered by these challenges. Addressing
these limitations may require coordinated efforts to improve infrastructure, enhance digital skills and
also develop cultural resonant strategies.
To explore the potential applications of the metaverse in enhancing
e-tourism marketing strategies
The performance expectancy and hedonic motivation constructs are central to understanding perceived
beneﬁts of metaverse applications. This framework directs the investigation toward how stakeholders
believe virtual tours, digital twins and blockchain-enabled transactions (Dwivedi et al., 2022; Prabawati et
al., 2024) could enhance destination marketing. The cultural authenticity preservation extension
speciﬁcally addresses how stakeholders evaluate the potential of immersive storytelling and gamiﬁed
experiences (Deng et al., 2024) while maintaining the integrity of Zimbabwe's cultural heritage
The metaverse, which comprises of augmented reality, virtual reality and blockchain technology offers
many transformative opportunities for e-tourism marketing in Zimbabwe. The metaverse allows
immerse interactional experiences that can overcome physical limitations. According to (Dwivedi et al.,
2022), the metaverse allows for users to explore and experience landmarks, for example virtual
destination tours allows tourists to explore Eiffel Tower (Paris, France) in real time using VR headsets,
thereby fostering pre-travel engagements. In like manner, a study by (Prabawati et al., 2024) revealed that
digital twins of hotels enable potential tourists to walk through accommodations, thereby enhancing
booking and conﬁdence.
The integration of blockchain technology in the metaverse supports loyalty programs where tourists and
potential tourists can earn points for engagement. Such innovations may align with the growing need
and demand for personalised tourism experiences. The metaverse has the potential to enhance e-
tourism marketing strategies through immersive storytelling. (Deng et al., 2024) argue that the
metaverse allows tourist centres or destinations to come up with narrative driven campaigns. Examples
may include virtual re-enactments of historical cultural sites that can deepen the emotional connections
with tourists. The metaverse allows for virtual events and collaborations that can enhance e-tourism.
Destinations can host events in the metaverse, using virtual realities or inﬂuencers and in-turn reach a
wider range of tourists (Dwivedi et al., 2022). Metaverse enhance e-tourism through data driven
personalisation. The metaverse platforms can generate data, behavioral data that can enable target
marketing. This helps in tailor making packages based on user preferences (Prabawati et al., 2024). The
metaverse can enhance sustainable tourism promotion. Virtual Experiences from VR reduce the carbon
foot print of physical travel thereby aligning to the eco-conscious trends. Taking for example, tourist
destinations like Iceland are using virtual reality to promote their off season visits.

Despite the potential application of the metaverse, it comes with its challenges in e-tourism especially in
the Zimbabwean context. Some of the noted barriers include technological infrastructure, which is
characterised by high costs of virtual realities and uneven internet connectivity and accessibility that
limit global participation (Palinkas et al., 2015) (Deng et al., 2024). Other scholars like (Dwivedi et al.,

application of the metaverse in e-tourism.
Additionally, the metaverse has a tendency of over reliance on digital experiences. These has been seen
to dilute cultural authenticity of cultural heritage and hence low adoption (Prabawati et al., 2024). There
is lack of standardised policies for virtual transactions and intellectual property in the metaverse which
then limits implementation.
Metaverse technologies adoption readiness in Zimbabwe’s tourism
sector
The effort expectancy construct examines the perceived ease of use, directly linking to digital literacy
and skills gaps identiﬁed in the workforce (Marketers Association of Zimbabwe, 2025; Chikuta et al.,
2023). The infrastructure readiness extension assesses the physical and technical foundations for
adoption. Facilitating conditions evaluates whether the necessary organisational and government
support exists, while price value considers economic feasibility. Together, these constructs provide a
comprehensive readiness assessment framework.
The advent of 3D virtual world immersive networks has transmogriﬁed the way the tourism sector
operates globally. These digital technologies are impacting the way tourist destinations are now being
planned, managed and developed (Keertana & Vijay Vishnu Kumar, 2025). The metaverse is giving rise to
the development of smart destinations (ibid). Traditionally e-tourism has had its focus on two-way
information exchanges while the Metaverse integrates Augmented Reality (AR), Virtual Reality (VR) and
Artiﬁcial Intelligence (AI) (Teknolojileri & Turizm, 2025). The use of these state-of-the-art technologies
presents both opportunities and challenges for players in the developing destination tourism sectors like
Zimbabwe. Historically Zimbabwe has registered high levels of literacy among its population in the SADC
region thereby creating an enabling environment for the adoption of Smart Tourism. However, Saljoughi
& Hassani, (2025) posit that infrastructural constraints and the digital divide present major challenges
for the developing nations. Kunarz (2025) also concurs with these sentiments by highlighting that the
high costs of metaverse infrastructure hinder smart tourism in developing destinations.
Metaverse adoption in the Zimbabwean context poses to be a major challenge owing to erratic power
supplies, high costs of hardware components (VR headsets) and high data costs (Ministry of ICT, Postal
and Courier Services, 2025). Efforts by the Zimbabwe Tourism Authority (ZTA) to encourage young
innovators to embrace immersive technologies by developing sustainable digital solutions have been
initiated through the “Tourism Innovation Challenge” (ZTA, 2025).

Framework for integrating the Metaverse in Zimbabwe’s e-
tourism
The UTAUT2 model's predictive capacity - explaining behavioral intention and use behavior - informs the
development of a phased integration framework. The performance expectancy and hedonic motivation
constructs suggest strategies that emphasises tangible beneﬁts and enjoyable experiences. The
facilitating conditions and infrastructure readiness constructs underscore the importance of
foundational investments. Social inﬂuence indicates the need for government and ZTA leadership. The
cultural authenticity preservation extension ensures that proposed strategies are culturally sensitive,
aligning with the study's ﬁnding that the metaverse should complement rather than replace physical
experiences.
The integration of the Metaverse in the Zimbabwe’s smart tourism requires marketing campaigns to shift
from traditional static advertisements to highly interactive branded experiences on online platforms.
Mudzimba & Mashero (2024) note that the Metaverse in African tourism presents opportunities for
destination branding which allow for interoperability, creatability and economic ﬂow. The framework for
tourism should encompass a three-stage framework involving planning, development and
implementation. At the planning stage, digital twinning can be engaged by identifying iconic branded
heritage sites like the Great Zimbabwe and the Victoria Falls. Mckinsey (2024) deﬁnes a digital twin as “A
digital replica contextualized in a digital version of its environment to simulate real situations and
outcomes.” The digital twin bridges the gap between the actual physical heritage site and the immersive
digital tour site. The use of digital twins is in line with the Tourism 3.0 concept which facilitates the
cultivation of meaningful connections, community building, sustainability, inclusivity and meaningful
connections in physical and virtual environments (International Telecommunications Union (ITU) Report,
2024).
The digital twin assumes the role of a typical Decision Support System (DSS), the digital twin facilitates a
traveler’s decision-making process through the outcomes (McKinsey, 2024). The digital twin should be
updated with real-time data from the Internet of things (IoT) sensors. This creates temporal reality of the
actual terrain being represented where visitors have a feel of the actual physical environment of the
targeted tourist destination. The idea is to allow for potential visitors to destination Zimbabwe to engage
on virtual tours to trigger their propensity to visit the actual sites physically. At the development stage,
potential visitors can take virtual tours to the sites prior making physical visits (Mudzimba & Mashero,
2024). The augmentation of planning and pre-travel research optimizes tourism hospitality services. At
implementation stage, AI driven personalisation can be customized to individual visitor preferences. The

The potential economic, social and environmental impact of
Metaverse driven e-tourism in Zimbabwe
Economic impact
The Metaverse technologies have the possibility of monetizing tour packages, products and services.
This is achieved through the sales of virtual souvenirs (NFTs) and digital tour tickets (Iliuţă et al., 2024).
Payment gateways are made available online to support various payment platforms like visa cards, bank
transfers, master cards, Ecocash, PayPal, Pay Now and crypto currencies to access tourism packages
and services. This makes transaction processing both economic and convenient.
Folgieri, Gricar & Baldigara (2025) posit that NFTs are a blockchain technology application which
potentially disrupt activities particularly in the tourism sector. Post Covid19 era, in 2021, the United
States of America posted transactions to the tune of USD$17 billion when the NFT market boomed
(Folgieri et al., 2025). NFTs are unique digital assets lined to speciﬁc online real-world assets mainly
related to blockchain art and collectible markets. In the tourism sector, these NFTs can represent any
asset in digital form comprising, event tickets, digital art and collectable items, content videos or audios
Page 11/24

and in-game items. (Ibid) further assert that NFTs are block-chain based cryptographic assets
characterized by unique identiﬁcation codes and metadata. Rawat & El Alami, (2023) emphasize the
importance of digital security in the metaverse as these NFTs are associated with ﬁnancial fraud. These
augment the physical tourist visits to tourist destinations. Dogru et al., (2018) posit that blockchain has
four features which enable the facilitation of smart transaction which comprise shared leger, security,
eﬃciency and smart contracts.
The use of the Metaverse blockchain applications can lead to disintermediation of intermediaries, for
example, travel agents whereby potential tourists can make online bookings prior to travelling to their
holiday destinations. Önder & Gunter, (2022) concur by noting that individuals can avoid intermediaries
by using these digital platforms. When disintermediation occurs, the tourism operators incur opportunity
costs. The Metaverse has the power to cripple a whole tourist sector in a developing nation if it is not
balanced with physical transactions and visits.
The highly developed nations are technologically developed and diffusion of these innovations is
accelerated by the willingness of the populations to use the new innovations. On the contrary, the risk of
possible virtual substitution which may affect revenue generation by individual tourism operators who
rely on generating income by offering in-person services to tourists. The Medium to Small Enterprises
(MSMEs) in the Zimbabwean tourism sector can be adversely affected by such developments
economically.
Digital addiction may negatively impact Tourism operators like tour guides, travel agents, Air BnBs,
restaurants, ﬁshing clubs, hunting clubs, water rafting clubs, bungee jumping clubs, national parks, curio
sales, pubs, night clubs and night spots, braai/ barbeque spots safari train operators, clothing shops,
ﬁnancial institutions, supermarkets, service stations, boat cruise lines, helicopter tours, traditional
dancing groups and caravan and campsites would be hard hit. The top of the range ﬁve-star hotels and
boutique hotels and safari lodges will also not be spared from this predicament.
Social impact of the Metaverse technologies
According to Aktas et.al. (2023), the Metaverse can foster inclusions of individuals from different
backgrounds by providing potential beneﬁts of access for physically challenged individuals who may not
be able to travel physically to the sites and those who may be ﬁnancially crippled who cannot afford
holidays. Individuals with various physical impairments including audio impairments can be catered for
through virtual sign language aided tours. This concurs with ﬁndings from the ITU Report (2024) which
noted that the adoption of Metaverse technologies leads to democratization of tourism experiences in
terms of facilitation of gaining access to travel visitations through virtual visitations to tourist
destinations. More people from all walks of life can explore various cultures and geographic locations in
dependence of location real-time without the need to travel to the sites. This also aligns with the
provisions made by the SDG10 of the United Nations which calls for inclusivity of all individuals in
accessing tourism facilities.

According to the ITU Report (2024), the Metaverse technologies have led to the emergence of
community owned economies and social tokens. These technologies also build trust and curb

The MDPI (2025) highlights the possible challenges associated with digitalization of heritage sites

Environmental impact of Metaverse technologies
The Metaverse technologies have been viewed as a platform for the shift towards sustainable tourism.
The reduced carbon footprint emanating from the use long haul ﬂights boarded by distant tourists as
they make their way physically to the heritage sites. In contrast, the World Economic Forum (2023)
argues that the use of server farms and digital block-chain networks that power the Metaverse could
result in adverse carbon emissions if not replaced by sustainable sources of energy. In developing
nations like Zimbabwe with rampant power cuts can lead to Green Data Centers since the Metaverse will
be powered by renewable sources of energy like solar power.
The virtual substitution of physical site visits can reduce carbon footprint by 50% and enhance
operational eﬃciency by the use of digital twins (EY, 2026). This decarbonisation is almost a fallacy
owing to the interdependence between digital platforms and the physical infrastructure which require
fossil fuels for powering (The Shift Report, 2024). The use of Metaverse technologies already
contributes 4% to the carbonization of the environment. This calls for the use of more sustainable,
renewable sources of energy to power the Metaverse infrastructure, devices and data centers. France’s
usage of technology accounted for 10% of total energy consumption in the year 2022 (Project, n.d.).
The use of Metaverse technologies also curbs challenges associated with over tourism by decongesting
the physical heritage spaces. The pressure on available infrastructure, hotel occupancy and the physical
environment is managed. Online virtual tours lower pollution levels noise pollution, air and water
pollution as well as sanitation is well managed. The risk of damage to natural resources is reduced as
well as the threat to endangered ﬂora and fauna species.
Methodology
This research adopted an interpretivism research philosophy, which aligns with qualitative inquiry by
prioritising practical problem-solving and recognising that qualitative approaches offer valuable,
complementary insights into complex phenomena (Rianty et al., 2022). The interpretivism paradigm is
particularly appropriate for investigating emerging technologies like the metaverse in tourism contexts,
Page 13/24

as it allows researchers to employ multiple methods to understand both measurable outcomes and lived
experiences (Putra et al., 2025). The study adopted the qualitative approach whereby data was collected
from local and international tourists through focus group discussions. The qualitative exploratory design
aims to uncover the meanings, opportunities, barriers and potential applications of the Metaverse in the
Zimbabwean e-tourism context from the perspectives of key stakeholders.
According to the ZTA statistics, there are 1199 registered tourism operators in Zimbabwe comprising of
hotels, booking agencies, lodges, travel agencies and restaurants (Zimbabwe tourism.net). Victoria falls
being the largest and one of the most popular holiday resort areas has the largest pool of tourists
annually and has been registering high visitations on an annual basis. According to Gay & Diehl, (1992)
10% of the population can give valid results and if the population is under 1000, they then suggest a
higher threshold of 20%. The 10% rule of thumb informed the sample size determination of 120
respondents. The sample was divided proportionately amongst the three resort towns comprising:
Victoria Falls which was allocated the largest proportion of 55 respondents, Hwange 40 and Masvingo
25.
Primary data was collected through semi-structured, in-depth interviews with domestic and international
tourists, hotel managers in Victoria Falls Zimbabwe's tourism and hospitality sector. Participants
included hotel managers, tour operators, travel agents and e-commerce platform developers selected in
Victoria Falls, one of the natural wonders of the world with tourists from all over the world. Participant
selection was determined based on the principle of information power, which suggests that the more
relevant information a sample holds, the fewer participants are needed (Malterud et al., 2016). Focus
Group Discussions (FGDs) were conducted with groups of tourists who had used e-commerce platforms
for tourism and hospitality services in Victoria Falls and Hwange. FGDs were particularly useful for
capturing diverse perspectives and generating rich interactive data (Morgan, 1996).
A purposive sampling technique with convenience sampling was used to select participants who are
directly involved in the tourism and hospitality sector. The sample size was determined by data
saturation, where data collection continued until no new themes or insights emerged from the interviews
and FGDs (Braun & Clarke, 2021). The data analysis process was guided by the principles of thematic
analysis, which is consistent with the interpretivist approach. The steps for data analysis were data
transcription, familiarization, coding, thematic development and data interpretation (Braun & Clarke,
2006). The study adhered to strict ethical guidelines to ensure the protection of participants' rights and
conﬁdentiality.
Results and Discussion of Findings
This section presents the ﬁndings of the qualitative study investigating the role of the metaverse in
shaping the future of e-tourism marketing in Zimbabwe. The data was analysed using Braun and Clarke's
(2006) six-step thematic analysis procedure. Four primary themes emerged from the qualitative data
Page 14/24

analysis, aligning with the study's objectives. These themes are discussed and supported by authors, to
provide a holistic interpretation of the phenomenon.
Thematic Analysis Findings
Theme 1: Infrastructural Deﬁcits as a Critical Barrier to E-
Tourism and Metaverse Adoption
This theme was one of the most consistently and emphatically raised by participants across all
stakeholder groups (tourists, hotel managers and tourism professionals). It captured the profound
challenges posed by Zimbabwe's infrastructural landscape to the current e-tourism efforts and the future
adoption of metaverse technologies.
Qualitative ﬁndings revealed that participants perceive infrastructural deﬁcits as a primary hindrance to
e-tourism. Hotel managers and tourism professionals in interviews frequently described the erratic
power supply, high data costs and unreliable internet connectivity as constant obstacles. As one
manager noted,
We try to maintain our online presence, but power cuts and slow internet make it impossible to manage
bookings or update our website reliably.’ A tourist during an FGD remarked, ‘The websites are often slow or
unavailable, and payment gateways fail. It makes booking from outside Zimbabwe a frustrating experience.
These qualitative insights align with and expand upon the challenges identiﬁed in the literature. (Mupﬁga,
2015) asserts that Zimbabwe is characterised by poor internet and erratic power supply, which hinders
digital engagement. Similarly, a recent study on leveraging disruptive technologies in rural Zimbabwe
identiﬁed ‘inadequate infrastructure and facilities’, ‘limited availability of technology and internet
connectivity,’ and ‘intermittent power cuts’ as key constraints. The World Bank has also highlighted
digital infrastructure gaps as a major impediment to the full digital economy potential in Zimbabwe
(Digital Economy for Zimbabwe, 2021). This suggests that Zimbabwe's e-tourism is not only constrained
by the presence of infrastructure but also its affordability.
The ﬁndings highlight a critical gap. While there is recognition of the potential of e-tourism and
metaverse technologies, the fundamental infrastructure required for their operation remains deﬁcient.
This creates a cyclical problem where limited adoption fails to drive the investment needed to improve
the infrastructure.
The dominance of the ‘Infrastructural Deﬁcits’ theme directly addresses the study's ﬁrst objective to
examine the current state and limitations of e-tourism in Zimbabwe. The results empirically conﬁrm the
ﬁndings of (MKWIZU, 2025) and (Mupﬁga, 2015), demonstrating that infrastructural challenges remain
the most signiﬁcant barrier, even as the conversation shifts toward more advanced technologies like the
metaverse. The high costs of VR headsets and data, as noted in the literature review (Ministry of ICT,
Postal and Courier Services, 2025), are not just a potential barrier but a present reality for the tourism
Page 15/24

industry. This infrastructure deﬁcit fundamentally limits the feasibility of the ‘low-cost virtual tourism
experiences’ that the metaverse might otherwise offer (Guttentag, 2010). Overcoming this barrier may
require a foundational investment in national ICT infrastructure, possibly through public-private
partnerships, before the advanced applications of the metaverse can be realized.
Theme 2: Digital Literacy and Skills Gaps in the Tourism
Workforce
This theme emerged from interviews and FGDs as a signiﬁcant internal barrier to metaverse adoption.
Participants expressed concerns about the readiness and capacity of the current tourism workforce to
engage with sophisticated digital tools.
Qualitative data revealed a perceived gap in digital skills. A hotel manager stated:
We have the intent, but the staff are not trained. They can post on Facebook, but creating a virtual tour or
managing a metaverse presence is beyond our current capabilities.’ This was echoed by a tourism
professional who noted that ‘many of our marketing campaigns are poorly optimised because we lack the
expertise in data analytics and content creation for digital platforms.
These sentiments were particularly strong regarding the lack of skills for data-driven personalisation, a
key beneﬁt of metaverse applications. This aligns with the literature review's identiﬁcation of digital
literacy gaps as a key challenge (Chikuta et al., 2023). The Marketers Association of Zimbabwe (2025)
has also noted the need for capacity building to upskill marketing practitioners in digital skills to create
branded experiences. Furthermore, a recent study on digital marketing adoption by Zimbabwe's National
Tourism Organisation (NTO) found that despite embracing digital marketing, the sector still faces
challenges due to limited ﬁnancial resources and implicitly, a lack of specialised skills to fully implement
the concept. The literature also suggests that while disruptive technologies like digital marketing and
virtual tours offer signiﬁcant prospects, their adoption is hampered by a lack of digital literacy and skills.
The ‘Digital Literacy and Skills Gaps’ theme emphasises that the challenge is not purely technological. A
government policy or private investment in VR headsets will have limited impact without a workforce
capable of leveraging these tools to create compelling marketing content. This ﬁnding supports the
study's objective to assess the readiness of Zimbabwe's tourism industry. The current readiness is low,
not just due to infrastructure, but due to a signiﬁcant deﬁcit in human capital. The proposed framework
for integrating the metaverse, with its reliance on technologies like Generative AI and spatial UI/UX,
would be unattainable for many current staff. Therefore, a comprehensive capacity-building strategy, as
recommended by the Marketers Association of Zimbabwe (2025), is a prerequisite for any successful
metaverse adoption.
Theme 3: Perceived Potential, Risk of Diluting Cultural Authenticity and the Strategic Use of Digital
Twins
Page 16/24

This theme captured the dualistic nature of participants' views on the metaverse excitement for its
transformative potential alongside deep concern about the potential loss of authenticity and the
perceived value of digital twins. On the positive side, participants were highly enthusiastic about the
immersive potential of the metaverse. During FGDs, tourists were particularly excited about the prospect
of ‘virtual pre-visits.’ As one international tourist explained:
‘If I could walk through the Victoria Falls rainforest or explore the ruins of Great Zimbabwe in VR before I
book, it would deﬁnitely inﬂuence my decision and build my excitement.’ This supports the assertion that
virtual reality tourism increases destination awareness and inﬂuences travel decisions (Tussyadiah &
Park, 2018).
The concept of ‘Digital Twins’ was central to this discussion. Participants, especially tourism
professionals, understood this as a virtual replica that could offer a ‘try–before-you–buy’ experience.
One professional noted, ‘A digital twin of a lodge could allow a tourist to virtually walk through the rooms
and the grounds, which would build conﬁdence in the booking.’ This is consistent with (Prabawati et al.,
2024), who found that digital twins of hotels enhance booking conﬁdence. McKinsey (2024) deﬁnes a
digital twin as a digital replica contextualized in a digital version of its environment to simulate real
situations and outcomes, a concept that aligns with the participants' understanding and its potential use
for decision support and trip planning (Deng et al., 2024)
However, a signiﬁcant counter-theme emerged: the fear of diluting cultural authenticity. A tourism
professional in an interview raised a critical concern:
‘If we digitise everything, does the physical experience still hold the same value? Could a virtual tour of
Great Zimbabwe make the actual visit feel less special?’ Another participant in an FGD expressed that the
metaverse should not be a substitute but a supplement, stating, ‘We don't want people to just watch a
video; we want them to come and feel the spray of the Falls. The metaverse should be a window, not a
replacement.’ This concern is supported by (Prabawati et al., 2024), who warned that an over-reliance on
digital experiences can dilute the cultural authenticity of heritage sites. The MDPI (2025) report also
noted the risk of virtual tourists ‘trivializing the legacy’ of sites by viewing them as mere entertainment.
The data suggests that while many see metaverse applications like digital twins as a powerful tool for
marketing and accessibility, they are acutely aware that the value proposition of Zimbabwe's tourism lies
in its authentic, tangible experiences. The Great Zimbabwe monuments, a UNESCO World Heritage Site,
derive their signiﬁcance not just from visual representation but from the material history they embody.
The digital twin is seen as a bridge, not an endpoint.
This theme links directly to the objective of exploring the potential applications of the metaverse.
Participants identiﬁed and validated the key applications from the literature, including virtual tours and
digital twins. These technologies are seen as having a high potential to enhance marketing and pre-trip
engagement. The research by Mudzimba and Mashero (2025) on the metaverse and agritourism
Page 17/24

marketing in Africa similarly highlights the opportunities for destination branding and interoperability
(Mudzimba & Mashero, 2025).
However, the thematic ﬁndings strongly emphasise the need to safeguard cultural heritage. The
perceived risk of virtual substitution (as raised by participants) and the fears about diluting authenticity
mean that any framework for metaverse adoption must prioritise authenticity and view the metaverse as
a complement to, rather than a substitute for, physical travel. This supports the paper's proposed
framework, which suggests digital twins be used as a Decision Support System (McKinsey, 2024) and a
conversion funnel (TV BRICS, 2025) to drive physical visitation.
Theme 4: Economic Viability, Revenue Streams, and the
Challenge of Disintermediation
This ﬁnal theme explored the economic possibilities and challenges perceived by participants, with a
particular focus on new revenue streams and the potential threat of disintermediation. Interviews with
hotel managers and tourism professionals revealed a strong interest in the potential to create new
revenue streams. The idea of selling NFTs as digital souvenirs or virtual tour tickets was a recurring
topic. One manager commented, ‘If we can sell a limited-edition digital art piece of a sunset over Victoria
Falls as an NFT, it could be a signiﬁcant new revenue stream.’ This aligns with the literature, which
highlights how blockchain and NFTs can be used to monetise tour packages and products (Folgieri et al.,
2025). The study by Rawat and Alami (2023) on metaverse security also underscores the importance of
digital security in managing these assets.
Conversely, a signiﬁcant concern was the risk of disintermediation, where potential tourists could bypass
traditional travel agents and local operators by using the metaverse. A tour operator stated, ‘If someone
can plan their entire trip in a virtual environment and book directly with a global platform, our role as local
agents and guides becomes less relevant.’ This is a fear that is documented in the literature, researchers
like (Dogru et al., 2018) and (Önder & Gunter, 2022) note that blockchain and digital platforms can lead to
disintermediation. This is particularly worrying for Zimbabwe's tourism sector, where MSMEs and local
communities rely heavily on in-person services, as noted in the paper's section on economic impact.
The ﬁndings on Economic Viability focus on the objective to explore the potential for creating new
revenue streams. The enthusiasm for NFTs and digital assets is evident, but it is tempered by the very
real economic threat of disintermediation. The literature is clear that blockchain features like a shared
ledger and smart contracts can facilitate smart transactions and disintermediation (Dogru et al., 2018).
This creates a crucial policy and strategic dilemma. The proposed framework must consider how to
leverage the new revenue streams (like NFTs) while mitigating the threat of disintermediation. This could
involve empowering local communities to create and manage their own metaverse content or ensuring
that digital marketplaces are structured to give them a direct stake, which aligns with the study’s
observation that the metaverse can lead to "community-owned economies" (ITU Report, 2024). The
Page 18/24

ﬁnding that digital addiction could affect local service providers like tour guides and restaurants (World
Economic Forum, 2023) is a signiﬁcant concern that must be addressed in any implementation strategy.
The qualitative methods approach reveals a complex landscape for metaverse adoption in Zimbabwe's
e-tourism marketing. Participants across all stakeholder groups consistently identify infrastructural
deﬁcits as the most pressing external barrier, validating concerns in the existing literature about poor
internet and erratic power supply ((Mupﬁga, 2015). A closely related internal challenge is digital literacy
and skills gaps, with a clear split between larger enterprises, which have some digital capacity and
smaller operators who lack the expertise to leverage metaverse technologies effectively, a ﬁnding
supported by studies on the Zimbabwean tourism workforce (Chikuta & du Plessis, 2019). Despite these
challenges, there is signiﬁcant optimism about the potential applications of the metaverse, particularly
through the use of digital twins and immersive virtual tours to enhance marketing and pre-trip
engagement. This aligns with the literature on how virtual reality inﬂuences travel decisions (Tussyadiah
& Park, 2018). However, this enthusiasm is tempered by a strong counter-narrative, with stakeholders
expressing deep concern about the risk of diluting cultural authenticity and turning heritage sites into
mere digital entertainment (Prabawati et al., 2024). Lastly, the economic viability of the metaverse is
viewed as a double-edged sword, while participants see potential in new revenue streams like NFTs
(Folgieri et al., 2025), they are acutely aware of the threat of disintermediation, which could bypass local
operators and negatively impact the MSMEs that form the backbone of the Zimbabwean tourism sector
(Dogru et al., 2018); Onder & Gunter, 2022).
Conclusions
This study has examined the role of the metaverse in shaping the future of e-tourism marketing in
Zimbabwe, revealing both signiﬁcant opportunities and formidable challenges. The metaverse presents
transformative potential for enhancing destination marketing, creating immersive experiences and
opening new revenue streams for tourism stakeholders. However, the realisation of this potential is
contingent upon addressing the infrastructural deﬁcits, digital literacy gaps and economic constraints
that currently characterise Zimbabwe's tourism landscape.
The study concludes that a phased, strategic framework is essential for metaverse adoption,
encompassing planning, development and implementation phases. This framework should be
underpinned by investment in digital infrastructure, skills development and supportive policy
frameworks. The active involvement of government, the Zimbabwe Tourism Authority, the private sector
and local communities is critical for ensuring that metaverse adoption is inclusive, sustainable and
culturally sensitive.
As Zimbabwe positions itself for recovery and growth in the post-pandemic era, the metaverse offers a
unique opportunity to leapfrog traditional marketing approaches and establish a competitive advantage
in the global tourism marketplace. The successful integration of the metaverse into e-tourism marketing
strategies could enhance Zimbabwe's global visibility, attract a new generation of tech-savvy travelers
Page 19/24

and create sustainable economic opportunities for local communities. The journey toward metaverse-
enabled e-tourism is not without its risks, including the potential for cultural dilution, economic
disintermediation and environmental impacts. However, with careful planning, inclusive stakeholder
engagement and a commitment to authenticity and sustainability, the metaverse can serve as a powerful
tool for advancing Zimbabwe's tourism sector and contributing to the country's broader economic
development goals.
Recommendations
Given the unique characteristics of Zimbabwe's tourism sector and the metaverse's speciﬁc affordances,
this study proposes extending the UTAUT2 framework with two contextual constructs: infrastructure
readiness and cultural authenticity preservation.
Infrastructure readiness is conceptualised as the extent to which Zimbabwe's digital and physical
infrastructure (connectivity, power supply, hardware accessibility, technical support) enables metaverse
adoption. This extension is necessitated by the empirical ﬁndings (Theme 1) and literature
demonstrating that technological infrastructure is a fundamental prerequisite for digital transformation
in African tourism contexts (Mkwizu, 2025; Saljoughi & Hassani, 2025). Unlike developed nations where
infrastructure is assumed, Zimbabwe's context demands explicit consideration of this factor as a
mediating variable between facilitating conditions and actual adoption.
Cultural authenticity preservation addresses the concern identiﬁed in Theme 3 - the risk of diluting
cultural heritage through digital representations. This construct captures stakeholders' perceptions of
whether metaverse experiences maintain or diminish the authenticity of Zimbabwe's cultural and natural
attractions. The literature on digital twins and cultural heritage (Prabawati et al., 2024; MDPI, 2025)
suggests that perceived authenticity threats can signiﬁcantly inﬂuence adoption decisions, particularly
for heritage sites like Great Zimbabwe and Victoria Falls.
