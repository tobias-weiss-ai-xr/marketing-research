1. Introduction

Digital culture has emerged as a central organizational phenomenon in the twenty -first century, arising from digital
transformation as a profound socio -economic reconfiguration rather than a purely technological shift (Moiras, 2025). In the
context of the Fourth Industrial Revolution (4IR), rapid advances in artificial intelligence, the Internet of Things, and big  data
are reshaping how organizations operate, interact, and create value, rendering digitalization a strategic imperative for survival
and competitive advantage (Hoe, 2019; Moiras, 2025). However, despite massive investments in digital technologies, empirical
evidence shows that fewer than 30% of digital t ransformation initiatives succeed, largely because organizations prioritize
technology over the human and cultural dimensions of change (Takyi et al., 2025) . Consequently, scholarly and managerial
attention has increasingly shifted toward digital culture as the ultimate enabler —or barrier —of digital transformation,
reframing it as a social a nd organizational transformation driven by leadership, mindset, and shared values rather than
technical infrastructure alone (Moiras, 2025).
The urgency of digital culture was further amplified by the COVID-19 pandemic, which acted as a historical catalyst that
accelerated digital adoption and forced organizations across sectors to transition abruptly to remote and hybrid work mod els
(Mihu et al., 2023) . While many organizations possessed advanced digital tools, the crisis exposed a critical gap in cultural
readiness, as the absence of shared digital values and behaviors undermined effe ctive technology use and organizational
resilience (Rani & Goh, 2022; Moiras, 2025) . In parallel, digital culture has increasingly converged with sustainability and ESG
agendas under the notion of “twin transformation,” positioning digitalization as a strategic path way for achieving Sustainable
Development Goals through transparency, resource efficiency, and social responsibility (Kisahwan et al., 2025). This relevance
is further reinforced by emerging paradigms such as Industry 5.0 and the metaverse, which emphasize human –machine
collaboration, societal well -being, and immersive digital environm ents, thereby requiring organizations to cultivate a digital
mindset capable of navigating complexity, ambiguity, and fluid organizational boundaries (Grover et al., 2022).
Abstract Digital culture has become a central organizational phenomenon shaping how firms navigate digital
transformation, emerging technologies, and sustainability pressures, yet prior research remains fragmented across
disciplines and contexts. This study provide s the first large -scale bibliometric mapping of digital culture research from an
organizational perspective. Using the Scopus database, we analyzed 212 publications published between 2008 and 2025.
Bibliometric techniques and VOSviewer were applied to exam ine (i) annual publication trends, (ii) thematic structures
through keyword co-occurrence networks, (iii) influential knowledge producers across authors, affiliations, and countries,
and (iv) document type distribution to infer field maturity. The results show a clear acceleration after 2020, culminating in
more than 60 publications per year by 2025, indicating that digital culture has shifted from a niche conceptual topic to a
consolidated research stream. Keyword co-occurrence reveals eight interconnected thematic clusters, with “digital culture”
and “digital transformation” acting as the primary conceptual anchors and linking strongly to (1) digitalization and Industry
4.0 in SMEs, (2) performance and dynamic capabilities, (3) sustainability/ESG -oriented transformation, (4) digital strategy
and structural alignment, (5) AI and IoT as emerging technological drivers, (6) the digital economy, (7) marketing and
consumer interaction, and (8) leadership -driven innovation. Country production patterns highlight a recent geographic
diversification, with rapid growth from emerging economies alongside established contributors. Document type analysis
further indicates a field dominated by journal articles (68.0%), supported by book chapters (18.5%) and conference papers
(11.0%), while reviews remain scarce, suggesting strong empirical expansion but limited synthesis. Overall, the study
clarifies the intellectual structure, growth trajectory, and research frontiers of digital culture, offering a structured
knowledge map to guide future theory building and research agendas.

From an organizational perspective, digital culture is commonly defined as the shared values, beliefs, and practices that
shape how members interact with digital technologies and with one another (Hoang & Hien, 2024; Kwil inski et al., 2024) .
Beyond software adoption, it functions as an overarching cultural toolkit—comprising values, stories, and rituals—that enables
organizations to transform practices and generate economic or social value (Grover et al., 2022) . A mature digital culture is
characterized by a digital mindset developed within agile and creative work environments, emphasizing collaboration,
innovation, accountability, transparency, customer -centricity, and human development (Abdallah et al., 2022) . Academically,
the concept of digital culture extends the Dynamic Capabilities View by embedding adaptability within organizational culture
(Hoang & Hien, 2024) , bridges functionalist and structuralist perspectives 21, advances socio -technical analyses of human –
technology interaction (Islam et al., 2023; Gianni et al., 2025)  and refines technology adoption models by explaining
heterogeneous outcomes across cultural contexts 52. Practically, digital culture is widely regarded as a decisive factor in
transformation success, financial and operational performance, innovation capability, sustainability and ESG outcomes,
workforce resilience, an d organizational agility (Oswald & Kleinemeier, 2017; Rani & Goh, 2022; Grover, 2022; Bhatti et al.,
2024; Hoang & Hien, 2024; Kisahwan et al., 2025).
Despite the rapid growth of digital culture research—particularly after 2020—the literature remains fragmented across
disciplines and contexts, lacking a coherent synthesis of its temporal evolution, domina nt themes, and key knowledge
producers. Existing studies are often theory -specific or sector -bound, leaving unresolved gaps regarding the historical
development of the field (RQ1), its core thematic clusters (RQ2), the relative influence of authors, instit utions, and countries
(RQ3), and the distribution of document types as an indicator of  field maturity (RQ4). To address these limitations, this study
adopts a bibliometric approach that enables a systematic and replicable mapping of publication trends, the matic structures,
and scholarly influence over time. By applying bibliometric techniques —supported by VOSviewer and Biblioshiny—to Scopus-
indexed publications, this research aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the intellectual landscape of digital culture,
clarify its conceptual architecture, identify global research hubs, and assess the maturity of the field through document typ e
analysis, thereby reducing fragmentation and informing future research agendas.

2. Methodology

As illustrated in Figure 1, the data collection and screening process followed a structured multi -stage procedure:

2.1. Data source

This study uses Scopus as the primary data source for the bibliometric review of digital culture. Scopus is widely
recognized as a comprehensive abstract and citation database with strong coverage in business, management, social sciences,
and information systems, which is appropriate f or the interdisciplinary nature of digital culture research. Using a single high -
quality database is common in bibliometric studies because it improves metadata consistency and reproducibility and reduces
duplication and intensive harmonization problems th at often occur when merging multiple databases (Ellegaard & Wallin,
2015; AlRyalat et al., 2019).

2.2. Search strategy

The search strategy was designed to be focused on the core  construct while maintaining sufficient inclusiveness for
mapping the field. Following the logic of the sample paper, we applied the main term “digital culture” to the Title –Abstract–
Keywords fields in Scopus to capture studies explicitly engaging with the  construct and to minimize ambiguity from loosely
related digitalization terms.  The search covered the period 2008 –2025 (as reported in the manuscript’s results section), and
was restricted to English -language publications to ensure comparability and becau se English dominates Scopus -indexed
scholarly outputs in this domain. Document types were limited to: Article, Book chapter, Conference paper, Review, and
Conference review, while Book, Note, and Editorial were excluded to enhance academic rigor and reduce  non-research items
in the dataset.

2.3. Selection process

After running the query, all retrieved records were exported from Scopus with full bibliographic metadata (e.g., authors,
titles, abstracts, keywords, affiliations, sources, references, citation s). The screening process then proceeded in three steps:
Eligibility screening: Records were checked against the predefined inclusion criteria (topic relevance, language, and documen t
types). Deduplication and cleaning: Duplicate entries were removed; inco nsistent naming variants (e.g., author names and
institutional labels) were harmonized where necessary to avoid split counts in productivity analyses. Relevance check:
Titles/abstracts were reviewed to exclude items in which “digital culture” was used only marginally or in a non-relevant sense.
To strengthen transparency, the selection logic follows the PRISMA approach for reporting identification, screening, exclusion,
and final inclusion decisions (Page et al., 2021).

2.4. Tools & analysis

Two complementary toolsets were used:  VOSviewer was employed for bibliometric network construction and
visualization, especially keyword co-occurrence mapping and cluster detection, consistent with standard bibliometric mapping
practice Biblioshiny (the web interface of the Bibliometrix package) was us ed for descriptive performance analytics and
additional bibliometric summaries (e.g., annual scientific production, authors’ production over time, country production over
time, document type distribution), enabling cross-validation of patterns across tools and improving the robustness of reporting.
For thematic mapping (RQ2), a keyword co -occurrence analysis was performed primarily on author keywords, with (i) a
minimum-occurrence threshold applied to reduce noise and improve interpretability, and (ii) gene ric/uninformative terms
removed (e.g., “study”, “article”), following the methodological rationale used in the sample paper.  For performance mapping
(RQ1, RQ3, RQ4), publication counts and distributions were computed for authors, affiliations, countries, and document types.

2.5. Indicators

To align directly with the four research questions, the study applied the following indicators:

RQ1 – Publication volume and temporal trends

Annual publication trends (documents per year) to identify growth patterns and developmental phases of digital culture
research. This indicator is commonly used to infer whether a field is emerging, expanding, or stabilizing.

RQ2 – Prominent themes and clusters

Keyword co -occurrence networks and cluster structures to reveal dom inant themes, their interrelationships, and
emerging fronts. Network visualizations (including density/heat interpretations when applicable) were used to support cluster
labeling and interpretation.

RQ3 – Influential authors, institutions, and countries

Productivity indicators: publications by author, affiliation, and country, and their evolution over time (e.g.,
authors’/countries’/affiliations’ production over time). These indicators identify knowledge hubs and concentration/dispersion
patterns across the field.

RQ4 – Document types and field maturity

Document type distribution (Article, Book chapter, Conference paper, Review, Conference review) to infer the field’s
maturity. In bibliometric terms, a strong dominance of peer -reviewed journal articles o ften signals increasing academic
legitimacy, whereas a relatively higher share of conference outputs may indicate exploratory stages .
Collectively, these indicators provide a coherent methodological framework to map the evolution (RQ1), intellectual
structure (RQ2), knowledge producers (RQ3), and maturity signals (RQ4) of digital culture research, consistent with the
analytical logic adopted in the sample bibliometric article.

3. Result and Discussion

3.1.  Theoretical evolution and conceptual foundations of digital culture

3.1.1. Historical foundations and conceptual origins of digital culture

The concept of digital culture has emerged through a multi -layered historical process, evolving from traditional
information technology (IT) management toward a broader societal and organizational paradigm. Its earliest foundations can
be traced back to the 1960s with the introduction of early computing systems such as IBM’s System/360, which primarily aimed
to improve efficiency through digitization, understood as the conversion of analog information into digital bitstrings (Petrescu
et al., 2022). During this period, the dominant perspective was that of “IT culture,” in which techn ology was perceived mainly
as a supportive tool for existing business functions rather than as a transformative force capable of reshaping organizationa l
structures and practices (Grover, 2022) . A major conceptual shift occurred in the mid -1990s when Nicholas Negroponte
introduced the notion of the “digital economy,” signaling the emergence of a reality in which the boundaries between the
physical and digital worlds began to blur (Negroponte, 2015) . This shift was further consolidated in the early 2000s when
Charlie Gere conceptualized “digitality” as a cultural marker, arguing that digital artifacts and communicatio n systems
distinguish contemporary life from earlier historical eras, thereby framing digital culture as a defining feature of modern society
(Gere, 2002).

3.1.2. Conceptual evolution and differentiation from IT culture

Building on these foundations, the conceptual understanding of digital culture evolved significantly from the mid-2000s
onward. Deuze (2006) conceptualized digital culture as an emerging set of values, practices, and expectations that shape how
individuals act and interact within a networked society, emphasizing participation, remediation, and bricolage as its core
components (Deuze, 2006). A critical turning point in the literature was the recognition that digital culture is ontologically
distinct from IT culture: while IT culture focuses on hardware and software “boxes” designed to perform specific tasks, digital
culture emphasizes the continuous flow of digital resources that transform organizational identities, value propositions, and
modes of interaction (Grover, 2022). More recent conceptualizations have advanced a “toolkit perspective,” viewing digital
culture not as a purely top-down regulative mechanism but as a collection of cultural resources—such as stories, values, norms,
and rituals—that individuals actively draw upon to construct, justify, and coordinate their actions within digital environments
(Grover, 2022) . This shift reflects a broader move away from deterministic views of technology toward interpretive and
practice-based perspectives on digitalization.

3.1.3. Contemporary synthesis and societal perspectives

In contemporary scholarship, digital culture is commonly understood as an integrated state in which digital technologies
are woven into all facets of human achievement, encompassing both material and spiritual dime nsions of social life (Kwilinski
et al., 2024). It is characterized by an analytical mindset, openness to change, and collaborative habits cultivated within agile,
creative, and flexible work environments (Rani & Goh, 2022; Leal -Rodríguez et al., 2023). The COVID-19 pandemic acted as a
major historical trigger that accelerated this transition, forcing organizations to move beyond simple technology adoption
toward comprehensive cultural transformation in order to ensure resilience, relevance, and long-term survival (Zarkada, 2025).
From a broader societal perspective, digital culture is conceptualized as humanity’s achievements in spiritual and material l ife
viewed through the lens of developing and utilizing digital technologies (Kwilinski et al., 2024). It is also regarded as a cultural
marker because it encompasses the artifacts and systems of communication that distinguish contemporary life from previous
eras (Deuze, 2006; Sadiku et al., 2017; Higgins et al., 2023) . At the same time, it signifies a state of deep technological
integration in which digital technologies shape social norms, decision-making processes, and everyday behaviors across society
(Kwilinski et al., 2024).

3.1.4. Organizational and strategic perspectives on digital culture

From an organizational standpoint, digital culture is increasingly viewed as an overarching toolkit that enables firms to
leverage digital resources in order to transform practices and create social and economic value (Grover et al., 2022). It is widely
regarded as a strategic foundation that fosters innovation, creativity, and an entrepreneurial mindset, positioni ng digital
culture as an integral component of organizational strategy rather than a peripheral attribute (Cardoso et al., 2023; Leal -
Rodríguez et al., 2023) . Within organizations, digital culture also manifests as a workplace atmosphere in which digital
technologies are seamlessly embedde d into operational processes and communication practices (Annisa et al., 2024; Pucihar
et al., 2024) . Importantly, digital culture is frequently conceptualized as a critical enab ler of digital transformation, helping
organizations overcome resistance to change, skill gaps, and structural inertia that often hinder digital initiatives (Alrasheedi
et al., 2022; Hoang & Hien, 2024) . This perspective underscor es the idea that successful digital transformation depends as
much on cultural readiness as on technological capability.

3.2. Evolutionary trajectory and maturation of digital culture research (RQ1)

Research on digital culture as an organizational phenomenon has evolved from niche conceptual explorations into a
major global research priority, with publication trends displaying a clear three -phase developmental trajectory. As shown in
figure 2, annual scientific output remained extremely limited, typically producing fewer than five publications per year, as
scholars primarily sought to define the contours of the digital era and its cultural implications in the initial conceptual
foundation period (2008 –2014). During this phase, research focused on social software, the transition from analog to digital
formats—such as the impact of CDs on the music industry—and the early dynamics of Web 2.0, reflecting a largely exploratory
and descriptive orientation toward digital culture (Daniel, 2019). Early studies also emphasized the notion of “communities of
production,” examining how digital culture enabled the co -creation of digital goods and collaborative value creation in online
environments (Lang et al., 2009).
As shown in Figure 2, the second phase, characterized by diversification and the emergence o f organizational contexts
(2015–2019), witnessed a steady yet moderate increase in publication volume, with annual outputs generally remaining below
ten articles per year. During this period, the analytical focus shifted from broad societal interpretations  to more concrete
organizational applications, including virtual teams and distributed organizational forms, signaling a growing recognition of
digital culture as an organizationally embedded construct (Snyder, 2015). Research also expanded into new domains such as
e-sports business models, digital leisure , and identity construction through fashion blogging, reflecting the increasing
penetration of digital culture into diverse economic and creative sectors (Choi, 2020) . In parallel, a notable rise in studies
targeting “digital natives,” particularly Generation Y, highlighted how their embedded digital culture necessitated new
approaches to marketing, leadership, and management practices (Lendel et al., 2017).
As further illustrated in Figure 2, a pronounced and exponential surge in research output emerged after 2020, marking
the third phase of strategic integration (2020 –2025), with annual publications reaching an all -time high of over 60 articles by
2025. This dramatic acceleration is widely attributed to the COVID -19 pandemic, which is frequently described as a disruptive
shock that imposed technological responsibility on organizations and elevated digital culture to a critical determinan t of
organizational resilience and survival (Cardoso et al., 2025). Contemporary research in this phase increasingly positions digital
leadership as a central driver for cultivating digital mindsets and shaping organizational digital culture (Kamal et al., 2025) ,
while also ex ploring the socialization of artificial intelligence and its symbiotic relationship with human employees within
digitally embedded work environments (Wu et al., 2025). Moreover, sustainability and ethical considerations have emerged as
prominent themes, with growing attention to green digital culture and its influence on environmental, social, and governance
(ESG) performance (Wang & Zhang, 2025) . This period also shows a strong geographic expansion toward small and medium -
sized enterprises (SMEs) (Vu & Le, 2025).

3.5. Document types and implications for field maturity (RQ4)

The distribution of document types indicates that digital culture research is largely driven by empirical studies, reflecting
a field that has moved beyond conceptual exploration toward empirical validation. As shown in Figure 8, journal articles
dominate the literature (68.0%), suggesting that scholars primarily rely on quantitative and configurational methods, such as
PLS-SEM and fsQCA, to examine the effects of digital culture on organizational performance, innovation, and sus tainability.

4. Theoretical and Research Implications

Taken together, the findings position digital culture as a boundary -spanning construct that connects organizational
culture, digital transformation, leadership, sustainability, and emerging technologies. The results reinforce the view that digital
culture is not a static organizational attribute but a dynamic capability that evolves in response to technological disruption,
environmental uncertainty, and institutional pressures. At the same time, the observed thematic concentration and
methodological convergence suggest opportunities for future research to diversify theoretical lenses, adopt longitudinal and
mixed-method designs, and explore underexamined contexts and levels of analysis. Overall, this discussion highlights that
digital culture research has en tered a phase of consolidation, characterized by strong empirical foundations, growing global

5. Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive bibliometric overview of digital culture research, clarifying how the field has
evolved from fragmented conceptual discussions into a consolidated and empirically grounded research domain. By analyzing
Scopus-indexed publications, the study m aps publication trends, thematic structures, key authors and institutions, and
document types, thereby offering a structured synthesis of an increasingly complex body of knowledge.
The findings show that digital culture research has expanded rapidly since 2020, positioning digital culture as a core
organizational capability that enables digital transformation, sustainability, and resilience rather than a peripheral cultur al
attribute. The thematic analyses reveal a dense intellectual structure centere d on digital transformation, leadership,
performance, sustainability, and emerging technologies, while authorship and affiliation patterns indicate a clear globalization
of research activity, with strong recent contributions from Asia and the Middle East alongside sustained influence from Europe
and North America. The dominance of journal articles suggests a high level of empirical maturity, although the limited number
of review-based studies indicates that theoretical integration remains underdeveloped.
This study makes several contributions. It offers the first large -scale bibliometric mapping of digital culture research,
enhances conceptual clarity by identifying dominant themes and research clusters, and highlights global knowledge hubs and
collaboration patterns. From a practical perspective, the findings underscore the importance of digital culture as a strategic
foundation for successful digital transformation across diverse organizational contexts, including SMEs, public -sector
organizations, and higher education institutions.
Despite these contributions, the study is subject to limitations related to database coverage and the quantitative nature
of bibliometric methods. Future research should integrate multiple databases, employ qualitative or mixed -method
approaches, and develop integrative frameworks that synthesize existing findings. Greater attention to underexplored areas—
such as digital organizational culture, employee well -being, and ethical dimensions of AI —would further advance the field.
Overall, this review provides a coherent roadmap for future digital culture research and supports theory development in a
rapidly evolving scholarly domain.
