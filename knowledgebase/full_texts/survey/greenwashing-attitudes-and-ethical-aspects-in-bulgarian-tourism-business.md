Abstract. In the ever -expanding environment of the regulatory frameworks and rules of the European
Union, greenwashing is becoming a highly subtle and widespread marketing practice in different areas of
industries, including tourism. For that reason, the general pur pose of this article is to explore the
managerial attitudes towards potential greenwashing pitfalls amongst tourism companies in Bulgaria by
taking into consideration the ethical aspects and specific corporate communication. The current research
study is based on the of use of an embedded single-case design involving the expert views from eight tourism
companies in the role of representative business units from different tourism sectors (a seaside resort and
hotels, a city hotel, a tour operator and a trave l agency, a regional museum and an airport managing
company). For the empirical part of the study, data were collected during the period October -December
2025 in alignment with a hybrid survey tool combining the characteristics of structured Likert -scale
multiple-choice questions and additional open-ended questions in the form of unstructured interviews. The
results from the empirical research indicate the presence of certain sectoral differences across the surveyed
business units: companies with intensive use of infrastructural elements (the hotels and the tour operator)
report for their higher engagement in particular green practices like waste, energy, water management and
plastics reduction, whereas the travel intermediaries and the cultural institution primarily express their
preferences in the field of green initiatives in customer relations. For most of the interviewed experts the
explicit levels of high implementation costs, administrative burden and limited public incentives are
perceived as major barriers for transition of their companies towards a holistic green orientation. On the
other hand, respondents generally support stricter EU rules against misleading corporate green assertions
by taking into account the existent skepticism among tourists to wards green marketing communication.
Despite the revealed evidences of limited companies’ endorsements of misleading green assertions, it could
be suggested from the obtained results an existing evidence of a contextually determined tendency for the
implementation of low -budget green marketing tactics among certain market participants, as well as an
emerging trend towards cautious disclosure of sustainability-related actions (green hushing) in the face of
anticipated new regulatory scrutiny. Overall in this study are outlined the ethical boundaries of companies’
green assertions in Bulgarian tourism industry shaped under the joint influence of regulatory pressures,
market dynamics and communication risks.

Keywords: greenwashing, tourism, sustainable communication, regulatory framework, Bulgaria.

Introduction
Modern tourists are increasingly aware of the importance of their personal choices related to
lifestyle, vacations as well as the characteristics and impact of the tourism services they consume.
The rapid development of tourism consumption is gradually changing customers’ expectations and
attitudes, impacting both tourists and tourism companies in different ways. Many of these
companies are committed to develop sustainable tourism product s through green and

conflict with the actual behavior of some tourism companies which falsely, excessively, or
inaccurately present themselves  as environmentally friendly. At the same time, these companies
continue to use resources intensively without imposing restrictions on waste generation,
greenhouse gas emissions or environmental pollution as a whole. These practices are associated
with greenwashing strategies in which organizations declare that they are sufficiently sustainable
without actually minimizing or controlling their impact on the environment and tourism resources.
The greenwashing practices can be determined as misleading marketin g communication tactics
designed predominantly to gain trust of customers, who choose to support with consumption those
organizations which are dedicated to climate adaptation and conservation of nature.
The main reason for the wide spread of greenwashing  practices in the tourism industry is
the current change in consumer behavior. Numerous studies suggest  that younger generations
(particularly Gen Z) are more likely to purchase products and services which are communicated by
companies as ethical, sustaina ble and socially responsible. The  Nielsen report (2015) on global
corporate sustainability indicates that 66% of consumers are willing to spend more on product s
from sustainable brands and the figure even rises to 73% among millennials. The cited data suggest
that businesses, including those in the tourism sector, are increasingly encouraged to adopt socially
and environmentally responsible practices in response of changing consumer behavior.
The term “greenwashing” r efers to “the act or practice of  making a product, policy  or
activity appear to be more environmentally friendly or less environmentally damaging than it really
is” (Merriam-Webster, 2026). On common basis, greenwashing occurs when companies emphasize
or exaggerate their sustainability efforts instead of implementing meaningful actions to reduce their
environmental impact. Such practices can lead to  uncertainty and confusion regarding th e
authenticity of corporate  environmental commitments, highlighting the need for greater
transparency in green marketing communication (Szabo & Webster, 2021). Furthermore, Álvarez
and Sureda (2023) argue that greenwashing rarely reflects real environmental responsibility of
organizations. Rather, it  is often driven by economic motives and supported through vague
assertions for sustainability and misleading promotional messages. In this way, companies attempt
to benefit from the growing consumer demand for sustainable products and services not only in the
travel and tourism industry but also across many other economic sectors.
Due to the relevance of sustainable tourism development, the potential for consumer
deception has become significant over the years. A study of the European Commission (2024)
confirms these concerns, revealing that 53% of “green” claims are vague, misleading, or unfounded
and that 40% are entirely unsubstantiated. There is a large number of sustainable ecolabels in the
EU (230 voluntary ecolabels, 901 labeling schemes in the food area, 100 private green energy
labels), with very different levels of transparency. Almost half of them offer little or no verification,
and only 35% require specific data to demonstrate compliance with the criteria for sustainability
(European Commission, 2022). Greenwashing hinders consumers from making informed and
conscious choices and prevent truly sustainable companies fro m distinguishing themselves and
gaining a competitive advantages. Ultimately, greenwashing undermines trust in the
implementation of sustainable development in the tourism industry, increasing skepticism in both
companies and customers. The circumstances resented above determine the relevance of the topic
as well as the authors’ interest in revealing the green and sustainable claims made by tourism
companies. In this regard, the subject of this research corresponds to the main dimensi ons of
greenwashing. The object of the study is the green and environmentally oriented initiatives of
Bulgarian tourism companies, which currently operate in a context of rapid regulatory change and
increasing sustainability -related obligations. In general , these companies may place too much

Literature review
Greenwashing in hospitality in tourism industry
Nevertheless, that research on greenwashing in hospitality and tourism remains relatively limited,
the understanding of this phenomenon i s becoming increasingly important for the sustainable
development of the industry. Existing studies commonly describe greenwashing as a discrepancy
between companies’ actual environmental performance and the environmental image or messages
they communicate to the public (Wu et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2020; Du, 2015). A large share of the
scientific literature also focuses  on the influence of greenwashing on tourists’ attitudes and
behavior (Rahman et al., 2015; Phan et al., 2024). From the customers’ point of view, greenwashing
is commonly  perceived as a gap between what companies claim about their  environmental
responsibility and what they actually do in practice.
Greenwashing has also been widely discussed in relation to corporate social responsibility
(CSR). In this relation, Papagiannakis et al. (2024) examined 253 large tourism companies between
2004 and 2022. Using objective data from specialized gl obal databases such as Refinitiv and
Worldscope, the cited authors compared publicly communicated sustainability commitments with
the companies’ actual investments and actions. It is interesting that their findings do not support the
assumption that large tourism companies systematically engage in greenwashing. On the contrary,
many organizations appear to undertake more substantial sustainability actions than they publicly
communicate, a phenomenon described as “green hushing”. The commented study also revealed
notable differences across tourism subsectors. Hotel and restaurant companies demonstrated stronger
engagement in direct sustainability initiatives, while entertainment and leisure organizations showed
lower levels of involvement. Tour operator s, on the other hand  were found to play a distinctive
intermediary role within the tourism industry. Acting as professional “gatekeepers”, they tend to view
CSR as an important condition for building and maintaining relationships with suppliers. As a result,
they carefully assess whether suppliers’ sustainability claims correspond to their actual practices and
may be penalized in cases where communication exceeds real environmental performance. Such
inconsistencies are commonly interpreted as signs of greenwashing and weak organizational
integrity. According to the study, companies associated with misleading “green” practices may also
experience substantial declines in sales and consumer trust.
The growing emphasis on the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) in tourism, together
with the increasing number of environmentally conscious consumers, has encouraged tourism
businesses to communicate sustainability -related m essages more actively. At the same time,
implementing real “green” initiatives often requires significant investments in areas such as waste
management and  recycling, environmental restoration, energy efficiency and water resour ce
conservation. Limited financial and human resources  may therefore encourage some companies ,
particularly in the hospitality sector, to overstate or misrepresent their sustainability commitments
as a way to meet market expectations (Makeed & Kim, 2023). Suleman et al. (2025) also highlight
the availability of inconsistencies between sustainability awareness and the actual implementation

Greenwashing practices in the hospitality and tourism industry
The term “greenwashing” was first introduced in 1986 by the American ecologist and biologist Jay
Westerveld in an essay criticizing  the hypocrisy  behind the popular “save the towel”  hotel
initiatives. The practice had originally appeared several years earlier at a hotel on the island of Fiji.
In his  publication “It All Comes Out in the Greenwash”, Westerveld  argued that the hotel
management exaggerated the environmental benefits  to create a pos itive environmental image.
Data shows that the hotel in question was producing a considerable amount of waste, while there
was no evidence of real sustainability efforts. The researcher concludes that the “save towel”
message was launched primarily to save on laundry costs rather than to protect the environment.
The term “greenwashing” refers to unethical practices were organizations invest time and financial
resources to present themselves to customers and partners as sufficiently sustainable without
minimizing activities and practices that harm the environment. TerraChoice (2010), an
environmental marketing company, cl assifies the most commonly used techniques of misleading
sustainability claims as the “ seven sins” of greenwashing: hidden trade -offs; lack of proof;
vagueness; irrelevance; false labels; the lesser of two evils; fibbing.
Some of the most notable examples of greenwashing in the travel and tourism industry
involve airline advertising campaigns. For  instance, Ryanair’s claim to be the “ lowest-emitting”
airline was banned in the UK in 2020 due to insufficient evidence. This case illustrates misleading
environmental claims that were not supported by verifiable evidence and were therefore considered
inconsistent with any regulatory standards. Similarly , Delta Air Lines faced legal action in the
United Stated in 2023 following allegations that its carbon neutralit y claims were overstated and
insufficiently proved.
Leading cruise companies, such as Carnival and MSC, are investing heavily in ships
powered by liquefied natural gas (LNG). This allows them to advertise their ships as “green” and
“clean”. However, the main problem with LNG is the “methane slip”. Methane is a greenhouse gas
with a substantially higher global warming potential than CO2. Presenting LNG-powered ships as
“eco-friendly” is frequently cited in the literature as an example of the “lesser of two evils”
approach to environmental communication. Similarly, The TUI Group emphasizes eliminating
plastics straws and promoting recycling, which shift the focus towards marginal environmental
measures while the core carbon-intensive activities of the company remain largely unchanged.
Many hotels and other accommodations use logos featuring green leaves and globes with
the inscription “Eco-Friendly Certified”. The problem is that these “certificates” are not issued by
independent and officially recognized authorities such as the EU Ecolabel, Travel life  or Green

In 2024, Dutch regulators required Booking.com to remove the “green leaf” symbol from
its “Travel Sustainable” program. The symbol was used to designate hotels as sustainable, but the
program lacked clearly defined and verifiable criteria which created a risk of misleading consumers
about the establishments’ actual sustainability performance.
In summary, the above stated cases refer to some of the major tourism companies that
provide basic and complex tourism services and at the same time are market leaders in the specific
tourist sec tor. Notably, the tourism sector lacks a universally recognized and independent
certification system capable of consistently and objectively assessing sustainability claims across
different subse ctors. Ethical issues related to  greenwashing include mislead ing of consumers,
diversion of  people attention from critical proble ms, creation of unfair competition and
undermining trust in the tourism sector and environmentally friendly business efforts. A unified
certification system is needed to guarantee the use of certain terms under clear and indisputable
conditions. As Kundi and Ernszt (2024) noted, the certification criteria should be grouped
according to specific sectors within the travel and tourism industry. Taken together, these examples
illustrate that greenwashing in tourism most often emerge through selective disclosure, symbolic
sustainability actions and ambiguous environmental cla ims rather than through direct  false
statements. This pattern highlights the structural nature of greenwashing in the trave l and tourism
industry and underscores the need for clear standards, independent verification and strict regulatory
control.

Regulatory framework of greenwashing
Preventing and deterring deceptive green marketing undoubtedly requires an appropriate
regulatory framework that defines precise rules and criter ia. From a global perspective, t he UN
Tourism proposal for reliable, standardized indicators to measure sustainability in tourism is
noteworthy (World Tourism Organization, 2023). The ES G Framework for Tourism Businesses
project provides a sector -specific approach to the implementation and consistent  reporting of
sustainability indicators, encompassing  their economic, environmental and social aspects. The
WTTC’s reports and analyses also provide businesses with guidance on measuring and managing
their environmental impact. However, there are warnings about eroding trust in tourism companies
due to greenwashing. T he Net Zero Roadmap for Travel & Tourism (World Travel & Tourism
Council, 2024) is a significant document that emphasizes the importance of transparent targets and
data verification to mitigate the risk of deceptive “green” practices. In general, leading international
organizations recognize the need for standards and criteria to reliably and transparently measure
sustainability. These institutions also acknowledge the fragmented nature of reporting, limited data
comparability, and the risk of greenwashing. Nevertheless, they avoid using the term
“greenwashing” directly and do not publicly discuss the phenomenon.
In the European context, the primary regulation act is the Empowering Consumers for the
Green Transition Directive (European Union, 2024). The document establishes new regulations to
protect consumers from deceptive sustainability claims. The ECGT prohibits the use of claims such
as “eco”, “environmental ly friendly”, “climate neutral”, “biodegradable” and “offsetting” unless

they are supported by accurate, real and verifiable evidence. According to the Directive, commonly

authority or organization. These strict anti -greenwashing rules will come into action throughout
the EU, including Bulgaria from September 2026. In 2023, the EC proposed a Green Claims
Directive (European Commission, 2023). However, this Directive has been revised in order to be
excluded from its scope the microenterprises on the territory of the Union. The goal is to reduce
the administrative and bureaucratic burden s. These European regulations direc tly impact the
marketing activities and claims of tourism companies, which must be enough clear and verifiable.
The use of sustainability-related terms must be based on transparent data verification and accurate,
unambiguous information. Without  transparent data verification, such claims may be considered
as a kind of greenwashing. The demand  for evidence and transparency  is growing, and these
regulations pressure tourism companies to fulfill their sustainability promises.
At the national level, Bulgarian legislation does not explicitly address the possible existence
of pseudo- ecological practices. However, it  provides regulatory norms a gainst misleading
information, green claims and unfair commercial practices. The primary  regulatory document is
the Consumer Protection Act (2006), which establishes requirements for information disseminated
by traders. According to this legal act, information about goods and services must be accur ate,
comprehensive, clear, and understandable. The law specifies the main types of misleading
commercial practices. In the context of the ecological transition, the Consumer Protection
Commission, a specialized state authority  is empowered to ensure compli ance with consumers’
and tourists’ rights regarding misleading claims a bout the eco  advantages of goods and services
(Council of the European Union, 2025). Considering the transposed EU rules and the applicable
direct European regulation in Bulgaria, the r equirements for tourism businesses have increased,
aiming to justify their stated eco-claims. The Bulgarian institutions that are authorized to exercise
such control have the ability to sanc tion misleading “green” claims. Overall, the literature frames
greenwashing in tourism as a multidimensional phenomenon shaped by the interplay between
consumer perceptions, corporate sustainability communications  and regulatory frameworks.
However, the existing research studies remain fragmented and offer  limited insight into the way
these dynamics interact within a specific national context. The present study addresses this gap by
focusing on the Bulgarian tourism sector.

Methodology
As a result of the literature review it is evident that the nature of greenwashing is multidimensional
and contextual in its qualitative characteristics. For that reason , a qualitative case study approach
was preferred as particularly relevant for capturing specific stakeholder perceptions and ethical
notions. In this study, the authors adopted an embedded single-case study design according to Yin
(2018) in order to examine the levels of understanding and handling of current and potential
greenwashing activities across different market participants of the Bulgarian tourism industry. This
methodological design is considered as an appropriate research tool for the analysis of different
business units viewed as internal interrelated subjects within a broader nati onal tourism context.
At the same time the embedded single-case study design is suitable for the provision of analytical
depth of the research. The authors suggest that the current study is one of the first used case-based
survey of expert attitudes towards the greenwashing status in Bulgarian tourism. The internal units
of the survey include tourism businesses from the following areas: an autonomous seaside resort;
city and holiday hotels; a tour operator of mass recreational tourism; a travel intermediar y; a
regional museum; and an airport managing company (operator). This set of internal units allows

for the completion of a comparative analysis of the researched characteristics across the involved

impacts on the environment along with specific interactions with customers. In this way of thinking
the current research is methodologically based on the application of a qualitative exploratory design
by which the au thors try to examine companies’ attitudes and practices related to environmental
responsibilities, greenwashing, spin marketing and green hushing evidences. Considering the
ethical sensitivity of these categories, expert interviews were preferred as the main data collection
methods. The interviews were conducted between October and December 2025. The empirical
research was conducted in the following stages:
• Stage I: Conceptual framing and development of research categories. The first stage of
the research consisted of an extensive review of the academic literature on greenwashing, green
marketing ethics, spin marketing techniques, and emerging regulatory framework at EU level.
According to the conducted literature review, five analytical categories were dev ised for the
purpose of the field survey: 1. Environmental impact and green initiatives of companies; 2.
Companies’ attitudes towards regulatory frameworks; 3. Ethical flexibility of companies and their
temptations for greenwashing; 4. Use of spin marketing techniques of companies; 5. Detection of
green hushing evidences. The first four categories are related to the different dimensions of green
washing, awhile the fifth one is focused on its opposite phenomenon – green hushing.
• Stage II: Design of the survey tool. A semi-structured interview as a questionnaire form
was devised in relevance to the predefined research categories. The implemented i nstrument
combines a five-point Likert scale (with 3 indicating a neutral position) along with opportunities
for the provision of open-ended responses by the experts. By using this approach, the authors have
the possibility to adjust the received statements and numerical assessments of the different
respondents. At the same time, it is possible the shared opinions of respondents to be contextually
interpreted in relevance to the researched topic.
• Stage III: Selection of respondents. The empirical data were obtained by intentional
choosing of particular experts (senior managers) from key tourism sectors of hospitali ty industry,
tour operation business, cultural tourism and air transport. The interviewed experts were
representatives of hotel and restaurant businesses, passenger transport management, museum
management, tour operator and travel agency activities. Accord ing to this intended selection of
respondents the focus was put on economically significant tourism organizations operating in
Bulgaria. The limited number of respondents is consistent with the exploratory and case -based
nature of the current study, where the qualitative expert assessments are preferred as desired
outcomes instead of statistically representative data.
• Stage IV: Data collection. Eight expert interviews were conducted farce-to-face using a
two stage procedures. First, respondents provided their multiple -choice answers by giving
numerical assessments on the sequence of the posed questions from the questionnaire form.
Second, for additional clarification and explanation of respondents’ assessments follow -up
questions were used in an open conver sation manner about discussed sustainability and ethical
issues.
• Stage V:  Data summarizing and evaluation procedure. Following the process of
gathering data, the responses were summarized according to the five research categories and
comparatively evaluat ed across the involved companies and business sectors. The obtained
outcomes were subsequently discussed in the context of the existing theoretical concepts and
regulatory developments.

The current study differs from the dominant methodological a pproaches in the existing
literature both in terms of research design and ana lytical perspective . Previous studies on
greenwashing in tourism have mainly relied on systematic and scoping reviews (Onur et al., 2026;
Suleman et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2020), as well as quantitative empirical methods such as surveys
(Du, 2015; Elshaer et al., 2025) , quasi -experiments, and analyses of secondary company data
(Papagiannakis et al ., 2024). In contrast, this study adopts  a qualitative embedded  single-case
approach in order to provide a de ep and context -sensitive understanding of managerial attitudes
and ethical interpretations related to greenwashing. Unlike previous studies that typically analyze
the phenomenon from the perspective of different actors such as  consumers, employees , or
companies, the present  study brings together different business units wi thin a national tourism
system. This allows  for comparisons across interconnected tourism subsectors and reveals how
perceptions and communicat ion practices m ay differ within  the same institutional and market
environment. Furthermore, while quantitatively oriented studies often emphasize measurable
behavioral outcomes and market impacts, this research fo cuses on the ethical flexibility,
perceptions and communic ation strategies associated with greenwashing  in a specific national
context. Through its comparative and contextually grounded design, the  study responds to the
growing appeals in the scientific literature for more integrated research approaches which consider
the national features , the evolving regulatory dynamics  and the interactions among different
tourism subsectors.

Results and discussions
The assessments presented in this section are interpretative  and comparative in nature within the
framework of the qualitative case study design  in use. Therefore  for the purpose of the current
survey there is no application of statistical generalization beyond the specific context of the tourism
businesses and experts involved in the  study. Through the first group  of the research, a kind of
snapshots is taken about the current impact of tourism business on the environment and the
implementation of green initiatives (Table 1, 2 and 3). The green efforts of companies were
measured in the context of their relationships with customers.
Group I. Assessment of the company’s business effects on the environment and
challenges for the implementation of “green initiatives”.
Companies from the hotel sector outline their most significant green achievements in the
fields of waste and energy management systems, the reduction of use of plastics, food waste
management and recycling. Similar characteristics share the representative of the airport sector.
The tour operator, the travel agent and the museums respondent still do not give high priority for

The representatives from the seaside resort and the city hotel share opinions for high

strength and direction (Table 2).
Most of the interviewed experts (expert for the hotel chain) find out that high
implementation cost and increased administrative burden are major impediments for the
development of environmentally friendly practices in their business activities. The next significant
difficulty which they point out in question stems from the lack of support and incentives from
public authorities. Only the airport operator shows some levels of indifference about receiving
support from public authorities.

Conclusion
The phenomenon of greenwashing and the attitudes of Bulgarian tourism companies toward similar
practices represent a complex and multifaceted problem. This is confirmed by the ethical  aspects
analyzed in this study, which employs an exploratory qualitative research design. The focus is
placed on  identification of  motivational factors, perceptions, and contextual drivers of
greenwashing-related behavior rather than on reporting explicit cases. The results from the research
do not indicate the existence of any common, systematic or intentional greenwashing practices in

use by tourism organizations. On the other hand, it’s evident from the research that certain  types
of “green ethics” of companies combined with specific marketing communication strategies are
used in the Bulgarian tourism sector which looks as a premise for a superficial green marketing in
use. Following this in mind Bulgarian tourism enterprises  and more particularly those which are
focused on sustainability approach are exposed to additional regulatory and reputational risks. This
in turn impacts the marketing communication strategies of companies and their willingness to
publicly disclose environmental practices. The current research can therefore be regarded as a
pioneer attempt for the reveal of existent greenwashing activities and contextual environment in
the Bulgarian tourism industry. As a conclusion, it could be stated that the existence of a
considerable market pressure and outstanding price sensitivity among tourists as customers indicate
the diminishing willingness of companies to undertake further investments in sustainability. At the
same time, the increase of monitoring and regulatory control of public institutions would lead to
more conserved marketing communication of companies which in turn is expected to additionally
reinforce tendencies towards green hushing. According to the findings, from a policy perspective
in the Bulgarian tourism context, regulatory efforts should be elaborated to curb misleading green
claims complemented by apt incentives and clear communication guidelines. These measures are
indispensable for the stimulation of real investment in sustainability along with activities for
diminishing the risks of green washing and green hushing.
The present study has several research limitation s. As a qualitative exploratory study
involving a limited number of expert respondents , its findings should not be generalized to the
entire tourism sector in Bulgaria. Furthermore, the assessment relies on self -reported managerial
perceptions, which may be affected by subjectivity  particularly in re lation to ethically sensitive
issues such as pseudo-ecological practices, greenwashing and spin marketing. To partially address
this limitation, terms directly related to greenwashing and spin marketing were intentionally
omitted in the questionnaires and the following interviews. Moreover, the analysis highlights some
specific sectoral characteristics of tourism and does not aim to provide a comprehensive cross -
industry comparison. Future research studies could be based on the current scientific concept
revealed here in several important directions by : expanding the research sample considering its
type and size ; integrating quantitative data from tourism businesses and tourists as customers;
analyzing the impact of evolving EU sustainability regulations on  corporate communication
strategies.
Further research could also focus on the targeted processes of monitoring and analysis of
the affect of EU sustainability regulations over corporate communication strategies in the tourism
sector in the long run. Particular attention should be paid to the dynamics and interaction between
greenwashing practices and real  sustainability efforts. Research  studies could be additionally
focused on the survey of specific characteristics and behavioral patterns associated with the highly
competitive environment in the hospitality and tourism industry. In this context, it is important to
evaluate how regulations, including the risk of litigation, may stimulate more realistic and
responsible actions to promote the balanced development of the tourism industry in Bulgaria.
Additionally, scientific researches could be dedicated to the examination of the barriers and threats
due to greenwashing practices which in turn can influence the achievement of  the global
Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).
