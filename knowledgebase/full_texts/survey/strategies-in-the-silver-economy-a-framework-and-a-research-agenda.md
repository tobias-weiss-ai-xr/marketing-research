The world’s population is aging at an unprecedented rate,
giving rise to the “silver economy,” defined as the sum of
economic activities by consumers over the age of 50 (though
some classifications say 60) years. More than 16% of the
world’s population is expected to be older than 60 years as
of 2030 (WHO, 2024), and in some regions of Asia, even
higher percentages already have been reached. These sta -
tistics reflect a transformative shift, as the global consumer
base is growing considerably older.
Therefore, research into their consumption behaviors is
timely and critical, especially considering the immense eco-
nomic power of older adults. Seniors (whom we define here

that other scholars might define it differently) are among
the wealthiest age cohorts globally, particularly in devel -

tial accumulated assets and pensions. In the United States
for example, the 30% of the population who are 55 years or
older accounts for approximately 73% of all U.S. household
wealth (Reed, 2025). Despite such consumer clout, persistent

Abstract
The world’s population is aging rapidly, creating profound implications for businesses operating in the emerging “silver
economy,” which encompasses economic activities of consumers over 50 years of age. Although older adults hold signifi -
cant wealth and purchasing power, they are often underserved by marketing strategies that rely on stereotypes or overem -
phasize youth segments. This paper highlights the need for more nuanced, culturally informed strategies and approaches
to engaging senior consumers. We argue for gerontographic segmentation—based on needs and socioeconomic charac -
teristics rather than chronological age—and propose the 5As of silver marketing: Awareness, Accessibility, Affordability,
Affect, and Advocacy. These principles provide a framework for designing inclusive products, communications, and ser -
vices that resonate with diverse senior populations across global markets. We also explore cross-cultural distinctions, such
as wellness-oriented appeals in Western countries, family-centered approaches in China, and safety-focused marketing in
Japan. In turn, this paper sets out a research agenda to integrate insights from gerontology, psychology, and cross-cultural
studies into marketing theory. For practitioners, we emphasize opportunities to create value through age-friendly innova -
tions, universal design, and authentic engagement. Ultimately, we encourage both academics and managers to recognize
the silver economy as a driver of growth and innovation, rather than as a challenge to be managed. We hope the research
agenda that we have provided will help stimulate a better understanding of the Silver Segment.

Serving the silver economy
In one sense, the silver economy creates different demands
from other younger segments, and aging consumers often
face common, consistent challenges. Yet the dynamism of
this market also requires nuanced segmentation approaches.
In another sense, understanding and serving the silver econ-
omy requires a truly global approach that acknowledges the
diverse needs of different nations’ aging populations. There-
fore, in the next two subsections, we address both broad
consumer needs and distinctions across cultures (United
States, Europe, China, Japan, and Korea) to highlight some
critical adaptations required for businesses to serve mem -
bers of the silver economy, effectively and profitably.
Consumer needs in the silver economy
As people transition into new life stages, such as empty-
nesters or post-retirement, the patterns by which they allo -
cate their time and money shift significantly. To understand
these shifts, it is crucial to identify their common character-
istics while recognizing that the needs of elderly populations
are diverse and dynamic. Even people of the same age dif -
fer greatly in their physical abilities, cognitive functioning,
and psychological outlook. Relying solely on chronological
age to define consumer profiles is imprecise and misleading,
especially across post-retirement life stages that can span
decades.
Instead, senior consumers’ needs and motivations are
shaped by socioeconomic trends and technological advances.
They are also influenced by being empty nestors or being
retired (and increased loneliness) that further reflects in their
changing needs and consumption patterns. Additionally,
many older adults are active participants in digital interac -
tions, and their value systems and consumption behaviors
reflect dynamic contexts. Considering the importance of
establishing a refined, accurate understanding of their needs,
we propose a gerontographic segmentation approach based
on shared socio-economic characteristics and underlying
needs might be more appropriate. Rather than a segmentation
scheme based on ages, we call for one that is based on stages.
In detail, the needs of older consumers might evolve
through three distinct stages, as summarized in Fig. 1. The
three-stage framework was first popularized in Stein (1998),
which focused on health and physical energy. In contrast,
we define the three stages based on their shifting needs.
The first, labeled as the “go-go years” (Stein, 1998), occurs
when seniors recognize they are entering a new phase of
life but still feel physically and mentally vigorous. During
this period, they enjoy high energy levels and mobility. With
a future-oriented time perspective (Lang & Carstensen,
2002), they tend to view expenditures on time and money as
investments rather than mere consumption. Their demands
are diverse, reflecting basic needs, social needs, and growth
needs for self-improvement. Free from work obligations,
consumers in this stage often are willing to invest in travel,
pursue long-held aspirations, acquire new skills, and build
new relationships.

As aging progresses, older adults enter the second stage,
the “slow-go years.” They remain relatively active and
healthy, both mentally and physically, yet they also begin
to experience a decline in internal and bodily functions,
including reduced motor and sensory abilities (Weiner et al.,
2012), cognitive and psychological shifts, a weaker immune
system, and increased skin sensitivity. These challenges lead
to a shift in their time perspective too, orienting consumers
more toward present emotional fulfillment and life satisfac-
tion rather than future goals. Consequently, their consumer
needs lean less toward self-improvement and more toward
basic and social needs.
Once people reach the third stage, corresponding to
the “no-go years” (Stein, 1998), their needs change again.
Because their mental and physical functions have further
declined, their family roles tend to transition from care -
givers to care recipients, and their social circles narrow to
immediate family or primary caretakers. In terms of con -
sumption, their needs become focused on meeting necessi -
ties and maintaining a dignified life.
By defining consumer needs in the silver economy
according to stages instead of ages, marketing practitioners
can uncover new opportunities and assess market potential
more precisely, as well as design more effective targeting
for aging populations. By mapping the evolution of con -
sumer needs across gerontographic segments, researchers
and practitioners can take a more structured approach to
studying and accommodating consumers’ economic behav-
iors in later life.
Cultural distinctions
Global aging is a shared phenomenon, but cultural attitudes
toward aging and older consumers vary greatly. Western
countries currently have large Baby Boomer populations,
most of whom have reached retirement age. In Europe,
these “Boomers” are often equally or even more finan -
cially secure than younger generations. Smart businesses
in Europe regard the silver economy as a key opportunity
for growth. The EU has defined the silver economy broadly
(consumers 50 years and older) and encouraged innovation
to serve this group (Kaczmarczyk, 2024), prompting Euro-
pean firms to develop age-friendly products in diverse sec -
tors, such as tourism and food.
In Western, developed markets, many seniors seek to
maintain active, independent lifestyles, so marketing has
tended to tap into themes of wellness, lifelong learning,
and adventure, as demonstrated by the popularity of senior
travel offerings and television series like The Golden Bach-
elor. Other companies have started to address some previ -
ously neglected needs, such as fashion brands adding more
stylish apparel lines for older women or tech companies
introducing devices with senior accessibility modes. Les -
sons derived from Western marketing that depicts aging in a
positive light (e.g., celebrating “active agers”) highlight the
importance of avoiding age-related biases.
East Asia includes some of the most rapidly aging pop -
ulations in the world, each with unique cultural contexts.
Japan is the frontrunner, and it has been grappling with
its aging society for decades. Japanese businesses accord -
ingly are at the forefront of efforts to normalize age-friendly
practices, including “barrier-free” living and public space
designs (e.g., step-free entrances, elevators in subway sta -
tions). Japanese product developers pioneered designs for
mobile devices with big buttons and high-contrast screens.
However, Japanese seniors tend to exhibit relatively modest
spending, partly due to a persistent cultural norm that priori-
tizes saving for emergencies and consumer preferences for
reliable products and services. Marketing in Japan’s silver
economy often emphasizes safety, trust, and usefulness over
flashiness.
Like Japan, South Korea has become a super-aged soci -
ety; it is projected to host one of the oldest populations in
the world by 2050 (Seo & Lau, 2024), prompting both firms
and government agencies, often in partnership, to regard the
senior population as inspiration for innovation in diverse
sectors (e.g., caregiving, smart home systems, services).
The AI-enabled Hyodol companion robot offers daily con -
versation, emotional support, medication reminders, and
emergency alerts; local governments have distributed more
than 10,000 of these units through public welfare programs
(The Korea Times, 2025). Samsung offers AI-integrated
home appliances, which it markets to younger caregivers
to remotely monitor or control devices in their older rela -
tives’ homes, to mitigate risks such as unattended cooktops
(The Korea Times, 2024). SK Telecom’s “Smart Guardian”
instead functions outside the home, providing mobile, real-
time location tracking, geofencing alerts, and one-touch
emergency assistance, in support of dementia patients and
older adults who require continuous but nonintrusive super-
vision (SK Telecom, 2020).
In China, it is the sheer scale of the aging trend that
draws attention. The country will soon have more seniors
than the entire populations living on other continents. In the
next 10 years, the largest generational cohort in China (born
between 1963 and 1975) will enter their 60s, expanding the
senior population by approximately 30 million people annu-
ally. Traditional Chinese culture, influenced by Confucian
values, establishes strong filial piety norms. Therefore, as
parents age, their adult children take on a dual role as care -
givers and key influencers of their purchase decisions. This
factor represents an important consideration for marketers:
Appealing to family units and caregivers can be as impor -

The 5As of silver marketing
To address questions about how brands can appeal to and
communicate with consumers in the silver economy, by
tailoring marketing strategies for older, active consumer
segments, a fundamental principle arises: Marketers must
discard outdated age stereotypes and move beyond one-
size-fits-all approaches. Inspired by Philip Kotler’s mod -
els, including a 5 A framework for digital marketing (i.e.,
Awareness, Appeal, Ask, Act, and Advocacy; see Kotler et
al., 2021), we suggest a new 5 A framework for silver mar-
keting. It suggests that marketers should ensure their offer -
ings are raising Awareness of seniors’ needs and feedback,
designed with Accessibility features, promoted with Afford-
ability in mind, capable of evoking Affect, and encouraging
seniors to Advocate for the brand (Fig. 2).

Accessibility through product innovation
A niche market for older adults exists in nearly every
product category. Through accessibility-focused innova -
tions, brands can develop new offerings or adapt existing
ones to meet the needs of senior consumers. Some firms
already have redesigned their offerings with (physical, digi-
tal, or intellectual) senior accessibility in mind. Explicitly
inclusive design and universal design efforts seek to create
products that are easier to use, more ergonomic, and sensi -
tive to age-related needs, such as easy-to-open packaging,
larger-font labels, and intuitive interfaces. Such innovations
might appeal particularly to senior consumers, but they also
promise to improve accessibility (and usability) for all con-
sumers. In addition, some consumer-goods companies have
begun retooling products that initially targeted children to
appeal to adults, including incontinence products and nutri-
tion-rich protein “pouches.”
In addition to consumer goods, health technology inno -
vations targeted at aging populations range from wearable
health monitors and fall-detection devices to telemedicine
platforms and smart-home sensors designed to help older
adults live independently. As the market for senior well -
ness technology expands, these products can create spill -
over benefits for other consumer groups and thereby drive
business growth. A related consideration pertains to how
older adults engage with digital technology. Despite the
persistence of outdated stereotypes, today’s senior consum-
ers actively engage online and through social media, and
they use digital devices more than ever. As older consumers
embrace e-commerce, telehealth, and online services, vast
opportunities arise for providers that can establish digital
marketing channels to reach them.
Even acknowledging such trends though, significant
digital divides persist, making it critical to address the barri-
ers that continue to hinder the full digital inclusion of older
adults, including accessibility (and usability) issues in app
design, limited digital literacy, or trust considerations. Nota-
bly, internet penetration among senior consumers in China
remains relatively low, prompting government and industry
efforts to increase it. These efforts often focus on simplifying
interfaces and streamlining core functions to make technol-
ogy easier to use, as exemplified by WeChat’s “Care Mode.”
When this mode is activated, devices display significantly
larger text and buttons, as well as stronger color contrasts
to enhance visibility (Deng, 2021). In addition, a one-touch
video call function allows users to place calls directly from
the phone’s lock screen, such that they can bypass the mul-
tiple steps required to initiate a conventional call.
A similarly named “Caring Version” of the DiDi ride-hail-
ing app presents a greatly simplified interface and supports
voice inputs. This algorithm also automatically matches
convenience, comfort, or adventure—rather than framing
them around age.
To enhance awareness of these benefits, firms could audit
their current communications and the customer experiences
they provide from the perspective of older consumers. Based
on such analyses, they can undertake retraining of product
teams and marketing teams to avoid ageist assumptions,
ensure older adults are portrayed in authentic and empow -
ering roles in advertising, and diversify media budgets to
include channels favored by seniors (e.g., Facebook among
Baby Boomers, WeChat Video among Chinese seniors).
Several companies are leading by example. Telecommu-
nications and financial services firms have streamlined their
customer processes while maintaining aspirational mes -
saging. Fashion and beauty brands have also taken steps to
include older role models, such as Louis Vuitton’s campaign
featuring the actress Catherine Deneuve when she was in her
70s. These practices align with findings in consumer psy -
chology (Yoon et al., 2009) that emphasize the importance
of genuine emotional connections for increasing awareness
and trust among older consumers. Beyond these communi -
cation elements, firms (and researchers) should consider the
tactical impact of seemingly minor adjustments: increasing
font sizes on websites, simplifying calls to action, and offer-
ing both online and offline service options, for example.
These straightforward changes can significantly improve
awareness, accessibility, and the effectiveness of marketing
efforts aimed at older adults.
Countries and firms are realizing that technology might
be a great driver to help build awareness. However, a pre -
requisite is to reduce barriers to technology (e.g., AI, GenAI,
robotics, smart products) adoption. In response to demands
for education and empowerment, some companies and non-
governmental organizations have undertaken training ini -
tiatives to enhance older adults’ digital skills. An inclusive
digital ecosystem can both improve seniors’ quality of life
and expand companies’ customer bases. Therefore, we call
on marketers to find new ways to leverage technology to
engage older consumers (e.g., voice-operated apps, tailored
social media content) and overcome the challenges of bring-
ing less-connected consumers onboard. Such efforts would
also support public policy goals of promoting active aging
and digital equality (Thompson et al., 2005). The need for
continued education has grown even more pronounced with
the arrival of AI (Davenport et al., 2020) and generative
AI (Grewal et al., 2025), which can make diverse content
readily accessible. If researchers can establish meaningful
insights into which barriers remain most prominent, they
also can develop relevant digital outreach strategies, such
as simplifying user interfaces, using audio- and video-based
multimedia formats, or providing more accessible customer
support.

Even if senior consumers are less inclined to pay a premium
for branding than younger consumers, they still can derive
meaningful emotional and social utility from brand offer -
ings, which creates room for brand-driven profitability.
Social benefits can be very important to seniors as they eval-
uate service encounters (Grougiou & Pettigrew, 2011). For
example, despite the cost, premium small-group travel is
increasingly popular among Japanese and Chinese seniors,
due to the exclusivity, convenience, and the high personal -
ization that these services provide.
The meaning of “affordable” is also heterogenous, both
across and within nations. Firms need to take subsegment
income levels into account when they design and price
their offerings. They could plan products and services for
maximum reach (i.e., most affordable). Alternatively, some
firms’ segmentation efforts might involve identifying the
most valuable subsegments of the Senior Economy, such
as targeting mature consumers who represent top spenders
(Witschi et al., 2023). The spending behaviors exhibited by
such subsegments likely reflect deeper psychological and
economic motives too, such as their desire to bequest wealth
to their heirs or sensed needs to engage in precautionary sav-
ing. Bequest motives, linked to a desire to accumulate and
preserve wealth for heirs (Bernheim et al., 1985), tend to
reduce discretionary spending. Precautionary savings goals
(Lugilde et al., 2019), motivated by consumers’ anticipa -
tion of future expenses related to declining health or sudden
illness, also may underlie older adults’ tendencies to save
more and spend less. Although both motives may manifest
as greater price sensitivity, the reasons that older consumers
become more cautious with their financial resources differ,
so they require distinct and targeted marketing approaches
to highlight why a particular brand offering is affordable, in
the sense that it is worth the cost required.
Affect during consumer journeys
As we have noted, older adults may tend to favor deci -
sions that offer immediate emotional satisfaction and a
sense of connection (Lang & Carstensen, 2002), such that
they often respond more positively to affective advertis -
ing than to rational appeals, regardless of whether the pro -
moted product is hedonic or utilitarian in nature (Drolet et
al., 2007). To be effective, marketing campaigns therefore
must deliver authentic messages that resonate emotionally
with and empower older consumers. Across each touchpoint
in the customer journey, brands must actively listen and
thoughtfully address senior consumers’ needs, because only
through such attentiveness can they earn these consumers’
trust, positive affect, and foster lasting adoption.
We identify some potentially effective communication
practices, such as integrating respectful and inclusive tones
users with a default car type, eliminating the need to review
and choose from among dozens of complex options. 2 Xia-
odu Tiantian, a sub-brand of the Chinese tech giant Baidu,
identified a substantial market opportunity among older
adults who demonstrate strong demand for digital content
like news and music yet lack the ability to navigate digital
apps. In response, Xiaodu developed an innovative smart
screen series, which combines visual displays with voice-
controlled functionality. This hybrid interface solution
achieved remarkable market success, adopted by more than
45 million households nationwide. 3 Such dual-approach
designs resonate particularly powerfully with seniors, due
to their ability to help users gather information through clear
visual presentations, while also enabling them to maintain
consistent operations with effortless voice commands.
AI has a lot of promise (and peril) (Grewal et al., 2024) as
it goes about addressing a host of senior needs, ranging from
combatting loneliness, to assisting with health care (e.g.,
continuous monitoring), assistive care (e.g., robots), and so
on. AI and GenAI have the potential to address a variety of
societal concerns, however we need to be cautious in the
implementation and adoption of the new technologies and
solutions. They would likely need to be personalized to spe-
cific needs of the seniors.
Affordability as a key value proposition
Affordability is a critical value proposition for companies
operating in the silver economy. As people age, their spend-
ing patterns and price sensitivity change significantly. Older
adults tend to prioritize expenditures on essential catego -
ries, such as at-home food and nutrition (Harris & Blisard,
2002), driven by two key factors: the income effect and
the time effect. The income effect is straightforward: After
retirement, consumers’ incomes often decline more than
their expenses do, resulting in heightened income elastic -
ity. The time effect refers to the limited future time perspec-
tive experienced by many older adults, which leads them
to favor emotional satisfaction and short-term goals over
long-term aspirations (Lang & Carstensen, 2002). With a
present-focused outlook, older adults become more cau -
tious about both financial and time investments. Therefore,
businesses in the silver economy should strive to develop
sustainable models centered on affordability and ensure that
products and services remain accessible to those who need
them most.
Affordability does not equate to being cheap though.
Older adults are not necessarily seeking the lowest price;
rather, they value higher returns on the money they spend.

Research and theory
First, we seek to enrich marketing theory related to consumer
aging and segmentation (Boduroglu et al., 2006; Yoon et al.,
2009). By synthesizing data and research across geographic
regions, we can illuminate patterns in older consumers’
behavior, which highlight the need to develop novel theoreti-
cal frameworks. We propose rejecting the status quo focus
on the 18–49-year-old age demographic; to encourage this
shift, we attempt to establish the importance of the expand-
ing, heterogeneous, senior consumer segments. In turn, we
outline clear pathways for relevant research to study them
and the implications for marketing. Among senior popula -
tions, we suggest research that works to identify diverse
subsegments by stages, defined in terms of health, psycho -
graphics, and tech adoption, to support more nuanced seg -
mentation efforts (e.g., gerontographic segmentation). We
hope researchers will heed our call to generate novel insights
pertaining to the customer segments that constitute the silver
economy, moving beyond research conducted only with stu-
dents or members of online panels (Prolific, MTurk), whose
mean and median age levels tend to be younger.
Second, in relation to innovation diffusion and technol -
ogy adoption theory, we note the importance of understand-
ing how older adults adopt new products and technologies.
As firms launch newer AI-based technological solutions,
it is important to keep in mind and develop ethical guide -
lines in providing such care. Classical diffusion models, and
research on new products and services that applies those
models, seldom address older adopters. Cross-generational
considerations, including both barriers and drivers of tech
usage among seniors, can better predict the potentially
unique adoption curves displayed by older users or specify
how personal networks (e.g., family influences, community
caregivers) affect their adoption.
Third, interdisciplinary work can help build a bridge
between gerontology and marketing, connecting insights
from demographic studies, the sociology of aging, and
psychology with core marketing concepts. Academics can
leverage a comprehensive literature synthesis and develop
novel conceptual frameworks for silver economy market -
ing studies. We hope that this paper will stimulate research
inquiries and theoretical developments focused on age-
inclusive marketing strategies (and their impacts on brand
images and adoption).

Consider the example of KaiKai, a tech startup in China
that offers online entertainment education (Zhou et al.,
2024). Noting the importance of building affection and
trust, it has established a reliable, personalized customer
service function that actively checks to ensure that senior
learners receive tailored experiences throughout their learn-
ing journeys. Its key performance indicator is retention rate,
a metric that reflects customers’ satisfaction and emotional
connection with the service. By paying close attention to its
“affection index,” the company has achieved strong prod -
uct–market fit, enabling it to attain profitability within three
years of its founding.
Advocacy through social influence
Advocacy can constitute a powerful marketing advantage
in the silver economy. Older consumers often exhibit strong
brand loyalty and form deep emotional connections with
products and brands (Sung & Yoon, 2024). Compared with
younger consumers, they derive greater utility from familiar
products and social interactions that require less cognitive
effort. Furthermore, older adults may be more susceptible
to social influence, due to their heightened affective empa -
thy and emotional responsiveness (Su et al., 2024). Thus,
they are more likely to trust product recommendations from
acquaintances, which suggests the likely benefits of word-
of-mouth promotions, as well as the potential for building
strong brand loyalty. This effect is amplified among older
adults who work to maintain their extensive social net -
works, actively participate in social clubs, and exert signifi-
cant influence within their families (i.e., multigenerational
word of mouth impact).

Practice and business leadership
We hope that marketers, strategists, and product developers
after reading this paper have a stronger grasp of the mar -
ket potential of the silver economy; we believe we cannot
overstate the need for carefully developed actionable strate-
gies for this segment. Beyond marketing communications,
engaging these consumers requires tailoring existing offer -
ings or creating new ones. Key initiatives might include
developing age-accessible versions of existing products
(e.g., simplified banking app, grocery delivery services that
include assistance with unloading heavy items), as well as
totally new products and services (e.g., small-group travel
options designed with seniors’ interests in mind), particularly
those that address age-related challenges (e.g., in-home care
services, health monitoring devices, AI-enabled health-man-
agement solutions, mobility aids). All these efforts should
prioritize a human-centered design approach, such that they
engage older adults in usability testing and feedback provi -
sion, to support more meaningful product innovations, while
also clearly signaling respect for this demographic.
Furthermore, innovations designed for older customers
often evoke broader market appeal, in line with universal
design standards. For example, when packaging is easier to
