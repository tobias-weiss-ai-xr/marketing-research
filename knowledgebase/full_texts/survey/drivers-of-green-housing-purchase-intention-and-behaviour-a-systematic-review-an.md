Green housing is increasingly viewed as a pathway for climate mitigation,
energy efficiency and sustainable urban living, yet market adoption
depends on whether favourable purchase intentions translate into actual
behaviour. This article reviews studies on  green housing purchase
intention and behaviour published between 2021 and 2025. Guided by
PRISMA 2020, the study combines thematic synthesis and bibliometric
mapping to identify dominant theories, knowledge clusters and
emerging determinants of green hous ing adoption. The review shows
that the theory of planned behaviour remains the main theoretical
foundation, but its explanatory power is limited in high-cost, high-risk and
information-intensive housing markets. Recent studies therefore extend
behavioural models by incorporating perceived value, technology
acceptance, signalling, trust and institutional -market conditions. The
review positions the intention-behaviour gap as a central theoretical and

1. Introduction

Climate change, energy transition and decarbonisation agendas have made
green housing an important concern for governments, real estate developers,
investors and consumers. Green housing promises  less energy use, improved
resource efficiency, healthier indoor environments and reduced environmental
externalities. From a market perspective, however, the presence of technically
superior or environmentally certified housing does not automatically create
demand. Green housing must be perceived by consumers as valuable, credible,
affordable and worth choosing over conventional alternatives.

This issue is particularly important because housing purchase is not an ordinary
consumer decision. It is a high-involvement, high-cost and long-cycle decision
in which buyers must evaluate financial risks, mortgage constraints, location
preferences, devel oper reputation, building quality and future performance.
Consumers may support sustainability in principle, but still hesitate to purchase
green housing when price premiums are high, expected savings are unclear,
green claims appear promotional rather than verifiable, or suitable green homes
are not available in the desired market. The gap between favourable intention
and actual purchasing behaviour is therefore a central problem in green housing
adoption.

Research on green housing and related green consumer behaviour has grown
rapidly, but the evidence remains fragmented. Some studies emphasise attitude,
subjective norms and perceived behavioural control. Others focus on perceived
value, trust, environmental concern, certification, technology acceptance,
policy incentives or market barriers. These streams are valuable, but often
remain theoretically separated. As a result , the literature explains why
consumers may intend to buy green housing more clearly than it explains when,
why and under what conditions they actually buy.

Three gaps motivate the present review. First, previous research has often
examined green purchase intention, green building adoption or sustainable
consumption separately, with less attention to the conversion of intention into
actual green housing purchase behaviour. Second, dominant models such as the
TPB have been widely adopted, but their limitations in high-cost, high-risk and
policy-dependent housing markets have not been critically synthesised. Third,
psychological, value -based, technological, signalling and institutional
perspectives have not been su fficiently integrated into a marketing -oriented
explanation of the green housing consumer journey.

This review addresses four research questions  (RQs). RQ1: What theoretical
perspectives have been used to explain green housing purchase intention and
behaviour during 2021 -2025? RQ2 : What psychological, value -based,
technological, signalling and contextual factors influence green housing
purchase intention and actual purchase behaviour? RQ3 : How do bibliometric
patterns reveal the thematic structure and emerging emphases of this research
field? RQ4 : How can existing evidence be integrated into a conceptual
framework that explains the intention -behaviour gap in green housing
adoption?

The study contributes to the literature in three ways. First, it provides a
systematic and bibliometric synthesis of recent research on green housing
purchase intention and behaviour. Second, the work offers a critical reading of
the main theoretical streams, especially the use and limits of the TPB in real
estate markets. Third, the study proposes an integrated marketing framework
and research propositions that explain how consumer motivation becomes
market behaviour when value, trust, technology readiness and institutional
support align.

2. Theoretical Background

2.1 Theory of Planned Behaviour and Normative Extensions

The TPB remains one of the most widely used theories for explaining purchase
intention. The theory proposes that intention is shaped by attitude, subjective
norms and perceived behavioural control (Ajzen, 1991). In green housing
markets, attitude reflects the evaluations of consumers of the environmental,
financial and lifestyle benefits of green housing. Subjective norm s capture the
influence of family members, peers, professional networks and policy
discourse. Perceived behavioural control reflects whether consumers believe
they have the financial ability, information and market access required to
purchase a green home.

The TPB is useful because it provides a clear motivational structure. However,
the theory is not sufficient on its own for explaining green housing purchase

behaviour. Consumers may have favourable attitudes and strong sustainability
values but still avoid purcha sing because green housing requires substantial
capital, mortgage access, credible information and suitable supply. In this sense,
the TPB is more robust  in explaining intention formation than intention
conversion. This limitation is important for marketing and real estate research
because the managerial challenge is not simply to make consumers like green
housing, but to make green housing feel credible, valuable, financially feasible
and actionable.

Normative extensions enrich the TPB by adding personal norm s, moral
obligations and environmental responsibility. These constructs are relevant
because green housing can be interpreted as both a private household
investment and a pro -environmental act. Yet moral motivation alone rarely
removes affordability constraints or information  asymmetry. For that reason,
the TPB must be integrated with value, technology, signalling and institutional
perspectives.

2.2 Perceived Value, Perceived Quality and Green Purchase Trade-
offs

The perceived value theory explains consumer choice as a trade -off between
perceived benefits and perceived sacrifices (Zeithaml, 1988). Green housing
benefits may include lower energy bills, improved comfort, healthier indoor
environments, reduced environmenta l impact s, reputational benefits and
potential long -term property value. Perceived sacrifices include price
premiums, maintenance uncertainty, technical complexity, longer search time
and doubts about whether promised benefits will materialise.

This value perspective is essential for a marketing -oriented understanding of
green housing. Consumers rarely purchase housing because of environmental
attributes alone. They must be convinced that the green offer provides a
persuasive value proposition. T he green premium becomes acceptable when
buyers see a credible link between higher upfront costs and long -term
functional, financial, emotional and social benefits. Conversely, when the value
proposition is vague, green claims are weakly verified or perfor mance
outcomes are unclear, perceived sacrifice can outweigh environmental
motivation.

2.3 Technology Acceptance for Smart and Energy-efficient Housing
Features

Green housing increasingly includes energy-saving technologies, smart meters,
digital monitoring, renewable -energy interfaces and automated building
systems. Technology acceptance models  (TAMs) and unified theory of
acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) are therefore relevant because they
explain how perceived usefulness  and ease of use, performance  and effort

enabled housing (Davis, 1989; Venkatesh et al., 2003).

In practice, consumers not only evaluate whether a home is green, but also
whether its technologies can be understood , reliable and easy to live with. A
smart energy system may strengthen perceived value when it is presented as
simple, useful and cost -saving. It may weaken adoption if it appears
complicated, expensive to maintain or dependent on ambiguous infrastructures.
Technology acceptance therefore interacts with perceived value and trust rather
than operating as a separate mechanism.

2.4 Signalling Theory, Information Asymmetry and Trust

A persistent difficulty in green housing markets is information asymmetry.
Buyers cannot easily verify energy performance, material quality, indoor
environmental conditions or long -term maintenance requirements before
purchase. The s ignalling theory explains how credible signals reduce
uncertainty when one market actor possesses more information than another
(Spence, 1973). In green housing, such signals include green building
certification, third-party labels, transparent energy perf ormance data, verified
standards, audited materials and developer reputation.

Trust is the marketing bridge between information and intention. Certification
may fail to influence purchase intention if consumers do not trust the certifying
institution or suspect that green claims are promotional. Conversely, transparent
performance data and credible third -party verification can increase perceived
quality, reduce perceived risk and justify willingness to pay. In high -value real
estate transactions, trust is not a peripheral variable; it is a condition for
converting sustainability claims into market confidence.

2.5 Digital Influence and Green Housing Consumer Journey

Housing search has become increasingly platform -mediated. Consumers can
obtain green housing information through developer websites, listing portals,
social media, virtual tours, online reviews and digital advertising. These
channels shape awareness, perceived value and trust before consumers visit a
project or speak with sales staff . Digital communication can clarify green
features, but also increase scepticism when claims are generic, inconsistent or
unsupported by evidence.

From a marketing perspective, digital influence should not be merely treated as
a communication channel. It is part of the decision environment in which
consumers compare alternatives, assess credibility, imagine future use and
interpret price premiums. Well -designed digital information can reduce
perceived complexity; poorly designed information can increase uncertainty.

2.6 Integrative Theoretical Logic

The reviewed theories are complementary rather than competing. The TPB
explains the motivational basis of purchase intention. The p erceived value
theory explains how consumers evaluate benefits and sacrifices. The TAM and
UTAUT explain how smart and energy -efficient features are assessed. The
signalling theory explains how credible information reduces uncertainty and
builds trust. Institutional and market perspectives explain why even motivated
consumers may not act when affordability, financing, regulation and supply
conditions are unfavourable.

An integrated explanation is therefore necessary. Green housing purchase
behaviour emerges when consumers are motivated, perceive a strong value
proposition, understand the technology, trust the information and operate within
enabling market conditions. This is the theoretical logic that informs the review
and proposed framework.

3. Method

3.1 Review Design

This study adopts a systematic review design informed by PRISMA 2020
reporting logic and evidence -informed management review procedures (Page
et al., 2021; Tranfield et al., 2003). The systematic review is complemented by
bibliometric mapping to identify keyword patterns, thematic clusters and
influential knowledge structures. A meta -analysis was not conducted because
the reviewed studies use heterogeneous theories, c onstructs, measurement
scales, contexts and outcome variables. A thematic and bibliometric synthesis
is therefore more appropriate for explaining the conceptual development of the
field.

3.2 Search Strategy and Eligibility Criteria

The search targets peer-reviewed research articles published between 2021 and
2025. The databases include Web of Science, Scopus and Google Scholar. The
search focuses on green housing, green building, sustainable housing, energy -
efficient housing, prefabricated housing and related consumer outcomes,
including purchase intention  and behaviour, adoption intention and consumer
behaviour. Google Scholar is used as a supplementary source to capture relevant
articles that may not have appeared in the initial database exports. The database-
specific search strings used for article retrieval are presented in Table 1.

Studies are included when they meet five criteria: (1) peer -reviewed journal
article; (2) publication year between 2021 and 2025; (3) English -language
publication; (4) direct relevance to green housing  and building, sustainable

3.4 Data Extraction and Synthesis

For each core included study, data are extracted on authorship, year of
publication, country or market context, research design, theory, constructs,
mediators, moderators, dependent variables and key findings. Thematic
synthesis is used to identify recurring explanatory mechanisms. Bibliometric
mapping, supported by VOSviewer, is used to interpret co-occurrence patterns,
thematic concentrations and intellectual linkages (Van Eck and Waltman, 2010;
Donthu et al., 2021). The synthesis then integrate s the narrative and
bibliometric findings into a conceptual framework for green housing purchase
intention and behaviour.

4. Results

4.1 Knowledge Structure and Thematic Clusters

As shown in Figure 2, the review identifies three broad knowledge clusters,
which are summarised in Table 3.  The first cluster concerns behavioural
foundations and research methodology.  This cluster  is anchored in  the TPB,
normative extensions and structural modelling. This cluster shows that the field
has been mainly concerned with explaining intention formation through
attitudes, norms and perceived control. Its limitation is that intention is often
treated as the end point, even though green housing adoption depends on the
conversion of intention into actual purchase behaviour.

The second cluster concerns measurement and modelling quality.  This shows
attention to the construct validity, reliability, common method bias and the use
of structural equation modelling ( SEM) or partial least squares structural
equation modelling (PLS-SEM). This cluster is valuable because green housing
purchase research often relies on latent psychological constructs. However,
stronger designs are still needed to move beyond cross -sectional intention
models and toward longitudinal or behavioural evidence.

The third cluster concerns perceived value, technology acceptance and trust. It
is the most relevant cluster for a marketing interpretation of green housing
because it links consumer motivation to value propositions, willingness to pay,
technology readiness and credible information. This cluster indicates that green
housing should be marketed not only as an environmental product but as a
credible, high-value, low-risk housing solution.

4.4 Interpreting the Intention-behaviour Gap

The central finding of the review is that the intention -behaviour gap in green
housing is not caused by the lack of environmental concern alone. The gap
emerges from the interaction of motivational, financial, informational,
technological and institutional barriers  (Table 4) . A consumer may value
sustainability and intend to purchase green housing, but still fail to act when
price premiums are high, mortgages are difficult to obtain, green housing supply

marketing therefore requ ires credible signals, transparent performance

explanatory problem. The framework integrates five mechanisms that are often
examined separately: psychological -social motivation, value -market
evaluation, technology readiness, information signalling and institutional -
market moderation (Figure 5 and Table 5). These mechanisms jointly determine
whether green housing is perceived as desirable, credible, affordable and
actionable.

6. Discussion and Implications

6.1 Theoretical Implications

The review shows that single -theory explanations are insufficient for
understanding green housing purchase behaviour. The TPB remains useful for
explaining intention formation, but does not fully capture the structural and
market conditions that determine whether intention becomes behaviour. This
finding extends sustainable consumption research by showing that green
housing adoption must be analysed as a high -involvement market decision
rather than a simple pro-environmental choice.

The framework also contributes to the marketing theory by emphasising the role
of value propositions and market signals. In green housing, consumers do not
simply respond to environmental appeals. They evaluate whether the offer is
credible, financially se nsible, technologically usable and supported by
trustworthy institutions. Trust and signalling therefore operate as central
mechanisms in the consumer journey.

6.2 Managerial and Marketing Implications

For developers and real estate marketers, the findings suggest that green
housing communication should move beyond generic sustainability claims.
Consumers need concrete, comprehensible and credible evidence of value.
Marketing messages should translate te chnical green features into consumer -
relevant benefits such as lower operating costs, better indoor comfort, healthier
living, long-term asset value and reduced risk.
Trust-building should be treated as a strategic marketing activity. Developers
can strengthen trust through third -party certification, transparent energy -
performance data, clear explanations of materials and systems, warranties, post-
purchase support and c onsistent reporting. Digital platforms can support this
process by making certification documents, energy-saving projections and user-
friendly comparisons visible to prospective buyers.

416     Phan et al.

The review also highlights the importance of affordability signals. When
consumers perceive the green premium as excessive or uncertain, purchase
intention weakens. Developers and financial partners can reduce perceived
sacrifice by communicating total cos t of ownership, offering green financing
options, and demonstrating how incentives or energy savings offset higher
upfront prices.

6.3 Policy Implications

For policymakers, the findings indicate that consumer motivation alone is
unlikely to deliver large -scale green housing adoption. Policy incentives,
preferential mortgage schemes, subsidies, tax benefits, transparent certification
systems and public performance databases can reduce uncertainty and improve
affordability. Stronger regulation of green claims is also needed to reduce
greenwashing and protect certification credibility.

Policy support should address both demand and supply. Demand-side measures
help consumers to overcome financial and informational barriers. Supply -side
measures encourage developers to produce more certified green housing and
disclose performance data. The intention -behaviour gap narrows when
consumers can access credible, affordable and available green housing options.

6.4 Future research directions

Future research should move beyond cross -sectional intention models.
Longitudinal studies can examine how intention changes during the housing
search and mortgage process. Transaction-based studies can test whether stated
intention predicts actual purchase . Experiments can compare the effects of
different certification labels, energy -performance disclosures, financing
incentives and developer reputation cues on trust, perceived value and
willingness to pay.

Comparative studies are also needed across countries and cities with different
policy regimes, housing affordability conditions and certification systems.
Future research should test the proposed framework by using multi -group
analysis across income levels, homebuyer segments, market maturity and prior
experience with green or smart housing.

7. Conclusion

This article has reviewed research work on green housing purchase intention
and behaviour published between 2021 and 2025. The evidence shows that the
TPB remains the dominant theoretical foundation, but recent research
increasingly integrates perceived value, technology acceptance, signalling, trust
and institutional -market conditions. The main insight is that green housing

By positioning the intention -behaviour gap as the core problem, this review
