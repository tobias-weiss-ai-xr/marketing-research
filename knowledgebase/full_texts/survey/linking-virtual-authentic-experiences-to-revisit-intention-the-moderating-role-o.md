Abstract
Virtual reality (VR) has emerged as a strategic tool for destination marketing; however, the mechanisms through which
VR-based authentic experiences translate into revisit intention remain insufficiently theorized. Drawing upon multidimen -
sional authenticity theory and relationship marketing perspectives, this study develops and empirically tests a structural
model explaining how VR authentic experience influences revisit intention through differentiated authenticity perceptions,
relational orientation, and advocacy mechanisms. Using survey data from 857 respondents and PLS-SEM analysis, the
findings demonstrate that VR authentic experience significantly activates all four authenticity dimensions. Existential
authenticity (β = 0.481) and constructive authenticity (β = 0.479) exhibit nearly identical and strongest effects, followed
by objective authenticity (β = 0.423) and postmodern authenticity (β = 0.317). Results reveal that authenticity perceptions
primarily influence revisit intention indirectly through perceived destination brand relationship orientation (PDBRO),
which emerged as the most powerful direct predictor of revisit intention (β = 0.574). Mediation analysis confirms that
relational orientation constitutes the dominant transmission mechanism, particularly for constructive and objective authen -
ticity, whereas advocacy plays a comparatively weaker role. Furthermore, destination preference selectively moderates
the advocacy-revisit relationship but does not strengthen the relational pathway, indicating that relational embeddedness
operates independently of comparative prioritization. Overall, the findings reposition relational consolidation as the central
mechanism through which VR-based authentic experiences generate future visitation intentions. By integrating authen -
ticity theory with relationship marketing in immersive digital contexts, this study advances theoretical understanding of
VR tourism and provides strategic insights for destination managers seeking to convert virtual engagement into sustained
behavioral commitment.

Authenticity in immersive contexts has been linked to trust

remain theoretically fragmented. Tourism theory has long
distinguished authenticity into multiple forms, including

emotional engagement) (Wang, 1999). Yet, empirical studies
in VR contexts often treat authenticity as a single aggregate
perception or focus narrowly on realism-based authen -
ticity. Consequently, little is known about whether differ -
ent authenticity dimensions exert differentiated effects in
immersive digital environments. It remains unclear whether
behavioral outcomes in VR tourism are driven primarily by
cognitive credibility, socially constructed meaning, experi -
ential pleasure, or self-relevant engagement.
A second unresolved issue concerns the mechanism
through which authenticity perceptions translate into revisit
intention. Much of the existing literature implicitly assumes
a relatively direct pathway between authenticity and behav-
ioral intention. However, tourism behavior research con -
sistently demonstrates that future visitation is embedded
in relational and commitment-based processes rather than
immediate experiential evaluations alone (Ramkissoon,
2015; Kim et al., 2020). Relationship marketing theory
conceptualizes destinations as relational partners capable
of forming enduring psychological bonds with tourists
(Fournier, 1998). In immersive digital contexts, where expe-
riences precede physical travel, relational orientation may
play a crucial role in converting authenticity perceptions
into sustained destination commitment. Nevertheless, rela -
tional constructs have rarely been systematically integrated
into multidimensional authenticity frameworks within VR
tourism research.
In addition, advocacy behaviors have been widely rec -
ognized as expressions of strong destination support and
social endorsement (Chiu et al., 2016). Yet, it remains theo-
retically ambiguous whether advocacy constitutes a pri -
mary transmission mechanism between VR authenticity and
revisit intention or whether relational orientation provides
a more stable psychological bridge. Furthermore, compara-
tive evaluative constructs such as destination preference
may condition how relational and advocacy responses are
translated into revisit intention, particularly in competi -
tive destination choice environments. The boundary role of
destination preference in immersive tourism contexts, how-
ever, remains largely underexplored. Against this backdrop,
the present study seeks to address these theoretical gaps by
integrating multidimensional authenticity theory with des -
tination relationship marketing within a unified structural
framework. Specifically, VR authentic experience is con -
ceptualized as an experiential stimulus that activates objec-
tive, constructive, postmodern, and existential authenticity
perceptions. These differentiated authenticity dimensions
are proposed to influence revisit intention through relational
orientation and advocacy mechanisms, while destination
preference is incorporated as a boundary condition shaping
the strength of these relationships. By disentangling authen-
ticity dimensions and embedding relational mechanisms into
VR tourism research, this study aims to advance theoretical
understanding of how immersive digital experiences gener-
ate sustained behavioral commitment. Rather than viewing
authenticity as a uniform perceptual outcome or assuming
direct effects on intention, this research positions authen -
ticity as a layered psychological construct whose influence
unfolds through relational consolidation processes within
immersive environments.
2 Literature review
2.1 VR authentic experience
Virtual reality (VR) has been increasingly adopted in tour -
ism as an experiential medium that enables potential tourists
to explore destinations before physical visitation. Within this
context, VR authentic experience refers to tourists’ overall
perception that a VR-based tourism experience feels genu -
ine, credible, and meaningful, reflecting a holistic experien-
tial evaluation rather than mere technological realism (Kim
et al., 2020; Atzeni et al., 2022).
Existing research consistently highlights authenticity as
a central determinant of tourists’ evaluations of virtual tour-
ism experiences. Studies grounded in the stimulus-organ -
ism-response (SOR) framework conceptualize VR authentic
experience as a key stimulus that triggers tourists’ internal
affective and cognitive responses, such as enjoyment, emo -
tional involvement, and flow, which subsequently influence
destination-related behavioral intentions (Kim et al., 2020;
Tussyadiah et al., 2018). Rather than functioning as a substi-
tute for real-world travel, VR authentic experience enables
tourists to cognitively and emotionally engage with desti -
nation representations in a pre-consumption stage, thereby
shaping expectations and evaluative judgments (Kim et al.,
2020; Tussyadiah et al., 2018).

2.2 Objective authenticity
Objective authenticity originates from heritage and museum
tourism research and refers to the extent to which tourism
experiences are perceived as genuine, original, and factually
accurate (Wang, 1999). In VR tourism contexts, objective
authenticity has been operationalized as tourists’ percep -
tions of accuracy, realism, and credibility of virtual desti -
nation representations. Prior studies indicate that accurate
visual reconstruction, realistic spatial layouts, and reliable
informational content enhance tourists’ cognitive evalu -
ations and reduce uncertainty during virtual destination
exploration (Kim et al., 2020; Tussyadiah et al., 2018). Such
perceptions are particularly relevant at the pre-visit stage,
where tourists rely on VR experiences to assess destination
feasibility and trustworthiness. However, recent empirical
research suggests that objective authenticity primarily influ-
ences cognitive-based experiential responses rather than
deeper emotional or relational outcomes. While objective
authenticity supports credibility and initial engagement,
it alone does not sufficiently explain sustained destination
attachment or long-term behavioral commitment in immer -
sive digital environments (Atzeni et al., 2022).
2.3 Constructive authenticity
Constructive authenticity conceptualizes authenticity as a
socially constructed perception shaped by tourists’ inter -
pretations, cultural expectations, and mediated representa -
tions rather than inherent properties of destinations (Wang,
1999). From this perspective, authenticity emerges through
narratives, symbols, and contextual framing that influence
how tourists make sense of tourism experiences. VR tour -
ism experiences are particularly conducive to construc -
tive authenticity, as virtual environments are intentionally
designed and curated to convey specific destination mean -
ings. Prior studies demonstrate that tourists often perceive
VR representations as authentic when they align with their
expectations, prior knowledge, and socially shared destina -
tion imagery, even when these representations are selec -
tively constructed (Kim et al., 2020; Jung et al., 2016).
2.4 Postmodern authenticity
VR tourism contexts closely align with postmodern authen-
ticity, as users generally recognize that VR environments are
artificial yet still evaluate them as authentic when they are
engaging and enjoyable. Empirical studies show that tourists
are willing to accept simulated VR experiences as authen -
tic if they provide immersive, entertaining, and emotionally
satisfying experiences (Kim et al., 2020; Tussyadiah et al.,
2018). Recent findings indicate that postmodern authenticity
primarily influences tourists’ affective responses, such as
enjoyment and experiential pleasure, rather than deeper
relational attachment or identity-based outcomes (Wang,
1999; Kim et al., 2020; Zhang & Yin, 2020). Consequently,
postmodern authenticity is commonly associated with short-
term experiential responses and exploratory motivation
within VR tourism experiences.
2.5 Existential authenticity
Existential authenticity shifts the focus of authenticity
from external objects to tourists’ internal subjective states,
emphasizing feelings of self-realization, emotional engage-
ment, and being true to oneself during tourism experiences
(Wang, 1999). Recent tourism studies suggest that immer -
sive VR experiences can evoke existential authenticity by
fostering emotional resonance, involvement, and a sense of
presence. Empirical evidence indicates that when tourists
feel emotionally absorbed and personally engaged in VR
tourism experiences, they are more likely to perceive the
destination as meaningful and relevant to their own iden -
tity (Atzeni et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2020). Moreover, exis-
tential authenticity has been linked to deeper psychological
outcomes, such as emotional bonding and self-destination
connection, which extend beyond immediate experien -
tial evaluation. In VR tourism contexts, this dimension is
increasingly recognized as a key antecedent of relationship-
oriented and identity-based responses toward destinations
(Atzeni et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2023).
2.6 Perceived destination brand relationship
orientation
Perceived destination brand relationship orientation
(PDBRO) refers to tourists’ perceptions of the relational
bond between themselves and a destination brand, reflect -
ing the extent to which a destination is viewed as a relational
partner rather than a mere consumption object. Drawing
from relationship marketing and destination branding litera-
ture, this construct emphasizes tourists’ tendency to form
enduring psychological relationships with destinations
based on emotional connection, perceived compatibility,
and symbolic meaning (Fournier, 1998; Aurier & Séré de
Lanauze 2012; Kumar et al., 2023).
Prior tourism research indicates that authentic and mean-
ingful tourism experiences play a crucial role in fostering
destination–tourist relationships. When tourists perceive
destinations as authentic and personally meaningful, they
are more likely to develop relational orientations character-
ized by trust, emotional attachment, and perceived closeness
(Ramkissoon, 2015; Kumar et al., 2023). Such relationship-
oriented perceptions extend beyond satisfaction-based

2.7 Destination advocacy
Destination advocacy refers to tourists’ willingness to
actively recommend, support, and defend a destination to
others. Unlike general word-of-mouth intention, advocacy
reflects a stronger form of behavioral commitment that
involves proactive endorsement and social support for the
destination (Brown et al., 2005; Chiu et al., 2016; Kumar et
al., 2023). Tourism literature consistently demonstrates that
advocacy behaviors emerge when tourists develop strong
emotional and relational bonds with destinations. Authentic
tourism experiences have been shown to enhance advocacy
by reinforcing positive attitudes, emotional attachment, and
relational identification, motivating tourists to promote des-
tinations within their social networks (Ramkissoon, 2015;
Kumar et al., 2023).
2.8 Revisit intention
Revisit intention refers to tourists’ willingness to visit or
revisit a destination in the future and is widely used as a
key indicator of favorable tourism outcomes and destina -
tion success. Prior research suggests that revisit intention is
influenced by tourists’ experiential evaluations, emotional
responses, and relational perceptions formed during tour -
ism experiences (Kim et al., 2020; Kumar et al., 2023). In
VR tourism contexts, revisit intention is shaped by tourists’
evaluations of virtual experiences that reduce perceived risk
and uncertainty associated with destination choice. Studies
indicate that positive and authentic VR experiences enhance
tourists’ confidence in future visitation decisions by provid-
ing experiential familiarity and emotional reassurance (Tus-
syadiah et al., 2018; Atzeni et al., 2022). Recent literature
further highlights that revisit intention extends beyond satis-
faction-based mechanisms and is increasingly influenced by
relational and identity-based factors. Tourists who develop
relational orientations and advocacy tendencies toward des-
tinations are more likely to express strong revisit intentions,
as these behaviors reflect deeper psychological commitment
to the destination (Kumar et al., 2023; Ramkissoon, 2015).
2.9 Destination preference
Destination preference refers to tourists’ comparative eval -
uation and prioritization of a destination relative to alter -
native destinations within their consideration set. Unlike
revisit intention, which reflects behavioral willingness, des-
tination preference captures tourists’ attitudinal positioning
and comparative judgment of destinations (Mao & Zhang,
2014). Tourism research suggests that destination preference
is shaped by cumulative experiential impressions, emotional
resonance, and perceived relational value. Authentic and
meaningful tourism experiences enhance destination pref -
erence by strengthening perceived uniqueness, relevance,
and emotional appeal (Mao & Zhang, 2014; Ramkissoon,
2015). In VR tourism contexts, recent studies indicate that
authenticity perceptions and relational outcomes contribute
to destination preference formation by enabling tourists to
evaluate destinations in a pre-consumption stage. Empiri -
cal evidence suggests that tourists who develop relational
orientations and advocacy behaviors toward destinations are
more likely to rank those destinations favorably compared
to alternatives (Kumar et al., 2023; Mao & Zhang, 2014).
3 Hypothesis development & the framework
model
3.1 Hypothesis development
Prior studies in virtual tourism consistently indicate that
authenticity perceptions do not emerge independently but are
activated through tourists’ holistic evaluations of VR-based
experiences. Rather than being a direct function of techno -
logical realism, authenticity in VR contexts is shaped by
tourists’ perceptions of experiential meaningfulness, cred -
ibility, and engagement, which are collectively captured by
the construct of VR authentic experience (Kim et al., 2020;
Atzeni et al., 2022). Within the stimulus-organism-response
framework, VR authentic experience functions as an expe -
riential stimulus that triggers differentiated authenticity per-
ceptions by influencing how tourists cognitively interpret,
emotionally respond to, and personally engage with virtual
destination representations. From a cognitive authenticity
perspective, VR authentic experience is expected to posi -
tively affect objective authenticity by enhancing tourists’
perceptions of the accuracy and reliability of destination
representations. Prior research shows that when VR experi-
ences are perceived as credible and meaningful, tourists are
more likely to evaluate virtual destinations as faithful reflec-
tions of their real-world counterparts, particularly in terms
of spatial configuration and informational trustworthiness
(Tussyadiah et al., 2018; Atzeni et al., 2022). In addition,
VR authentic experience facilitates constructive authentic -
ity by supporting tourists’ interpretive processes through
which destination meanings are constructed and validated.
Empirical studies suggest that meaningful VR experiences
enable tourists to align virtual destination narratives with
their existing cultural expectations and socially shared
images, thereby reinforcing perceptions of authenticity as

Beyond cognitive interpretation, VR authentic experi -
ence also influences authenticity perceptions through affec-
tive and experiential mechanisms. Specifically, engaging
and enjoyable VR experiences are likely to enhance post -
modern authenticity, as tourists tend to accept simulated
environments as authentic when they provide pleasurable,
immersive, and experientially satisfying encounters, even
when the artificial nature of the experience is recognized
(Wang, 1999; Kim et al., 2020; Zhang & Yin, 2020). Fur-
thermore, emotionally resonant VR experiences can evoke
existential authenticity by fostering feelings of personal
involvement and self-congruence during the virtual encoun-

facilitate moments of self-reflection and emotional connec -
tion, allowing tourists to experience a sense of being true
to themselves independent of the objective authenticity of
the destination (Wang, 1999; Atzeni et al., 2022). Accord-

foundational antecedent that activates multiple dimensions
of authenticity through distinct cognitive, interpretive, and
affective pathways.

Objective authenticity reflects tourists’ perceptions that
destination representations are accurate, credible, and factu-
ally reliable (Wang, 1999). In mediated tourism contexts,
such perceptions serve as an important cognitive basis for
destination evaluation, particularly by reducing informa -
tional uncertainty during the pre-consumption stage (Tus -
syadiah et al., 2018).
From a relationship formation perspective, objective
authenticity facilitates perceived destination brand relation-
ship orientation by enhancing trust and confidence in the
destination brand. Kim et al. ( 2020) show that authentic
VR experience supports favorable behavioral responses,
while Kumar et al. ( 2023) directly demonstrate that desti -
nation brand authenticity strengthens perceived destination
brand relationship orientation. This relationship-marketing
evidence supports the expectation that credibility-based
authenticity can reinforce trust, legitimacy, and relational
engagement. Accordingly, objective authenticity is expected
to positively affect perceived destination brand relationship
orientation.
Beyond relationship orientation, objective authenticity
may also influence destination advocacy through a con -
fidence-based mechanism. Because advocacy places the
tourist’s judgment in a social arena, credible destination rep-
resentations reduce the risk of recommending an experience
that others may later view as misleading. Positive word-
of-mouth and identification research further indicates that
credibility, identification, and relational attachment encour-
age active endorsement (Ahearne et al., 2005; Brown et al.,
2005; Kumar et al., 2023). In VR tourism, objective authen-
ticity therefore increases tourists’ confidence in destination
representations, strengthening their propensity to engage in
advocacy behaviors.
Based on these arguments, the following hypotheses are
proposed:
H2a: Objective authenticity positively affects perceived
destination brand relationship orientation.
H2b: Objective authenticity positively affects destination
advocacy.
Constructive authenticity conceptualizes authenticity
as a perception that emerges through tourists’ interpreta -
tions, narratives, and socially constructed meanings rather
than objective accuracy alone (Wang, 1999). In VR tour -
ism contexts, destination meanings are primarily conveyed
through curated narratives and visual storytelling, which
shape how tourists interpret and evaluate destinations (Jung
et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2020). From a relationship-oriented
perspective, constructive authenticity facilitates perceived
destination brand relationship orientation by enabling tour -
ists to cognitively and symbolically relate to destinations.
Ramkissoon ( 2015) shows that meaning-based authentic -
ity perceptions enhance psychological closeness and emo -
tional bonds between tourists and destinations; Kumar et
al. (2023) further show that destination brand authenticity
strengthens PDBRO and downstream loyalty outcomes.
These findings indicate that meaning-based authenticity can
enhance symbolic alignment, psychological closeness, and
perceived relevance. Accordingly, constructive authenticity
is expected to positively affect perceived destination brand
relationship orientation.

tination advocacy through a meaning-based social mecha -
nism. When destination meanings are coherent, socially
communicable, and identity-relevant, tourists can endorse
them with greater confidence. Positive word-of-mouth and
destination relationship research indicates that identifica -
tion and relational orientation encourage proactive recom -
mendation and support (Ahearne et al., 2005; Brown et
al., 2005; Kumar et al., 2023). In VR tourism, constructive
authenticity thus strengthens tourists’ propensity to engage

Postmodern authenticity conceptualizes authenticity as
an experiential perception that does not depend on objec -
tive accuracy but instead arises from enjoyment, playful -
ness, and experiential satisfaction, even when tourists are
aware of the simulated or staged nature of the experience
(Wang, 1999). In tourism research, postmodern authenticity
emphasizes the acceptance of “constructed” or “hyperreal”
experiences as authentic when they deliver engaging and
emotionally rewarding encounters.
In the context of VR tourism, postmodern authenticity
has been shown to influence how tourists relate to destina -
tions through experiential and affective mechanisms. Kim
et al. ( 2020) demonstrate that enjoyable and authentic VR
experiences generate affective and behavioral responses,
while Kumar et al. ( 2023) provide destination-level evi -
dence that authenticity supports relationship orientation.
Thus, even when authenticity rests on enjoyable simulation
rather than objective accuracy, positive experiential reso -
nance can still contribute to relational evaluation.
Beyond relationship orientation, postmodern authen -
ticity may also influence destination advocacy through
a hedonic-social mechanism. Advocacy behaviors often
involve sharing enjoyable and novel experiences with oth -
ers, particularly when such experiences are perceived as
entertaining and socially appealing. Research on positive
word-of-mouth shows that novel, enjoyable, and identity-
relevant experiences are more likely to be shared and rec -
ommended (Brown et al., 2005). In destination contexts,
authenticity also supports advocacy through relationship
orientation and identification (Kumar et al., 2023).
Based on these arguments, the following hypotheses are
proposed:
H4a: Postmodern authenticity positively affects per -

H4b: Postmodern authenticity positively affects destina -
tion advocacy.
Existential authenticity refers to tourists’ subjective feel-
ings of self-realization, emotional involvement, and being
true to oneself during tourism experiences, independent of
objective accuracy (Wang, 1999). In immersive tourism
contexts, such authenticity emerges when experiences foster
personal engagement and emotional resonance. In VR tour-
ism, existential authenticity influences destination–tourist
relationships through identity-based mechanisms. Atzeni
et al. ( 2022) show that existential authenticity is behavior -
ally consequential in virtual heritage experiences, while
Kumar et al. ( 2023) link authenticity-based evaluations to
destination relationship orientation. Together, this evidence
suggests that self-relevance and emotional involvement can
foster perceived closeness with the destination. Accord -
ingly, existential authenticity is expected to positively affect
perceived destination brand relationship orientation.
Existential authenticity may also facilitate destina -
tion advocacy as a form of identity expression. Ramkis -
soon ( 2015) links self-related authenticity and attachment
to behavioral intentions, while Kumar et al. ( 2023) show
that authenticity-based relational orientation supports des -
tination advocacy. In VR contexts, existential authenticity
therefore increases tourists’ propensity to engage in destina-
tion advocacy by enabling destinations to serve as vehicles
for self-expression.

Perceived destination brand relationship orientation
reflects tourists’ tendency to view a destination as a rela -

satisfaction-based evaluations. From a relationship-behav -

key driver of revisit intention. Kumar et al. ( 2023) directly
show that PDBRO is associated with revisit intention and
destination advocacy, while Ramkissoon ( 2015) identifies
attachment and place bonding as important precursors of
return-related behavior. In VR tourism, perceived destina -

proximal psychological mechanism that translates authen -

to identification and commitment (Ahearne et al., 2005;

high, relationship-oriented perceptions, such as trust, emo -

3.2 The model framework
Figure 1 presents the proposed conceptual framework
explaining how VR authentic experience shapes tourists’
revisit intentions through multiple authenticity perceptions
and destination-related behavioral mechanisms. The model
positions VR authentic experience as a core experiential
stimulus that activates four distinct dimensions of authen -
ticity: objective, constructive, postmodern, and existen -
tial authenticity, capturing tourists’ cognitive, interpretive,
experiential, and self-related evaluations of virtual tour -
ism encounters. The framework further proposes that these
authenticity dimensions exert differentiated effects on two
key mediating mechanisms: perceived destination brand
relationship orientation (PDBRO) and destination advo -
cacy. Objective and constructive authenticity primarily
operate through cognitive and meaning-based processes,
supporting trust formation and relational orientation toward
the destination. In contrast, postmodern and existential
authenticity emphasize experiential enjoyment and per -
sonal engagement, fostering affective bonds and expressive
advocacy behaviors. These parallel pathways reflect the
multifaceted ways in which authenticity perceptions trans -
late VR-based experiences into relational and behavioral
outcomes. Finally, revisit intention is conceptualized as the
ultimate behavioral outcome, influenced by both relational
orientation (PDBRO) and advocacy-related commitment.
To account for boundary conditions in this process, desti -
nation preference is incorporated as a moderating variable
that strengthens the effects of PDBRO and destination advo-
cacy on revisit intention. Overall, the proposed framework
integrates authenticity theory, relationship marketing, and
destination behavior literature to explain how VR-based
authentic experiences shape tourists’ future visitation inten-
tions through layered psychological mechanisms.
4 Methods
4.1 Sample
This study employed a quantitative research design to
examine the proposed structural relationships among VR
authentic experience, multidimensional authenticity per -
ceptions, relational mechanisms, and revisit intention. Data
were collected through a structured questionnaire admin -

4.2 Measurement scales
All constructs were operationalized using previously vali -
dated measurement scales to ensure content validity and
conceptual rigor. Minor wording adjustments were made
to contextualize the items within a VR-based tourism set -
ting while maintaining their original theoretical meaning.
VR authentic experience (VR) was measured using four
items adapted from Kim et al. ( 2020) and Vishwakarma et
al. ( 2020), capturing perceived authenticity, uniqueness,
genuineness, and experiential richness within VR tourism
environments.
Drawing on Wang’s ( 1999) conceptualization and the
empirical operationalization of Zhang and Yin ( 2020),
authenticity was modeled as a multidimensional con -
struct comprising objective authenticity (OA), construc -
tive authenticity (CA), postmodern authenticity (PA), and
existential authenticity (EA). Objective authenticity reflects
perceptions of historical preservation and factual credibil -
ity. Constructive authenticity captures socially constructed
representations of culture and tradition. Postmodern
authenticity assesses perceptions of simulation and blurred

boundaries between the real and the reproduced. Existential
authenticity measures self-related experiential outcomes,
including release from routine and self-development. The
full item wording is reported in Appendix A.
Perceived destination brand relationship orientation

experiential judgments rather than as objective verification of
whether a destination was historically or materially authen-

encountered by respondents, but it was not intended to elim-
inate individual interpretation; such interpretation is central
to constructive, postmodern, and existential authenticity.
To reduce avoidable measurement noise, all items were
adapted from validated scales, contextualized consistently
to VR tourism, presented anonymously, and later assessed
through reliability and discriminant-validity diagnostics.
4.3 Data collection procedure
Data were collected between August and October 2025
using a controlled, standardized exposure design to ensure
that respondents evaluated authenticity perceptions based
on a shared VR tourism stimulus rather than recalled expe -
riences. Before completing the questionnaire, participants
were exposed to the same standardized 12-minute, 360° VR
tour of Hoi An Ancient Town, Da Nang City, Vietnam (for-
merly Quang Nam Province). The stimulus was delivered
through Meta Quest 2 headsets using the Meta Quest TV
media player and the same locally stored 360° MP4 master
file. Every participant followed the same fixed sequence: the
Japanese Covered Bridge, Phung Hung Old House, Fujian
Assembly Hall, Hoi An Market and the central shophouse
streets, the Thu Bon riverside, and the lantern-lit old town.
Participants remained seated and could look freely in all
directions through head tracking; the hand controller was
used only to recenter the view, while scene selection, skip -
ping, and pausing were disabled, and scene order, exposure
time, audio, and instructions were held constant across par-
ticipants. The destination was selected because its conserved
heritage fabric, culturally interpreted streetscapes, staged
tourism elements, and self-reflective experiential cues
allowed all four authenticity dimensions to be assessed
within one coherent destination context.
All sessions were conducted individually under a com -
mon administration checklist. Across Ho Chi Minh City,
Hanoi, Hue, Da Nang, and Can Tho, 10 identically con -
figured headsets and 10 trained data collectors were used,
with two headsets and two data collectors assigned to each
city. Appointments were scheduled in pre-booked 35-min -
ute slots during weekday-evening and weekend collection
blocks, with a maximum of two concurrent sessions per city,
and each session comprised a 3-minute study and safety
briefing with written consent, a 2-minute headset fitting and
visual/boundary calibration, the 12-minute standardized
exposure, an immediate 10–12-minute survey, and a 5-min-
ute device reset and sanitation period. The same instruction
script, seating arrangement, audio setting, and content file
were used at all five sites. The final valid cases were dis -
tributed across Ho Chi Minh City (n = 242), Hanoi (n = 177),
Hue ( n = 83), Da Nang ( n = 172), and Can Tho ( n = 183).
Immediately following the VR experience, respondents
completed a structured survey measuring VR authentic
experience, four dimensions of authenticity (objective, con-
structive, postmodern, and existential), perceived destina -
tion brand relationship orientation, destination advocacy,
destination preference, and revisit intention.
Procedural remedies were implemented to mitigate
potential common method bias. Participation was voluntary
and anonymous, instructions emphasized honest responses,
and item order was randomized. These ex ante remedies fol-
low the procedural guidance of Podsakoff et al. ( 2003). As
a post hoc structural diagnostic, the inner-model VIF values
for all predictor sets, including the interaction terms, ranged
from 1.000 to 2.854. These values are below the conven -
tional 5.0 threshold for structural collinearity, indicating
that problematic predictor collinearity is unlikely. However,
inner-model VIFs are not equivalent to Kock’s ( 2015) full-
collinearity common-method test and are not used to claim
that common method bias is absent. Because the design did
not include a marker variable or a properly executed full-
collinearity common-method test, residual common method
variance cannot be ruled out conclusively and is acknowl -
edged as a limitation. The controlled exposure design nev -
ertheless improves stimulus consistency by grounding the
focal evaluations in a common experience rather than het -
erogeneous prior travel memories.

5 Research results
5.1 Demographic statistics
A total of 857 valid responses were retained for analy -
sis after data screening procedures were completed. The
demographic profile of the sample reflects a diverse group
of respondents and provides a robust basis for examining
authenticity perceptions and destination-related behav -
ioral responses within virtual tourism contexts. In terms of
gender distribution, 36.5% of the participants were male,
and 63.5% were female. The majority of respondents were
between 23 and 30 years old (50.5%), followed by those
aged 16–22 (30.7%) and 31–40 (16.5%), while only 2.3%
were above 40 years old. This distribution indicates a pre -
dominantly young adult sample, a demographic segment
commonly associated with higher engagement in digital and
immersive environments.
Regarding educational background, 44.6% of par -
ticipants held an undergraduate degree, 22.8% reported a
college qualification, 22.2% had completed high school,
and 10.5% had pursued postgraduate studies. In terms of
occupation, office staff (30.2%) and business professionals
(25.1%) constituted the largest groups, followed by tech -
nical workers (15.4%), students (14.5%), and other profes -
sions (14.8%). Monthly income levels were relatively well
distributed, with 36.9% earning between 7 and under 15 mil-
lion VND, 28.4% between 15 and under 25 million VND,
22.4% above 25 million VND, and 12.4% below 7 million
VND. Geographically, respondents were drawn from major
Vietnamese cities, including Ho Chi Minh City (28.2%),
Hanoi (20.7%), Can Tho (21.4%), Da Nang (20.1%), and
Hue (9.7%), enhancing regional representation. In addi -
tion, 61.5% of participants reported engaging with VR or
digital immersive environments for two to four hours per
session, indicating substantial familiarity with interactive
virtual experiences. Overall, the demographic composition
suggests an appropriate and sufficiently varied sample for
examining experiential and relational mechanisms in VR-
based tourism settings (see Table 1).
The predominance of women and younger respondents
does not invalidate the analysis, but it does shape the popu-
lation to which the findings are most directly applicable.
Younger VR-familiar participants may be more responsive
to immersive, self-related, and emotionally engaging des -
tination cues, whereas older or less digitally experienced
tourists may evaluate VR authenticity more cautiously. Sim-
ilarly, the female majority suggests that the results should
not be read as gender-neutral population estimates. For this
reason, the demographic profile is interpreted as appropri -
ate for an urban VR-engaged tourism segment, while future
studies using gender- and age-stratified sampling would be
useful for testing the stability of the model across broader
tourist populations.

5.5 Moderation effects
To examine the boundary conditions of the proposed frame-
work, the moderating role of destination preference (DP)
was assessed using interaction terms within the PLS-SEM
bootstrapping procedure (5,000 resamples). The results
indicate a differentiated moderation pattern.
The interaction effect between destination preference
and perceived destination brand relationship orientation
(PDBRO) on revisit intention (RI) was not statistically sig -
nificant (H8a: β = -0.023, p = 0.433, bias-corrected 95% CI
[-0.081, 0.033], f² = 0.001). This finding suggests that rela -
tional orientation toward a destination translates into revisit
intention relatively independently of the degree to which the
destination is prioritized within tourists’ consideration set.
In other words, once a strong relational bond is established,
its influence on revisit intention appears robust and does
not require reinforcement from attitudinal preference. This
result implies that relational embeddedness may function
as a self-sufficient psychological driver of revisit behavior.
In contrast, the interaction between destination preference
and destination advocacy was significant and positive (H8b:
β = 0.062, p = 0.019, bias-corrected 95% CI [0.011, 0.115]).
This indicates that destination preference conditions the
effect of advocacy on revisit intention. However, the f² of
0.008 shows that the incremental moderation effect is very
small and should not be interpreted as a major behavioral
driver. When tourists both advocate for a destination and
regard it as a preferred choice, the consistency between atti-
tudinal prioritization and expressive commitment modestly
increases the likelihood of future visitation. Conversely,
when destination preference is low, advocacy may reflect
situational or socially driven behavior that does not neces -
sarily translate into personal revisit commitment.
Notably, the direct effect of destination preference on
revisit intention was negative and significant (β = -0.202,
p < 0.001, bias-corrected 95% CI [-0.263, -0.143], f² =
0.063). Post hoc VIF diagnostics for the RI equation were
all close to 1.0 (1.057–1.125), including the two interac -
tion terms, indicating that this counterintuitive coefficient
is unlikely to be caused by collinearity among DP, PDBRO,
and the interaction variables. The coefficient is a conditional
partial effect estimated after relational orientation, advo -
cacy, and both interaction terms are controlled; it should
not be interpreted as a simple causal claim that preference
reduces revisit intention. One possible explanation is com -
parative choice tension, whereby respondents can prefer a
destination relative to alternatives without converting that
evaluation into stable revisit commitment once relational
factors are held constant. However, the data do not directly
test this explanation, so the negative path is interpreted cau-
tiously and requires replication.
Overall, the moderation analysis reveals that destina -
tion preference does not uniformly amplify all downstream
behavioral mechanisms. Instead, it selectively strengthens
advocacy-based pathways while leaving relational effects
largely unaffected. These findings highlight the nuanced
role of attitudinal prioritization in shaping how relational
and behavioral commitments translate into revisit intentions.
6 Discussion
This study examined how VR authentic experience trans -
lates into revisit intention through multidimensional authen-
ticity perceptions and relational mechanisms. The findings
reveal a structured and differentiated transmission process.
First, VR authentic experience significantly enhanced all
four authenticity dimensions, with existential authenticity
(β = 0.481) and constructive authenticity (β = 0.479) show-
ing nearly identical and strongest effects, followed by objec-
tive (β = 0.423) and postmodern authenticity (β = 0.317). The
f² results reinforce this interpretation: VR → EA (0.301) and
VR → CA (0.297) were medium and practically equiva -
lent, VR → OA was medium (0.218), and VR → PA was
small (0.112). The result therefore does not support a strong
claim that one authenticity dimension clearly dominates
at the activation stage; rather, VR simultaneously evokes
self-related and meaning-based authenticity. This extends
Kim et al. ( 2020), who modeled authentic experience as a
broader experiential response, by showing that existential
and constructive components are activated to almost the
same degree when authenticity is decomposed. It also com-
plements Atzeni et al. (2022), who distinguish object-based
and existential authenticity in virtual heritage settings. The
weak R² for PA (0.101) further indicates that acceptance of
simulation may depend on antecedents beyond overall VR
authentic experience, such as playfulness or entertainment
value.
Second, authenticity perceptions influence revisit inten -
tion primarily through relational orientation rather than
advocacy. Perceived destination brand relationship orien -
tation (PDBRO) exhibited a strong direct effect on revisit
intention (β = 0.574, bias-corrected 95% CI [0.522, 0.624])
and a large incremental contribution (f² = 0.542), represent-
ing the most influential predictor in the model. The media -
tion results sharpen this conclusion: the PDBRO-specific
indirect coefficients ranged from 0.053 to 0.204, whereas
the DA-specific coefficients ranged only from 0.009 to
0.023. Constructive authenticity produced the largest total
indirect effect (0.213), followed by objective (0.135), post -
modern (0.096), and existential authenticity (0.077). This
ordering reveals an important distinction between activation
and conversion: existential authenticity was readily elicited

by VR, but its strongest downstream path was toward advo-
cacy (EA → DA  = 0.262), and the weak DA → RI link
limited its eventual contribution to revisit intention. By
contrast, constructive and objective authenticity were more
efficiently converted into PDBRO and then RI. This pat -
tern is consistent with Kumar et al. ( 2023), who position
destination brand relationship orientation as a key conse -
quence of authenticity and a precursor of revisit intention
and advocacy, as well as with Ramkissoon’s (2015) empha-
sis on attachment-based behavioral intentions. DA → RI
remained significant but substantively limited (β = 0.089,
bias-corrected 95% CI [0.035, 0.149], f² = 0.012), which
is weaker than the close coupling of recommendation and
revisit outcomes commonly assumed in destination-loyalty
research (Chiu et al., 2016).
Third, the moderation analysis revealed a selective
boundary condition. Destination preference did not sig -
nificantly moderate the relationship between PDBRO and
revisit intention (β = -0.023, 95% CI [-0.081, 0.033], f² =
0.001), indicating that relational orientation exerts a stable
effect regardless of the level of attitudinal prioritization.
However, destination preference positively moderated the
advocacy-revisit link (β = 0.062, 95% CI [0.011, 0.115]).
This result highlights that preference operates as a condi -
tional enhancer of advocacy-based pathways rather than
relational pathways. At the same time, the moderation
effect was very small (f² = 0.008), so its practical impor -
tance should be treated as limited even though it is statis -
tically supported. The non-significant PDBRO moderation
diverges from H8a and suggests that an established rela -
tional bond is relatively robust to comparative preference.
The positive advocacy interaction is consistent with a
conditional-consistency interpretation: preference can help
convert public endorsement into personal revisit intention,
but it does not create the relational effect itself.
Notably, destination preference showed a negative direct
effect on revisit intention (β = -0.202, bias-corrected 95%
CI [-0.263, -0.143], f² = 0.063). Additional VIF checks
reduce the likelihood that this pattern is a statistical artifact
of the moderation specification. Thus, the negative coef -
ficient should not be interpreted as evidence that prefer -
ence is undesirable; rather, it is a small conditional partial
effect estimated after relational orientation, advocacy, and
both interactions are controlled. While relational orientation
strongly predicts revisit intention, preference alone does not
necessarily translate into stable revisit commitment when
relational consolidation is accounted for. This coefficient
differs from Mao and Zhang ( 2014), who report positive
links among destination preference, satisfaction, and loy -
alty. The divergence is important because the present esti -
mate is conditional and should not be interpreted as a simple
bivariate claim that destination preference reduces revisit
intention. The proposed comparative-choice interpretation
remains tentative and requires replication.
Overall, the results support a layered mechanism in
which VR authentic experience influences revisit intention
indirectly through multidimensional authenticity percep -
tions and, more critically, through relational embedding.
The findings demonstrate that relational orientation plays
a central role in converting authenticity-based evaluations
into sustained behavioral intention, whereas advocacy and
preference function as secondary and conditional mecha -
nisms within the broader structural framework.
7 Conclusion
7.1 Theoretical and managerial implications
The findings of this study carry both theoretical and practical
implications. Theoretically, the results advance authenticity
research in VR tourism by showing that the authenticity
dimension most strongly activated by VR is not necessarily
the dimension most effectively converted into revisit inten -
tion. VR elicited existential and constructive authenticity
with almost identical strength, yet constructive authenticity
produced the largest downstream effect because it was more
strongly translated into PDBRO. Objective authenticity also
generated a substantial relationally mediated effect, whereas
existential authenticity primarily stimulated advocacy and
therefore contributed less to revisit intention because the
DA → RI path was weak. This separation between activa -
tion and conversion refines multidimensional authenticity
theory and reinforces relational orientation as the principal
transmission mechanism. Practically, VR tourism strate -
gies should combine self-relevant immersion with coherent
cultural narratives and verifiable heritage cues. Emotional
liberation and self-development can encourage expressive
support, but relationship-building requires the destination
to communicate consistent meaning, credibility, and care.
Destination marketers should therefore prioritize features
that help users interpret the destination as a trustworthy
relational partner rather than relying only on spectacle or
emotional intensity. While advocacy contributes to revisit
intention, the preference-based amplification is statistically
significant but very small (f² = 0.008); it should be treated
as a secondary refinement rather than the main manage -
rial lever. Overall, VR initiatives are most effective when
designed not merely to impress users, but to convert authen-
tic destination meaning into an enduring relational bond.

7.2 Limitations and future research
Despite its contributions, this study has several limita -
tions that open avenues for future research. First, the study
adopted a cross-sectional design, which restricts causal
inference over time. Although the structural relationships
were theoretically grounded and statistically supported,
revisit intention remains a stated behavioral intention rather
than an observed longitudinal outcome. Future studies may
employ experimental or longitudinal designs to examine
whether relational orientation formed in VR contexts trans-
lates into actual visitation behavior over time. Second, the
empirical context focused on VR-based tourism experi -
ences within a specific national setting. Cultural norms may
influence how authenticity, relational orientation, and desti-
nation preference are constructed and interpreted. Compara-
tive cross-cultural research would be valuable in assessing
whether the dominance of relational mediation observed
in this study holds across different sociocultural contexts.
Third, while the model incorporated multidimensional
authenticity, relational orientation, advocacy, and prefer -
ence, other psychological mechanisms were not examined.
For instance, constructs such as perceived value, emotional
attachment intensity, or experiential flow may further clarify
the transmission process between authenticity and behav -
ioral intention. Future research may extend the model by
incorporating additional mediators or boundary conditions
to refine the explanatory architecture. Fourth, destination
preference demonstrated a negative direct effect on revisit
intention within the structural model. Although the interpre-
tation was confined to construct-level differentiation, future
research could further investigate how comparative evalu -
ation processes interact with relational attachment within
broader decision-making frameworks. Fifth, this study
conceptualized authenticity perceptions as outcomes of VR
authentic experience. Future research may explore reverse
or reciprocal relationships, particularly in contexts where
prior destination familiarity influences authenticity evalu -
ation within immersive environments. Sixth, the demo -
graphic profile was skewed toward younger and female
respondents due to the purposive, voluntary sampling
design. Future research should use stratified or quota-based
sampling to test whether the model remains stable across
gender and age groups. Seventh, DA and RI were each mea-
sured with two items. DA demonstrated adequate reliability,
whereas RI had lower alpha and rho_A despite acceptable
loadings, rho_c, and A VE. Future work should consider
expanded item pools, particularly for RI. Eighth, common
method variance was managed procedurally and the revised
inner VIF values indicate no problematic structural collin -
earity, but the study did not include a marker variable or
a correctly executed full-collinearity common-method test.
Residual common method bias therefore cannot be ruled out
conclusively, and future work should combine stronger tem-
poral or source separation with an appropriate data-based
diagnostic.
