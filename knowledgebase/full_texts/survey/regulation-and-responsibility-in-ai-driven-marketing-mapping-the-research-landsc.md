# Regulation and Responsibility in AI-Driven Marketing: Mapping the Research Landscape

Regulation and Responsibility in AI-Driven Marketing:
Mapping the Research Landscape

Andra Blaj*
Anca Popescu
“Transilvania” University of Brasov,

 andra.blaj@unitbv.ro

Abstract

Artificial intelligence is transforming marketing practices, from algorithmic personalization to
digital avatars and virtual influencers. Our previous research showed that, in the literature
specifically dedicated to AI-driven avatars, regu lation remains the most is olated concept in the
keyword co-occurrence network, while responsibility  is weakly conceptualized. The present article
extends this perspective, mapping how regulation and responsibility are structured in the literature
in relation to AI marketing. Through a study of 266 Scopus-indexed publications, selected from an
initial set of 1082, the analysis identifies six thematic clusters. Regulation and responsibility do not
form a unified cluster; they appear fragmented across three distinct clusters — AI governance and
ethics, data governance and compliance, and the legal and strategic framework. The results provide
a bibliometric foundation for future research on ethical marketing and AI avatars.

Key words: artificial intelligence, marketing, regulation, responsibility,  bibliometric analysis,
VOSviewer, ethics, AI governance

1. Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has moved beyond an automation too l. It is radically changing how
organizations implement marketing activities, becoming a significant and active element in decision-
making and the brand-consumer relationship (Puntoni et al., 202 1). Recommendation algorithms,
predictive systems, generative models, and digital avatars are increasingly used to personalize
communication, optimize customer experience, and automate commercial processes. Alongside the
economic and operational benefits they offer, these technologies generate new challenges regarding
transparency, data protection, and consumer autonomy (Susser et al., 2019), as well as organizations'
responsibility for AI-assisted marketing decisions (Kumar and Suthar, 2024; Hermann and Puntoni,
2025).
The accelerated development of generative AI and the adoption of EU Regulation 2024/1689 on
artificial intelligence (the AI Act) have shifted the academic debate from technological performance
toward the governance and respon sibility of AI systems. Recent literature increasingly addresses
concepts such as AI governance, AI ethics, data governance, and  consumer protection. Still, these
are usually examined through distinct and rarely integrated con ceptual frameworks applicable to
marketing. As a consequence, moral responsibility is portrayed either as a matter of technical
compliance or as a general ethical principle, without a coherent conceptualization of the relationship
between regulation, governance,  and AI-driven marketing practic es (Kumar and Suthar, 2024;
Hermann and Puntoni, 2025).

In a previous bibliometric analysis dedicated to AI avatar mark eting, we highlighted that
regulation is one of the most peripheral concepts in the keyword co-occurrence network (Blaj et al.,
2026). Responsibility is predominantly treated as a persuasion mechanism rather than a criterion for
evaluating the legitimacy of marketing practices. A preliminary check of 1,082 publications on AI,
marketing, and regulation/responsibility showed that only 2.7% explicitly mention avatars or virtual
influencers, further underlining that avatars remain a marginal subject within the broader discussion
on AI regulation in marketing.
These two observations converge toward the same conclusion: the literature does not yet employ
a unified conceptual framework linking regulation, responsibility, and AI-driven marketing
practices. This paper presents a bibliometric analysis of the l iterature on regulation and moral
responsibility in AI-driven marketing. Its main objective is to examine the evolution and conceptual
structure of the field by identifying publication trends, influential studies, major research areas, and
thematic clusters, and by analyzing how regulation and moral re sponsibility are positioned within
the AI-driven marketing research landscape. To achieve this obj ective, the study addresses the
following research questions:
RQ1. How has the scientific literature on regulation and moral responsibility in AI-driven
marketing evolved?
RQ2. Which research areas and influential publications have shaped the development of this
field?
RQ3. What is the conceptual structure of the literature, and what does it reveal about the
positioning of regulation and moral responsibility within AI-driven marketing research?

2. Literature review

Current studies consistently indicate multiple ethical and legal challenges raised by the use of AI
marketing, from algorithmic discrimination to a lack of clarity  on data protection (Kumar and
Suntha, 2024). These challenges are amplified by the rapid exte nsion of AI applications in e-
commerce, where security and consumer data confidentiality rema in the main sources of concern
(Alhitmi et al., 2024).
Another area of the literature focuses on AI avatars and anthro pomorphized virtual influencers,
which raise questions about perceived authenticity and responsibility in interactions with consumers
(Kim and Wang, 2023). Data governance is often proposed as a te chnological solution for these
concerns by combining AI with blockchain to protect e-commerce consumers' data (Singh et al.,
2024).
Among the few studies that explicitly relate ethical principles  to marketing practices, Hermann
and Puntoni (2025) advance a conceptual framework for the ethic al application of generative AI,
structured around the marketing cycle. International regulatory  standards continue to be
underexplored. However, the tension between the efficacy of AI-driven marketing techniques, such
as neuromarketing, and consumer rights protection is increasingly visible in the literature, especially
in the GDPR context (Goncalves et al., 2024).
Auditing and the external certification of AI systems are inten ded as complementary to legal
regulation and offer consumers a benchmark for evaluating AI-dr iven legit marketing practices
(Blösser și Weihrauch, 2024). Precedent methodologies to struct ure the research agenda starting
from a systematic literature review can be found in adjacent fields of study, such as client-oriented
financial services (Hentzen et al., 2022).
There is a recurrent pattern pointed out by our research: regul ation, data governance, and ethics
are predominantly discussed, but rarely integrated into the com mon conceptual model.
Responsibility is regarded as a technical issue (security, conformity) or an abstract principle, not an
operational criterion for evaluating AI-driven marketing practices.
The absence of a conceptual framework doesn't seem to be random ; it reflects the structural gap
between the development trajectory of artificial intelligence a nd the capacity of regulatory
institutions and communities to produce conceptually unified an swers. Compared with previous
technologies that evolved in a linear model within an applicati on domain, AI is diversifying

3. Research methodology

To understand how regulation a nd responsibility are positioned in the literature, a bibliometric
analysis is suitable because it allows identification of concep tual patterns across the entire domain
(Moral-Muñoz et al., 2020). The research has been conducted in the Scopus database, using a simple
string buit from three blocks o terms connected through the AND operator: a first block combining
artificial intelligence with its embodiment forms in marketing,  a v a t a r s  a n d  v i r t u a l  i n f l u e n c e r s ,
entities that may be AI-mediated but are not synonymous with it  (AI, avatar*, virtual influencer*)
marketing keywords (marketing) and the regulation and responsibility terms.
The initial research has retrieved 1082 publications. We applied a selection process adapted from
PRISMA (Page et al., 2021) (Figure no. 1): in the identificatio n phase, four publications were
excluded (3 Erratum, 1 Retracted), and the remaining 1,078 arti cles were checked based on their
title. Only the articles whose title explicitly contained at le ast one AI-related term and at least one
marketing/consumer-related term were kept, which excluded 812 p ublications and reduced the set
to 266 publications. At the eligibility stage, all 266 publications met the established criterion (none
further excluded), constituting the actual core of the domain rather than merely tangential mentions.

The bibliometric analysis used the 1.6.20 VOSviewer version (va n Eck and Waltman, 2010) to
create a keyword co-occurrence map. Before the map generation a  thesaurus file was applied for
obvious spelling variants and synonyms (e.g., "ai" and "artificial intelligence (ai)" merged into
"artificial intelligence"; "consumer trust" merged into "trust"; the variants "ethical aspects," "ethical
concerns," and "ethical considerations" merged into "ethics") w ere consolidated to avoid artificial
fragmentation of the network. The terms avatar and virtual infl uencer were included as alternative
search terms (using the OR operator) within the first search bl ock, alongside AI-related terms. The
inclusion aimed to capture publications addressing avatars and virtual influencers that do not
explicitly use the term artificial intelligence, ensuring continuity with our previous bibliometric
study (Blaj, Neacșu, & Grancea, 2026). This search strategy doe s not restrict the corpus to avatar-
related research; instead, the dataset stays centered on AI-driven marketing, with avatars and virtual
influencers considered specific manifestations of AI applications in marketing. The result was a map
of 78 keywords, organized into six thematic clusters, presented in the following section.

4. Findings

In This section is structured based on the three research questions established in the Introduction.
To address RQ1, we have analyzed the time evolution of the publication number to examine the pace
of development of the field, which became an active research area.

RQ1. How has the scientific literature on regulation and moral responsibility in AI-driven
marketing evolved?
RQ1 is addressed by analyzing the annual publication trends of the 266 selected studies, showing
how the field has evolved.
The development of publications confirms the acceleration of academic interest in AI, marketing,
and regulation/responsibility: from 1-2 annual publications dur ing 2019-2021, to 35 in 2024, 89 in
2025, and already 126 in 2026 (Figure no. 2). This trajectory s hows that this became an active
research domain in 2023, following the emergence of ChatGPT, Cl aude, and other large language
model-based systems, which brought generative AI into mainstream marketing practices.

5. Conclusions

The analysis of the strength of connections in the strength map shows that the pattern goes beyond
what can be explained by a simple model and indeed strengthens this pattern beyond just the
separation of clusters: the ten keywords relating to governance and regulation (AI governance, data
governance, regulatory compliance, laws and legislation, AI eth ics, algorithmic bias, privacy by
design, government data processing, consumer protection, privac y) together make up only 629 of
the network's 10,158 total link strength units. This is 6.2%, fewer than the independent connectivity
of the central term "artificial intelligence" (845). Not only are regulation and AI governance
thematically separate from the main part of the field, but they  are also structurally marginal in the
conceptual network of the literature.
Overall, the findings indicate that regulation and responsibility have not yet been integrated into
a coherent conceptual framework for AI-driven marketing. Curren t research continues to focus
mainly on AI implementation and marketing applications, while g overnance and responsibility are
often treated as complementary rather than central research themes.
The fact that the concepts are so fragmented is consistent with the results of our earlier
bibliometric study concerning AI avatar literature (Blaj et al, 2026), in which regulation emerged as
the most isolated concept in the network. When the two studies are considered together, it becomes
clear that the lack of an integrated regulatory framework is no t a feature unique to avatar literature
but is instead a general trait of the AI-driven marketing sector. These findings highlight the need for
future research to develop an integrated conceptual framework linking the three identified
perspectives (AI ethics and governance, data governance, and the legal framework) and to examine
their application in AI avatar-mediated marketing interactions.
The article has some methodological limitations which suggest p ossible areas for further
research: it relies on a single Scopus database without checkin g other databases such as Web of
Science or Google Scholar, and the use of title-based selection  criteria may result in relevant
publications being excluded. In order to advance the analysis of clusters 2, 3, and 6, future research
should carry out a qualitative review of suitable publications in order to develop an integrated
framework for evaluating the legitimacy of AI avatar-based marketing practices, using the existing
theory of avatar marketing (Miao et al., 2021; De Silva & de Campos, 2024).
