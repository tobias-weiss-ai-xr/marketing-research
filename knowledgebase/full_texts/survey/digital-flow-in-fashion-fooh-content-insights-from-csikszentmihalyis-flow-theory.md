# Digital flow in fashion FOOH content: insights from Csikszentmihalyi’s flow theory

Digital flow in fashion FOOH content:

Introduction
Fashion communication utilizing media technologies has increasingly evolved around
user experience and immersion. In particular, fashion brands are required to move
beyond the unidirectional transmission of messages and develop experience-based,
immersive communication strategies designed to capture users’ attention and encour -
age emotional engagement across both digital and physical environments. Against this
Abstract
Fashion brands increasingly employ immersive digital content to engage audiences
within platform-based communication environments. This study examined manifesta-
tions of digital flow in fashion brands’ fake out-of-home (FOOH) content on Instagram
and observable audience responses. A theory-informed grounded theory approach
was adopted, with Csikszentmihalyi’s flow theory used as a sensitizing framework
rather than as a set of predetermined coding categories. The analysis drew on 115
Instagram Reels from 20 fashion brands and 8,794 text-bearing public comments,
with videos, captions, and comments examined as complementary case-level materi-
als. The findings identified novelty, enjoyment, and curiosity about production tech-
nologies as conditions relevant to flow-related engagement, while familiarity with digi-
tal media and recognizable landmarks constituted contextual conditions. Users’
recognition of marketing intent emerged as an important evaluative condition. Across
the cases, visual shock and fantastical representation, concise messaging, and feedback
and prompts for user communication and participation were identified as key action/
interaction strategies. Observable audience responses included enjoyment, reported
repeated viewing, comment-based interaction, product interest, purchase-oriented
inquiry, and critical evaluation. Critical responses were particularly evident when users
raised concerns about authenticity, sustainability, ethical responsibility, or creative
originality. At a higher interpretive level, the findings were synthesized across cognitive,
multisensory, and affective dimensions. The study proposes an interpretive relational
model that organizes the empirically developed categories and their conceptual
relationships, offering a theoretical basis for understanding digital flow in FOOH-based
fashion communication and informing future research and strategic practice in plat-
form-based media environments.

Literature Review
FOOH
FOOH is a compound term combining “fake” and “OOH (out-of-home), ” which refers
to outdoor advertising. It denotes virtual outdoor advertisements created by overlaying
sophisticated CGI onto real spatial backgrounds. FOOH involves producing outdoor

advertising content that does not exist in physical reality but is rendered to appear real
within digital media contexts. The term “FOOH” was first introduced by the digital artist
Ian Padgham. In June 2021, Padgham produced a short FOOH video depicting a train
shaped like a wine bottle running through the city of Bordeaux, France, and released it
on social media. The video attracted widespread attention when the French transporta -
tion authority issued an official clarification stating that the train shown in the video
did not actually exist (Fulleylove, 2024). Following the COVID-19 pandemic, interest in
FOOH as a new form of outdoor advertising has expanded, and various fashion brands
have strategically adopted FOOH content in their communication efforts.
FOOH content differs from other media content in terms of production methods,
expressive techniques, and anticipated effects. Its key characteristics can be summarized
as follows. First, as virtual imagery grounded in real environments, FOOH is presented
as realistic content within online spaces. It combines real footage with computer graph -
ics technologies, including CGI and VFX (visual effects), to construct situations that do
not exist in the physical world but are perceived as if they were real (Kornilov, 2025). This
characteristic distinguishes FOOH from AR (augmented reality) and VR (virtual real -
ity), which rely on specialized devices such as smartphones or headsets to facilitate user
interaction. In addition, FOOH exists exclusively within online media environments,
which differentiates it from DOOH (digital out-of-home), where digital technologies are
used to install content in actual physical outdoor locations. Although FOOH is virtual, it
employs real urban environments as backdrops to heighten the sense of realism, creating
the impression that events are occurring in the physical world. When such backdrops
feature fashionable places that are familiar to users or globally renowned fashion cit -
ies, fashion brands can strategically leverage placeness by foregrounding signature prod -
ucts within these settings. This place-anchored familiarity can enhance product salience
by situating branded objects within iconic, fashion-signifying locations, which in turn
strengthens attention and evaluative appeal. Consequently, FOOH content is deliber -
ately designed to blur the distinction between physically installed outdoor advertise -
ments and digitally composited ones, thereby effectively capturing public attention.
Second, FOOH is typically produced as short-form video content, usually under one
minute in length, and optimized for social media platforms including Instagram, TikTok,
and YouTube. It is intentionally designed to be highly shareable and attention-grabbing,
aligning with the interactive, participatory, and viral dynamics of social media, particu -
larly their fast-scrolling interfaces (Fernández-Gómez & Feijoo, 2024). This format facili-
tates rapid content consumption and encourages swift viral diffusion (Kornilov, 2025).
To achieve this, FOOH employs performance-oriented visual techniques and concise
narrative structures that momentarily capture users’ audiovisual attention. This empha -
sis on momentary capture foregrounds the immediacy of FOOH, which is designed to
generate affective impact during initial exposure rather than through prolonged inter -
pretation. By concentrating meaning into dense, high-salience audiovisual cues, FOOH
can elicit rapid emotional appraisal and response with minimal time for deliberate cog -
nitive processing. In this way, it secures a competitive edge within the fast-paced social
media ecosystem by efficiently attracting viewers’ attention and fostering organic forms
of participation, thereby offering brands new modes of public engagement (Attar, 2024).

Third, FOOH intentionally relocates familiar objects and spaces into non-ordinary
contexts to elicit surprise through perceptual incongruity (Hutter & Hoffmann, 2014).
This defamiliarizing strategy is conceptually comparable to the surrealist technique of
dépaysement, which produces schema disruption by rendering the familiar perceptually
out of place (Jurca & Madlberger, 2015). Within FOOH communication, dépaysement
can be operationalized as contextual displacement that draws attention and prompts
interpretive processing by foregrounding atypical relations among objects, space, and
scale. In digital media environments, this form of displacement can generate percep -
tual shock that rapidly captures attention and triggers immediate affective responses
(Hutter & Hoffmann, 2014). Building on this mechanism, FOOH often integrates sen -
sorially orchestrated, incongruent visual elements with salient brand or product cues,
thereby fostering heightened attentional focus and favorable affective evaluations. This
structured combination can also promote digital flow by sustaining users’ absorption
and engagement through high-salience audiovisual cues within an interpretable brand
context.
Digital fashion media and digital flow
Digital fashion media constitute an environment in which users can communicate openly
and access information without temporal or spatial constraints. With the emergence and
diffusion of social media around 2010, the post-media era enabled unrestricted partici -
pation in the production, reproduction, adoption, and dissemination of fashion through
social platforms, thereby dissolving the traditional boundaries between producers and
users (Kawamura, 2023). Since the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, digital transforma -
tion in the fashion sector has accelerated, and the diversification of content designed
to enhance both accessibility and digital flow has become a critical factor in elevating
brand value within digital media environments. In this context, accessibility refers to the
extent to which digital content can be encountered quickly and with minimal effort by
a broad range of users through platform affordances such as mobile-first interfaces and
algorithmic distribution. Correspondingly, digital flow denotes an immersive experien -
tial state characterized by sustained attention and engagement, in which users remain
absorbed in content for longer durations through heightened concentration and reduced
awareness of external distractions. Conceptually derived from Csikszentmihalyi’s flow
theory, it frames users’ online engagement as a state in which psychological energy is
intensely invested in the ongoing activity, thereby supporting intrinsically rewarding
involvement and facilitating creative accomplishment within digital media environments
(Csikszentmihalyi, 2002). This development parallels the rapid evolution of immersive
technologies, including CGI, VFX, VR, AR, MR (mixed reality), and XR (extended real -
ity), and reflects users’ increasing tendency not only to view and consume content but
also to participate proactively, engage interactively, and co-create meaning (Boughlala
& Smelik, 2024; Rocamora, 2017). These technologies expand the scope and intensity
of digital experience by enabling perceptually rich, multisensory, interactive, and spa -
tially augmented content environments. In this way, immersive technologies function as
enabling resources for digital flow by sustaining focused attention and deepening users’
involvement and multisensory experience within digital media contexts.

Digital fashion media, notably social media platforms, have increasingly served as
primary sites in which fashion experiences are mediated through multisensory cues,
thereby shaping conditions conducive to digital flow. In this context, digital flow, con -
ceptually derived from Csikszentmihalyi’s flow theory, refers to users’ subjective expe -
rience of deep absorption and sustained engagement during media interaction. Within
online environments, this experience can be supported by media interfaces that afford
varying degrees of immersion and enable users to perceive comprehensive, life-like vir -
tual experiences (Dede, 2009). Through such experiences, users become deeply engaged
with content, form affective bonds with fashion brands, and report positive sensory and
emotional responses (Rocamora, 2017). Hamilton et  al. (2016) emphasize that social
media introduces new modes of interaction between brands and consumers, in which
satisfaction derived from interaction and immersion plays a pivotal role in brand value
creation. Sharma et al. (2024) similarly find that digital flow experiences foster stronger
brand attachment and loyalty. Further, previous research (Kiran et al., 2023; Mesjar et al.,
2023; Tarnanidis et al., 2023; Workman et al., 2022) indicates that social media engage -
ment operates as an antecedent of digital flow, mediating users’ psychological involve -
ment and behavioral outcomes.
To summarize, digital flow experiences mediate psychological bonding with brands,
fulfill users’ creative and participatory needs, and contribute to the formation of new
fashion cultures. Because the effectiveness of fashion communication strategies depends
on users’ ability to enter states of emotional digital flow (Hamilton et al., 2016), the stra -
tegic importance of content design that reflects the distinctive affordances of digital
media is heightened. Therefore, a systematic understanding of the factors and attributes
that facilitate active user engagement in the digital flow of fashion content is essential.
Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi’s flow theory
Grounded in cognitive psychology, Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi’s flow theory provides a
systematic framework for explaining the psychological state of deep involvement in a
given activity through the mechanisms of flow (Csikszentmihalyi, 2002). It has become
one of the most widely applied theoretical models for analyzing absorption and engage -
ment across diverse domains, including psychology, education, management, cogni -
tive science, sports, music, dance, gaming, and design. In this study, flow theory serves
as a sensitizing framework for examining flow-related experiential characteristics and
observable manifestations of engagement within digital media environments (Csiksze -
ntmihalyi, 2002). According to Nakamura and Csikszentmihalyi (2014), the concept of
flow offers a structured framework for identifying the major components under which
users become deeply engaged in audiovisual digital experiences, as well as the core pat -
terns that define the flow state. On this basis, the present study employs Csikszentmi -
halyi’s flow theory as a sensitizing framework for qualitatively examining flow-related
conditions and experiential manifestations in digital media environments.
Concept of flow
Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi’s flow theory originated in research aimed at elucidating the
psychological mechanisms underlying autotelic experience (Nakamura & Csikszentmih -
alyi, 2014). The term autotelic derives from “auto (self)” and “telos (goal)” and denotes an

orientation in which intrinsic motivation operates independently of external incentives
or rewards. This orientation constitutes a foundational prerequisite for the emergence of
the flow state (Csikszentmihalyi, 2002).
According to Csikszentmihalyi (2002), individuals with an autotelic orientation can
attain flow, understood as an optimal state of consciousness, by voluntarily directing
focused attention toward a specific activity. In this state, they experience deep absorp -
tion, during which peripheral concerns recede from awareness, and satisfaction emerges
intrinsically from the activity itself (Csikszentmihalyi, 2002). Enjoyment operates simul-
taneously as the internal motive that sustains engagement and as the primary outcome
of the flow experience (Csikszentmihalyi, 2002). Furthermore, when the activity itself
becomes the ultimate end rather than a means to external rewards, the flow experience
acquires existential significance through the actor’s processes of meaning-making (Csik -
szentmihalyi, 2002). In this sense, flow is not merely a transient psychological state but
a deeply meaningful experiential mode that contributes to the formation of life values.
Eight flow‑related components
Csikszentmihalyi (2002) specifies the conditions necessary for attaining the flow state,
conceptualized as an optimal experience characterized by a sense of voluntary accom -
plishment. He identifies eight components that elicit flow: (1) challenging activity that
requires skills; (2) merging of action and awareness; (3) clear goals; (4) immediate feed -
back; (5) concentration on the task at hand; (6) sense of control; (7) loss of self-con -
sciousness; (8) altered sense of time.
First, flow begins with engaging in a challenging activity that requires skills (C1). Flow
arises when individuals participate in activities that demand an appropriate balance
between challenge and skill, creating a dynamic tension that facilitates entry into the
flow state. Csikszentmihalyi (2002) emphasizes that individuals are most likely to expe -
rience flow when they actively mobilize their abilities to meet task demands, thereby
generating a sense of accomplishment. Conversely, activities that exceed or fall short of
one’s skill level tend to elicit anxiety or boredom, both of which hinder the onset of flow.
Moreover, when competition itself becomes the primary objective, intrinsic enjoyment
diminishes. Second, flow involves the merging of action and awareness (C2). Within
optimal experience, individuals become so deeply absorbed in the activity that distinc -
tions between action and thought dissolve. Attention is fully directed toward the task
goal, and intentions are seamlessly translated into execution. Third, the presence of
clear goals is essential for flow (C3). Clarity of goals provides a structural foundation
for flow, as explicit objectives and anticipated outcomes enable individuals to autono -
mously guide their actions toward successful accomplishment. Fourth, flow is sup -
ported by immediate feedback (C4). Timely information regarding one’s performance
relative to task goals allows individuals to recognize progress and adjust their behavior
accordingly, thereby sustaining the flow state. Fifth, flow requires concentration on the
task at hand (C5). Deep involvement necessitates undivided attention to the ongoing
activity, free from external distractions or intrusive thoughts, which maintains immer -
sive engagement. Sixth, flow is characterized by a sense of control (C6). This cognitive
assurance that one can effectively manage and influence the situation reduces anxiety
and fosters persistence, reinforcing the continuation of flow. Seventh, flow entails a loss

of self-consciousness (C7). During flow, reflective self-awareness, social self-conscious -
ness, and evaluative concerns temporarily recede, while attention remains anchored to
the immediate task. This psychological state is a hallmark of optimal experience. Eighth,
flow is accompanied by an altered sense of time (C8). The perception of temporal pas -
sage becomes distorted, often seeming to accelerate or decelerate relative to clock time.
As attentional intensity deepens, temporal awareness diminishes, and the extent of time
distortion corresponds to the depth of the flow experience.
Three dimensions of flow experience
Nakamura and Csikszentmihalyi (2014, p. 247) describe concentration, involvement,
and enjoyment as three dimensions used to provide a proxy for the reported flow state
in Experience Sampling Method research. They emphasized that these dimensions serve
as a proxy for the considerably more complex state of consciousness that characterizes
flow.
The first dimension, “concentration, ” marks the entry point into flow. It involves a
state of total attentional focus in which external stimuli and task-irrelevant thoughts
are excluded (Nakamura & Csikszentmihalyi, 2014). Such concentration is structured
by the presence of clear goals and immediate feedback, and it emerges when a balance
is achieved between task difficulty and individual skill (Csikszentmihalyi, 2002). This
dimension functions simultaneously as the gateway to and the catalyst for the flow state.
The second dimension, “involvement, ” refers to complete immersion and absorption
in the activity. Reflective self-awareness and evaluative concerns recede, and individu -
als become so fully engaged that peripheral elements lose significance. Nakamura and
Csikszentmihalyi (2014) describe deep involvement as a condition in which cognitive
and experiential energies are fully invested, anchoring attention entirely in the ongoing
activity.
The third dimension, “enjoyment, ” is characterized by a sense of satisfaction and worth
derived from the activity itself, independent of external rewards or instrumental out -
comes (Csikszentmihalyi, 2002). When autotelic orientation is achieved, individuals are
intrinsically motivated to seek out and repeat flow experiences through active self-initi -
ation. The rewards associated with flow are inherently aesthetic and emotional, produc -
ing enjoyment that transcends mere pleasure. This enjoyment is linked to processes of
meaning-making through accomplishment and fulfillment (Nakamura & Csikszentmi -
halyi, 2014). Ultimately, the core aim of the flow experience resides in the enjoyment
associated with “autotelicity” and in the discovery and creation of meaning that arises
from it (Csikszentmihalyi, 2002).
Within flow theory, the eight flow-related components and the three dimensions rep -
resent different but complementary levels of conceptualization. The eight components
provide a differentiated account of the conditions and phenomenological characteristics
through which flow emerges and is experienced, whereas concentration, involvement,
and enjoyment offer a more parsimonious representation of the overall experiential
quality of the reported flow state. Thus, these two conceptual levels capture interrelated
aspects of an integrated flow experience rather than constituting mutually exclusive clas-
sifications. Building on this conceptual distinction, the present study draws on the eight
flow-related components to examine the conditions and experiential characteristics

Methods
Research design and methodological approach
The purpose of this study is to explore, as the central phenomenon, manifestations of
digital flow in fashion brands’ FOOH content and observable expressions of audi -
ence response in public comments on Instagram. To address this purpose, a grounded
theory–based qualitative approach was adopted following Strauss and Corbin (1990).
Grounded theory provides systematic procedures for examining conditions, actions,
interactions, and conceptual relationships within empirically observed social phenom -
ena and for developing categories grounded in qualitative data (Strauss, 1987; Strauss
& Corbin, 1990). Accordingly, the paradigm labels causal conditions, contextual condi -
tions, intervening conditions, action/interaction strategies, and consequences are used
as organizing categories to examine conceptual relationships in the data and do not
imply empirically demonstrated causal effects or temporal sequences. Given the evolv -
ing nature of FOOH and digital flow within social media contexts, this approach was
considered appropriate for identifying emerging patterns while allowing existing theo -
retical concepts to inform the analysis without predetermining its categories.
FOOH represents a relatively recent form of digital brand communication for which
scholarly research remains limited. Its experiential characteristics cannot be fully cap -
tured through numerical indicators alone and require qualitative interpretation of how
flow-related manifestations are expressed through content characteristics and observ -
able audience expressions. Grounded theory, informed by symbolic interactionism,
emphasizes the situated construction of meaning through social interaction and there -
fore provides an appropriate basis for examining how users engage with and interpret
FOOH content within the social media environment. Public comments were analyzed
as situated textual and affective expressions related to audience experience, providing an
interpretive basis for examining manifestations associated with digital flow in relation to
FOOH content.
The analysis combined theoretical sensitization with iterative, data-driven category
development. Csikszentmihalyi’s flow theory provided the initial theoretical orientation,
while coding remained open to patterns and relationships emerging from the FOOH
content and user comments. Theoretical concepts and emergent empirical patterns were
continuously compared throughout the analytical process, allowing the data to shape
category development while retaining theoretical sensitivity to flow-related experiences.
The specific use of flow theory as a sensitizing framework is described in the following
section.

Sampling and case selection
FOOH content in digital media environments typically takes the form of short-form
video and is most frequently disseminated through Instagram Reels. Accordingly, FOOH
content posted on the Reels of fashion brands’ official Instagram accounts was selected
as the case study sample. Before sample selection, a preliminary search was conducted
using the English keywords “fooh” and “fake out of home” on Google, as well as the
Instagram hashtag #fooh. Google searches were effective for identifying major fashion
brand cases through relevant news articles, whereas Instagram hashtag searches were
less suitable for isolating cases aligned with this study’s objectives. Because FOOH con -
stitutes a commercial advertising format, brands rarely employ the term as a hashtag on
their official accounts. In contrast, Instagram accounts belonging to digital marketing
agencies and FOOH production companies were useful for identifying a diverse range of
cases and trends. Reflecting these characteristics, the data collection strategy combined
the complementary strengths of Google and Instagram searches. The detailed proce -
dures are outlined below.
First, fashion brands were identified through a preliminary Google-based media
search. The search was conducted from May 1 to May 31, 2025, using combinations
of English keywords, including “fashion, ” “FOOH, ” and “fake out of home. ” The search
yielded 284 news articles addressing fashion-related FOOH cases. These articles served
as the media corpus for brand identification rather than as individual analytical cases.
Brand names associated with identifiable FOOH cases were extracted from the articles,
and repeated references to the same brand across multiple articles were consolidated,
resulting in 46 unique fashion brands. At the brand-selection stage, the frequency with
which each brand was mentioned in the 284-article corpus was calculated. To focus the
subsequent analysis on brands demonstrating substantial visibility in FOOH-related
media coverage, brands mentioned fewer than ten times were excluded. Twenty of the
46 identified brands met the criterion of at least ten media mentions and were retained
for subsequent Instagram screening.
Next, all Instagram Reels posted on the official accounts of the twenty selected fashion
brands were systematically screened to identify candidate FOOH posts. The observa -
tion period was defined as April 2023 through April 2025, beginning with the release
of an early prominent FOOH case identified in the preliminary review by Jacquemus,
which recorded the highest number of views among the cases examined in this study
(48.9 million views). Based on the characteristics of FOOH identified in the theoreti -
cal review, purposive sampling was subsequently employed to select cases demonstrat -
ing substantial public visibility and observable audience engagement. View counts were

treated as indicators of the extent to which FOOH content had been exposed to and
circulated among audiences, whereas likes were regarded as observable indicators of
audience endorsement and engagement (Lee & Lee, 2024). Previous studies have used
comparable platform metrics to characterize substantial social media visibility and
engagement, including more than 500,000 views for widely circulated content and more
than 10,000 likes for a high level of social endorsement on Instagram (Kim et al., 2015;
Lee & Lee, 2024; Lutfeali et al., 2020). Accordingly, thresholds of at least 500,000 views
and 10,000 likes were adopted to identify high-visibility and high-engagement FOOH
cases. The two criteria were applied jointly because visibility and engagement represent
complementary dimensions of social media response. High view counts indicate sub -
stantial exposure but do not necessarily imply active audience response, whereas likes
indicate explicit engagement without independently establishing the breadth of content
circulation. The combined criteria therefore enabled the study to focus on FOOH cases
characterized by both extensive circulation and substantial observable audience engage -
ment. Based on these considerations, the sampling criteria were established as shown in
Table 2, and only Reels satisfying all specified criteria were retained in the final sample.
As an additional verification step, the FOOH content archive (fooh.com) was searched
using the “Fashion & Apparel” category to identify potentially relevant cases that might
not have been captured through the brand-account screening procedure. Final inclusion
decisions were reviewed by two evaluators, both holding doctoral degrees in fashion-
related fields, and disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was
reached. This procedure resulted in a final sample of 115 FOOH Reels.

Ethical considerations and de‑identification
The study was conducted as a non-interventional analysis of pre-existing, publicly acces-
sible social media data comprising FOOH content and associated comments posted
exclusively on the official Instagram accounts of the sampled fashion brands. No pri -
vate accounts, direct messages, or access-restricted content were accessed, and the
researchers neither interacted with individual commenters nor sought to identify them.
The research involved no participant recruitment, direct interaction with individuals,
or physical or psychological intervention. Before qualitative coding, usernames, dis -
play names, account handles, and other direct account identifiers were removed from
the working analytic corpus and, where necessary for data organization, replaced with
non-identifying study codes. No contact information or other private account informa -
tion was collected, and identifying information was not used as an analytical variable at
any stage of the study. De-identification measures were implemented to minimize the
possibility of identifying individual users in the reporting of the findings (Chilman et al.,
2021). For excerpts selected for publication, any remaining personal account mentions
or other potentially identifying references were removed or replaced with standardized
identifiers or generic descriptors (e.g., user). Where necessary, punctuation and sentence
structure were minimally adjusted to reduce the risk of traceability while preserving the
substantive meaning, tone, and analytic interpretation of the original comments (Neu -
gebauer et al., 2025). These publication-stage modifications were limited to the excerpts
reported in the manuscript and did not alter the substantive textual content used for
qualitative coding and analysis.
Data analysis
Data were analyzed following the three-stage grounded theory procedures of open cod -
ing, axial coding, and selective coding described by Strauss and Corbin (1990). The anal -
ysis proceeded iteratively through coding, constant comparison, analytic memo-writing,
and category refinement. Each Instagram Reel constituted a case-level analytical unit,
within which the video, accompanying caption, and associated comments were exam -
ined as complementary analytical materials. The 8,794 text-bearing comments consti -
tuting the primary analytic corpus were subjected to qualitative coding, whereas the
broader comparison corpus of non-textual responses, including emoji-only and account-
handle-only comments, was used as contextual evidence for interpreting recurring pat -
terns of audience expression and engagement.
In the first stage, open coding was conducted to identify meaningful concepts emerg -
ing from the data. The visual data were reviewed by the researcher through more than
fifteen repeated viewings, during which salient observations and preliminary concepts
were documented in analytic memos. The videos were comparatively examined with
particular attention to visual techniques, narrative structures, sensory characteristics,
and other analytically significant features. Relevant news articles were also consulted

Results
This study conducted a qualitative grounded theory investigation of the central phe -
nomenon, which concerns the digital flow experiences manifested in fashion brands’
Instagram FOOH content. Specifically, it analyzed the causal conditions, contextual con-
ditions, intervening conditions, action/interaction strategies, and consequences related
to digital flow in fashion FOOH content, and subsequently derived an interpretive rela -
tional model. The model, presented in Fig. 1, conceptualizes the conceptual relationships
among categories organized around the central phenomenon of digital flow.
Causal conditions
The causal conditions refer to analytical conditions identified in relation to flow-related
engagement and its observable manifestations in fashion brands’ FOOH content. These
conditions were organized into two domains: (1) psychological attributes, and (2) tech -
nological attributes.
Desire for novel experience and enjoyment
From the perspective of psychological attributes, users’ comments reflected positive
interest in novel stimuli and enjoyable experiences, including interest in unexpected
digital content and visually distinctive experiences. As illustrated by comments such as
“What I want for watching, ” “Unusual, ” “Must have been so exciting to see, ” “Inspiring, ”
and “Who made this incredible, ” users responded to fresh visual stimuli and enjoyment-
related aspects of the content, expressing observable affective reactions associated with
unexpected experiences, including astonishment, awe, and excitement. These comments
were interpreted as expressions of novelty-related engagement within the study’s flow-
related framework. This pattern was further interpreted in relation to the challenge–skill
condition (C1), insofar as novel and unfamiliar visual stimuli may introduce perceptual
and interpretive demands relevant to engagement with the content (Csikszentmihalyi,
2002).
Curiosity about production methods
With respect to technological attributes, users’ curiosity regarding production methods
was evident in recurring information-seeking and interpretive responses to FOOH con -
tent. Users exchanged hypotheses, questions, and answers in the comments concerning

production techniques, as illustrated by remarks such as “Can anyone tell me if this was
made with AI? how?” “Is this VR or they made it as OOH?, ” “What motion edit is this
and what’s the software name?, ” “Guys, this is not AI. It’s CGI art. It’s a very hard pro -
cess involving tracking, adding 3D objects, texturing, and compositing, ” and “For those
asking if it is AI nope. It is very good CGI. ” Recent technologies frequently mentioned
included AI, CGI art, VR, and VFX. The exchanges observed in the comments suggest
that users approached the technical complexity of FOOH as a subject for interpretation
and discussion. Accordingly, this pattern was interpreted in relation to the flow condi -
tion concerning the balance between challenge and skill (C1), particularly the interpre -
tive demands involved in identifying and discussing production methods.
Contextual conditions
Contextual conditions refer to the specific environmental factors identified in relation
to flow-related engagement and experiential manifestations. In other words, they rep -
resent the concrete situations and settings within which digital flow-related responses
were expressed and interpreted. In this study, the contextual conditions were classified
into two domains: (1) the non-physical environment of the digital media space to which
users are exposed, and (2) the physical environment that functions as the background
space within FOOH content.
Familiarity with digital media
From the perspective of the non-physical environment, digital media constitute a sym -
bolic space linked to cultures of fashion-content consumption. Instagram users typically
exhibit interest in new technologies and a high level of involvement with digital media
content (Rocamora, 2017). Within social media–based experiences, users’ familiar -
ity with media may support more fluent interpretation of digital content (Papacharissi,
2015). FOOH operates within this digital media environment. As reflected in comments
such as “Serious props to this new digital ad, ” “ Again, fabulous digital, ” “Reminds me of
that absolutely new digital approach, ” and “Feels like it’s going to blow up on TikTok, ”
these responses suggested recognition of FOOH as a digital media form (C1), along
with explicit interest in its technical staging (C2). The comment corpus also included
responses addressing composition, production techniques, and directorial intent, as well
as participatory commentary. These patterns illustrate interpretive engagement with
FOOH content across the observed comment environment.
Use of symbolic landmarks
From the perspective of the physical environment, FOOH content employed widely
recognizable public sites, particularly urban landmarks, as backdrops. Representative
examples included the Eiffel Tower and the Louvre Museum in Paris, Times Square and
the Empire State Building in New York, Tower Bridge and Big Ben in London, the Colos-
seum in Rome, and the Oriental Pearl Tower in Shanghai. These symbolic landmarks are
socially prominent and visually recognizable within digital media contexts. Comments
frequently referenced the real-world settings in which FOOH scenes were situated,
including places that users explicitly identified as familiar, as seen in comments such as
“I see the Empire State Building every day, ” “Just love Paris, ” “Only in NYC, ” and “Omg!

I love Regent St. ” The use of familiar places appeared to provide recognizable reference
points through which users related the digitally constructed scenes to known physical
settings. Such place-based familiarity was interpreted as contextually relevant to users’
engagement with the content (C2) and to the accessibility of the virtual scenes.
Intervening conditions
Intervening conditions refer to the analytical factors identified in relation to variations in
flow-related engagement and observable responses to fashion brands’ FOOH content. In
other words, these conditions provide an additional context for examining how action/
interaction strategies are interpreted in relation to flow-related responses. In this study,
the key intervening condition identified was users’ recognition of FOOH as a marketing
strategy and their corresponding evaluative responses.
Recognition of marketing strategy
As a component of advertising strategy, FOOH is grounded in a commercial orientation.
Fashion brands produced FOOH content with the underlying aim of promoting specific
products or reinforcing brand identity. Comments frequently acknowledged this mar -
keting intent, as illustrated by comments such as “Loving the marketing, ” “What a com-
mercial, ” “Super promo, ” and “The greatest marketing concept in decades. ” Such explicit
recognition of commercial intent formed part of the evaluative context through which
FOOH content was interpreted. When comments indicated alignment between the
perceived brand image and the message and visual strategies embedded in the content,
responses reflected recognition of the campaign’s communicative intent (C3). Although
some users expressed skeptical or negative reactions when overt commercial messag -
ing raised questions about authenticity (e.g., “too commercial”), many responses were
favorable and emphasized appreciation of the campaigns’ perceived strategic creativity,
as seen in remarks such as “This is brilliant, this AD is so smart” and “The marketing
never ceases to amaze. ”
Action/interaction strategies
Action/interaction strategies refer to the content and interactional approaches identified
in relation to flow-related responses (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). In this study, the princi -
pal strategies observed across the FOOH cases included: (1) the delivery of visual shock
and the fantastical representation of extraordinary situations, (2) the emphasis on con -
cise messaging, and (3) the provision of appropriate feedback and prompts for audience
response.
Visual shock and the fantastical representation of extraordinary situations
Csikszentmihalyi (1998) conceptualizes flow as an experience characterized by deep
involvement and focused attention. This attribute of flow is closely associated with the
expressive characteristics of FOOH content, which juxtaposes unreal yet novel scenes
with real locations to create visual shock. Users responded with spontaneous exclama -
tions such as “Genius, ” “Wow, ” “That vibe, ” “Masterpiece, ” “Impressive, ” “Incredible, ”
“Magnifique, ” “Magic, ” “Omg, ” and “That is freaky. ” These brief comments primarily
represent immediate affective reactions to the unexpected visual creativity. Given the

visually salient characteristics of the content, these responses were interpreted in rela -
tion to attentional engagement (C5). The comments further reflected reactions of sur -
prise, admiration, awe, excitement, and shock in response to the illusionistic visual
effects.
The narratives of FOOH content frequently incorporate familiar yet fantastical sce -
narios that may be readily imagined despite not occurring in everyday life. Examples
included oversized gift boxes pouring from the sky, a tram shaped like a giant bag tra -
versing city streets, and the appearance of imaginary animals. Although these scenarios
were fantastical, the comments suggest that they remained imaginatively recognizable
to users. Users responded to these imaginative scenarios, as reflected in comments such
as “I remember seeing a life-size King Kong on the Empire State Building when I was
young, remember it?” “What a dream!” “ A heavenly match, ” and “This is my fantasy. ” A
defining technical characteristic of FOOH is its flexible capacity to render imagination
as if it were real, without physical constraints. Comments linking these scenes to memo -
ries, dreams, and personal fantasies were interpreted in relation to imaginative partici -
pation and the control-related component of flow (C6). These responses indicate that
fantastical imagery could acquire personal and affective significance within the observed
comment environment.
Furthermore, Csikszentmihalyi (2002) notes that flow can arise when individuals
experience cognitive ambiguity that blurs the boundaries between reality and virtuality.
FOOH can be examined as a media form that visually stages this ambiguity. The findings
revealed perceptual ambiguity arising from the juxtaposition of digitally constructed
objects and real-world environments. This was evident in user remarks such as “Is that
real?” “Not VFX, real, ” “Looks great is real or AI, ” “I still don’t understand how this magic
works, ” and “Funny that people actually believe it. ” FOOH renders virtual objects within
real spaces with a high degree of verisimilitude, visually complicating the distinction
between digitally constructed and real elements. Users did not approach FOOH content
solely as something to be verified as fake. Instead, some comments engaged with the vir -
tual stimuli as if they formed part of the depicted reality. Such responses foregrounded
the realism and perceptual ambiguity of the FOOH scenes within the comment dis -

toward the represented scene and its apparent reality.
Emphasis on concise messaging
FOOH content commonly conveys concise narrative messages, reflecting the char -
acteristics of the Instagram platform, where attention must be captured rapidly.
Concise and explicit messages provide readily identifiable communicative goals (C3)
and a manageable level of interpretive complexity (C1). To achieve this, FOOH con -
tent typically employs narratives centered on symbolic objects, such as a brand’s
signature products, stores, logos, characters, and symbols. Representative exam -
ples include Jacquemus’s Bambino bag, adidas’s Superstar sneakers, Lacoste’s polo
shirt, and Ralph Lauren’s Polo Bear. When users encountered FOOH content fea -
turing objects from brands they already knew or favored, their comments reflected
both interest and familiarity with the featured brand or product (C6). This pattern

was evident in comments such as “This is so cool! I have this bag and love it” and
“It’s my favorite brand. ” Widely recognized symbolic objects thus function as salient
visual cues through which the brand’s message can be readily identified. In FOOH,
these objects are often rendered at exaggerated, oversized scales, a strategy that
heightens visual salience and draws attention to the content. This visual emphasis

Appropriate feedback and user action inducement
According to Nakamura and Csikszentmihalyi (2014), immediate feedback plays a
critical role in sustaining flow because it provides individuals with ongoing infor -
mation about their actions and progress within an activity. Such feedback can help
maintain continuity of engagement by making the consequences of action readily
perceptible. In the analyzed FOOH posts, fashion brands prompted user participa -
tion directly through the content. For example, Jacquemus encouraged interaction
through the prompt “Where will we see you next? Please comment on the city. ”
Brands also replied to comments and nested comments, with further user responses
observed within these exchanges (C4). Users, in turn, participated in bidirectional
exchanges by responding to brand prompts and contributing their own opinions.
Illustrative responses included “Barcelona, please!, ” “Brussels, ” “Peru, ” “One here in
Italy please, ” and “Where exactly are you located?” These exchanges demonstrated
the continuation of interaction beyond the initial viewing of the post and the active
extension of participation through comment-based feedback (C8). This continued
interaction was further interpreted within the flow-related framework in terms of
continued engagement in the exchange (C5) and user-initiated participation (C6).
Brands further operationalized feedback (C4) through offline activations that
materialized FOOH imagery in physical spaces. To commemorate the first anni -
versary of its initial FOOH upload, Jacquemus posted the message “We did it for
real, 1 year anniversary!” along with footage of an actual Bambino Bag car driving
through city streets. This elicited enthusiastic reactions, including “I feel I need to
get this car, ” “Original, ” and “Need more content like this. ” Additional cases involved
Jacquemus’s Seoul pop-up store, which replicated the Bambino Bag featured in its
FOOH campaign, and The North Face’s installation of a giant puffer jacket in Shang -
hai, both of which transformed virtual concepts into tangible experiences. These
feedback strategies provided additional opportunities for interaction around FOOH
content, particularly through comments and replies. The physical realization of pre -
viously encountered FOOH imagery enabled users to revisit and reinterpret the con -
tent, thereby reinforcing feedback-based interaction (C4). This continuity between
digital and physical experiences sustained audience interest, elicited positive evalua -
tive responses, and extended the communicative impact of FOOH beyond the origi -
nal post.

Consequences
Consequences refer to the outcome-related patterns identified in relation to the
action/interaction strategies and are reflected in flow-related responses. Two over -
arching outcome patterns were identified: (1) a positive pattern, characterized by the
extension of flow-related engagement, and (2) a negative pattern, characterized by
constraints on such engagement in relation to negative or critical user responses.
Reinforcement of the digital flow experience
Aesthetic responses and purchase‑oriented interest toward the brand According to Csik-
szentmihalyi (1998), flow achieved through aesthetic experience becomes inscribed as a
meaningful value in an individual’s consciousness. FOOH content is created by mobiliz -
ing technologically generated illusions to deliver brand messages in sensorial ways. The
visually immersive presentation of brand narratives was interpreted in relation to con -
centration-related aspects of flow (C5). These aesthetic and flow-related manifestations
were accompanied by curiosity and favorable responses toward the products featured in
FOOH content, as evidenced by user comments including “hope one day to buy a bag, ”
“When are we getting them?, ” “How can I order??, ” “[user] give me please, ” “Where can I
get that shoe box, ” and “Is this available in store now. ” These remarks provide observable
indications of product interest and purchase-oriented expressions related to the FOOH
content. These responses, interpreted in conjunction with other flow-related manifesta -
tions identified in the analysis, suggest a correspondence between flow-related aesthetic
experiences and favorable evaluations of the featured products and brands. The combina-
tion of positive aesthetic responses, product interest, and purchase-oriented comments
suggests that FOOH may be accompanied by product-oriented interest and favorable
responses toward the featured products and brands. From a strategic perspective, this
pattern highlights the potential relevance of FOOH content to brand-related audience
responses.
Emotional satisfaction through  enjoyment Users responded to visual stimuli with
comments that framed viewing as a playful experience, with many comments express -
ing enjoyment and positive affective responses. A substantial number of comments
conveyed positive affect and enjoyment, a pattern evident in remarks including “Love, ”

am here to see, ” “Iconic, ” “Just fantastic, ” “This was one of my favourite moments, ”
“More of this please, ” “Watched this twice, ” and “Need more content like this. ” These
responses reflect spontaneous expressions of admiration and enjoyment, as well as
observable indications of repeated viewing and continued interest in similar content.
When interpreted within the flow framework, these patterns were considered in rela -
tion to enjoyment and attentional engagement (C2, C5), without reference to explicit
extrinsic rewards.

Inhibition of flow‑related engagement associated with adverse user responses
Flow originates in a conscious process in which the actor actively directs attention
according to an autotelic orientation (Csikszentmihalyi, 2002). When FOOH stimuli
are evaluated as inconsistent with users’ interests or expectations, negative affective and
critical responses may be interpreted as constraints on the conditions associated with
flow-related engagement. In the present data, such responses were particularly evi -
dent when users questioned the authenticity, relevance, or ethical implications of brand
messages.

piece commemorating the Chelsea Football Club in London, depicting an oversized mint-
colored jersey draped over Tower Bridge. This content drew negative reactions from

Discussion
This study applied grounded theory to derive a paradigm model of digital flow as mani -
fested through fashion brands’ Instagram FOOH content and observable audience
responses, followed by a refinement process through selective coding to integrate the
findings into higher-order analytical dimensions. By organizing these categories around
the central phenomenon, the study generated a coherent theoretical framework, thereby
extending the interpretation of digital flow within platform-based fashion communica -
tion. Through the integration of the theoretical literature and empirical findings, three
author-derived higher-order analytical dimensions were identified: cognitive, multisen -
sory, and affective. These dimensions represent an interpretive synthesis of the findings,
capturing how multiple factors interact across distinct yet interrelated aspects of digital
flow.
Cognitive dimension: focused attention and self‑directedness
In the cognitive dimension, digital flow is interpreted in relation to observable responses
associated with focused attention when encountering FOOH content, active exploration
of the material, and a tendency to interpret the content’s context in a self-directed man -
ner. In other words, this dimension foregrounds attentional concentration and active
engagement as cognitive qualities relevant to the flow experience (Nakamura & Csiksze -
ntmihalyi, 2014).
Social media platforms provide environmental conditions for real-time response and
immediate interaction. At the same time, they encourage continuous partial attention,
in which users’ focus disperses amid the abundance of content, often leading to rapid
disengagement. Fashion brands strategically utilized the properties of FOOH to differ -
entiate their content within habitual scrolling environments on social platforms. When
clear and highly intuitive messaging was combined with surreal expressive techniques,
FOOH content presented strong visual immediacy within the platform environment.
This mechanism can be understood through the concept of suspension of disbelief.
Upon encountering digital content, users temporarily defer critical judgment of ele -
ments that are impossible or illogical in physical reality and engage with the narrative

according to its internal logic (Böcking, 2008; Dalby, 2016). Even while recognizing that
FOOH is fabricated, the observed responses suggest that users could engage playfully
with virtual stimuli according to the internal logic of the depicted reality. From this per -
spective, digital flow may extend beyond suspension of disbelief to concentrated engage-
ment with the mediated scene, whereby virtual stimuli become a salient focus within
users’ expressed responses. From a media-ecological perspective, fashion brands’ FOOH
content is designed with careful consideration of the characteristics of attention-inten -
sive and visually immersive media environments. The findings further suggest that flow-
related engagement was expressed not only through brief reactions but also through
more interpretive and participatory forms of response. This interpretation is consist -
ent with Csikszentmihalyi’s (2002) account of flow as involving intense concentration in
which attention becomes strongly organized around the ongoing activity.
Multisensory dimension: sensory‑integrated experience
Digital flow in the multisensory dimension was characterized by the sensory-integrated
qualities of FOOH content. This experience was not generated through the explicit
delivery of information, but rather through the orchestration of condensed perceptual
cues. These cues were primarily visual and auditory, and at times, suggested haptic and
spatial qualities. In digital media environments, this type of sensory integration medi -
ated the visual signification and embodied perception of fashion by translating material
and bodily qualities into perceptible and interpretable audiovisual forms.
The findings indicated that fashion brands prioritized audiovisual presence and per -
ceptual impact over communicating functional product information in their FOOH
content, with these features interpreted in relation to digital flow-related experience.
As a form of short-format media, FOOH content combined brief yet intensified kinetic
movement, amplified visual exaggeration, and sound design that evoked texture and
spatial atmosphere. These elements were interpreted as inviting haptic imagination and
supporting sensory involvement in the virtual scene. The resulting perceptual configu -
ration presented digitally constructed scenes with a heightened impression of realism.
CGI- and VFX-based staging further supported this process by converting the sensory
logic of "wearing" into the experiential logic of "seeing," providing a basis for interpreting
the mood of a style and the brand’s multisensory image through visually mediated sen -
sory imagination despite limited exposure time. These results suggest that haptic imagi -
nation represents an important experiential aspect of how FOOH is strategically used in
fashion brands’ social media communication.
FOOH frequently used representational techniques that disrupted ordinary perceptual
expectations. For example, the content collapsed conventional scale relations or posi -
tioned familiar objects within unfamiliar scenarios. These techniques were interpreted
as forms of sensory dissonance associated with heightened visual salience and aligned
with demand for novelty and heightened stimulation characteristic of fast-scrolling
media environments. The findings suggest that responses to perceptually striking con -
tent were associated with exploratory and multisensory forms of interpretation rather
than immediate visual recognition alone. Under these conditions, sensory integration
can be understood as an interpretive dimension of flow-related experience. Compressed
narrative structures and high-intensity audiovisual cues were interpreted in relation to

attentional and continued engagement with the unfolding content. This process simulta-
neously reconfigured fashion’s inherent multisensoriality and symbolic meaning within
digital spaces, while accompanying affective responses and observable indications of
continued involvement, such as reported rewatching and requests for similar content.
These results indicate that multisensory integration in FOOH is not merely the jux -
taposition of stimuli, but rather a structured experiential configuration interpreted in
relation to focused attention and affective engagement. Strategically, FOOH provides
fashion brands with a means of presenting content as a sensory-integrated experience
while incorporating flow-related experiential qualities within platform-based communi -
cation. This interpretation is consistent with Csikszentmihalyi’s (2002) conceptualization
of flow as an experience in which attention becomes strongly concentrated on the ongo -
ing activity, particularly in activities that are experienced as intrinsically engaging and
aesthetically rewarding.
Affective dimension: voluntary participation and creative enjoyment
In the affective dimension, digital flow is interpreted through users’ observable expres -
sions of positive affect, voluntary participation, and creative engagement with FOOH
content. FOOH does not directly compel participation; instead, its sensory qualities pro-
vide a context for self-initiated forms of engagement. Csikszentmihalyi (1998) empha -
sizes the intrinsically rewarding nature of flow and the voluntary investment of attention
in activities pursued for their own sake. From this perspective, the sensory and affective
qualities of FOOH provide a basis for understanding voluntary engagement with fashion
brand content.
Positive flow-related responses to FOOH were manifested in users’ self-directed
enjoyment of brand content and in the creative expressions that followed. When users
responded favorably to FOOH content, they expressed preferences and personal inter -
pretations through likes and comments. The findings also revealed repeated viewing,
continued interest in similar content, and voluntary participation in comment-based
interaction. Such practices illustrate forms of audience participation that extend beyond
the initial act of viewing. Users’ interaction and creative input formed part of the vis -
ible participatory activity surrounding brand content within the platform. Accordingly,
users’ creative reinterpretation and extension of brand narratives can be understood
through the concept of playbour, a form of participatory labor in which play and labor
become intertwined and user-generated activity produces informational, cultural, and
economic value (Kücklich, 2005). Originally developed to explain productive forms of
digital play that simultaneously generate value, the concept is extended here to users’
creative participation in platform-based brand communication. Such participation was
reflected in comments and interactional exchanges that expressed enjoyment and were
accompanied by further responses and continued interaction. These patterns were inter-
preted in relation to involvement and enjoyment within the flow framework (Nakamura
& Csikszentmihalyi, 2014), while also illustrating how users participated in the interpre -
tation and extension of brand meanings within the platform. In this sense, users’ volun -
tary participation can function as a strategic asset by extending both the visibility and
symbolic value of fashion brands in digital environments.

At the same time, the affective dimension of flow-related engagement was interpreted
in relation to intervening conditions, particularly users’ recognition of marketing strat -
egy. Although users generally understood that FOOH is conceived and produced as
advertising content for brand communication, positive affective responses were con -

directed at fashion brands associated with insufficient creativity and sustainability. In
particular, copycat controversies arising from similar concepts and executions con -

tial of FOOH depends not only on sensory impact and novelty, but also on the extent
to which its creative and ethical framing is compatible with users’ expectations of the
brand.
Conclusions
This study investigated flow-related experiential manifestations in fashion brands’ Ins -
tagram FOOH content through a qualitative analysis based on grounded theory. An in-
depth examination of FOOH videos and captions, along with user comments, revealed
a set of psychological and technological conditions associated with digital flow, contex -
tual characteristics of both digital and physical environments, and users’ recognition of
marketing strategy as an important evaluative condition. The principal action/interac -
tion strategies associated with flow-related engagement included visual shock and the
fantastical representation of extraordinary situations, the emphasis on concise messag -
ing, and the provision of feedback and prompts for comment-based communication
and user participation. These strategies corresponded with distinct patterns of audi -
ence response, reflecting both supportive and constraining forms of digital flow-related
engagement. Contemporary users increasingly prioritize sensorial pleasure and affective
connection, rather than the passive reception of information, in their interactions with
brands (McKinsey & Company 2022). In light of these findings, flow-related engagement
may be relevant to favorable brand-oriented responses. Building on these findings, the
study proposes the model presented in Fig.  1, which illustrates the factor-specific char -
acteristics and relational structure associated with digital flow in fashion brands’ FOOH
content. By integrating patterns related to focused attention, involvement, and intrin -
sic enjoyment, the model developed in this study provides a conceptual basis for under -
standing the key elements, relationships, and outcome-related patterns of digital flow,
while accounting for the bidirectional dynamics between fashion brands and users.
The findings not only identify conditions and relational patterns associated with digi -
tal flow, but also indicate that the manifestation of flow-related engagement may vary
according to users’ evaluations. Notably, ethical concerns and evaluative responses
toward digital creativity emerged as significant factors associated with how FOOH was
perceived and evaluated as branded creative work. Social media platforms possess a dual
character in that they facilitate the diffusion of brands’ creative expression while simul -
taneously intensifying surveillance of content similarity (Bruns, 2008; Rocamora, 2017).

In this respect, the present study sheds light on both the supportive and critical audience
responses associated with flow-related engagement observed within communication
processes. Users encountering FOOH content in digital media did not simply respond
favorably to brands’ visual stimuli. Instead, their comments demonstrated active evalu -
ation of creative distinctiveness and brand identity. Controversies over similarity and
creativity were interpreted as inhibition of flow-related engagement, consistent with
the view that creativity involves not only the pursuit of newness but also ethical judg -
ment regarding creative expression (Glăveanu, 2014). More broadly interpreted, flow-
related engagement cannot be understood solely in terms of visual shock and sensory
stimulation. Its expression also appeared to vary with users’ sociocultural expectations
regarding what constitutes legitimate creative expression. Accordingly, in planning and
producing digital content, including FOOH, fashion brands should complement sensory
strategies with a creative sensibility that reflects their role as tastemakers. This includes a
commitment to brand authenticity, social congruence, and ethical responsibility.
A further implication concerns the political economy of participation that accompa -
nies flow-oriented engagement on social media platforms. The findings suggest that the
forms of user participation observed in relation to FOOH content can be interpreted
both as creative engagement and as a form of digital labor. Digital labor refers to unpaid
and voluntary practices that users routinely perform on online platforms, including
social media. These activities have generated debate because, although users participate
with enjoyment, their creative and affective contributions are appropriated for corpo -
rate commercial gain without adequate compensation (Scholz, 2012; Terranova, 2000).
In engaging with FOOH content, users contribute time, attention, energy, and creative
input through observable practices such as likes, comments, and reply-based interac -
tion, thereby generating forms of communicative activity that can hold value for fashion
brands. Brands may strategically incorporate such user activity into broader marketing
practices and processes of value creation. From a digital labor perspective, users’ vol -
untary and pleasurable participation with FOOH can thus be understood as productive
labor that may contribute to corporate value creation. While such participation may
provide intrinsic rewards, including aesthetic satisfaction and emotional enjoyment, it
can simultaneously be incorporated into brands’ commercial activities. This dynamic
raises questions about the recognition and compensation of user contributions, which
warrants critical scholarly and industry attention. Moreover, because FOOH circulates
through highly visible social media environments while maintaining a commercial ori -
entation as advertising, its dual nature requires careful consideration. In the present
findings, concerns about authenticity or creative novelty were accompanied by critical
responses interpreted as constraints on flow-related engagement. These findings raise
broader questions about the recognition of user contributions and offer practical impli -
cations for developing sustainable and mutually beneficial strategies in digital fashion
communication.
The divergent positions observed in audience responses associated with digital
flow-related engagement were most frequently concentrated on fashion brands that
were perceived as lacking in creativity and sustainability. This pattern suggests that,
before the planning and deployment of FOOH content, fashion brands should antici -
pate and address environmental concerns, ethical violations, and perceived deficits

in originality, including similarity-based controversies and accusations of imitation,
in a substantively grounded and sincere manner. Notably, some controversial FOOH
cases were accompanied by substantial likes and comment-based interaction. Criti -
cal responses were also highly visible within some comment threads. These findings
suggest directions for future research to clarify how controversy-driven engagement
develops within platform infrastructures and translates into brand communication
outcomes over time. Comparative analyses across platforms and cultural contexts
would help determine whether the relative speed and intensity of negative diffusion
reflect platform-specific affordances, audience norms, or broader sociocultural expec -
tations regarding creativity and sustainability. Methodologically, future work could
integrate qualitative interpretation with behavioral indicators, such as engagement
velocity, sentiment trajectories in comment threads, and network diffusion patterns.
This would clarify how controversial FOOH content may transition from momentary
attention-related responses to longer-term reputational outcomes.
However, this study has several limitations related to its qualitative design, pub -
lic comment data, and sampling criteria. Instagram commenters represent a self-
selected subset of viewers; therefore, the comments should be interpreted as publicly
expressed responses rather than as representative evidence of the psychological expe -
riences of the entire audience. The English-only criterion may also have excluded
culturally and linguistically distinctive responses, while automated or bot-generated
comments could not be definitively identified despite data screening. In addition,
selecting Reels with at least 500,000 views and 10,000 likes introduced a selection
effect favoring relatively high-visibility and high-engagement cases. Accordingly, the
findings may more strongly reflect such cases and should not be considered repre -
sentative of fashion FOOH content on Instagram as a whole. Future research should
incorporate multilingual data, lower-engagement contrast cases, and complementary
methods such as interviews, surveys, or experiments to examine audience experiences
across broader contexts. The primary contribution of this study lies in establishing a
theoretical foundation for understanding digital flow in fashion FOOH communica -
tion by identifying the key factors and interactional relationships involved in digital
flow-related engagement, thereby informing strategic approaches to fashion commu -
nication. As digital media and fashion content continue to evolve, scholarly inquiry in
this area should develop accordingly. The interpretive relational model of digital flow
proposed in this study provides a conceptual framework for future investigations.
