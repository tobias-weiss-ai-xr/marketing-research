Abstract
Technological advances are beginning to change the timeline and finality of death, and these emerging death technologies
affect the dying, survivors, and society in unprecedented ways. We systematize current, emerging, and imagined techno -
logically mediated or enabled human-death relationships through our REAL framework, which considers three areas of
death technologies: digital Remembrance, life Extension, and digital AfterLife. These technologies extend consumption
beyond the consumer life cycle into a consumer death cycle, raising profound ethical and marketing questions. Based
on a thematic synthesis of recurring ethical principles, we conceptualize the CARE principles— Control, Accountability,
Respect, and Equity—and illustrate implications for the (future) deceased, the bereaved, providers, and policymakers. Our
work offers a starting point for understanding the complex ethical and marketing challenges of death technologies and
the digital afterlife by developing a conceptual and ethical framework that clarifies how these technologies are organized,
embedded, and governed within market systems. We also extend foundational concepts of consumer vulnerability, co-
creation, and extended self into posthumous contexts, and lays out a research agenda.

In this conceptual and interdisciplinary paper, we draw
on death studies in marketing (e.g., Dobscha, 2016; Sud -
bury-Riley et al., 2024) alongside work in AI ethics (e.g.,
Morse & Birnhack, 2022; Öhman & Floridi, 2018) to lay
out a coherent conceptual framework for understanding
death technologies as an emerging market phenomenon. We
then turn to the ethical challenges these technological devel-
opments raise, particularly around dignity, privacy, and
autonomy, and introduce the CARE principles of Control,
Accountability, Respect, and Equity to provide ethical guid-
ance for organizing consent, responsibility, access, and gov-
ernance in posthumous markets. These principles speak to
the concerns of multiple stakeholders: the (future) deceased,
the bereaved, death technology providers, marketers, and
policymakers. In doing so, our work provides three main
contributions to the literature. First, by laying out the REAL
and CARE frameworks, we offer an integrated perspective
on technologically mediated human–death relationships.
This perspective links what death technologies do and
enable with normative guidance regarding how they should
be designed and governed. Second, we add knowledge to
the evolving research on technologically-driven life exten -
sion, digital afterlife, and immortality in marketing (e.g.,
Lima & Belk, 2022; Takhar et al., 2025), while also expand-
ing the scope of AI ethics, which has so far overlooked such
challenges (e.g., Rana, 2024). Third, we extend the founda-
tional concepts of extended self (Belk, 1988, 2013, 2024),
consumer vulnerability (Hermann et al., 2024), and value
co-creation (Vargo et al., 2022) into posthumous contexts,
conceptualizing a consumer death cycle that continues iden-
tity work and market participation after biological death.

they do not account for the ways new technologies extend

As we, society, further embed digital technologies into
our everyday routines, mourning rituals increasingly unfold
in online spaces. Metaverses (Kennell & Šuligoj, 2024),
social media platforms (Navon & Noy, 2023), memorial
websites (Myers & Donley, 2024), and live-streamed cer -
emonies (DeBerry-Spence & Trujillo-Torres, 2022) cre -
ate enduring digital tributes where the bereaved continue
to share memories, images, messages, and digital goods
well beyond traditional rites. These technology-mediated
rituals, for example, help bridge geographical divides, per -
haps soothing the pain of loss through collective grieving
while ensuring distant friends and family can partake in
remembrance. While some embrace these innovations to
remain part of the lives of their loved ones, others argue
that prolonged digital mourning prevents closure and might
complicate the grieving process (Beaunoyer & Guitton,
2021). Salisbury and Nenkov (2016), for example, explain
that some death-related technologies may transform griev -
ing into an open-ended and monetized process rather than
a finite emotional experience. By lacking definitive end -
points, digital memorials can intensify death anxieties,
leading the bereaved to cling to the deceased’s online per -
sona for fear of letting go (Greenberg et al., 1986; Fischer-
Preßler et al., 2019). On the other hand, SST (Carstensen,
1992) suggests that older adults might deliberately limit or
deactivate these online tributes to preserve emotional well-
being, prioritizing intimate connections over indefinite
virtual engagement (Carstensen, 1992; Gourmelen et al.,
2016). Here, the possibility of an indefinite digital presence
brings a critical duality: while it may emotionally connect
people across distances, it risks prolonging psychological
tension.
These tensions extend beyond private mourning to public
tragedies and celebrity deaths, which often prompt large-
scale consumption of memorabilia, tribute products, and
charitable donations in the deceased’s name (Marcoux,
2017; Willon, 2025). Such practices foster collective griev-
ing and cultural memory but also highlight the commercial-
ization of death (Dobscha, 2016). In this sense, grief is not
merely an emotional response but also a structured cultural
practice embedded in market dynamics (Huang et al., 2018).
Whether through private remembrance or public commem -
oration, mourning has become a negotiation between grief
and consumer culture, shaped by technologies that expand
both memory and market. These developments stress the
need for a systematic understanding of how technologies
mediate human-death relationships and reshape consump -
tion itself. Human–death relationships describe the evolv -
ing socio-technical interactions among the living, the dead,
and digital systems that structure how consumers antici -
pate, experience, and continue relations beyond death. They
encompass both material connections (e.g., bodies, artifacts)
and symbolic connections (e.g., memory, legacy, identity).
These relationships reflect a transition from the consumer
life cycle—bounded by birth, growth, and physical death—
to a broader consumer death cycle, where posthumous con-
sumption, communication, and identity management persist
through technological mediation.
In our work, we focus on three areas of current, emerg -
ing, and imagined technologically mediated or enabled
human-death relationships, which we organize within the
REAL framework: (1) Remembrance, (2) Extension, and
(3) AfterLife. These three areas are not mutually exclu -
sive. Together, they represent a set of forces beginning to
change how consumers think about their finite existence,
death, and their identity, including their post-mortem
legacy. These forces reflect the importance of technology
to shape and enable remembrance, perpetuate life, and
enable digital immortality. Rather than viewing death as
an absolute end, consumers might increasingly encoun -
ter it as an opportunity for continued identity work and
identity development after death. If the self continues
to evolve throughout life (Gallagher, 2013), and a new
form of digital life begins after biological death (Bas -
sett, 2022), then the self’s epistemic boundaries are no
longer constrained by the physicality of human beings.
Consumers and their families thus may still purposefully
consume and work on their identities even after the decay
of their biological bodies. In other words, the traditional
consumer life cycle extends to the consumer death cycle,
and each domain of the REAL framework captures a dis -
tinctive yet overlapping stage of this cycle.

Remembrance
Technology may have a facilitating role in easing us through
grief as with online memorials (i.e., online spaces for a
deceased person or group to be mourned and/or remem -
bered; Öhman & Floridi, 2017). Gabel (2015) suggests that
there has been a decoupling of death into physical and social
components and that while the physical self dies, eternal life
for the social self may be possible through online memori -
alization. For those posting to online memorial sites, Gabel
(2015) sees a joint cathartic process in recalling times spent
with the deceased, aided by particular possessions remem -
bered as acting as linking objects (e.g., cars, clothes, music;
V olkan, 2021). In addition, Gabriel ( 2015) notes that such
reminiscences are used in the co-construction of the self
(Belk, 2013) of the deceased. More accurately, we see this
as the co-reconstruction of the self (Russell & Levy, 2012).
Bassett (2022, p. 7) noted that, “… the term ‘digital immor-
tality’ seemed an appropriate way to describe the research….
However, …it is already inept when used to describe the
‘posthumous persistence of digital data’.” We suggest that
digital remembrance is one form of digital immortality.
And, from a marketing perspective, these practices extend
identity projects into the posthumous realm. They position
the deceased as participants in the value creation of digital
platforms and service providers that monetize engagement
and memory.
Roberts and Vidal (2000) report four large online memo-
rial sites as early as 1996. They included both static content,
such as profiles resembling obituaries, and dynamic content,
like “guestbooks” that allowed visitors to add comments.
Such sites still exist and have become even more dynamic,
with close family and friends often addressing the deceased
directly as if they were still alive, and more distant visitors
addressing the bereaved and seeking to insert themselves in
the deceased’s life (Brubaker et al., 2013). The co-recon -
struction of the deceased’s identity is evident in these more
distant comments by acquaintances. Church ( 2013) com -
pares the interchanges and remembrances on these sites to
the memento mori popular in the West from the 16th to early
19th centuries. More specifically, he suggests a similarity in
their tone to the mid-18th century “garden romance grav -
escapes” identified by Morris ( 2006). These involved both
celebrating the life of the deceased and communing with
their spirit.
But the Victorian cult of the dead (Hunter, 2000) was
already dying by the close of the 19th century:

remains of the dead. Rarely would a lock of hair be
kept by, to be worn as jewelry, nor did one dwell on

(Life) Extension
One of the key aspects of our existence is our never-ending
confrontation with human biological limitations (e.g., Arndt
et al., 2004; Bapuji et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2022; Li & Gal,
2024; Mecit et al., 2022; Park et al., 2022; Whitley et al.,
2022). At least for now (Cordeiro & Wood, 2023), death
seems to be the most persistent and frustrating limitation
of all (Mathras et al., 2016; Rindfleisch et al., 2009). Such
a condition is part of Bostrom’s ( 2005) transhumanist cri -
tique of human mortality. He offers a fable that invites us to
reconsider death as an unquestioned fact of life. The story
features a menacing dragon that demands human lives as
sacrifices, with society accepting this as inevitable. How -
ever, as society progresses, a scientist rejects this blind
acceptance and seeks a way to defeat the dragon. After years
of research, a high-tech weapon is developed to kill the
dragon. Despite this breakthrough, many resist the idea of
using it, viewing the suffering and death as serving a higher
purpose. Bostrom’s (2005) fable criticizes conservative bio-
ethicists who oppose bold approaches to life extension and
advocate for caution due to the technologies’ side effects on
humanity. Such caution, he says, prevents mastering aging
and shortens our lifespan.
The ethical argument Bostrom ( 2005) articulates is
quite straightforward: There are strong moral reasons for
the people to kill the dragon. The way we, as a society,
handle human decay and biological aging is ethically and
issue of consumer vulnerability, digital property rights, and
marketing ethics.
New practices of post-mortem identity management
include cemetery technology (“CemTech,” Allison et al.,
2024) and gravesite media such as QR codes and even
video screens embedded in tombstones (Cann, 2013;
Yancey, 2018). When scanned with a smartphone, a QR
code website presents photos, videos, and biographies of the
deceased, describing and showing their histories, appear -
ance, accomplishments, and personalities. Sometimes there
are guest registries on which visitors can also leave mes -
sages (Yancey, 2018). But Kasket ( 2019) worries that QR
codes will someday become technologically obsolete and
that agreeing to care for the QR tombstone or linked web -
site is probably a bad business arrangement in any case.
And there are still those who maintain that a granite tomb -
stone will still be there long after the internet has turned to
dust. Here, remembrance is explicitly commodified: grief
becomes a subscription service, subject to pricing, brand -
ing, and obsolescence. Something similar is happening with
automobile services (e.g., heated seats, speed boosts) that
can be remotely switched on and off by the auto company.
But the digital identity of the deceased is only one of
the concerns of the bereaved. Their do-it-yourself (DIY)
mourning practices (Cengiz & Rook, 2016; Lingel, 2013)
include messaging and talking to the dead, sometimes for
years after their death. Bassett ( 2022, p. 21) argues that
digital artifacts are more intimate and profound than the
physical artifacts of the dead: “This difference is because,
for many inheritors, these digital memories and messages
embody the essence of the dead.” She continues a bit later,
“These digitally mediated messages and memories may
indeed endure, and some may live on in perpetuity; or at
least until our digital essence disappears and a Google
search states we are code 404 file not found” (Bassett,
2022, p. 22). For the bereaved, these digital artifacts and
artifices can be an aid to grieving and conversations with
these digital memorials can be therapeutic. However, they
can also prolong grief and make it hard to give up these
felt connections. As Marcoux ( 2017) finds with those who
once bought Twin Trade Towers souvenirs to remember the
2001 attack by terrorist-hijacked planes, putting them away
is now a way to forget or only selectively or occasionally
remember. For the bereaved, Sisto ( 2021) finds that often
the dead desire(d) to be remembered, while the living desire
to forget. Bassett ( 2022) urges that memorial sites also
build mechanisms that assist forgetting. And just as people
sometimes create DNR (do not resuscitate) living wills, she
recommends that people should also have the option to cre-
ate DDNR (digital do not reanimate) orders in the same
way and to make their wishes binding for their next of kin.

limit in expanding the human lifespan and healthspan, Cor-

relying on cryonic procedures to preserve the human body,

the declaration of legal death, the person is cooled down to
−196 °C, so in the future, it can be brought back to life with
new technologies (Farman, 2020). Those in the US who
wish to hire this type of service have the option to freeze
their whole bodies for US$200,000 or US$80,000 for just

costs almost the same, and there is a monthly membership
fee starting at as low as €25 (Tomorrow.bio, 2024). It is then
speculated that the consumer will be resurrected in a stron -
ger, healthier body, much like the portrayals in the Netflix
movie Oxygen (Lima & Belk, 2022), the Amazon Prime
series Upload, and the novels Immortal Life (Bing, 2017)
and Mindscan (Sawyer, 2005). Although extending life
through cryonics implies a certain continuity of the physi -
cal self (i.e., the body), all of this will only be possible if
every ACGT (DNA type) in the human body is converted
into 010101 in an artificial, silicon-based platform. That is,
a datafied body is what will enable consumers to achieve
superlongevity in the near future, according to transhuman-
ists, and thereby further the process that Cordeiro and Wood
(2023) call “deprogramming” death. Here, death becomes
a subscription-based promise, marketed as a long-term bet
on science. Cryonics is less a proven medical treatment than
a branded product offering consumers the possibility of
immortality on layaway (Cave, 2020).
Radically extending human lifespan and healthspan, and
even achieving some form of immortality, is an immensely
complex and still speculative endeavor; some would even
practically comparable to how people in the fable deal with
the dragon. Therefore, Bostrom concludes, we have com -
pelling moral reasons to halt human senescence and con -
sequently “kill death” (the dragon). In practical terms, this
notion emerges as a daring and contentious reimagining of
both biological aging and mortality. In contrast to the con -
ventional view of aging as a natural, inevitable, and intrinsic
biological phenomenon (e.g., Park et al., 2020; Wu et al.,
2025; Yoon et al., 2009), it is framed as a disease requir -
ing treatment akin to any other medical illness (Sinclair &
LaPlante, 2016). Biological aging is a cascade of cellular
and molecular impairments culminating in the decline of the
human physiological system, constraining our species from
realizing its utmost potential (Vita-More, 2021). This con -
troversial portrayal aligns with the transhumanist vision of
human enhancement (Mende et al., 2023), driving the tech-
nological pursuit not only of extending our lifespan but also
our healthspan (Wareham, 2016). This narrative thus exem-
plifies how transhumanist discourses position life extension
as a desirable and aspirational consumer project, one that
reframes aging from a natural process into a solvable prob -
lem (Takhar et al., 2025).
Life extension projects in contemporary consumer cul -

and eternal youth, all enhancing overall self-esteem (Liu &

Control
The first set of ethical issues involves control and consent
(Danaher & Nyholm, 2025). Control relates to a person’s
autonomy and self-determination (Floridi et al., 2018).
Through informed consent, they should decide on their own
whether they want to be re-created and—if so—provide
the necessary data (Danaher & Nyholm, 2025). However,
the way the re-created entity will behave and interact, and
whether and when their re-creations will be shut down/
retired, seems to be out of their control, unless this can be
specified in a legal will. There is necessarily a lack of self-
determination when there is no pre-mortem decision on re-
creation (e.g., in the form of a last will), and the family of
the deceased decides to re-create the deceased. An extreme
violation of control and autonomy would be the creation of
“sexbot” replicas without consent (Kuksenok & Santagati,
2019; Lancaster, 2021), even if the replicant is the surviving
partner’s former spouse. The only countermeasure would be
presumptive DDNR (Digital Do Not Reanimate) if there is
no pre-mortem consent. This measure might translate into
product design and the legitimacy of virtual re-creations.
Companies face strategic choices in how they construct
consent defaults (opt-in versus opt-out), whether they allow
customizable settings for re-creations, and how they com -
municate these options (Cadario et al., 2026). Companies
that foreground consumer-centric control mechanisms can
differentiate themselves in a sensitive market, while those
that obscure or ignore consent risk reputational damage and
erosion of trust.
Control further pertains to the ethically controversial
issue of privacy and the control of personal data, since con-
sumers “have an interest and a normative stance in extend -
ing ante-mortem privacy management posthumously”
(Morse & Birnhack, 2024, p. 5241). Specifically, the conti-
nuity principle of digital remains implies that (a) consumers
expect posthumous privacy, (b) society should acknowledge
and respect this wish, (c) technology should contribute to
meeting these expectations, and (d) individual data protec -
tion established in life should persist posthumously (Morse
& Birnhack, 2024). In this regard, two forms of posthu -
mous privacy paradoxes can emerge. The first one relates
to consumers who are interested in preserving privacy post-
humously but who do not act accordingly. Conversely, for
consumers who are interested in sharing their personal data
posthumously but who do not make the appropriate pro -
visions, an inverted privacy paradox can occur (Morse &
Birnhack, 2022). That is, consumers may wish to allow full
or partial access to their digital remains, but do not enable
access accordingly. Thus, others’ posthumous access to their
digital remains will be more constrained than the consumers

Virtual re-creations constitute the most technologically
advanced domain of human–death relationships and extend
Belk’s (1988, 2013) notion of the extended self into a post -
humous self. However, they can also introduce substan -
tial posthumous consumer vulnerability, as the deceased’s
identity can theoretically be shaped, manipulated, or com -
modified without their consent. At the same time, these
technologies can be a new frontier of posthumous co-cre -
ation: mourners and AI technologies (providers) jointly
create interactions, emotions, and consumer identity perfor-
mances that evolve over time. This stage of the consumer
death cycle shifts from identity persistence to identity con -
struction, as technologies no longer merely preserve but can
continue to shape identities.
In sum, Remembrance, Extension, and AfterLife out -
line the contours of technologically mediated human–death
relationships. At the same time, they raise substantial ethi -
cal challenges and questions (Basilan, 2023; Lindemann,
2022; Öhman & Floridi, 2018), highlighting the urgent need
for ethical guidelines that address the stakeholders’ role in
shaping these technologies and services.

and identity of the person, and—if so—at what age or stage

curate their digital data as a form of post-mortem identity

Death technologies raise the possibility and issue that
only certain (wealthy or economically advantaged) consum-
ers can afford life extension measures or virtual re-creation.
Thus, another avenue for future research concerns how
pricing models, subsidies, and insurance coverage shape
access to death technologies and respective services. Again,
a global perspective is important, because inequalities in
data infrastructure, digital literacy, and cultural norms could
create significant disparities in who can be remembered, re-
created, or digitally memorialized.
In addition to the ethical principles we suggest, death
technologies also imply new research avenues in respect
to consumer vulnerability (Hermann et al., 2024), co-
creation (Vargo et al., 2022), and the extended self (Belk,
1988, 2013, 2024). Death technologies can introduce new
forms of vulnerability of the bereaved, such as emotional
dependency, overreliance, prolonged grief, and exploi -
tation. Future research should examine how consumers
respond to virtual-re-creations and how different tech -
nological features (e.g., conversational responsiveness)
increase or decrease potential negative side effects and
vulnerability dynamics. Therefore, longitudinal studies
are needed to assess how prolonged interactions with vir -
tual re-creations influence coping strategies, the mourning
process, and consumer well-being. Death technologies can
also reconfigure value co-creation by incorporating new
actors into service ecosystems (i.e., the deceased as re-
created entities) and extend it into posthumous context.
Thus, research could examine how co-creation unfolds
when one “actor” is a re-created entity, and how providers
balance meaningful co-creation with the risks of commod -
ification when managing digital estates and posthumous
data. Another research question—also related to consumer
vulnerability—is how providers can design services that
foster meaningful co-creation without exploiting the
and commodification of death (Abidin, 2022; Figueroa-Tor-
res, 2024; Hurtado, 2024; Öhman & Floridi, 2018). From
an ethical and human dignity perspective, digital remains of
the deceased (i.e., their informational corpse) should not be
solely considered as means to ends (e.g., profit) but rather
should be construed as entities holding an inherent (non-
human) value (Bassett, 2022).
From a marketing perspective, equity is inseparable from
pricing, accessibility, and inclusion strategies (Park et al.,
2023). Companies must decide whether death technolo -
gies will be positioned as exclusive premium services or
as tiered offerings accessible to diverse consumer groups.
Marketing choices, such as subscription models, cross-sub-
sidization, or partnerships with insurers and public agen -
cies, will determine whether these technologies deepen
inequality or promote fairness. For global brands, questions
of cultural sensitivity and geographic availability further
complicate equity: who is targeted, who is excluded, and
on what terms? Ethical marketing in this scenario, there -
fore, requires designing business models that expand access
rather than restrict it, ensuring that vulnerable consumers
are not excluded from dignity-preserving services (Park et
al., 2023). In sum, the equity principle extends the call for
diversity, equity, and inclusion in marketing (Park et al.,
2023) to the final act of consumption (i.e., death), and ensur-
ing access to dignified digital afterlife services prevents the
emergence of a “two-tiered immortality,” that reflects and
perpetuate existing social inequalities.

work directly translate into avenues for future research (see
also Table 2), which will be addressed in the following.

Conclusion
Emerging death technologies have the potential to redefine
how individuals and society interact with death, memory,
and mourning. While these technologies offer opportunities
to preserve legacies and enhance emotional connections,
they also present profound ethical challenges that provid -
ers and public policy officials must address. Importantly,
these challenges are inseparable from marketing practice.
The ways companies design consent flows, build trust,
frame dignity, and set prices are not neutral choices. They
are deeply ethical choices with societal consequences. We
believe that our CARE framework provides a principled
starting point to manage these implications across multiple
stakeholders. Rather than treating Control, Accountabil-
ity, Respect, and Equity in isolation, their interconnections
highlight the tensions and trade-offs marketers must con -
front. One key insight is that these principles reinforce one
another. For instance, control without accountability risks
token consent mechanisms that do not protect consumers
in practice. Respect without equity may lead to dignified
offerings that only the wealthy can afford. Accountability
without respect may result in functional safeguards but still
commodify grief in ways that harm consumer well-being.
Thus, ethical marketing of death technologies requires an
integrated application of CARE.
While we believe that the CARE framework of princi -
ples is a useful set of general considerations, implement -
ing them can be challenging. However, we believe that
this set of guidelines is a better starting point than try -
ing to start out with a more specific set of requirements.
In the same way that national constitutions are guiding
frameworks that are later adjudicated and amended, we
believe that a set of guiding principles is a good start -
ing point, but subject to subsequent modifications. This

rapid pace. If we were attempting to offer a set of general
