Beyond the Middle: Rethinking Midpoint and "No Idea" Options in Likert

Abstract
The aim of this research is to investigate the underlying mechanisms by which the consumer's sensitivity level
regarding the measured issue interacts with the Likert scale structure to influence response styles.  An online survey
was conducted with 1333 students, exploring four different versions of the Likert Scale (forced 4-point and unforced
5-point, with and without a "no idea" option) toward three products (beer, coke , and energy drink) in the context of
sensitive (beer) and non-sensitive (coke and energy drink) issues using Zaichkowsky's product involvement scale. The
results of the research indicate that the structure of the Likert scale that offers the best measurement validity is not
independent of the issue being measured. Therefore, in the fields of marketing and consu mer behavior when
determining the most ideal scale structure in terms of including a midpoint and/or no idea option on the scale, the
concepts of response bias and social desirability bias should be considered together.  This study fills a gap in the
marketing research literature by systematically investigating the differential effects of Likert scale midpoints and "no
idea" options on consumer responses to products varying in their sensitivity.  This research offers novel insights into
the measurement of con sumer attitudes toward sensitive issues, providing valuable implications for marketers and
researchers seeking to develop more accurate and unbiased market and consumer research instruments.
Keywords: Likert scale, response styles, response bias, social desirability bias, consumer sensitivity
1. Introduction
In the field of marketing and consumer behavior, surveys emerge as an indispensable reservoir of data, where
questionnaires assume a pivotal role. Within the domain of marketing and consumer surveys, the Likert scale, allowing
respondents to articulate their degree of agreement with specific statements, garners widespread favor  but there is
limited documentation on how scale formats impact data. Dawes (2008: 65, 66 ) assumed the need for further
exploration, for three main reasons: First, the use of advanced analytical methods is increasing. Techniques like
confirmatory factor analysis and structural equation modeling are sensitive to data characteristics such as variance,
kurtosis, and skewness. Thus, more knowledge on how scale format affects these characteristics is needed. Second,
Likert Scale data is often analyzed to explain variance in dependent variables like customer satisfaction, purchase
probability, or brand attitudes. Analysts aim to identify variables that significantly influence these outcomes. Higher

variance in the dependent variable is desirable for analysis. If variance is low, standard OLS regression may not be
suitable, and more complex techniques like logistic regression might be necessar y. Third, many organizations
regularly assess consumer sentiment using Likert -type scales to measure customer satisfaction and service quality.
However, the choice of scale, such as a 5 -point or 7 -point scale, can vary due to factors like personnel changes  or
organizational restructuring. While the number of scale points influences the calculated mean scores, a critical question
arises: Does the choice of scale significantly impact the relative magnitude of these mean scores? Theoretical
considerations suggest that scale format can potentially affect the data, but empirical evidence remains inconclusive.
Despite the practical importance of this issue, there is a dearth of definitive guidance. Therefore,  as highlighted by
different researchers further research is necessary to gain a deeper understanding of the impact of scale changes on
consumer behavior measurement (Albaum 1997; Bergkvist 2021; Coelho and Esteves 2007).
When examining the marketing literature in this field, it can be stated that the knowledge accumulation regarding the
validity and reliability of the Likert scale progresses through two distinct headings.
Firstly, there are studies that focus on the format of the Likert scale to enhance the validity and reliability of collected
data. When constructing Likert rating scales, a researcher must make several key decisions, including the  midpoint,
and number of scale categories (Smith and Albaum 2005). Response bias are related to response style, which refers to
systematic patterns of responding that are not directly related to the content of the question but rather reflect the typical
behavioral characteristics of the participants (Albaum et al. 2007; Baumgartner and Steenkamp 2001; Oskamp 1977).
Studies show that responses to Likert scales may be susceptible to biases introduced by response styles, signifying the
respondents' proclivity to disproportionately favor particular response options. A notable illustration is the
acquiescence response style, marked by a predisposition to favor options expressing agreement disproportionately.
However, respondents may also exhibit a disproportionate inclination towards extreme options, the midpoint choice,
or options expressing disagreement. Despite persistent warnings highlighting the distorting impact of response styles,
a substantial segment of survey research tends to overlook or downplay the need to mitigate or rectify their influence.
This oversight can be attributed, in part, to an incomplete grasp of response styles and their antecedents, compounded
by challenges in accurately gauging these styles.
Secondly, it is possible to observe studies that discuss the validity and reliability of the obtained information not
through the scale format but rather through the lens of social desirability bias. Social desirability bias is to the tendency
of individuals to respond to surveys in a manner that presents themselves favorably, often aligning with societal norms

or expectations. To elaborate further, an individual may tend to underestimate  (overestimate) culturally disapproved
(desirable) attitudes, thoughts, values and behaviors about themselves . It is known that most studies have not been
successful in controlling for the reluctance to express candid opinions on issues that may be conside red sensitive for
consumers. But if bias is not controlled, it directly affects the validity of these studies.
However, in the field of marketing and consumer behavior, considering  and evaluating response  style and social
desirability bias as separate  from each other  is not accurate. Evaluating the respondent style independently of the
subject matter being measured can limit our perspective on the matter by threatening the validity of the data. Therefore,
there is a need for more comprehensive studies in the field. Based on this assumption, this study will examine the role
of the midpoint on a scale when collecting information in consumer research, simultaneously considering sensitive
and non-sensitive products. The role of the "no idea" option in this process will also be scrutinized in the same context.
For this purpose, a web-based survey was conducted with 1333 students, exploring forced (4 -point) versus unforced
(5-point) Likert scale with and without a "no idea" option, using Zaichkowsky's product involvement scale toward
three products (beer, coke and energy drink) in the context of sensitive (beer) and non -sensitive (coke and energy
drink) issues. This study reveals that the issue of the midpoint on the scale and should not be considered independently
of the sensitivity of the subject being measured. Likewise, the consumers are prone to give wrong ratings either
accidentally or due to their sensitivity about the subject being measured.
In this context, this study seeks to understand the di fferential effects of Likert scale midpoint and "no idea" options
on consumer responses to products that vary in their sensitivity levels. Specifically, it is explored whether the optimal
Likert scale structure (forced vs. unforced, with or without a midpo int) varies across sensitive and non -sensitive
product categories. By crystalizing that considering scale format (in terms of including a midpoint and/or no idea
option), response style, sensitive issues  and social desirability bias in an integrative manner when considering the
validity of the data, this study aimed to contribute to the understanding of the processes that underlying response styles
and thus the validity and reliability of data.

reason, consumers' attitudes are usually converted into verbal expressions and measured through these  expressions
(Thurstone 1929). So consumers' behaviors are tried to be predicted through their attitudes. Therefore, measuring
consumer attitudes is extremely important in marketing research. As a verbal scale that measures the level of

one of the most widely used instruments in marketing research in Turkey (Kurtuluş et al. 2009) as well as globally.
While it is true that neurophysiological tools such as fMRI, EEG, and eye -tracking have gained attenti on in recent
years, Likert -type scales remain the most widely used and central measurement tool in marketing and consumer

Methodological reviews indicate that self-report surveys—particularly Likert scales—continue to dominate marketing

survey-based measurement (Plassmann et al. 2015).
Therefore, we maintain that investigating Likert scale structures, especially in the context of response bias and
sensitive consumer issues, continues to provide valuable insights. Additi onally, recent works emphasize that the
validity of Likert scale data is not only a methodological concern but also a theoretical one, especially when social
desirability and response styles are at stake (Vomberg and Klarmann 2022; Weijters et al. 2021).
In this regard, our study does not merely revisit Likert scales as a general concept, but rather addresses a critical and
underexplored dimension: how the structural elements of the scale (midpoint and "no idea " option) interact with
consumer sensitivity an d bias, which has significant implications for researchers and practitioners aiming for data
accuracy.
Since Likert scales are prone to response biases, such as response style and social desirability, as emphasized by Amoo
and Friedman (2000), a significant body of research addresses the challenges of designing questions and rating scales
that are both objective and relatively free from measurement errors  (Churchill and Peter 1984; Friedman and Amoo
1999; Schoenmakers et al. 2024 ; Schuman and Pre sser 1981 ; Weijters et al. 2021 ). However, empirical findings
remain fragmented, with inconsistent conclusions regarding optimal scale design and the management of response
biases. The key findings in the areas of response style and social desirability bias in Likert scales are summarized in
the following section of the study.
2.1. Response Style
Response style refers to the inclination of individuals participating in a survey to answer questions in a consistent
manner, irrespective of the specific content of the questionnaire (Beuthner et al. 2018; Harzing 2006). Other definitions
include (Greenleaf 1992a: 330) the variability or spread of individuals' responses around the average response or the

ARTICLE IN PRESS
midpoint of the rating scale . Certain respondents tend to opt for intermediate values, while others are drawn to the
extremes. These response styles act as biases since the preferences conveyed by a response style introduce distortion
into the survey results. Therefore, neglecting thei r potential impact on the scores could undermine the equity and
accuracy of the evaluations. Additionally, measurement errors induced by response styles can impact the reliability
assessments and inflate convergent validity by exhibiting stronger correlations with other Likert-scale-based measures
(Kreitchmann et al. 2019:1). Likert scale can be manipulated by unsavory survey designers aiming to influence the
survey outcome by aligning preferred outcome options with the response style biases of the survey t akers. Yet,
response style can also trap diligent researchers who might misinterpret survey outcomes and derive inaccurate
conclusions from their research findings if biases are overlooked (Clarke III 2001).
As one of response styles, extreme response style (ERS) refers to the tendency of survey respondents to consistently
choose the highest or lowest rating options regardless of the specific content of the questions (Greenleaf 1992a).  Non-
extreme response style (NERS) refers to the tendency of survey respondents to provide a more balanced and varied
selection of responses across the rating scale without consistently favoring the highest or lowest options (Wetzel et al.
2013). Midpoint response style (MRS) is to the inclination of survey respondents to consistently select the neutral or
middle point on a rating scale, often indicating indecision or a lack of preference (Kieruj and Moors 2010). Participants
who are reluctant to fill out questionnaires, especially in attitude scales, may take the easy way out and mark the
midpoint of the scale repeatedly. Therefore, the arithmetic averages of attitude scales are concentrated around the
neutral point. However, as a result of the excessive marking of the neutral point, a small variance value is detected
(Bardakçı et al. 2010: 101).  Agreement response style  (ARS) can be defined as the tendency of survey respondents
to consistently choose options indicating agreement, often influenced by a desire to conform to social expectations or
present a positive image (Cl arke III 2000). On the other hand, disagreement response style (DRS) refers to the
inclination of survey respondents to consistently choose options indicating disagreement, often influenced by a
tendency to express dissent  or opposition (Beuthner et al.  2018). Net agreement response style (also known as Net
Acquiescence Response Style) is the ratio of "completely agree" responses to "completely disagree" responses,
expressed as a proportion of the total responses provided by an individual.  This response style pertains to the degree
to which respondents tend to exhibit a higher inclination toward agreement (tendency to agree) rather than
disagreement (tendency to disagree) with items, regardless of content (Weijters et al. 2010a).  Also, we can mention
“No Idea Response Style” (NIRS) as to the tendency of survey respondents to consistently choose options indicating

Considering the diverse nature of these response tendencies among various populations, cultures, and the subject
matter being studied, disregarding their potential impact on the scores could jeopardize the fairness and validity of the
evaluations. Additionally, errors in measurement caused by response styles may influence the reliability estimates and
inflate convergent validity by displaying stronger correlations with other Likert-scale-based measures (Kreitchmann
et al. 2019:1).
Exploratory research indicates that the response styles are influenced by the format of the scale. However, attempts to
explain response styles through the format of the scale yielded mixed results. For instance, Hui and Triandis (1989)
demonstrate how different formats lead to significantly different response distributions independent of content. Arce-
Ferrer (2006), Greenleaf (1992b) and We ijters et al. (2010a) have presented findings indicating that the perceived
meaning of response categories holds significance in response styles. Kieruj and Moors (2013) illustrated that ERS
exhibits a degree of stability across various surveys and is correlated with individuals' selected personality
characteristics and age cohorts. Comparing acquiescent and extreme response styles in face-to-face and web surveys
Liu et al. (2017) found that face-to-face respondents demonstrate more acquiescent and extreme response styles than
Web respondents. Findings showed that decreasing the count of response categories and introducing cognitive burden
amplifies the extent of ERS and ARS , corr espondingly (Knowles and Condon 1999; Weijters et al.  2010a).
Concurrently, diverse response structures have demonstrated the ability not solely to diminish but also to elevate
response styles (Böckenholt 2017). The classification, position, and labe ling of response categories may influence
respondents' response styles (Bergkvist 2021; Kulas et al. 2008; Revilla 2015; Weijters et al. 2021).
There are also many studies on response styles which has concentrated on demographic and personal differences
between individuals or groups of individuals (e.g., Baumgartner and Steenkamp 2001; De Jong et al. 2008; Greenleaf
1992a, b; Kreitchmann et al. 2019; Rossi et al. 2001; Weijters et al. 2010b).  In their study conducted in 2009, Bardakçı
et al. examined the effect of the demographic characteristics of the respondents on their response style in Turkey and
found that the disagreement style was common among the respondents and that the education level of the respondents
affected both the use of extremes and the use of the scale midpoint.

Variations in response styles across cultures have been consistently documented in the literature. Early research
demonstrated that scale format may produce different response distributions independent of content (Hui and Triandis
1989). Subsequent cross -national studies linked response tendencies to broader cultural dimensions, showing that
respondents in individualistic cultures are more likely t o select extreme categories, whereas those in collectivist
cultures display a stronger tendency toward midpoint responding (Chen et al. 1995; Hamamura et al. 2008). Empirical
evidence from European consumer data further confirmed that response styles can d istort rating distributions and
affect cross-national comparisons in marketing research (Van Herk et al. 2004). A large-scale 26-country investigation
by Harzing (2006) provided robust evidence that response styles systematically vary across nations, raising concerns
about measurement equivalence in international survey research. More recent analyses continue to emphasize that
extreme response styles may significantly compromise survey validity if not properly accounted for (Schoenmakers
et al. 2024). Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of methodological sensitivity when interpreting
Likert-scale data across cultural contexts.
Studies testing the Likert scale in different cultures indicate that the ideal number of points on the scale also  varies
from culture to culture. In their 2002 study, Lee et al. stated that the construct validity of the Likert scale is higher for
4 points in Chinese and American samples, while it is higher for 7 points in the Japanese sample. Again, Skelly et al.
(2000) stated that in the data collection study conducted with African Americans on the telephone, the participants
had difficulty in filling the 6-point Likert scale and therefore reduced the number of points in the scale to four. Bernal
et al. (1997) observed that the 6 -point Likert scale was difficult to understand for the Spanish sample and suggested
the use of a 4-point scale, but they stated that they could not reach strong findings regarding the difference between 4
and 6 points in terms of scale validity and that new studies are needed in the field. Li et al. (2020) examined whether

It is possible to assert that the accumulation of knowledge in the field of response style is investigated through two

2.1.1. Ideal number of points
Cox (1980) argued that the ideal number of response options balances informational sensitivity with measurement
accuracy, allowing respondents to express meaningful distinctions without introducing unnecessary response error.
As Coelho and Esteves (2007:316) emphasized, among the major decisions when constructing a rating scale are the
number of response alternatives and the use of an odd or even number of categories. The choice between attitude
scales and particularly the choice regarding the number of response alternatives is not new in marketing theory, but
there is clearly a lack of empirical work on this issue. Moreover, previous studies have not reached a clear consensus
regarding the ideal number of response options (Alwin 1997; Neumann and Neumann 1981) or whether scales should
include an odd or even number of response categories (Malhotra and Birks 2007).
Matell and Jacoby's (1972) em phasis on minimizing midpoint misuse suggests that maintaining a low number of
anchors in the scale encourages more accurate use of the midpoint.
Preston and Colman (2000) highlight the significance of tailoring the number of points based on respondents'
discrimination abilities, allowing for flexibility by using different scales with different numbers of points within the

same questionnaire. They prioritize understanding respondent preferences over strict adherence to a universal
standard, even if it compromises validity and reliability.
Cummins and Gullone (2000) suggested employing 10-point scales with clear endpoints. These scales provide a rating
system (ranging from one to ten) that aligns with everyday experience and enhances the precision of the meas uring
tool.
Dawes (2008) conducted a comparison among 5, 7, and 10 point scales, revealing that none of the scale formats
resulted in significantly lower variances around the mean. This indicates that none of the three formats is less
preferable in terms o f obtaining data suitable for regression analysis. Kurtosis and skewness were also found to be
very similar across all formats. Therefore, 5 -, 7-, or 10-point scales are all equally suitable for analytical techniques
such as confirmatory factor analysis or structural equation models in this regard.
Lozano et al. (2008) assert that the optimal range for response options lies between four and seven, where both
reliability and validity are heightened. Deviating from this range, specifically having fewer than f our options or
exceeding seven, tends to compromise the psychometric properties of the scales with only marginal improvements.
Chomeya (2010) discovered that Likert scales with 6 points generally yield discrimination and reliability values that
are superior to those of Likert scales with 5-points.
Weijters et al. (2010a) proposed employing fully annotated 5-point scales for the broader population and completely
labeled 7-point scales for cohorts exhibiting elevated cognitive abilities. Bora Semiz and Altunışık (2016) found that
as the number of points on the scale increases Turkish participants tend to move away from extremes, and tend towards
intermediate values.
Adelson and McCoach (2010) offer a practical consideration, proposing a five-point scale with a midpoint as the ideal
configuration, particularly when surveying children in grades three to six.
Leung's (2011) recommendation to employ an 11 -point scale, considering the normality assumption, for enhanced
psychometric performance introduces a perspective favoring a larger scale. In contrast, Chen et al. (2015) approach
the issue from an information-processing standpoint, suggesting that a 5-point scale aligns more effectively with the
cognitive capacity of the human mind.
Recent applied researc h comparing 5 - and 7 -point Likert scales has similarly reported differences in means and
reliability coefficients, highlighting the practical implications of scale length decisions (Russo et al. 2021).

In conclusion, while diverse perspectives exist, no singular ideal number of points on the Likert scale emerges. Rather,
the optimal number depends on respondent characteristics, cognitive capacity, and the specific aims of the survey.
Accordingly, scale design should be tailored to the target population and research objectives.
Building on the literature summarized above, the present study deliberately focused on 4- and 5-point Likert formats.
While some scholars argue that three-point scales may suffice for capturing general attitudinal tendencies (Jacoby and
Matell 1971), shorter scales may reduce discriminatory power and attenuate variance. Conversely, scales exceeding
seven or nine points may introduce cognitive burden, response fatigue, and semantic ambiguity—particularly in online
surveys and among younger populations (Chen et al. 2015; Lozano et al. 2008). Empirical evidence further indicates
that reliability and variance characteristics tend to stabilize within  the four -to-seven-point range (Dawes 2008;
Weijters et al. 2010a), suggesting diminishing psychometric gains beyond this interval. Recent studies also highlight
that measurement conclusions may vary depending on the scaling approach used (e.g., Likert vs. Best–Worst Scaling),
underscoring the importance of scale-format decisions in applied consumer research (Heo et al. 2022).
More recently, psychometric analyses based on Item Response Theory have revisited the optimal number of response
categories. Aybek a nd Toraman (2022), comparing 3 -, 5-, and 7 -point Likert formats, found that five -point scales
provide superior reliability compared to three -point formats while offering no substantial disadvantage relative to
seven-point scales in terms of test informatio n. These findings reinforce the view that measurement efficiency tends
to stabilize within the mid-range of response options.
In addition, linguistic structure and cultural usage patterns should be considered when determining scale length. In
Turkish surve y research, the five -point Likert scale is both the most widely adopted and the most semantically
transparent format, as it allows for clear and commonly understood verbal anchors such as “Strongly agree,” “Agree,”
“Neither agree nor disagree,” “Disagree,” and “Strongly disagree.” Studies examining marketing research practices in
Turkey report that the five -point format is the dominant and most comprehensible structure in the field ( Bas 2017;
Kurtuluş et al. 2009), while higher-point scales may introduce interpretative ambiguity due to the limited availability
of culturally stable intermediate expressions.

2.1.2. Midpoint and No Idea Option
The issue of the midpoint in the Likert scale constitutes another focal point of considerable interest within the context
of the ideal scale format. The "no idea" option, along with the issue of the midpoint, has been the subject of research
and has been approached in various ways. A brief summary of the findings in the field can be presented as follows.
Matell and Jacoby (1972) argued that the number of options in the scale should be increased in order to reduce the use
of the midpoint or the midpoint can be removed from the scale completely. Ryan (1980) emphasized that a distinction
should be made between don't know, undecided and no idea on the Likert scale. He pointed out that while "neutral" is
a definite opinion, "no idea" is not definite. Ray (1982) tackled the matter from a methodological angle and uncovered
that while the reliability of the unforced scale surpasses that of the forced scale, the forced scale demonstrates greater
validity compared to the unforced scale. A meta-analysis conducted by Churchill and Peter (1984) also found that the
presence or absence of a midpoint had no significant effect on reliability. Garland (1991) stated that the inclusion or
exclusion of the midpoint in the scale is largely based on the preference of the researchers and argued that the social
desirability bias can be minimized by neglecting the midpoint. Raaijmakers et al. (2000) emphasized that it would not
be correct to remove the midpoint only on the basis of the problem with the binary meaning, and that it is also important
to offer the option "I don't know".
Weems and Onwuegbuzie (2001) stated that the midpoint can be ignored when participants are less interested in the
content, but emphasized that the "don't know" option should also be added to the scale.  Johns (2005) argues that the
midpoint on the scale can be removed when participants are comfortable with the topic and under pressure of social
desire.  Coelho and Esteves (2007) state that the high number of points in the scale pushes the participants to be lazy
and simply brings them closer to the midpoint and suggest not to use the mid point if there are many answer options
in the scale. Kulas et al. (2008) approached the issue through the data collection format and stated that surveys should
include an N/A option in addition to the midpoint, especially in online forms.  Kulas and Stachow ski (2009, 2013)
proposed one more option for the ideal scale structure and argued that as well as “no idea” the option "Varies" should

scale structure. They  argued to use a midpoint cautiousy.  Kankaraš and Capecchi (2024) found that including a

compared to scales without it. A s mall subset of participants also used the midpoint to avoid socially sensitive

Consequently, response styles emerge regardless of the specific topic being assessed. Social desirability bias stands
out as a prominent contributor to response distortion. This bias arises when participants opt for socially acceptable
answers rather than expressing their genuine sentiments, particularly in delicate domains such as politics, religion, and
personal matters (Grimm 2010). Consequently, it is expected to be universally present in survey research endeavors.
Hence, for a thorough understanding of responses gathered through questionnaire -based data, a precise grasp of
response tendencies is indispensable.
2.1.3. Social Desirability Bias
The ideal scale format does not guarantee the validity and reliability of the data collected through the scale. This is
because the subject to be measured itself has a direct impact on the determination of the ideal scale structure and type.
In marketing and consumer behavior literature, the social desirability tendencies of the respondents have an important
role in ensuring the validity and reliability of the data, as well as the structure of the scale.  The negative aspects of
consumer behavior such as theft, gambling, and smoking (Mick 1996) are significantly influenced by prevailing social
norms and sensitive soci etal issues (Chatzidakis et al.  2007). Since consumers' perceptions of societal expectations
shape their attitudes and intentions, researchers i n consumer behavior must recognize this aspect. When consumers
perceive social pressures regarding their behavior, it can distort their attitude formation and subsequently affect their

responses. Consequently, socially desirable responding may subtly impac t research findings, potentially
compromising the validity and reliability of conclusions drawn. Several scholars (La Trobe and Acott 2000; Roozen
and Pelsmacker 1998) agree that individuals may hide their true attitudes and purchasing behaviors either to impress
researchers or to conceal undesirable conduct. Steenkamp et al. (2010) define socially desirable responses as those
that present the respondent positively, conforming to cultural norms regarding the desirability of specific values, traits,
attitudes, inclinations, perspectives, and actions. Social desirability bias involves answering questions in a manner that
portrays oneself more positively than is objectively warranted, with two distinct forms: impression management and
self-deception positivity (Beins 2013).
Therefore, when discussing the ideal scale structure and type, it should first be assessed whether the subject to be
measured is a sensitive issue for consumers, and then the ideal structure should be discussed.  Considering that a
considerable amount of prior research has depended on direct assessments of self -reports regarding socially delicate
topics, where respondents might be hesitant to hide their genuine attitudes and actions , compared to the importance
of the topic, the number of studies addressing response styles along with social desirability bias is quite limited.
As one of them, Albaum et al.  (2014) investigated the influence of topic sensitivity, forced answering (FA), and
response options on respondent motives in internet-based surveys. Findings reveal that topic sensitivity significantly
affects motives, while FA does not have a significant impact. Overall, it highlights the importance of considering topic
sensitivity and response options in survey design an d identifies differences in respondent motivation based on these
factors. Gal and Rucker (2011)  also introduced the concept of "respons e substitution ", suggesting that survey
respondents may sometimes convey attitudes not directly addressed by the research er's questions. Through three
experiments, they confirm this phenomenon, provide process explanations, and delineate its boundaries in marketing
research.
Tan et al. (2021) investigated the inclusion and significance of Social Desirability Bias (SDB) measurement in ethics-
related studies over the past two decades. Through a systematic review of articles from business ethics journals, it
reveals that only a small percentage of studies have addressed SDB, with varying approaches across regions. The
findings underscore the importance of considering SDB in ethics research and advocate for its inclusion in self -
reported surveys to effectively control for bias. Mohamed and Adalı (2022) conducted an empirical examination of
the impacts of survey subject, survey sponsor, introduction engagement, and perceived subject sensitivity on socially
desirable responding. The results indicated that the inclination toward socially desirable responding did not show

in the introduction, with more engaged respondents exhibiting a greater tendency toward socially desirable responding.
Additionally, the inclination toward socially desirable responding marginally di ffered based on the perceived

As can be seen, despite compelling empirical support for the dangers of response bias, this has been a widely neglected
topic in marketing and consumer research. Given the recognized disparity between what consumers express they are
inclined to do and their actual behaviors (referred to as social response bias), response styles of consumer should be
examined in comparison with socially sensitive topics and non -sensitive topics. In this context, the aim of this study

as a gauge of the potential success of a product or service could prove sufficient.
Taken together, the literature reviewed above highlights both the historical grounding and the ongoing methodological

5. Discussion
The Likert scale format has long been studied from various angles, including the ideal number of points, the inclusion
of a midpoint, and its impact on response styles. Research suggests that the optimal format depends on the measured
concept, cultural context, and factors like social desirability bias.
From a marketing discipline perspective, it is evident that researchers have largely followed their predecessors by
utilizing the Likert scale without thoroughly investigating the diversity of scale structures (Kurtulu ş et al. 2009).

Moreover, measuring consumer attitudes is extremely important in marketing research because markete rs’ primary
challenge is to understand consumers. Apart from emerging measurement methods like neuromarketing, directly
assessing consumers' emotions, thoughts, attitudes, and behaviors is quite difficult and requires a rigorous decision -
making process. Although the Likert scale is the most widely used measurement tool in marketing, Likert scales are
prone to response biases, such as response style and social desirability.  Against this broader methodological
background, the present study aims to clarify how scale structure and topic sensitivity jointly shape response behavior.
This study makes three primary contributions to the literature on survey methodology and consumer research. First, it
advances response style theory by demonstrating that response styles should not be understood solely as stable
individual or cultural traits, but also as context -dependent phenomena shaped by the interaction between topic
sensitivity and scale design. Second, the study provides methodological insight by showing that commonly used Likert
scale formats do not function equivalently across different issue domains, challenging the widespread assumption of
a “one-size-fits-all” measurement approach. Third, the findings offer practical implications for marketing researchers
and survey designers by indicating that scale validity and response behavior are contingent upon the social sensitivity
of the topic being evaluated.
In this context, this study examined whether different Likert scale structures affect response styles by considering both
scale format and topic sensitivity, comparing consumer reactions to sensitive (beer) and non -sensitive (coke and
energy drink) products.
Firstly, The ANOVA test revealed that scale type (4-point, 5-point, with/without “no idea”) had no significant effect
on the coefficient of variation across coke, beer, and energy drink categories. This indicates consistent variability
across scale formats and supports Albaum et al.'s (2007) finding that format differences are not statistically significant
in terms of predictive ability.
Contrary to studies suggesting that different Likert formats affect reliability and discriminant validity (Chomeya 2010;
Weijters et al. 2010a), this study finds no significant difference in coefficient of variation across 4- or 5-point scales,
with or without a "no idea" option. This supports earlier findings (Churchill and Peter 1984; Garland 1991; Matell and
Jacoby 1972) indicating that scale type has minimal impact on response variability. Nonetheless, as Garland (1991)
noted, further research in marketing is still needed.
The only exception was the significant variation between forced and unforced scales for energy drink. Given that
Turkey's energy drink market is relatively underdeveloped compared to US, Japan and European countries and needs

support (Gok and Ulu 2019), consumers may be less familiar with these products, making the midpoint more relevant.
Future studies could further explore the structure of the Likert scale in relation to product familiarity (low versus high),
which could enrich our understanding of this subject.
When specifically considering the issue of the midpoint on the scale, the findings indicate that the midpoint option,
especially for coke and energy drink, encouraged more balanced responses and reduced extreme answers, helping
reveal participants’ true opinions. In contrast, for beer, the "no idea " option led participants to avoid negative
responses, highlighting the influence of social desirability and a tendency to conceal views.
It is worth reiterating that changes in product categories diversify the impact of the midpoint. For sensitive products
like beer, social pressure leads participants to conceal opinions, reducing explicit responses. In less sensitive categories
like energy drink, midpoi nt use becomes more complex. While midpoints help avoid extreme responses, their
effectiveness varies by product type, shaped by social desirability and participants’ perceptions. In this context, the
unique dynamics of each product category and participan ts' perceptions play an important role in determining the
impact of the midpoint on the scale.
From the perspective of response styles, it is evident that changes in scale format lead to noticeable differences in
response styles across coke, beer, and energy drink, aligning with findings from other fields as psychology, education,
and tourism (Mayr and Zins 2011; Moors 2008; Moors et al. 2014; Newstead and Arnold 1989; Ory and Poggio 1981;
Van Herk et al. 2004). Most response styles (ERS, ARS, DRS, MRS, NIRS) vary by scale type, highlighting the risk
of scale-induced bias in consumer research.
In sum, this study shows that response style can be influenced as much by the specific issue under study as by the
scale structure. This emphasizes the need for res earchers to design questionnaires carefully, taking into account
product category-specific perceptions and social influences.
Again, the findings indicate that offering the "no idea" option may allow respondents to express themselves more
freely, thus enab ling more accurate and realistic data to be obtained. In this context, the increase in NIRS scores
suggests that respondents tend to consciously express less specific opinions in "some product categories " and that
measurement instruments should be designed accordingly when designing research.
Therefore, it is necessary to use the Likert scale very carefully in marketing and consumer research. In Turkey, the
Likert scale is generally used as a five -point scale ( Bardakçı et al.  2015; Kurtuluş et al. 2009 ). In order to prevent
ambiguities arising from the linguistic structure of Turkish and to minimize possible problems such as the level of

education of the participants, it can be predicted that using a five-point scale in Turkey would be appropriate in terms
of discriminant validity (Bardakçı et al. 2015). This study suggests that removing the midpoint when appropriate does
not significantly affect validity or reliability. Thus, both four- and five-point Likert scales can yield valid and reliable
data under suitable conditions in the Turkish context.
However, the inclusion of a midpoint should be considered in relation to the topic's nature. For straightforward topics
where consumers can easily form opinions, a four -point scale may reduce response bias by discou raging default
selection of the middle option.
It is also important to keep in mind that participants may use the midpoint and "no idea" options interchangeably —
either unintentionally or deliberately, especially on sensitive topics (Nadler et al. 2015). Thus, if omitting the midpoint
in sensitive contexts, a "no idea" option should be included as a filter.
Researchers should employ pilot studies to assess how scale formats affect response styles across different audiences
and product categories, justifying their choices accordingly. Prior studies (Dalal et al. 201 5; Garland 1991; Johns
2005) suggest omitting the midpoint when participants are familiar with the topic or under social desirability pressure,
as they may avoid neutral responses in such cases. While this approach has merit, it overlooks an important condition:
in marketing and consumer research, removing the midpoint is only valid if participants are demonstrably
knowledgeable about the subject. Contrary to Krosnick (1991) and Colman et al. (199 7), it may even be appropriate
to exclude the midpoint in socially sensitive questions, provided that a "no idea" option is offered as a filtering
mechanism.
The findings indicate that response styles are influenced not only by the scale structure but also  by the sensitivity of
the topic, underscoring the need for caution in measuring sensitive issues —even for seemingly low -involvement
products (Arndt and Ekebas-Turedi 2017). This highlights the critical role of pilot testing. Researchers must be
context-aware and explore the personal relevance of topics to consumers using both qualitative and quantitative
methods before finalizing question formats. As noted in prior studies (Albaum 1997; Peng and Finn 2016; Terblanche
and Boshoff 2008 ), Likert scales remain  widely used in marketing research, but their validity depends on
methodological rigor, including careful design and appropriate statistical analysis.
The findings also highlig ht the need to consider both response style and social desirability biases joint ly. Although
response style bias is commonly addressed in the social sciences, social desirability remains underexamined in
marketing and consumer research, despite its critical relevance due to the reliance on self -reported consumer data

(Dursun et al. 2019; Fisher 2000; Krosnick and Fabrigar 1997; Taherdoost 2019). Ensuring data validity and reliability
is therefore essential, especially when exploring theoretical gaps. Moreover, researchers must account for the influence
of subjective norms on reported v alues, beliefs, and attitudes. As Paulhus (2017) emphasizes, mitigating social
desirability bias requires proactive methodological safeguards.
Arndt and Ekebas -Turedi (2017) emphasized discrepancies between lab and field experiments, noting that social
norms significantly influence participants’ responses and behaviors. To mitigate social desirability bias, indirect
methods—such as third-person or scenario-based techniques—are increasingly recommended (Cartwright et al. 2017;
Drake et al. 2018; Dresden et al. 2018; Sáinz et al. 2016; White and White 2006; Wilbourn and Kee 2010).
As an alternative method to ensure the validity of responses, indirect measurement is a useful approach for exploring
sensitive issues like consumer ethics by eliciting participants’  views via third parties, thereby reducing social
desirability bias. A marketing study (Basfirinci 2020) comparing direct and indirect questioning over eight weeks with
the same sample found that implicit and explicit methods yielded differing responses to the same question.
If using the Likert scale appears to be the only viable option for measuring sensitive consumer topics, it is
recommended that the "no idea" option be employed as a filter question. This allows exclusion of unqualified
respondents, enha ncing data validity and reliability. As Foddy (1993) noted, surveys are only effective when
participants are willing to provide information.
The findings of this study highlight the necessity for further research exploring the underlying mechanisms through
which the sensitivity of the measured topic interacts with scale format to influence response styles. Additionally, it
emphasizes the need to investigate the extent to which these mechanisms can be generalized to measurement variables
with varying levels of sensitivity.
To the best of our knowledge, while Likert scale structure (e.g., number of response options, presence or absence of
midpoint, forced vs. unforced formats) has been extensively studied (e.g., Dawes 2008; Lozano et al. 2008; Weijters
et al. 2010a), and topic sensitivity and social desirability bias have likewise received focused attention (e.g., Albaum
et al. 2014; Grimm 2010), very few , if any, studies have systematically tested the interaction between these two
variables.
Our study, therefore, attempts to address a notable gap in the literature by empirically examining whether and how
the effectiveness or neutrality of a given scale structure may depend on the sensitivity of the topic being measured. As

far as we could determine, this is the first study in the marketing literature to experimentally test the non-independence
of scale format and topic sensitivity using real consumer product categories.
While prior research, such as Harzing (2006), has predominantly focused on response styles (R S) as stable cross -
national or cultural traits, the present study differentiates itself by examining RS as a context-dependent phenomenon
within a single cultural framework. We extend this line of inquiry by demonstrating that RS is not merely a fixed
respondent characteristic but is dynamically influenced by the interaction between topic sensitivity and specific scale
design elements (i.e., midpoint and 'no idea' options). Thus, our findings refine existing literature by showing that the
validity of a scale format is contingent upon the nature of the issue being measured, challenging the 'one-size-fits-all'
approach in survey methodology.
Furthermore, the practical implications of this interaction are significant: survey designers and market researchers
often assume that a “one-size-fits-all” scale format (e.g., standard 5-point Likert scale) is equally valid across topics.
Our findings challenge this assumption by demonstrating that response styles and validity indicators may
systematically vary when the same scale format is applied to sensitive vs. non-sensitive issues.
In sum, while the conclusion may appear commonsensical, empirical demonstration of this phenomenon is absent in
the literature, and our study provides a structured framework and data to supp ort or question long -standing
assumptions in scale design.
Future research in the field of marketing on how social desirability and response bias affect data should encompass a
broader range of variations to better understand their impacts across different product classifications and demographic
groups. This will provide deeper insights into how these biases may influence the validity and reliability of data within
diverse consumer contexts. The study sample consisted of university students, which raises concerns regarding
representativeness and generalizability, as noted by Henry (2008), Ashraf and Merunka (2017), and Pala and Atılgan
(2022). To validate the findings, future studies should replicate the research with diverse populations. Although Chi-
square tests confirmed demographic similarities across the 12 sub samples, each group remains distinct. Future
research should explore different product categories and Likert scale variations within the same sample across time to
test the robustness of results. In future studies, further investigations should also address potential measurement issues
by examining question wording, emphasis, scale balance, and response biases. While exploratory factor analysis
(EFA) is a widely used method for data reduction and construct validation, it is not without limitations. Specific ally,
EFA assumes linear relationships among variables and may oversimplify multidimensional response patterns,

particularly in contexts where non -linearity or asymmetry exists. This can be especially critical in studies involving
socially sensitive topics, where response behaviors may reflect contrarian tendencies or latent subgroups not captured
by linear factor structures. Moreover, factor analysis provides a symmetrical view of the data, which may obscure
meaningful heterogeneity in how different indivi duals or subgroups respond to the same stimuli. To address these
issues, future research might consider complementing traditional factor analytic methods with configurational
approaches such as fuzzy -set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA), which can better accommodate complex,
non-linear relationships and identify causal asymmetries across subgroups. Acknowledging these methodological
constraints enhances the interpretive robustness of the study and invites a more versatile approach to the analysis of
response behaviors in consumer research. Finally, while results suggest participants may be reluctant to disclose
personal information on sensitive topics, the use of convenience sampling limits generalizability. Repeating the study
with varied samples and methods would strengthen the findings.
