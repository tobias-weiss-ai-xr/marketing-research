Abstract
We draw on the marketing-as-prac tice framework to integrate and  expand the concept of
diversity, specifically addressing issues of race and colourism within a value-creating system.
Research on diversity in marketing has not kept pace with the t imes, resulting in cultural
insensitivity as racial and colourist stereotypes continue to b e perpetuated in marketing. This
creates exclusionary marketing pr actices that lead to a misalig nment between customers and
firms.

This article expands the marketing-as-practice framework by inc orporating diversity through
its key pillars: marketing practice, marketing practitioners an d marketing praxis. This study
can be utilized to explore various concepts related to diversit y, equity and inclusion (DEI) as
they pertain to value-creating marketing activities between firms and their customers.
To conduct this study, we employed a qualitative, exploratory r esearch strategy that included
a literature review and synthesis of key theories. Our framewor k proposes integrating critical
race theory and considerations of colour blindness to reduce ba rriers to diversity and enhance
marketing effectiveness. By doing so, we aim to foster practica l value-creating marketing
activities that contribute to value co-creation. We note that i ntegrating diversity strengthens
customer–firm engagement. This research contributes conceptuall y to the marketing-as-
practice framework.

Keywords: Colour blindness, discrimination, diversity, marketing-as-practice, critical race
theory, value co-creation

Introduction
Background of the Study
In recent years, cultural insensitivity has become increasingly prevalent in marketing practice,
despite efforts towards diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) ( Klinner & Walsh, 2013). The
exploration of diversity in marketing practice research is limi ted, despite evidence of
misalignment between firms and customers. Therefore, this research aims to integrate diversity
into the marketing-as-practice (MAP) framework, focusing on val ue creation to better align
firms with society at large.

Value creation is affected when there is misalignment or value co-destruction between firms’
value-creating marketing activities and society at large, speci fically, customers. It is reported
that customers have disengaged from brands due to misaligned ma rketing campaigns. These

‘brand blunders’, whether intentional or unintentional, negativ ely affect multicultural
consumers. As a result, customers’ trust and loyalty may be red uced, leading them to engage
in negative word of mouth and express their dissatisfaction on social media, ultimately
choosing not to make a purchase (Farmaki et al., 2021; Hirschma n, 1970; Pittman, 2020;
Schmitt et al., 2014).

Critical race theory (CRT) and colour-blindness (CB) theory are employed as diagnostic lenses
that reveal how discrimination may be normalized within the marketplace.
Research Problem
To our knowledge, there is currently no framework that integrat e s  d i v e r s i t y  i n t o  t h e  M A P
framework, even though evidence indicates value co-destruction. Therefore, we aim to address
the primary research question: How can we incorporate diversity  into the MAP framework to
create value?
Purpose of the Article
The purpose of the article is to extend the MAP framework by grounding it in extant diversity
theory embedded in marketing practices, practitioners and praxi s (PPP). By introducing a
conceptual framework that integrates diversity, the co-creation of customer and company value
can be enhanced, thereby strengthening the alignment between value-creating activities within
firms.
The Rationale of the Article
The paucity of diversity in marketing practices has significant cultural and social implications,
as consumers often perceive their purchases as reflecting their values, beliefs and attitudes and
refuse to engage with brands that do not reflect diversity (Ede lman, 2021; Yang et al., 2021).
This article explores the integration of diversity theory withi n the MAP framework. Doing so
can enhance value co-creation for both firms and customers, while also revealing new research
opportunities to strengthen the MAP framework.
Methodology
We employed the theory synthesis, which allowed us to summarize  and integrate existing
knowledge to provide a new perspective on a topic, and the theo ry adaptation, which focuses
on expanding or altering an existing theory by applying it to a  new context or domain, as
suggested by Jaakkola (2020). The two approaches enabled us to provide an enhanced
perspective of a phenomenon by linking previously unconnected pieces into a coherent whole.
Furthermore, the integration of diversity presented a new persp ective on the established
conceptualization of the MAP framework. This conceptual study is based on a literature review
that focuses on the MAP framework, followed by diversity, and o n how they are connected
through the three main pillars of the MAP framework. It applies the two theories, CRT and CB,
as diagnostic lenses to investigat e how inclusion and exclusion  are embedded in marke ting
practices that shape value-creating activities within firms, as shown in the MAP framework.

Literature Review
MAP Framework
The MAP framework (Figure 1) is a guide and research agenda for  value-creating marketing
activity, in its core propositions, and focuses on how value-cr eating marketing activities are
performed (Skålen et al., 2023). The MAP framework highlights the relationships among three
key concepts, marketing practice, marketing practitioners and m arketing praxis, in relation to
value creation for society at large. MAP focuses on analyzing h ow marketing is conducted
through value-creating practices a nd the role of practitioners.  It emphasizes marketing
activities, or services-as-practices, that either create or pot entially destroy it. However, in the
framework, the diversity of participants is not a primary concern. The framework aims to align
marketing with ‘society at large’, but it lacks essential social considerations, such as diversity.

The MAP framework in Figure 1 centres on three interconnected p illars of value-creating
marketing activity in firms (marketing practices, marketing practitioners and marketing praxis),
which are directly connected to society at large (customers, cl ients and partners), forming an
ongoing cycle. This is indicated by the continuous circular arr ows that visually represent an
iterative, cyclical process, unlike linear models that have a c lear beginning and end. Circular
arrows indicate that the output of the final stage feeds back i nto the first stage, creating a
continuous loop of improvement, evaluation, or development and alignment with society at
large (customers, clients, and partners).

Marketing practice consists of ‘templates of organized activiti es that marketing practitioners
routinely enact to create value for and with customers, clients , partners, and society’ (Skålen
et al., 2023, p. 194). Marketing practices that create value consist of four main elements (Skålen
et al., 2023):

1. Materials: Materials encompass both tangible and intangible res ources used in
marketing activities and are a key component of marketing practice (Shove et al., 2012).
This includes physical tools, technologies like software and AI  and representational
artefacts such as marketing plans and brochures. Our focus is on these representational
artefacts, as they directly engage consumers and shape marketing execution (Skålen et
al., 2023).

4

2. Procedures: Explicit rules and principles, also known as ‘discu rsive knowledge’.
Materials (such as Customer Relationship Management software) d ictate the specific
regulations and instructions marketers must follow.
3. Understandings: Skills and practical knowledge regarding what a ctions to take. The
available tools influence the practical ‘know-how’ and skills r equired by the
practitioner.
4. Engagements: Emotionally charged goals that reflect personal co mmitment. The
quality and availability of materials can affect the emotional commitment and goals of
those performing the marketing activity.

Marketing practitioners are individuals who perform activities to implement marketing
practices to achieve value-creating outcomes (Reckwitz, 2002; S chatzki, 2019; Shove et al.,
2012; Skålen et al., 2023). Additionally, market studies show how actors adopt similar practices
and emphasize their shared identity (Hawa et al., 2020).

Marketing praxis refers to the value-creating activities that result from marketing practitioners’
enactment. The framework’s ‘praxis’ element refers to the actual performance of marketing in
day-to-day work. Marketing practices do not determine praxis; i nstead, individuals enacting
the praxis diverge from or even go against them (Skålen et al., 2023). MAP studies support this
position, emphasizing the intentionality of actors who can purp osively replicate or
incrementally alter practices (Hawa et al., 2020).
Value Co-creation
The three interconnected pillars of value creation indicate how  value co-creation occurs in
practice (Skålen et al., 2023). Value is not simply ‘created’; it is influenced by the commitment
and time that participants invest in performing value co-creati on practices (VCPs). The more
proficient practitioners become at a practice, such as utilizin g a digital interface for research
or, for purposes of this research, diversity practice, the greater the value co-created. The MAP
framework indicates that the identities of marketing practition ers form the ‘nexus’ of the
practices they master. Successful value co-creation relies on h ow these identities correspond
with the firm’s practice templates.

Skålén and Gummerus (2023) assoc iated practices with redefined services, described as
bundles of VCPs, which they called ‘practice bundles’. Expandin g on this, Skålén (2024)
refined the definition to acknowledge that, while these practic e bundles aim for value co-
creation, they can lead to value co-destruction if not enacted correctly.

The beneficiary uniquely and phenomenologically determines valu e; it is subjective and
experiential (Vargo et al., 2017, p. 4; Vargo & Lusch, 2008, p. 7). Value is a multidimensional
construct that emerges from interactions among various actors a nd institutions. Therefore,
marketing decisions must consider cultural distinctions to avoi d stereotypes and ensure that
messaging does not adopt a one-size-fits-all approach. Cultural appropriation entails borrowing
elements from a specific culture with proper understanding and respect (Rona, 2023, p. 47).
When marketing to diverse, multicultural audiences, understandi ng social and cultural values
is crucial to avoid disrespect and protect the firm’s reputation.

The social and cultural context plays an essential role in valu e creation, arising from
collaboration among various actors, and should not misalign wit h the participants involved
(Akaka et al., 2013; Ertz, 2024; Mahajan, 2017; Vargo & Lusch, 2016). Engaging with

multicultural customers through online feedback allows their perspectives to be heard, thereby
reducing exclusion and shaping the firm’s image through consume r dialogue (Prahalad &
Ramaswamy, 2004). Value is emergent; it exists through the rela tionships between an actor
and the system. Some brands have aligned their marketing practi ces to changes in society at
large (Ferraro et al., 2023).
Value Co-destruction
Misalignment occurs when marketing praxis and the needs of exte rnal stakeholders are
misaligned. This misalignment results in the co-destruction of value, contributing to a shift in
marketing practices. Misalignment is an essential element because it ultimately leads to the co-
destruction of value between firms’ value-creating activities a nd their stakeholders.
Consumers, the focus of this article, are increasingly demanding that companies participate in
social justice initiatives, and companies are justifying their involvement in boycotts to benefit
the greater social good (Afego & Alagidede, 2021).

This misalignment can be particularly evident in organized mark eting activities, which can
exhibit bias against multicultural markets. Yoon and Kelly (2023) referred to marketing failures
stemming from racial insensitivity as ‘brand blunders’. Whether  intentional or unintentional,
these errors have societal and ethical implications that extend beyond financial loss; they also
affect public discourse and consumer trust, particularly among multicultural consumers. As a
result, customers may reduce their trust and loyalty, engage in negative word of mouth, express
their dissatisfaction on social media and ultimately choose not to make a purchase (Farmaki et
al., 2021; Hirschman, 1970; Pittman, 2020; Schmitt et al., 2014 ). Since a brand’s value is
closely tied to its emotional connection with consumers (Doyle, 2011), it is essential to research
the integration of diversity in marketing practices to ensure that marketing strategies align with
consumer expectations regarding diversity.
Diversity
The MAP framework views diversity as a marketing practice pheno menon where routines,
roles and enactment influence inclusion and exclusion. Diversit y is embedded in marketing
practices and enacted by practitioners, highlighting that ethni c consumer groups are not
monolithic (Cui, 1997). It is redefined as an active mental pro cess and strategic adaptation
influenced by cultural identity and situational context. Traditional research often overlooks the
nuances of language and trust within ethnic communities. True diversity in marketing extends
beyond merely featuring diverse models (Sekhon & Szmigin, 2009) . American literature
typically uses ‘ethnicity’, while European literature often use s ‘diversity’ to reflect varied
experiences. Recognizing differe nces is vital as consumer popul ations diversify in race,
ethnicity, sexual orientation and ability, with many seeking gr eater representation (Brodzik et
al., 2021).

Stigmas and negative stereotypes related to race, religion, gen der or socio-economic status
affect consumer interactions and self-perception (Arsel et al.,  2022). Diversity framing arises
from individuals’ perceptions of valued traits, shaping social identity. A group is considered
diverse when recognized traits distinguish them, often resultin g in minority status (Bernstein
et al., 2020). The customer DEI framework suggests that compani es are shaped by societal
actors, minority markets, resources and marketing activities, l eading to entities characterized
by racial and income representation (Park et al., 2022). In this context, DEI refers to diversity.

Diversity Embedded in Marketing Practices
Diversity embedded in marketing p ractices transforms marketing into a socia lly e mbedded,
relational and contextual activity rather than a standardized, functional one (Grieco, 2025;
Spurling et al., 2013). The MAP framework emphasizes what marke ting practitioners do by
integrating diverse perspectives  into routines, tools and norms  (Skålén, 2024). Traditional
marketing practices are often guided by templates and segmentat ion models that reflect the
dominant social group’s assumptions, potentially marginalizing other communities. Failing to
incorporate diversity can result in repetitive and unconsidered  marketing. By embracing
diversity, marketing routines become more inclusive, drawing on  varied cultural and
demographic insights, which inform updated segmentation and bra nd guidelines. This shift
leads to inclusive content development that accurately represents diverse communities, utilizes
inclusive language and selects media channels that effectively reach diverse audiences, rather
than relying solely on mass media.
Diversity Embedded in Marketing Practitioners
The MAP framework emphasizes the critical role of the individua ls involved in marketing
practices. A key element of this framework is promoting diversi ty within marketing teams
(Murto et al., 2025). Marketing practitioners, who address the essential question of ‘who does
it?’, are often influenced by the assumptions inherent in exist ing marketing strategies. By
integrating diversity into these teams, organizations can enhan ce the composition and
perspective of their practitioners. A diverse team brings a wea lth of lived experiences, which
can significantly drive creativity and innovation while providing deeper insights into customer
needs (Hewlett et al., 2013).

Moreover, diverse teams are generally better at identifying and eliminating unconscious biases
in campaigns, thereby reducing the risk of harmful, stereotypic al portrayals of consumer
segments (Jackson, 1992). Internal diversity initiatives, such as targeted hiring and mentorship
programmes, are crucial for cultivating authentic marketing str ategies, which have become
integral to practitioners’ roles (Murto et al., 2025).

Additionally, the emotional aspect of diversity is often overlo oked. Marketing practitioners
frequently focus solely on extern al behaviours, neglecting the internal feelings of belonging
that motivate consumer consumption (Jamal & Chapman, 2000). By recognizing and valuing
both the rational and emotional dimensions of diversity, market ing teams can create more
meaningful and effective connections with their audiences.
Diversity Embedded in Marketing Praxis
It is in marketing praxis (values and context) that diversity becomes visible to society at large.
Diversity integration is essential because it resonates with or alienates customers. An analysis
of diversity at this level will identify alignment or misalignm e n t .  P r a x i s  i s  t h e  p r a c t i c a l
application of knowledge, including the ethical and social dime nsions of marketing work.
Active, ongoing attempts to reduce barriers and advance equity to ensure that representation is
not just employed as tokenism, such as ‘diversity washing’, but  a commitment to genuine
representation (Protopapa & Plangger, 2023). When diversity is integrated into marketing
practice, practitioners and praxis, the potential for value co-creation or co-destruction becomes
more apparent. Integrating diversity into the MAP framework mak es it a core analytical
dimension.

Diagnostic Lenses: CRT and CB
CRT and CB are two concepts that, while often viewed as opposing, share common goals and
historical roots. Both emerged in the wake of the 1960s Civil R ights Movement, with CB
initially promoted as a moral standard to advance the movement’ s aims, and CRT developing
as a legal framework to assess progress.

In CRT, we highlight four tenets that address the research ques tion. First, ‘racism as usual’
positions racism as a designed part of ordinary life, contrary to a deviation or a role of a limited
number of racist persons (Cabrera, 2018). It is the ordinary wa y people conduct business, the
daily treatment of multicultural people. CRT recognizes the durable ubiquity of racism—from
private opinions to personal relationships, offices and establishments, such as the marketplace
(Delgado & Stefancic, 2017). Therefore, racism is not the summa tion of discriminatory
behaviours and individual positions, but a mental condition entrenched in the minds of people,
cultures, structures and organizations (Bonilla-Silva, 2016; Cu ellar, 2017). Second, interest
convergence or material determinism suggests that the problem o f racism is being addressed.
Still, below the exterior, this is not in the best interest of previously disadvantaged consumers
(Bell, 1980). Racism physically expands the material luxuries o f the privileged and suburban
populations. However, many in society have little motivation to  eliminate it. Third, race is a
social construction and a classification society form based on bodily characteristics that society
creates, deploys or withdraws when deemed suitable. Individuals with shared backgrounds are
allocated selected physical characteristics, including skin col our and hair texture (Delgado &
Stefancic, 2017). Ultimately, intersectionality is the concept t h a t  e v e r y  p e r s o n  p o s s e s s e s
multiple identities that may intersect, and no individual has a  singular, uniform identity
(Mitchell, 2020).
CB
Colour-blind racism is the belief that racism no longer exists.  This form of racism is indirect
and subtle, making it harder to identify and address. Four ways  have been identified in which
individuals employ CB to perpetuate racism. Cultural racism occ urs when individuals are
biased against others based on their cultural backgrounds. Abst ract liberalism involves
individuals supporting liberal principles, particularly in matt ers of race. The naturalization of
racism refers to the belief among advantaged groups that their actions are not racially
motivated, even when those actions contribute to systemic racis m (Bonilla-Silva, 2003).
Finally, minimizing racism involves denying its existence and d ownplaying its impact, often
leading to the blame directed at Black individuals for ‘playing the race card’ (Gwaltney, 1993).

Cosmetic colour blindness is a concept that emphasizes race whi le steering clear of direct
discussions about racism. It reveals marketing strategies associated with colourism (Mitchell,
2020) that categorize racial issues in ways that allow for conversations about diversity without
directly addressing racism, signalling that diversity goals have been achieved (Ernst, 2010).

By examining marketing practices and practitioners through the lenses of ‘racism as usual’,
‘race as a social construct’ and ‘infinity of racism’, we see h ow segmentation, targeting and
data management can disadvantage marginalized consumer groups (Poole et al., 2021). These
practices, integral to the MAP framework, focused on ‘What we d o’ and prompted marketers
to assess how systemic racism may be perpetuated critically. This evaluation encourages a shift
from ‘colour-blind’ approaches to more inclusive, ‘colour-consc ious’ practices, essential for
developing equitable marketing strategies that promote representation and challenge systemic

8

biases and authoritative structu res. The interest convergence t enet and abstract liberalism are
linked to the marketing praxis pillar, which includes actions a nd value-creating activities in
marketing. These perspectives provide tools for analyzing marke ting outcomes and assessing
whether systemic inequalities are reinforced. Recognizing that racism operates at a systemic
level (Poole et al., 2021), implementing proactive, diverse mar keting practices is crucial for
achieving meaningful success and promoting a more equitable marketplace, measured not only
by return on investment (ROI) but also by advancing equity within the industry.

considers how seemingly neutral marketing practices can be excl usionary and misalign value
co-creation. The integration of these aspects into the MAP framework offers conceptual insight
into misalignment and value destruction.

A Conceptual Framework: Integrating Diversity into the MAP Framework
Critical Diagnostic Lenses: CRT and CB
The critical diagnostic lenses of CRT and CB enhance the introd uction of the conceptual
framework that integrates diversity. The lenses reveal how excl usion and inclusion are
embedded in marketing work. With particular focus on abstract l iberalism, interest
convergence, minimizing racism, differential racialization, inf inity of racism and racism as
usual, the lenses provide vital insights into the performance of the three foundational pillars of
value-creating activities within firms. By examining marketing practices through the
perspectives of CRT and CB, we can uncover the embedded power d ynamics and biases that
operate within these PPPs.

These critical lenses help us understand how practice is interp reted and how it influences the
actions of marketing professionals as they engage in their work. Moreover, they play a crucial
role in shaping their own marketing practices. When we apply th ese diagnostic lenses, we

The implications for marketing practitioners are significant. These individuals are responsible
for implementing practices, yet they frequently operate within environments lacking adequate
representation and diversity, especially among decision-makers and those who hold
interpretive authority in marketing practices. This deficiency manifests in marketing initiatives
that encompass concrete campaigns, content creation, imagery, l anguage, tone and context-
specific decisions necessary for effective communication.

Moreover, the prevalence of colour blindness in marketing—where  issues of racism are
understated or dismissed—combined with the principles of abstra ct liberalism and
naturalization, further complicates the push for inclusion. Thi s can create the potential for
unintended offence and limit inclusivity, ultimately affecting society as a whole and alienating
customers. When marketing campaigns do not resonate with their audience’s historical, cultural
and social contexts, they risk being perceived as either inclusive or discriminatory.

Therefore, it is essential to recognize that customers interpret marketing messages through their
unique lenses, shaped by their experiences and backgrounds. A f ailure to align marketing
practices with these perspectives can result not only in exclus ionary practices but also in
significant detriment to consumer value.

We propose that integrating dive rsity into the three value-crea ting marketing pillars within
firms is essential to mitigate value co-destruction. Diversity- informed marketing practice
recognizes that the marketplace is not a neutral environment an d actively seeks to challenge
and dismantle systemic hierarchies. By acknowledging and addres sing these dynamics, firms
can create more inclusive strategies that enhance their value creation.

Marketing Praxis
1. Concrete campaigns and content: Assessing marketing content for  elements that may

Society at Large
Society in the MAP framework consists of three primary components: customers, partners and
clients, with our research speci fically focusing on customers. Customers play an interpretive
and evaluative role in their engagement with marketing activiti es, experiencing them through
various lenses such as historical, cultural and social contexts. These perspectives significantly
shape their responses and overall engagement, making them vital  participants in the value-
creation process.
The way customers interpret or misinterpret marketing activitie s can lead to two distinct
outcomes: value co-creation or value co-destruction. Value co-c reation—an ideal outcome—
occurs when customers and firms align effectively, fostering co llaborative relationships that
enhance mutual satisfaction. This alignment is facilitated thro ugh inclusive and reflexive
practices that invite customer participation and feedback.
Conversely, value co-destruction can arise from misalignment, o ften resulting from
exclusionary practices or perceived discrimination. Such barrie r s  c a n  i n h i b i t  c u s t o m e r
engagement, leading to negative responses toward marketing acti vity. Understanding these
dynamics is crucial for firms aiming to optimize their marketin g strategies and foster positive
customer relationships.
Implications for Theory and Practice
The findings suggest that scholarship on diversity in the MAP f ramework is lacking, and that
colourism and racism are subtle yet prevalent in the marketing of value-creating activities
within firms. By incorporating diversity into the MAP framework, we are making a significant
conceptual contribution that enhances the alignment between the  customer and the firm.
Furthermore, by linking co-creation and diversity, engagement i s reinforced, which further
builds on the existing MAP framework. These findings also suggest that the MAP framework
could be further expanded to incorporate other concepts from DE I theory, namely inclusion
and equity, to complement the value-creation theory. The concep t of Consumer Racism (CR)
should be addressed and further investigated, as it undermines customer–firm engagement. In

Research Agenda, Limitations and Conclusion
This section outlines a research agenda to guide future studies  on marketing activities that
advance alignment among multicultural/ethnic customers. As outlined in the initial and second
literature reviews, the area of diversity in brands, marketing and marketing practice lacks
scholarly research. Future, research should focus on (a) incorp orating equity, inclusion and
representation within the marketing practice (that shape value- creating activities); (b)
incorporating equity, inclusion and representation within the concept of marketing practitioners
(as the enactors of marketing activities); (c) incorporating equity, inclusion and representation
within the concept of marketing praxis (the daily activities of  m a r k e t i n g  p r a c t i t i o n e r s ) ;  ( d )
strengthening the divide between practice and theory, such as d eveloping marketing plans,
which are central to practitioners and (e) reducing perceived c onsumer discrimination and
mitigating the negative impacts on willingness to pay and purch ase intentions. Given the
vastness of the internet and the persistence of diversity issues in marketing across markets, this
article adopts a global perspectiv e. A focused single-country o r continental study would be
valuable for assessing the proposed framework’s similarities an d differences, as well as its
outcomes. We do not address CR in this article; therefore, futu re research should focus on
understanding and addressing it in marketing practice, as well as its impact on customers’
willingness to purchase. Customer perceptions of diversity are reportedly low, which impacts
how they engage with firms. There fore, further scholarship is r equired as customer needs
become discordant.

We argued that cultural insensitivity contributes to the misali gnment and perpetuation of
consumer discrimination resulti ng from marketing activities. We  aimed to expand the MAP
framework (Figure 1) by incorporating diversity to reinforce al ignment between firms’ and
customers’ value-creating marketing activities, drawing on CRT and the CB theory (Figure 2).
The alignment is supported through firm and customer engagement (co-creation and diversity).
By drawing on the MAP framework, we incorporated diversity in m arketing, which is the
primary contribution of this research article and its research agenda.
