Abstract1
As generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) continues to reshape decision-making
across industries, this paper re-examines Managerial School of Marketing Thought from a
contemporary perspective. Instead of dismissing foundational logic, the study introduces a
hybrid conceptual model called the Augmented Managerial Loop (AML), which integrates
GenAI capabilities into each stage of traditional marketing management such as
environmental scanning, goal setting, implementation, monitoring, and evaluation. The
model emphasizes how GenAI tools such as scenario simulation, adaptive content
generation, and intelligent dashboards can enhance human judgment rather than replace it.
It also proposes a shift in the marketer’s role from strategic planner to ethical orchestrator
within AI-augmented systems. By bridging established strategic models with emergin g
technologies, this work offers a forward-looking perspective on how marketing theory and
practice are evolving. Drawing from interdisciplinary insights in strategic marketing, digital
transformation, and AI ethics, the paper contributes to ongoing discussions around human–
AI collaboration and algorithmic responsibility. It also outlines future research directions
including co-agency, transparency in decision flows, and the educational transformation
required to prepare marketers for AI-enhanced environments. Ultimately, the study positions
GenAI not as a substitute for human reasoning but as a strategic ally in crafting more
adaptive, ethical, and resilient marketing strategies.
Keywords: Digital Marketing, Artificial Intelligence, Decision-making,
Generativity, Automation.

INTRODUCTION
Marketing thought has always evolved in step with social and technological
change. Among the many perspectives that have shaped the field over time, the
Managerial School of Marketing Thought has played a particularly central role.
Known for its structured, analytical, and decision-oriented approach, this school
helped define modern marketing practice. It emerged in the mid-twentieth century
during the height of industrial economies, a time when markets were relatively
stable, consumer behavior could be modeled with some confidence, and
organizations placed great value on efficiency and managerial control (Sheth,
Gardner et al., 1988). Today, however, the marketing landscape looks very different.
Generative Artificial Intelligence, often referred to as GenAI, is transforming how
marketing decisions are made and reshaping the roles involved in those decisions.
With tools such as ChatGPT, DALL·E, Midjourney, we are witnessing a shift where
algorithms not only support marketers but also participate actively in ideation,
segmentation, planning, and strategic thinking (Grewal et al., 2024). In this context,
many of the original assumptions of the Managerial School, including its emphasis
on human rationality, centralized control, and linear planning, are being critically
reevaluated.

This paper begins with a simple but timely question: can the core principles
of the Managerial School still hold relevance in an era where autonomous systems
are shaping marketing outcomes? This question becomes increasingly important as
organizations turn to GenAI not only to streamline operations but also to generate
campaigns, personalize offerings, and engage in real-time value creation. Although
the literature has grown in areas such as hybrid intelligence (Dellermann et al.,
2021), algorithmic governance (Pasquale, 2015), and augmented decision-making
(Sharma et al., 2024), there remains a gap in examining how traditional marketing
paradigms fit within these emerging conditions. The purpose of this study is to
address that gap through a conceptual synthesis. Instead of suggesting that classical
thinking should be discarded, the paper proposes a reframing that values both
enduring human insight and the expanding role of generative AI as a strategic
partner. The discussion unfolds in four parts. First, it revisits the historical
foundations and theoretical assumptions of the Managerial School. Second, it
explores the ways in which GenAI tools challenge and complement those
foundations. Third, it presents the Augmented Managerial Loop, or AML, which is
a conceptual model integrating traditional strategic stages with AI -enhanced
capabilities. Finally, the paper discusses the theoretical and practical implications of
this model and outlines directions for future research.
By linking the past with the future of marketing thought, this study
encourages a reimagining of strategy. It invites scholars to see the marketing
manager not only as a planner or decision-maker but also as an orchestrator
in an intelligent, collaborative ecosystem. To the best of the author’s
knowledge, the Augmented Managerial Loop introduced in this study is a new
contribution to the literature. By embedding GenAI functions into classical
marketing strategy, it offers a renewed perspective on leadership  and
decision-making in an increasingly AI-enhanced environment. Recent studies
have also begun to explore the implications of GenAI in marketing strategy
and innovation management, highlighting the need for new conceptual
models (Chan & Choi, 2025; Hermann & Puntoni, 2024).

THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS OF THE MANAGERIAL SCHOOL
The Managerial School of Marketing Thought, articulated in the influential
work of Sheth, Gardner, and Garrett (1988), marked one of the earliest
comprehensive efforts to define marketing as a strategic, decision-driven function
within the firm. Emerging after World War II, during a time of rapid industrial
expansion, this school responded to the growing need for structured approaches that
could help organizations compete more effectively in increasingly complex markets
(Kotler, 1967;Alderson & Shapiro, 1957)

At the heart of this school was the belief that marketing should be treated as
a science of decision-making.In this view, managers were expected to analyze
alternatives, allocate resources efficiently, and implement strategies using a set of
structured tools such as the marketing mix, segmentation–targeting–positioning, and
response models grounded in data. The approach was largely normative, seeking
answers to practical questions: What should managers do to improve outcomes?
Which frameworks would guide the most effective strategies?
Core Assumptions of the Managerial Paradigm
The theoretical foundation of the Managerial School is built upon four central
assumptions.
First, it assumes that marketing decisions are driven by logic, planning, and
rational analysis. Managers are portrayed as agents who aim to make utility -
maximizing choices based on available data and clear objectives (Howard, 1983;
Hunt, 1981).
Second, the model emphasizes centralized control. Strategy is typically
formulated at the top of the organizational hierarchy, where marketing leaders
define brand positioning and value propositions. In this model, customers are largely
seen as recipients rather than co-creators of value (Borden, 1964).
Third, the school operates under the belief that markets are relatively stable
and can be predicted using historical data. This view supported the rise of early
frameworks like AIDA, hierarchy-of-effects models, and linear forecasting tools
(Lavidge & Steiner, 1961).
Finally, the paradigm rests on the notion of linear causality. It assumes a
direct and measurable relationship between marketing actions and consumer
responses. In other words, what a company does—whether it’s pricing, placement,
or promotion—is expected to generate predictable reactions from the market
(Kotler, 1972).
These assumptions made perfect sense in the mid-twentieth century, when
economies were driven by mass production, standardized products, and relatively
slow-changing demand. However, in today’s fast-paced, service-oriented, and
digitally saturated markets, these assumptions are being questioned more than ever.
While much of the literature has focused on predictive AI, emerging work
emphasizes the distinct strategic value of generative AI in campaign ideation and
scenario planning (Chan & Choi, 2025). These contributions underline the necessity
for updated frameworks that reflect the evolving human -AI collaboration in
marketing environments (Mariani & Dwivedi, 2024).
Critiques and Evolutions
Concerns about the limitations of the Managerial School began to emerge as
early as the 1980s. Scholars like Hunt (1983) argued that marketing required a richer

theoretical foundation, one that moved beyond rigid decision models. Later, Vargo
and Lusch (2004) introduced the idea of Service -Dominant Logic, which
emphasized the importance of value co -creation, interaction, and long-term
relationships between firms and customers.
Other schools of thought also challenged the foundational premises of
managerial logic. The Systems School (Sheth et al., 1988) and the Behavioral
School (Wensley, 1995), for instance, questioned whether rationality and top-down
control truly reflected the way marketing worked in real life. They suggested that
marketing is often complex, messy, and deeply influenced by organizational
dynamics and consumer behavior.
Despite these critiques, the Managerial School has proven remarkably
resilient. It continues to be taught in business schools and applied in practice, largely
because of its clarity and actionable structure.
The Managerial School in Contemporary Strategic Thinking
In today’s fast-evolving marketing landscape, the legacy of the Managerial
School continues to shape both academic education and business practice.
Frameworks like SWOT analysis, strategic planning matrices, KPI dashboards, and
STP segmentation remain widely used, reflecting a persistent belief in the value of
structured, data-driven, and rational decision-making (Kotler & Keller, 2016; Ferrell
& Hartline, 2023). Many of the tools that marketers rely on today, including CRM
platforms and analytics dashboards, are still built on the assumption that information
moves in a single direction—flowing from the market to the manager, who interprets
the data and responds with top-down decisions (Chaffey & Ellis-Chadwick, 2019).
This continued dependence on classical managerial logic, however, may
create what scholars describe as “theoretical lock-in,” a situation in which familiar
mental models are applied to contexts that have fundamentally changed (Day,
2011). The emergence of artificial intelligence, real-time data ecosystems, and
personalized digital interactions challenges the notion that strategic insight must
come solely from human managers (Wedel & Kannan, 2016). Generative AI tools
now possess the ability to create conten t, simulate decision outcomes, and
autonomously refine marketing strategies, which pushes us to rethink what it truly
means to make decisions in marketing (Davenport, Guha, Grewal, & Bressgott,
2020).
At the same time, a growing number of scholars have questioned whether
rigid planning frameworks can still provide value in highly uncertain and dynamic
environments. Rather than being the product of deliberate top-down design, strategy
often emerges from ongoing adaptation and iterative learning (Mintzberg,
Ahlstrand, & Lampel, 2005). This reality suggests that a strict adherence to the
original assumptions of the Managerial School may limit the flexibility and
creativity needed to thrive amid rapid technological shifts.

Recognizing the tensions between tradition and transformation, as well as
between control and collaboration, opens the door to a renewed perspective on
managerial thought. Exploring how classical foundations can evolve alongside
generative technologies lays the groundwork for a more adaptive and collaborative
approach to strategic marketing.

DISRUPTION BY GEN-AI: FROM STRATEGIC RATIONALITY TO
ALGORITHMIC AGENCY
The arrival of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) technologies
represents a significant shift in how marketing strategy is imagined and carried out.
Earlier AI systems were primarily focused on automating routine tasks, classifying
data, or generating predictions based on historical inputs (Davenport et al., 2020).
GenAI, however, brings something fundamentally new to the table: machines that
can create, imagine, and ideate (Bubeck et al., 2023; Dwivedi et al., 2023). With this
development, algorithms are no longer mere assistants; they have become creative
collaborators that challenge long-established assumptions about how strategic
decisions are made (Sharma et al., 2024; Dellermann et al., 2021).
GenAI models such as GPT-4, DALL·E, Claude, and Sora are trained on
enormous volumes of text, images, and video, enabling them to generate content
that closely mirrors human creativity (Bubeck et al., 2023). In marketing, they are
now used for storytelling, campaign design, branding, and crafting personalized
customer experiences (Dwivedi et al., 2023). Their role is no longer limited to
execution; they are actively shaping the strategic processes themselves.
Applications like Copy.ai and Jasper enable marketers to create campaign
messages tailored to specific personas, channels, and tones, in real time and at scale.
This shifts the center of strategic activity from top-down planning to a dynamic
interaction between human insight and machine-generated alternatives (Grewal et
al., 2024). Algorithms no longer just support strategy—they influence and co-create
it (Sharma et al., 2024). This evolution in algorithmic creativity is consistent with
recent findings by Hermann and Puntoni (2024), who note that GenAI alters not just
execution but the core dynamics of strategic thinking and consumer interaction.
Reconsidering the Foundations of Managerial Rationality
The emergence of GenAI has prompted a deep rethinking of how strategic
decisions are made in marketing. Traditional models are grounded in principles of
rational planning, centralized control, and predictable outcomes. These models
assume that managers act as logical architects who design strategies based on data
and fixed objectives. However, GenAI challenges this foundation by bringing forth
an intelligence that is adaptive, creative, and constantly evolving through its
interaction with the environment.

Classical marketing frameworks rely on structured processes that guide
managers from analysis to action through a clear path. In contrast, GenAI functions
through pattern recognition and probabilistic modeling, allowing it to respond
fluidly to changing inputs (Boden, 2009). This approach shifts the nature of
decision-making from planning to emergence, where outcomes are shaped by real-
time interactions rather than pre-defined routes.
Strategic authority is no longer held exclusively by human managers. GenAI
systems now participate actively by suggesting ideas, evaluating alternatives, and
even enhancing decisions with insights that might not occur to a human strategist
(Dellermann et al., 2021). This interaction transforms the role of the manager from
a sole decision-maker to a co-creator in a more distributed cognitive system.
Where traditional analytics look to the past to forecast the future, GenAI can
simulate entirely new possibilities. It generates synthetic data, crafts imaginative
scenarios, and enables marketers to consider futures that go beyond historical
patterns (Sharma et al., 2024). These capabilities expand the boundaries of strategic
imagination.
Additionally, the conventional input-process-output logic is evolving. GenAI
systems learn continuously, incorporating feedback loops that refine and improve
their outputs over time (Lippert, 2024). This makes decision-making more iterative
and dynamic, aligning it more closely with how innovation and adaptation actually
happen in practice.
Rather than viewing GenAI as a disruption to managerial control, it can be
seen as a partner in building a more flexible, responsive, and collaborative approach
to strategy.
Rethinking Decision-Making with Hybrid Intelligence
The emerging idea of hybrid intelligence provides a compelling framework
to understand the evolving relationship between human managers and AI. Within
this view, strategic decision-making becomes less about individual authority and
more about distributed, cooperative insight. Both human intuition and machine
optimization are essential in this new environment (Dellermann et al., 2021).
Studies show that the greatest value arises when AI is used not to replace
people, but to augment their thinking. GenAI, for example, is capable of identifying
subtle changes in consumer sentiment that might escape human observation. This
enables quicker responses and more nuanced brand communication (Sharma et al.,
2024).
What makes this partnership especially powerful is that GenAI systems can
also reflect on their own outputs. They evaluate their performance, make
adjustments, and improve over time, just as seasoned managers do when learning
from past results (Dellermann et al., 2021; Lippert, 2024; Sharma et al., 2024).
Altogether, these developments signal a move away from isolated managerial

THE MANAGERIAL SCHOOL REVISITED IN THE AGE OF
GENERATIVE AI: A CONCEPTUAL SYNTHESIS
Rather than suggesting a departure from the Managerial School of Marketing
Thought, this paper offers a reimagining of its foundational logic through a hybrid
model known as the Augmented Managerial Loop (AML). This conceptual
framework retains the structured progression of classical strategy cycles, but
reinterprets each stage through the lens of generative  and adaptive artificial
intelligence. AML is built around five dynamic stages that mirror the traditional
phases of marketing strategy, each augmented with GenAI-enabled processes. It
signals a move away from rigid linear models toward an adaptive system in which
humans and machines collaboratively generate, implement, and refine marketing
strategies through ongoing feedback (Dellermann et al., 2021; Grewal et al., 2024;
Sharma et al., 2024).
Environmental Scanning :  AI-Augmented Sensemaking
In traditional marketing models, understanding the market began with well-
known tools such as competitor analysis, consumer surveys, and trend reports.
Managers relied on structured data to make sense of their environment. In the
Augmented Managerial Loop (AML), this process is enriched by the capabilities of
generative AI. Instead of simply reviewing what has already happened, AI tools now
allow marketers to listen to the market in real time, capturing billions of digital
signals from platforms like social media, product reviews, and online forums.
Advanced platforms such as Brandwatch, NetBase Quid, and Talkwalker can
detect subtle shifts in sentiment, identify rising consumer concerns, and uncover
hidden patterns in how people talk about products and brands (Daugherty & Wilson,
2018; Chatterjee et al., 2021). This goes beyond static analysis. AI brings marketers
closer to the pulse of the market, offering:
• Insights into where public sentiment is heading
• Early warnings about reputational risks or emerging demands
• The ability to group and interpret unstructured conversations through
semantic analysis (Grewal et al., 2024)
In short, AI transforms environmental scanning from a retrospective activity
into a real-time, forward-looking sensemaking process—giving marketers a clearer
and faster view of the world they operate in.

Goal and Strategy Formulation : Scenario Simulation and Generative
Planning
Traditionally, marketing strategy has relied on linear forecasting to set long-
term goals. However, within the Augmented Managerial Loop, this approach
transforms into a more fluid and exploratory process. Generative AI tools such as
ChatGPT and Claude allow marketers to simulate scenarios, test campaign ideas,
and explore alternative strategic directions before taking action. Methods like Monte
Carlo simulations, once primarily used for risk analysis, are now leveraged to
generate a range of possible outcomes and design adaptive planning environments
(Shrestha et al., 2019; Bharadwaj et al., 2023; Haenlein & Kaplan, 2022).
Tactical Implementation : Autonomous Execution and Generative Content
In traditional marketing workflows, the implementation of strategies typically
involved teams executing plans through manual coordination across various
channels. Within the Augmented Managerial Loop, this phase becomes significantly
more agile and automated. Generative AI transforms execution through tools that
enable programmatic media buying, real-time content creation, and adaptive visual
design (Haenlein & Kaplan, 2022).
Major brands such as Adidas and Coca-Cola have already embraced this shift
by using GenAI platforms like Runway and Midjourney to develop commercial
videos and promotional visuals, achieving results in a fraction of the time previously
required (Sullivan & Seldon, 2023). Across industries, marketing teams are
increasingly relying on AI-driven systems to generate visuals, write compelling
copy, and even produce audio content in real time. This evolution not only
accelerates the creative process but also reduces production costs and enhances the
ability to iterate quickly based on immediate feedback (Dwivedi et al., 2023;
Chatterjee et al., 2021).
Monitoring & Control : Dynamic Feedback, Self-Tuning Systems
In traditional marketing approaches, monitoring relied heavily on lagging
indicators such as sales reports or post-campaign analyses. Within the Augmented
Managerial Loop, this phase is redefined by the use of adaptive control systems
powered by machine learning. AI-driven dashboards now provide marketers with
real-time insights, predictive analytics, and even automated storytelling that
translates data into actionable narratives (Chatterjee et al., 2021; Lippert, 2024).
These systems continuously analyze live performance data and adjust
strategies on the fly. For example, machine learning algorithms can reallocate
budgets, optimize campaign elements, and personalize messaging without requiring
constant human intervention (Davenport & Ronanki, 2018). Instead of manually
managing every detail, marketers are freed to focus on strategic inflection points
and broader ethical considerations.

Managerial Judgment :  Orchestration and Ethical Curation
As generative systems grow more autonomous, the role of the marketing
manager undergoes a meaningful shift. Rather than functioning as strict controllers
of every task, managers become orchestrators of a collaborative ecosystem. They
are responsible for defining high-level goals, crafting meaningful prompts, and
ensuring that the outputs of AI systems align with brand strategy and consumer
values. This new role also carries ethical weight. Managers must monitor for issues
of bias, fairness, and transparency within generative processes (Pasquale, 2015;
Dignum, 2019). As AI-generated outputs increasingly influence consumer
experience, ensuring accountability becomes a key function. Recent research even
identifies emerging roles such as prompt designer, system tuner, and ethical
reviewer that reflect this new terrain (Kumar et al., 2023). Rather than relinquishing
authority, managers evolve into thoughtful co-creators—guiding meaning, ensuring
integrity, and curating impact in partnership with intelligent machines (Sharma et
al., 2024).
To make these ethical responsibilities more tangible in everyday marketing
practice, managers working within AI-augmented systems can follow a simple yet
structured reflection tool—a concise ethical co-agency checklist designed for the
“Managerial Judgment” stage of the AML:
• Bias awareness: Stay alert to potential bias or stereotype reinforcement in
datasets and AI-generated content.
• Transparency: Keep a clear record of prompts, data sources, and decision
logic so that every stage of the process can be traced and explained.
• Accountability: Define who is responsible for what—ensuring that human
and machine roles remain distinct and that ethical responsibility does not blur.
• Value alignment: Revisit whether AI-supported strategies continue to reflect
the brand’s core values and stakeholder expectations.
• Human oversight: Always preserve the human right to question, override,
or reinterpret AI outputs that conflict with ethical or strategic intent.
Incorporating such a reflective checklist into the managerial judgment stage
helps translate abstract ethical principles into daily decision-making practice. It
keeps human reasoning at the center of the AML, reminding managers that
responsible co-agency is as much about mindfulness and transparency as it is about
innovation.

DISCUSSION AND FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS
This study offers a timely rethinking of the Managerial School of Marketing
Thought by viewing it through the lens of Generative Artificial Intelligence
(GenAI). Rather than discarding classical foundations, it proposes a way to evolve
them—introducing the Augmented Managerial Loop (AML), a conceptual model

CONCLUSION
This paper offers a fresh perspective on the tried and true concepts of the
Managerial School of Marketing in the context of how they may be constructed in
harmony with the capabilities of Generative AI. Rather than sending traditional
strategic thinking packing, it suggests an enriching way to augment it through
synergy of human sagacity and machine minds. The following Augmented
Managerial Loop (AML) illustrates how all steps of the marketing strategy can be
augmented using AI-based tools to make it more agile, creative, and responsive
decision-maker.
By harmonizing disciplined managerial reflection with the adaptive and
generative power of AI, this study opens up new possibilities for strategy to be
conceived and implemented. The framework challenges marketers to escape rigid
planning loops and instead take on a more fluid, iterative, and values -driven
approach. With GenAI becoming a proactive participant in strategic processes,
marketing leadership is forced to reimagine itself to lead, curate, and co-create rather
than dictate.
This shift demands additional inquiry and reflection. How will the balancing
act between efficiency and ethical governance be carried out? How can marketers
get ready to guide in smart systems? And what new positions will emerge where
judgment and technology intersect? The answers to these questions will shape not
only the future of marketing thought but also the nature of managerial agency in a
world where computation and creativity meet.
Building on these questions, it is apparent that the discussion is not simply
one of technology take-up but of changing the very definition of marketing
leadership. For decades, the Managerial School has placed its bets on analytical
acumen, planning, and systematic decision-making. These principles remain sound
but are now complemented by an increasing requirement for flexibility and
awareness of the social and ethical consequences of artificial intelligence.
Generative AI not only means faster processing of information but also new forms
of creativity, fresh solutions, and the ability to model complicated marketplace
dynamics in real time. The marketers who acknowledge this two-ness — reconciling
rigorous analysis with creative discovery — will be the successful ones.
Another issue is the education revolution necessary to prepare the next
generation of marketing professionals. Business schools and schools of training
have long focused on case study, segmentation, and classical models of consumer
behavior. But if marketers are going to successfully manage AI systems, they must
acquire fluency in data ethics, algorithmic transparency, and human –machine

collaboration as well. Courses will need to introduce not only analytics but critical
analysis of bias, privacy, and social impact of AI-driven campaigns. Leaders will
require the ability to query outputs from machines and exercise judgment where
data-driven suggestions contradict brand values or long-term credibility.
Furthermore, organizational culture within marketing strategy may need to
adapt. Top-down decision-making in a traditional manner may be too slow and rigid
for environments where GenAI provides instant insights and rapid prototyping.
Organizations may become more networked, multidisciplinary arrangements where
marketers work closely with data scientists, ethicists, and technologists. Decision
cycles may become shorter but also increasingly iterative, involving ongoing
testing, feedback, and fine-tuning rather than fixed long-term plans.
Ethical issues become more relevant as AI systems start to influence not just
tactics but also strategic intent. Who is accountable when an AI -based strategy
unintentionally reinforces stereotypes, shapes consumer behavior, or violates
privacy? The AML model, while beneficial, subtly requests that marketers take on
a sense of stewardship — ensuring that the potential of AI is harnessed for good and
in harmony with human values. This methodology positions marketing leaders from
being sole decision-makers to being defenders of innovation and integrity.
Practically, companies might need to redefine their investment priorities and
measures of success. Success might no longer be defined solely in terms of
efficiency and ROI but also in terms of flexibility, ethical openness, and consumer
trust. AI-driven dashboards could enable real-time monitoring not only of fiscal
performance but also of social sentiment, fairness metrics, and long-term brand
durability. Marketers will need to advocate these wider measures of success inside
the C-suite.
Finally, this article points to the need for ongoing research. The questions
posed here — of co-agency, transparency in decision flow, and humanizing AI-
backed strategy — must be pursued through an interdisciplinary lens. Inputs from
philosophy, organizational psychology, and design thinking have the potential to
supplement the technical and analytical know-how already present in the marketing
literature. Through interlinking these fields, the discipline of marketing can lead
rather than be led by the AI revolution.
Ultimately, the study puts Generative AI not in the position of displacing
human intellect but as an ally — a collaborator that aids in creating more flexible,
moral, and resilient marketing approaches. For marketing to remain deeply human,
its leaders must learn not only how to master technology but to question it, to steer
it, and to weave it into the rich brocade of human imagination and judgment. This
synthesis of intelligence and imagination may define the future of the Managerial
School, that the eternal laws of strategy continue to hold even as the weapons and
arenas drastically shift.
