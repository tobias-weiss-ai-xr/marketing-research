1. Introduction
Artificial intelligence has changed how people shop online not gradually, but fast enough that marketing strategies from
five years ago already look outdated. Recommendation engines now anticipate what a buyer wants before the search
query is finished. Prices shift in real time based on browsing histories. Advertisements follow users across platforms with
precision that was impossible at scale before machine learning. For e-commerce platforms competing in saturated digital
markets, AI-driven personalization has become a primary differentiator enabling tailored product recommendations,
dynamic pricing, and context-aware promotions that transform browsing sessions into purchase decisions ( Huang &
Rust, 2021; Kumar et al., 2019 ). The strategic implications extend across the marketing function: AI now shapes how
firms acquire customers, retain them, and extract long-term value from relationships ( Davenport et al., 2020 ; Puntoni
et al., 2021 ).
Indonesia represents a particularly instructive case. The country ’s e-commerce market is among the fastest-growing in
Southeast Asia, with penetration reaching over 60% of the adult population by 2024, driven by mobile commerce
adoption and the rapid rise of social commerce on platforms such as TikTok Shop ( Google-Temasek-Bain, 2023). Yet
Indonesian consumers are not a homogeneous group. They differ substantially in digital literacy, platform familiarity, and
attitudes toward algorithmic recommendation, and what works in the Jakarta market does not automatically transfer to
secondary cities. These heterogeneities make Indonesia a productive empirical context for studying the conditions under
which AI personalization is associated with purchase behavior ( Ginting et al., 2023 ; Mudjahidin et al., 2022 ).
Prior research has consistently established that perceived personalization is positively associated with purchase intention
(An & Ngo, 2025; Tam & Ho, 2006). What is less understood are the mechanisms through which this association operates
and the conditions that amplify or suppress it. Specifically, the roles of Platform Trust, defined as consumers’ confidence
in a platform’s integrity, security, and reliability ( McKnight et al., 2002), and AI Transparency, defined as the degree to
which consumers perceive an AI recommendation process as understandable, explainable, and non-manipulative (Arrieta
et al., 2020 ), have received limited attention as mediating mechanisms in the AI marketing-purchase intention
relationship. This is a meaningful gap: information processing theories suggest that consumer responses to
AI-generated cues are not uniform but are filtered through prior beliefs about the system producing those cues ( Petty
& Cacioppo, 1986 ). If platforms consistently deliver personalized recommendations but consumers cannot understand
why those recommendations appear, or do not trust the platform generating them, the conversion potential of the AI
system may be substantially reduced ( Wang et al., 2022 ; Westphal et al., 2023 ).
Despite growing interest in AI-consumer interaction, no published study has simultaneously examined Platform Trust
and AI Transparency as mediators linking AI marketing capabilities - comprising perceived personalization, perceived
usefulness, and AI responsiveness - to purchase intention within the Indonesian e-commerce context. This constitutes a
theoretical and empirical gap that the present study addresses ( Pham et al., 2024 ).
This study proposes and tests an integrated model that positions perceived personalization, perceived usefulness, and AI
responsiveness as key predictors of purchase intention, with platform trust and AI transparency serving as mediating
variables. The analysis uses PLS-SEM via SmartPLS 4 with data from 248 active e-commerce users surveyed in
Indonesia between February and March 2025 (Ringle et al., 2024). The research makes four specific contributions: (1) it
provides empirical evidence of the mediating mechanisms through which AI marketing capabilities are associated with
consumer purchase behavior; (2) it extends the theoretical integration of the Technology Acceptance Model and
Elaboration Likelihood Model into a unified framework applicable to AI-mediated commerce in emerging markets
(Sarstedt et al., 2022); (3) it situates AI marketing capability within a strategic marketing capability perspective, treating
personalization, usefulness, and responsiveness as intermediate technological resources whose market value is contin-
gent on their conversion into relational (trust) and cognitive (transparency) assets; and (4) it connects these consumer-
level findings to the firm-level digital human resource capability literature, arguing that the algorithmic and interface
conditions that produce trust and transparency in the eyes of consumers are themselves an output of internal digital talent
capacity, not a purely technical artifact. The proposed model is illustrated in Figure 1.
2. Literature review
2.1 Technology acceptance model and AI marketing
The Technology Acceptance Model ( Davis, 1989) proposes that technology adoption is primarily shaped by perceived
usefulness - the degree to which a system is believed to enhance task performance - and perceived ease of use. In AI
marketing contexts, TAM has been extended to explain why consumers engage with personalized recommendations:
they do so because such recommendations are perceived as relevant, time-efficient, and conducive to better purchase
decisions (Guo & Zhang, 2024 ; Sohn & Kwon, 2020 ). Extended TAM applications in AI service environments have
demonstrated that perceived AI capability and responsiveness significantly predict behavioral intentions, particularly

when AI functions as a decision-support agent ( Ashfaq et al., 2020 ; Longoni et al., 2019 ). The chatbot literature
further demonstrates that AI responsiveness and usability directly shape customer experience and loyalty in e-retailing
(Chen J.S. et al., 2021 ; Eren, 2021).
2.2 Perceived personalization and purchase intention
Perceived personalization refers to the extent to which consumers believe an AI system has adapted content, recom-
mendations, and interactions to their individual preferences and needs (Tam & Ho, 2006). Personalization positively and
robustly predicts consumer engagement and purchase intention across diverse digital contexts ( Riegger et al., 2022 ;
Teepapal, 2025 ). The primary mechanism is cognitive: personalized recommendations reduce information overload,
lower search costs, and signal that the platform understands the user, increasing the perceived value of the interaction and
the probability of conversion. However, personalization can also backfire when consumers perceive it as intrusive or
manipulative, particularly when privacy concerns are activated ( Chen Y. et al., 2022 ). Understanding this boundary
condition is important for platform designers seeking to balance relevance with consumer comfort.
Based on this evidence, the following hypothesis is proposed:
H1: Perceived Personalization has a significant positive association with Purchase Intention.
2.3 Perceived usefulness and purchase intention
In AI-mediated shopping, perceived usefulness captures consumers ’ beliefs that AI-driven features - recommendation
filters, dynamic search results, price alerts - meaningfully improve their purchase decisions. Consumers who perceive AI
tools as functionally valuable show higher transactional intent than those who do not, regardless of the aesthetic or social
appeal of the interface ( Davis, 1989; Gefen et al., 2003 ). This utilitarian pathway is conceptually distinct from trust or
aesthetic motivation and is expected to exert an independent direct association with purchase intention. The mobile
shopping literature further corroborates this: perceived usefulness consistently predicts continuance intention across
e-commerce and mobile platforms in emerging digital economies ( Nguyen & Ha, 2021 ).
H2: Perceived Usefulness has a significant positive association with Purchase Intention.
2.4 AI responsiveness and purchase intention
AI responsiveness refers to the capacity of an AI system to adapt in real time to user behavior, queries, and contextual
signals (Chen Q. et al., 2023; Longoni et al., 2019). Responsive AI systems provide interactions that feel attentive rather
than generic, which increases the perceived value of the AI encounter and may elevate purchase intent. Research on
AI-powered information search confirms that chatbot responsiveness on shopping platforms is associated with search
behavior and downstream purchase decisions (Pham et al., 2024). However, the direct association of responsiveness with

purchase behavior may be attenuated when consumers cannot attribute the responsiveness to a clear mechanism,

H3: AI Responsiveness has a significant positive association with Purchase Intention.
2.5 AI transparency as a mediator
AI transparency reflects the degree to which consumers can understand and evaluate how AI systems generate
recommendations ( Leichtmann et al., 2023 ). When consumers perceive AI processes as transparent and non-
manipulative, they are more likely to engage in central-route processing of the recommendation content - carefully
evaluating quality rather than reacting with reflexive skepticism ( Petty & Cacioppo, 1986 ). Conversely, algorithmic
opacity can trigger reactance, privacy concern, and reduced compliance with AI suggestions (Westphal et al., 2023). The
anthropomorphism literature supports this further: AI systems that communicate their decision logic more clearly are
associated with higher willingness to share personal data and greater behavioral compliance ( Pizzi et al., 2023 ). This
suggests that transparency may mediate the relationship between AI marketing capabilities and purchase intention.
H4: AI Transparency mediates the relationship between Perceived Personalization and Purchase Intention.
H5: AI Transparency mediates the relationship between Perceived Usefulness and Purchase Intention.
H6: AI Transparency mediates the relationship between AI Responsiveness and Purchase Intention.
2.6 Platform trust as a mediator
Platform Trust refers to consumers ’ confidence that an e-commerce platform will act in their best interests, protect their
personal data, and fulfill its commercial commitments ( Gefen et al., 2003; McKnight et al., 2002). Trust is a particularly
potent mediator in AI-mediated contexts because consumers frequently attribute the intent of AI recommendations to the
platform that hosts them: a recommendation from a platform perceived as trustworthy carries greater persuasive force
than the same recommendation from a platform perceived as exploitative ( Mayer et al., 1995; Wang et al., 2022). High-
trust environments are associated with amplified effects of personalization by reducing perceived risk and increasing
consumers’ willingness to act ( Li et al., 2021 ). Meta-analytic evidence from social commerce specifically confirms the
robust positive association between platform trust and purchase intention (Blut et al., 2021). Early AI adoption research in
the FinTech sector similarly demonstrates that consumer trust is a prerequisite for AI-mediated service acceptance
(Belanche et al., 2019). The ELM framework further supports the trust-mediation logic: consumers who trust the platform
engage in deeper, central-route evaluation of AI-generated recommendations, increasing conversion potential (Huang &
Rust, 2022; Kumar S. et al., 2023 ). The following hypotheses are therefore proposed:
H7: Platform Trust mediates the relationship between Perceived Personalization and Purchase Intention.
H8: Platform Trust mediates the relationship between Perceived Usefulness and Purchase Intention.
H9: Platform Trust mediates the relationship between AI Responsiveness and Purchase Intention.
2.7 A strategic marketing capability perspective
Beyond the individual-level TAM/ELM mechanisms above, the constructs examined here can also be read through a
firm-level strategic marketing capability lens. The resource-based view (RBV) holds that sustained competitive
advantage arises when firm resources are valuable, rare, and difficult to imitate ( Barney, 1991 ), while the dynamic
capabilities perspective extends this to firms’ capacity to reconfigure resources in response to changing market conditions
(Teece et al., 1997 ). AI-driven personalization, usefulness, and responsiveness are, in this framing, technological
marketing capabilities - firm-level investments that do not automatically confer competitive advantage on their own,
but must be converted into harder-to-imitate relational and cognitive assets (platform trust, recommendation transpar-
ency) to generate a durable market effect. Framed this way, the mediation results reported below speak not only to
individual consumer psychology but to a strategic-management question: which AI marketing investments are convert-
ible into a defensible competitive position, and which remain isolated technological features with limited standalone
commercial value.
2.8 Digital human resource capability as an enabling condition
A further layer, complementary to both the individual-level and firm-level readings above, concerns who inside the
organization actually builds and sustains the AI marketing capability being measured. The digital human resource
capability literature argues that a firm’s ability to deploy AI-based systems effectively depends on internal digital talent -
employees’ data literacy, human-AI collaboration competency, and organizational capacity to manage algorithmic
change - rather than on the acquisition of AI technology alone (Islam et al., 2023, on Indonesian SMEs’ digital transition
capacity). This has a direct bearing on the constructs studied here: AI Transparency, in particular, is not simply a property

of an algorithm but the outcome of deliberate design and communication choices made by product, data, and customer-
experience teams. Producing recommendation explanations that consumers find comprehensible, or responsiveness that
feels attentive rather than generic, presupposes an internal workforce capable of interpreting model outputs, translating
them into consumer-facing explanations, and iterating based on user feedback - competencies that sit squarely within the
digital human resource capability domain rather than the data science function alone. Similarly, Platform Trust is unlikely
to be sustained by algorithmic performance in isolation; it depends on organizational processes - data governance,
incident response, complaint handling - that are themselves executed by digitally capable human resources. Under this
reading, the consumer-level mediation results reported below are best interpreted as the visible, downstream trace of
upstream digital HR capability: platforms with stronger internal digital talent are, all else equal, better positioned to
convert AI marketing technology into the trust and transparency that this study finds to be the actual drivers of purchase
intention. This proposition is not directly tested with firm-level HR data in the present study and is offered as an
interpretive extension warranting dedicated empirical attention.
3. Materials and methods
3.1 Design, population, and sampling
This study was conducted between February and March 2025, targeting active e-commerce users in major Indonesian
cities including Jakarta, Yogyakarta, Bandung, and Surabaya. A quantitative cross-sectional survey design was
employed. The target population comprised adults aged 18 years and above who had made at least one online purchase
within the preceding three months. Purposive sampling was applied, with data collected via structured online question-
naires. Following removal of incomplete or duplicate submissions, 248 valid responses were retained for analysis.
An a priori power analysis conducted using G*Power 3.1 for multiple regression indicated that a minimum sample of
119 respondents was required to detect a medium effect size (f-squared = 0.15) with four predictors, alpha = .05, and
statistical power of.95 ( Cohen, 1988 ). The obtained sample of 248 exceeded this threshold. We note that this power
analysis was performed for multiple linear regression rather than for PLS-SEM specifically; it is reported here as a
conservative benchmark of statistical adequacy rather than as a PLS-SEM-specific sample-size justification.
Questionnaires were distributed through Google Forms, disseminated via WhatsApp networks and university commu-
nities. The sample was predominantly female (62%), consistent with prior Indonesian e-commerce studies documenting
higher online shopping engagement among women ( Islam et al., 2023 ). The full demographic profile is presented in
Table 1.

3.3 Variable measurement
All constructs were operationalized using validated multi-item scales adapted from established literature and refined
through a pilot test with 30 respondents. Items were rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree; 5 = Strongly
Agree). Perceived Personalization (4 items) was adapted from Tam and Ho (2006); Perceived Usefulness (4 items) from
Davis (1989); AI Responsiveness (3 items) from Longoni et al. (2019) ; AI Transparency (3 items) from Arrieta et al.
(2020); Platform Trust (5 items) from McKnight et al. (2002) ; and Purchase Intention (3 items) from Pavlou (2003).
To assess common method bias - a risk inherent in cross-sectional self-report data - Harman ’s single-factor test was
performed. A single factor accounted for 31.4% of the total variance, below the 50% threshold, indicating that common
method variance is unlikely to seriously distort the results ( Podsakoff et al., 2003 ). Full collinearity VIF values for all
inner model paths ranged from 1.217 to 1.882 ( Table 2), all well below the critical threshold of 3.3.
3.4 Data analysis procedure
Data were analyzed using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) via SmartPLS 4.0 ( Ringle
et al., 2024). PLS-SEM was selected for its suitability for predictive models with moderately complex structures and its
robustness under non-normal data distributions (Hair et al., 2019; Sarstedt et al., 2022). Analysis proceeded in two stages:
(1) evaluation of the measurement model for reliability and validity, and (2) assessment of the structural model for path
significance, effect sizes (f-squared), and indirect effects. Bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples was used to evaluate
statistical significance for both direct and indirect (mediated) pathways.
4. Results
Prior to the assessment of the measurement model, common method bias was assessed using the full collinearity VIF
approach. The VIF values ranged from 1.217 to 1.882, all well below the conservative threshold of 3.3 ( Table 2 ). In
addition, Harman ’s single-factor test was conducted, with a single factor accounting for 31.4% of the total variance,
below the 50% threshold ( Podsakoff et al., 2003 ). Together these diagnostics indicate that common method variance is
unlikely to pose a material threat to the findings, though - consistent with Podsakoff et al. (2003) - neither test rules out
common method bias with certainty in the absence of a marker-variable design.

4.3 Structural model: Path coefficients and effect sizes

5. Discussion
This study examined the association between AI-driven personalized marketing and consumer purchase intention among
Indonesian e-commerce users, with Platform Trust and AI Transparency as parallel mediating mechanisms. The model
accounted for 60.4% of the variance in Purchase Intention, 46.2% in Platform Trust, and 33.2% in AI Transparency.
These figures suggest that purchase intention in AI-mediated commerce is better explained by an integrated framework
that accounts for how personalization quality, system usefulness, and AI responsiveness relate to consumer trust and
comprehension, than by any single predictor examined on its own. Because the design is cross-sectional, these results
describe statistical association and structural pattern, not confirmed causal sequence; the interpretation below should be
read with that qualification throughout.

Perceived Personalization showed the strongest association in the model - the largest single path coefficient, from
Perceived Personalization to Platform Trust, together with a significant direct association with Purchase Intention. One
plausible reading, consistent with Mayer et al. (1995) ‘s framework of trust antecedents, is that when consumers
repeatedly receive recommendations that feel relevant, they may be forming a broader evaluative judgment of the
platform as an entity that understands and serves their needs - a judgment that overlaps conceptually with the ability and
benevolence dimensions of trust.
The indirect association of Perceived Personalization through Platform Trust with Purchase Intention was the largest
mediated path in the model, exceeding the corresponding transparency-mediated path. This pattern suggests that, in this
sample, the association between personalization and purchase intention runs more strongly through the trust channel than
through consumers’ comprehension of how the recommendation was generated - a pattern consistent with Riegger et al.
(2022) in smart retail environments and Teepapal (2025) in AI-driven social media marketing.
Perceived Usefulness showed a significant direct association with Purchase Intention and contributed through both
mediators. This utilitarian pathway is conceptually distinct from the trust pathway: consumers who find AI-driven
features functionally valuable - more relevant search results, better product filters, time savings - may be exercising a
different evaluative process from those responding primarily to relational trust signals.
AI Responsiveness showed a different pattern from the other two predictors. Its direct association with Purchase Intention
was the smallest among the three structural predictors, while its indirect associations through Platform Trust and AI
Transparency were of roughly equal magnitude. One interpretation, consistent with Chen et al. (2023) ‘s finding for AI
chatbot service quality in e-retailing, is that consumers may not treat AI responsiveness as a direct conversion signal but
instead read it as evidence of system attentiveness that co-varies with trust and transparency perceptions.

relationships. The ELM framework ( Petty & Cacioppo, 1986 ) offers a plausible processing account: consumers who

5.1 A strategic marketing capability reading of the mediation structure
The pattern of results also speaks to a strategic-management question that sits alongside the individual-level psycho-
logical account above: which AI marketing investments convert into a defensible competitive position, and which remain
isolated technological features with limited standalone commercial value? Read through the resource-based view
(Barney, 1991 ) and the dynamic capabilities perspective ( Teece et al., 1997 ), Perceived Personalization, Perceived
Usefulness, and AI Responsiveness function as technological marketing capabilities - firm-level investments that are, on
their own, comparatively easy for competitors to replicate. What the mediation results indicate is that the competitively
defensible layer sits one step downstream: in the relational asset of Platform Trust and the cognitive asset of AI
Transparency, both of which are cumulative, path-dependent, and therefore harder to imitate quickly. Practically, this
reframes AI investment decisions for Indonesian e-commerce firms as a strategic capability-building problem rather than
a features problem.
5.2 A digital human resource capability reading
A further, complementary layer concerns how the relational and cognitive assets identified above (Platform Trust, AI
Transparency) actually get built inside an organization. If, as argued in Section 2.8, AI Transparency is substantially a
product-and-communication design outcome rather than a purely algorithmic one, then the strength of the Perceived
Personalization/Usefulness/Responsiveness to AI Transparency paths observed in Table 9 (beta = 0.263, 0.156, and
0.331 respectively) may in part reflect the digital HR capability of the teams responsible for translating model outputs into
consumer-facing explanations. Likewise, the central role of Platform Trust as the dominant mediator is consistent with
trust being sustained through organizational processes - data governance, complaint handling, incident response - that
depend on digitally literate human resources rather than on algorithmic performance alone. This reading implies that
closing the “personalization-to-trust” and “responsiveness-to-transparency” gaps documented in this study is not purely
an engineering problem: it is also a digital talent-management problem, encompassing recruitment, upskilling, and
retention of employees capable of operating at the interface between AI systems and consumer-facing communication. As
with the strategic marketing capability reading in Section 5.1, this is an interpretive extension consistent with the
observed consumer-level mediation pattern rather than a directly tested firm-level HR proposition; the present dataset
contains no firm-level digital HR capability measures, and this interpretation should be treated as a hypothesis for future
mixed-level (employee-consumer) research rather than a confirmed mechanism.
5.3 Implications for platform design
Four tentative implications follow, offered as design hypotheses rather than confirmed causal prescriptions given the
cross-sectional design. First, personalization quality might be usefully evaluated not only by immediate click-through
metrics but by its association with platform trust over time. Second, AI Transparency is currently treated by most
Indonesian e-commerce platforms primarily as a legal compliance matter; the present pattern of results is consistent with
transparency also carrying commercial relevance. Third, investment in AI responsiveness may be more productively
evaluated as a trust- and transparency-building input than as a direct sales mechanism. Fourth, and following from
Section 5.2, firms may need to pair AI technology investment with parallel investment in digital human resource
capability - training product, data, and customer-experience teams to translate algorithmic outputs into comprehensible,
trustworthy consumer-facing experiences - since the present results suggest the technology alone does not produce the
trust and transparency effects that drive purchase intention.
5.4 Limitations
Several limitations apply. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference; the associational language used
throughout this Discussion reflects that constraint. Second, the sample was concentrated in major Indonesian urban
centers and recruited through convenience/purposive networks, which limits generalizability. Third, the study relied on
self-reported data, which introduces the possibility of response bias. Fourth, AI Transparency was measured as a
subjective perception rather than an objective feature of platform design. Fifth, the a priori power analysis was conducted
for multiple linear regression rather than for PLS-SEM specifically. Sixth, Perceived Usefulness showed the lowest AVE
(0.578) and lowest single-item loading (PU1 = 0.719) among all constructs. Seventh, both the strategic marketing
capability reading (5.1) and the digital human resource capability reading (5.2) are interpretive extensions drawn from
firm-level and HR-level theory applied to individual-level consumer data; neither has been tested with firm-level
competitive outcomes or firm-level digital HR capability measures, and both should be treated as directions for future
research rather than findings of this study.

6. Conclusion
This study examined how AI-driven personalized marketing relates to consumer purchase intention among Indonesian
e-commerce users, with Platform Trust and AI Transparency as mediating mechanisms. Perceived Personalization,
Perceived Usefulness, and AI Responsiveness were all significantly associated with Purchase Intention, with Perceived
Personalization showing the strongest association, observed particularly through its path to Platform Trust. Platform
Trust and AI Transparency both mediated the relationships between the three predictors and Purchase Intention, with all
six indirect paths reaching significance. Viewed jointly through TAM/ELM, a strategic marketing capability lens, and a
digital human resource capability lens, these results suggest that AI marketing technology alone is an imitable resource:
the trust and transparency it generates are the more defensible strategic assets, and both appear contingent on the digital
talent capacity that configures and sustains them. Future studies should employ longitudinal designs using objective
behavioral data from platform logs, extend the model to cross-cultural comparisons across ASEAN markets, use a PLS-
SEM-specific power analysis, test firm-level competitive outcomes and digital HR capability measures associated with
the strategic and HR readings proposed here, and examine whether AI transparency effects vary with consumer digital
literacy levels.
