Abstract
Artificial intelligence (AI) is transforming word of mouth (WOM) and influencer marketing. This development gives
rise to synthetic WOM, a form of WOM in which AI participates in creating, mediating, evaluating, and/or acting on the
consumption-related communication. Despite its growing prevalence, existing research has not fully conceptualized syn -
thetic WOM or explained how consumers perceive varying degrees of machine involvement in WOM content. This article
addresses these gaps by developing a conceptual framework that integrates AI into the core WOM elements of source,
message, channel, and receiver. This article also introduces the notion of degree of syntheticity, defined as consumers’
perceptions of the extent to which WOM reflects machine-driven rather than human-driven processes, and conceptualizes
it as a multidimensional construct encompassing narrative, voice, appearance, and consumption syntheticity. Building on
this framework, this article outlines key directions for future research, including the role of AI agents as communicators
and receivers, AI-to-AI WOM environments, and the potential dark and regulatory implications of synthetic WOM. This
work advances understanding of how AI reshapes WOM and offers a foundation for future theory and research.

Introduction
Influencer marketing has evolved from a practice primar -
ily centered on reach, visibility, and follower counts into a
complex system in which creator identity, audience expecta-
tions, platform characteristics, and brand objectives jointly
determine marketing outcomes. The high interest in this
special issue, with over 80 submissions, reflects this grow -
ing interest. The nine articles in this special issue reflect this
evolution. Rather than asking simply whether influencer
marketing works, they investigate how influencer com -
munications should be designed, how influencers should
manage their public identities, and how brands can align
the sometimes-competing interests of creators, consumers,
and commercial partners. Taken together, the articles can
be organized into three broad research domains: design -
ing dynamic content and campaign execution, managing
influencer voice, identity, and audience norms, and aligning
influencer, brand, and consumer interests.

Designing dynamic content and campaign
execution
The first research domain concerns the architecture and
evolution of influencer content. A central insight across
these studies is that effectiveness depends not only on what
appears in a message but also on how its elements change
over time and across campaign executions. This represents
an important movement away from treating influencer posts
as static collections of textual or visual attributes.
Research on emotionality flips in long-form creator con-
tent shows that changes in emotional direction can increase
audience engagement by heightening arousal. The effective-
ness of these transitions, however, depends on characteristics
of both the content and the audience, including processing
ease and consumers’ need for affect. Related research on
livestreaming reveals a more complicated pattern. Frequent
emotional-valence transitions may initially stimulate viewer
engagement yet simultaneously undermine product sales.
The same expressive dynamics that hold viewers’ attention
may therefore interfere with their willingness or ability to
act on a sales message.
The visual-consistency research extends this dynamic
perspective from individual communications to entire cam-
paigns. Visual consistency generally benefits conventional
posts, whereas stories require a more feature-specific com -
bination of consistency and variation. These patterns also
differ according to influencer size, campaign format, and
whether the offering is a product or a service. Collectively,
the studies caution against universal prescriptions such as
“use more emotion,” “create more variation,” or “maintain
complete visual consistency.” Effective execution requires
managing the temporal and visual rhythm of communica -
tion to reflect the platform format, campaign context, and
desired outcome. Most importantly, the findings demon -
strate that attention and commercial performance are not
interchangeable: creative practices that generate engage -
ment may not necessarily generate sales.
Managing influencer voice, identity, and
audience norms
A second research domain examines how audiences interpret
the influencer as a social actor. Influencers do not communi-
cate in a vacuum; their messages are evaluated in relation to
category conventions, their established communication his-
tories, and the values and expectations of their audiences.
Consequently, seemingly authentic self-expression can be
beneficial in some contexts but costly in others.
The studies of profanity and political chatter illus -
trate these normative boundaries. Profanity is generally
associated with lower engagement, particularly in catego -
ries where such language is not expected. Its consequences
also depend on the influencer’s gender, the sponsored nature
of the communication, the politeness of the surrounding
content, and the influencer’s previous use of profanity. A
history of similar communication can sometimes reduce the
penalty by making the behavior more consistent with the
influencer’s established persona, although repeated profan -
ity cannot fully overcome strong category norms.
Political chatter by influencers known primarily for non-
political content creates a comparable challenge. Among
gaming influencers, greater political content generally
reduces engagement. Yet the effect is contingent: humor,
certain emotional expressions, and prior audience expo -
sure can mitigate the negative response, whereas moral
incongruence between influencer and audience intensifies
it. Thus, audiences appear to evaluate not only the topic
itself but also whether it is delivered in a familiar manner
and whether it is consistent with their relationship to the
influencer.
The study of apparent influencer age broadens identity
management from verbal behavior to visual self-presen -
tation. It challenges the assumption that younger-looking
influencers are invariably more effective. Instead, moderate
apparent ages produce the strongest outcomes because age
simultaneously increases perceived credibility while reduc-
ing attractiveness. The resulting inverted-U relationship
is further shaped by artificial intelligence (AI) disclosure,
gender, audience age, and perceived similarity. Across these
studies, authenticity emerges not as unrestricted personal
expression but as identity coherence: the influencer’s com -
munication and appearance must be credible relative to the
influencer’s history, audience, and category context.
Aligning influencer, brand, and consumer
interests
The third research domain treats influencer marketing as a
relationship among multiple actors whose objectives are not
always aligned. Influencers seek engagement and audience
growth, brands seek commercial and brand-related out -
comes, and consumers evaluate whether recommendations
serve their interests or those of the sponsor.
The product-salience study makes this tension espe -
cially visible. Posts in which the endorsed product is less
salient generate greater engagement with the influencer’s
post, whereas greater product salience generates stronger
engagement with subsequent brand-owned content. The
creative execution that benefits the influencer may therefore
differ from the execution that benefits the brand. Campaign
effectiveness cannot be assessed without specifying whose

The livestream fit research similarly demonstrates that
“fit” is multidimensional. Similarity between an influencer’s
followers and a brand’s target consumers is a particularly
strong driver of immediate sales. However, high image-
based fit between the influencer and brand can weaken this
effect by encouraging audiences to see the influencer as
serving the brand rather than serving followers. This back -
fire effect becomes especially important as influencer–brand
partnerships become established over time. More apparent
fit, therefore, is not always better.
Finally, deinfluencing shows how discouraging con -
sumption can paradoxically support later marketing effec -
tiveness. By reducing skepticism about an influencer’s
motives, deinfluencing content can strengthen trustworthi -
ness and increase purchase intentions for products subse -
quently endorsed by that influencer. At the same time, it
constrains future partnerships: recommendations become
less effective when an influencer later collaborates with a
brand whose products were previously discouraged. Trust-
building communication thus creates both relational value
and obligations for future consistency.
Across all three domains, the special issue presents
influencer marketing as a dynamic, context-sensitive, and
multi-objective activity. Engagement, trust, brand engage -
ment, purchase intentions, and immediate sales are distinct
outcomes whose antecedents may differ or even conflict.
The articles identify actionable levers such as emotional
dynamics, visual consistency, language, product salience,
source appearance, deinfluencing, and different forms of fit.
But they are not simple one-size-fits-all recommendations.
Their collective contribution is to show that successful
influencer marketing requires joint management of content
execution, creator identity, audience expectations, and
stakeholder alignment. In doing so, the special issue moves
the field beyond the generation of buzz toward a more pre -
cise understanding of when attention becomes trust, when
trust becomes action, and when the pursuit of engagement
may obstruct rather than advance business outcomes.
Synthetic word of mouth: How AI shapes
the future of word of mouth and influencer
marketing
AI is reshaping the landscape of word-of-mouth (WOM),
and AI-based influence presents a unique opportunity to
study WOM production, source perceptions, deception, and
market outcomes within a unified framework for synthetic
WOM. AI systems can generate, mediate, evaluate, and
even act on WOM content, creating what we refer to as syn-
thetic WOM (see the examples in Table 1). Recent research
suggests that autonomous AI agents can collect data from
the internet (including reviews and other consumption-
related content) and use this data to make decisions or exe -
cute transactions on behalf of humans, sometimes without
direct human authorization (Shahidi et al., 2025). At the
same time, the emergence of AI-exclusive social media
(e.g., Moltbook; Reddit-like, AI-to-AI agents social media)
offers a glimpse into a future in which AI agents exchange
consumption-related information among themselves (e.g.,
discussing which cybersecurity tools their human users
employ and how those tools affect performance) (Li, 2026;
Schmelzer, 2026). In a more problematic direction, there
are documented cases of deepfake videos in which irrespon-
sible actors appropriate others’ appearances and likenesses
to portray them as using or endorsing particular products

These developments demonstrate that synthetic WOM is no

Despite the growing practical relevance of AI in the con-
text of WOM, existing studies offer important yet partial
insights. The most established research stream concerns
reviews that are deliberately fabricated, many of which are
performed by bots. He et al. (2022) examine sellers that pur-
chase fake Amazon reviews through private markets. These
reviews temporarily increase average ratings, review vol -
ume, and sales, but subsequent organic reviews often reveal
lower product quality. Related research shows that artifi -
cial communication extends beyond conventional product
reviews. Liu and Cui ( 2026) examine paid trolls who use
fake social media accounts to influence public opinion.
Although their settings involve legal disputes rather than
consumer products, the underlying mechanism is similar:
strategically generated communication is presented as if it
were authentic, independent opinion.
Research also identifies intermediate forms between
organic and entirely fake WOM. Chen et al. ( 2021) ana -
lyze rebates conditional on posting positive reviews. Such
incentives can produce authentic but artificially inflated
reviews or partially fabricated communication. Similarly,
Qiao and Rui (2022) show that incentivized reviews may be
less impartial than organic reviews while also being more
coherent and detailed. These studies suggest that authentic-
ity is not binary. WOM can reflect a genuine consumption
experience while still being shaped by financial incentives,
external assistance, or strategic intervention. The most
direct evidence that AI is changing consumer WOM appears
in Gu and Spann’s ( 2026) quasi-experimental study of the
temporary prohibition of ChatGPT in Italy. They show that
access to ChatGPT increases review volume and length and
changes the subjectivity of review content. Their findings
indicate that AI lowers the cost of articulating and commu -
nicating consumption experiences and thereby changes both
the quantity and nature of WOM.
A second major research stream examines how consum -
ers respond when recommendations are explicitly attributed
to machines. Longoni and Cian ( 2022) document a “word-
of-machine” effect: consumers are relatively receptive to AI
recommendations in utilitarian contexts but prefer human
recommendations for hedonic decisions. This pattern
reflects the belief that machines are competent at evaluating
objective attributes but less capable of understanding sub -
jective experiences and tastes.
Synthetic WOM need not be produced entirely by AI. It
can also emerge when AI materially shapes how consum -
ers express their own opinions. Tsekouras et al. ( 2024)
show that the anthropomorphism of chatbots used to solicit
reviews affects review valence and helpfulness. The con -
sumer remains the nominal source, but the technological
interface alters how the consumption experience is com -
municated. This represents AI-mediated WOM, positioned
between organic communication and fully AI-generated
content.
Grewal et al. ( 2025) similarly conceptualize generative
AI applications along a continuum ranging from human
augmentation to automated content creation. Applied
to WOM, this continuum includes lightly edited human
reviews, jointly produced human–AI reviews, and fully
automated communication. Pauwels and Aksehirli ( 2026)
further extend WOM theory to AI, virtual influencers, and
technologically generated communication, emphasizing
that the conventional distinction between organic WOM
and firm-generated communication is becoming increas -
ingly difficult to maintain.
AI can also participate in review environments as an
identifiable organizational actor. Wang and Ge ( 2026)
develop an LLM fine-tuning method for producing manage-
rial responses to online reviews that better reflect domain-
specific human preferences. Although these responses are
not consumer WOM, the study demonstrates that market -
place conversations may increasingly involve interactions
among human consumers, AI-assisted consumers, firms
using generative systems, autonomous agents, and plat -
forms that summarize or rank their communications.
A central challenge in synthetic WOM is whether con -
sumers can recognize artificial communication. Yin et al.
(2025) find that consumers exhibit a pronounced truth bias
when evaluating online reviews. They are generally inclined
to classify reviews as genuine and are more successful at
recognizing authentic reviews than fabricated ones. This
tendency creates an important vulnerability because increas-
ingly fluent AI-generated reviews may be especially likely
to be accepted as authentic.
Synthetic WOM also creates a governance problem
because artificial reviews may affect rankings and sales
before platforms identify and remove them. Gu et al. (2025)
examine manipulated App Store reviews that were sub -
sequently filtered by the platform. Manipulated reviews
initially distort product rankings, and their effects per -
sist because corrections are delayed. Eventual detection
therefore does not necessarily eliminate their marketplace
consequences. This finding highlights an important asym -
metry: synthetic WOM can influence demand immediately,
while detection and correction occur only later. Platform
policy must consequently address not only whether content
should be removed but also how quickly it should be iden -
tified, whether it should be labeled, how rankings should

Mladenović et al. ( 2024) have provided an initial con -
ceptualization of synthetic WOM in the travel and hospi -
tality context, with two main limitations. First, it primarily
focuses on AI’s role in creating and disseminating WOM,
overlooking the expanding capacity of AI systems to evalu-
ate WOM and even act upon it autonomously. Second, it
does not explicate the degree of syntheticity in WOM con -
tent. In practice, some synthetic WOM may appear obvi -
ously machine-generated, whereas other forms may closely
resemble human communication, which may shape how
consumers interpret and evaluate the content.
Driven by these theoretical gaps, this research aims to
conceptualize synthetic WOM more comprehensively and
articulate the degree of syntheticity in its content. We pro -
ceed as follows. We first provide a brief history of WOM
across traditional WOM, electronic WOM, and influencer
marketing, as understanding how WOM has evolved is
necessary to situate synthetic WOM within the broader
development of consumption-related communication. We
then outline how AI is likely to shape the future of WOM
and influencer marketing by altering the source, message,
channel, and receiver elements that have long structured the
WOM theory. Building on this foundation, we conceptualize
synthetic WOM and define its core characteristics. Next, we
introduce the notion of degree of syntheticity in synthetic
WOM and explain how consumers may perceive varying
levels of machine-driven involvement in WOM content. We
then outline future research directions that emerge from our
conceptualization.
A brief history of WOM: Traditional WOM,
electronic WOM, and influencer marketing
The academic study of WOM begins with a premise that
seems deceptively simple: informal, interpersonal commu -
nication about products can meaningfully shape awareness,
evaluation, and adoption. Early work treated WOM as a
largely localized phenomenon, embedded in social struc -
ture, constrained by geography, and difficult to observe at
scale. Classic marketing research examined product-related
conversations as a mechanism of diffusion and emphasized
how interpersonal contact could accelerate new product
uptake (Arndt, 1967). Subsequent network-based evidence
sharpened this insight: the influence of WOM depends
not only on what is said, but also on who is connected to
whom, with tie strength and network structure shaping
referral behavior and persuasive impact (Aral & Walker,
2014; Brown & Reingen, 1987). Thus, the initial interest in
the effect of WOM on the growth of an innovation (Bass,
1969; Mahajan et al., 1990) has evolved into an interest in
the effect of network structure on innovation performance
(Muller & Peres, 2019).
This “person-to-person” era of WOM faced two defining
measurement problems. First, WOM was typically invisible
to the researcher and to the firm, unless captured through
surveys, diaries, or small-network observation. Second,
even when observed, it was difficult to disentangle “talk”
from correlated unobservables such as homophily or shared
exposure. These constraints encouraged theory emphasiz -
ing micro-social mechanisms, such as social ties, credibility,
and interpersonal influence, while limiting direct manage -
rial control and real-time analytics, concerns that continue
to motivate modern causal identification strategies in digital
settings (Eckles et al., 2017).
The rise of the commercial internet initiated a structural
break. WOM increasingly migrated into digitally medi -
ated environments (e.g., forums, bulletin boards, email
lists, and later consumer review platforms), giving rise to
what became known as electronic WOM. Two changes
were particularly consequential. First, the communication
channel shifted from private or semi-private conversation
to public or semi-public exchange. Second, WOM became
recorded, enabling retrieval, search, and aggregation. Del -
larocas (2003) characterized this transformation as the “dig-
itization of word of mouth,” highlighting both its promise
(scale, traceability, and novel reputation mechanisms) and
its challenges (manipulation, selection biases, and platform
governance), challenges that have intensified with algorith-
mic ranking and platform monetization (Beck et al., 2024).
Early empirical work on online discussions demonstrated
that electronic WOM could rival, and sometimes outweigh,
marketer-generated information in shaping consumer inter-
est and attitudes. Controlled evidence comparing exposure
to online forums versus corporate webpages showed that
forum-based discussions could be particularly influential,
suggesting that the perceived independence of peers (even
strangers) enhances persuasive impact in digital contexts
(Bickart & Schindler, 2001). Around the same time, the
expansion of consumer-opinion platforms and review sys -
tems broadened the scope of electronic WOM. In a semi -
nal contribution, Hennig-Thurau et al. ( 2004) formalized
motivations for posting on such platforms (helping others,
venting negative feelings, social benefits, and economic
incentives), thereby reframing electronic WOM not merely
as traditional WOM conducted online, but as a communica-
tion practice shaped by platform affordances and contributor
goals, a perspective extended in more recent work on influ-
encer incentives and disclosure (De Veirman et al., 2019).
Once conversations moved online, researchers could
observe WOM behavior directly rather than relying exclu -
sively on retrospective self-reports. A key methodological

In parallel, work on viral marketing and multi-stage influ -

and retransmission on social platforms (Goel et al., 2016).
The next historical shift, often associated with Web
2.0 and social media, altered not just the volume of elec -
tronic WOM but its underlying architecture. Social media
has accelerated WOM, made it more multimedia, more
identity-linked, and more algorithmically curated. Kaplan
and Haenlein (2010) defined social media as internet-based
applications built on Web 2.0 foundations that enable the
creation and exchange of user-generated content, capturing
how communication became participatory and networked
by default. From a managerial perspective, Mangold and
Faulds (2009) argued that social media constitute a “hybrid”
element of the promotion mix: firms communicate with cus-
tomers, but customers also communicate with each other in
ways that are difficult for managers to control. Historically,
this hybridization reframes WOM from a largely exogenous
social process into a partially designable system, shaped
indirectly through platform choice, seeding strategies, con -
tent design, and community participation, an idea reinforced
by recent work on platform governance and algorithmic
amplification (Lamberton & Stephen, 2016; Schweidel &
Moe, 2014).
As electronic WOM matured, the literature increasingly
differentiated among types of electronic WOM and their
effects. Social earned media (e.g., blogs and online commu-
nities) and traditional earned media (e.g., press coverage)
influence one another and sales, but with distinct dynamics
in frequency and marginal impact (Stephen & Galak, 2012).
Large-scale field settings further enabled direct comparisons
between WOM and traditional marketing in driving growth,
underscoring that electronic WOM can be both measurable
and managerially consequential in digital environments
where sharing, referrals, and invitation traces are recorded
(Babić Rosario et al., 2016). By the 2010 s, the field con -
solidated around integrative frameworks and systematic
reviews that conceptualized electronic WOM as a broad
domain encompassing product reviews, social network
conversations, and platform-mediated reputational signals
(Cheung & Thadani, 2012; King et al., 2014), with newer
reviews emphasizing algorithmic mediation and strategic
behavior (Babić Rosario et al., 2020; Kozinets et al., 2017).
Conceptual syntheses emphasized that WOM remains fun -
damentally interpersonal and goal-driven, serving functions
such as impression management, social bonding, and per -
suasion, even as the medium changes (Berger, 2014).
With respect to influencers, although some have reached
celebrity status, they are often perceived as ordinary con -
sumers who share everyday experiences (Leung et al.,
2022a, 2022b), which contributes to their credibility and the
trust followers place in their opinions. Marketing research
has examined how influencer characteristics such as fol -
lower size (Beichert et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024; Tian et
al., 2024), attractiveness (Barari et al., 2025), social identity
(Pan et al., 2025), indulgence behavior (Gamlin & Touré-
Tillery, 2025), and potential growth (Lanz et al., 2024) shape
engagement and influencer marketing outcomes. Other stud-
ies have focused on managing influencers to maximize firm
benefits (Haenlein et al., 2020; Leung et al., 2022a, 2022b;
Libai et al., 2025). Combined, this work reflects a growing
professionalization of WOM, as firms strategically manage
relationships, content formats, and performance metrics
within platform environments. Influencer marketing shifts
WOM from an exogenous market force to an endogenous
communication channel, strategically managed by brands,
influencers, and platforms (Libai et al., 2025).
In recent years, AI has become a significant force in the
WOM landscape, leading to the emergence of synthetic
WOM, as we have shown through examples in Table 1.
Synthetic WOM can circulate through configurations in
which human and non-human contributions intertwine, and
in which influence may arise before or beyond direct human
engagement. Rather than displacing earlier forms, this AI-
enabled WOM coexists with traditional WOM, electronic
WOM, and influencer marketing, while altering how influ -
ence is produced and received. These developments war -
rant a closer examination of how AI reshapes established
assumptions about WOM and influencer marketing.
Impact of AI on word of mouth elements
The emergence of AI challenges many long-standing
assumptions that underpin traditional WOM and influencer
marketing theories. Traditional frameworks treat the source,
message, channel, and receiver of WOM as human-centered
elements that shape how consumption-related communica -
tion forms and influences behavior (Babić Rosario et al.,
2020). AI reshapes these foundations by engaging with
each element in ways that differ from human involvement.
The source may no longer reflect a human talking about an
actual consumption experience; the message may not origi-
nate from human interpretation; the channel may transform
existing content or produce new content, rather than simply

interpret WOM before humans encounter it. These changes
expand the conceptual boundaries of WOM and necessitate
a reconsideration of how influence arises when human and
AI contributions interact. In the following, we describe these
changes in detail and explain the theoretical implications
that follow from these shifts (see Table 2 for a summary).
Source
In traditional WOM, the source refers to the consumer who
initiates the communication. This communication usually
stems from the consumer’s own consumption of market -
place offerings. Research on traditional WOM, electronic
WOM, and influencer marketing has assumed that humans
are the source (see e.g., Beichert et al., 2024; Maiberger et
al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2014). They consume the offering,
form an impression, and produce spoken, written, or visual
WOM content that reflects their personal experience.
AI introduces a different configuration. The source can
be a human, an AI system, or a hybrid between the two.
Humans may still consume the offering but rely on AI to
draft, revise, or structure the content. AI systems may gen -
erate WOM content without having experienced the offer -
ing and without human prompting, and virtual influencers
may appear in WOM communications even though they are
not human agents. Unlike traditional advertising, in which
promotional intent is usually explicit, AI-enabled WOM
appears to be peer-to-peer communication even when no
human is explicitly involved. This source of ambiguity chal-
lenges the long-standing assumption that WOM originates
from individuals with lived experiences, identifiable inten -
tions, and stable identities. It also influences how the field
should think about source characteristics, since consump -
tion-related communication may no longer reflect personal
experience, may reflect mixed authorship, and may feature
agents whose relationship to the offering differs from that of
human consumers.
Message
Message characteristics differ substantially across different
WOM types. Traditional WOM typically refers to spon -
taneous, context-bound expressions that convey personal
interpretations of marketplace offerings (Brooks, 1957).
In electronic WOM, messages appear as digital content,
such as detailed narratives, evaluative statements, or visual
depictions of consumption that document the consumer’s
experience (Babić Rosario et al., 2016). In influencer mar -
keting, the message is more curated and stylistically com -
posed, reflecting the influencers’ consumption experiences
with the marketplace offerings (Leung et al., 2022a, 2022b).
AI extends the message aspect of WOM by enabling
it to take forms that do not rely on human experience or
natural expression. A message may appear as an image of
a virtual agent using a product, a synthetic visual represen -
tation of consumption, or a compressed evaluative output
that reflects machine-generated interpretations rather than
personal accounts. These developments extend WOM
theory by broadening the definition of a WOM message
and by introducing content that conveys meaning without
being grounded in lived consumption. The emergence of

AI, therefore, challenges traditional assumptions that WOM

Channel
As messages change across WOM forms, so do the chan -
nels that carry them. Traditional WOM depends on inter -
personal interaction in physical or social settings, where
communication unfolds within shared environments (Engel
et al., 1969). Electronic WOM shifts the channel to digital
platforms that host consumption-related content and influ -
ence its visibility through functions such as ranking, filter -
ing, and highlighting specific contributions (Marchand et
al., 2017). Influencer marketing relies on the same digital
infrastructures, whether text-based or visual, but uses them
to distribute content created for their followers and tailored
for engagement (Haenlein et al., 2020).
AI introduces a more substantial shift in the channel
function. AI-driven systems do not merely display or sort
WOM content but actively reshape what appears to con -
sumers. A channel may condense thousands of reviews
into a short AI-generated summary or produce an image of
a virtual figure interacting with a product, even when no
original post contained such depictions. In these cases, the
channel serves as a medium through which WOM is inter -
preted, reformulated, or newly created rather than simply
conveyed. This shift requires WOM theories to treat chan -
nels as actors that influence the content and structure of
WOM, since the communication reaching consumers may
reflect algorithmic interpretation rather than the unaltered
contributions of other consumers.
Receiver
The receiver of WOM also evolves across its different
forms. In traditional WOM, the receiver is the individual
who listens to or observes another person’s consumption-
related communication during interpersonal interaction
(Money et al., 1998). Electronic WOM broadens the audi -
ence to include anyone (including consumers and brands)
who encounters consumer-related content online (Babić
Rosario et al., 2020). In influencer marketing, the receiv -
ers are the audiences who follow influencers (Libai et al.,
2025). Across these forms, the receiver remains a human
who interprets the message and links it to personal prefer -
ences and goals.
AI alters this structure by receiving and interpreting
WOM messages before humans do. Consumers increasingly
rely on AI systems to manage large volumes of information
on their behalf. AI systems can read, filter, classify, and sum-
marize WOM content to provide clearer, more manageable
inputs for consumer decision-making. In this arrangement,
AI serves as an initial receiver, processing information and
presenting humans with a simplified representation rather
than the full set of available WOM content. Beyond this,
AI systems, in the form of AI agents, may make decisions
on behalf of humans based on the WOM they have gath -
ered and interpreted (e.g., booking a hotel based on reviews
without consulting humans). This shift extends WOM theo-
ries by expanding the range of agents who can function as
WOM receivers. In synthetic WOM, the receiver may be a
human who encounters AI-mediated WOM representations;
an AI system that first receives and interprets WOM before
passing its output to a human; or an AI system that receives
WOM and makes consumption-related decisions on behalf
of humans. Influence, therefore, does not depend solely on
direct human reception of communication, but can emerge
through configurations in which WOM is interpreted or
acted upon by non-human receivers before or instead of
human involvement.
More broadly, AI-mediated WOM challenges a founda -
tional assumption in existing WOM theory that influence
requires direct reception and interpretation by a human con-
sumer. Prior research (e.g., Herr et al., 1991) has largely
conceptualized WOM as an interpersonal process in which
message effects arise through human attention, inference,
and persuasion. Yet when AI systems receive, filter, summa-
rize, and evaluate WOM before human involvement, influ -
ence may emerge without direct exposure to the underlying
communication. Moreover, when AI agents act on WOM-
derived inputs on behalf of consumers, behavioral out -
comes may result without human deliberation at the point of
choice. These developments suggest that WOM influence in
synthetic environments is shaped not only by who says what
to whom, but also by the intermediary systems that deter -
mine which communications become salient, how they are
transformed, and whether they culminate in action. Accord-
ingly, the receiver in WOM theory may need to be recon -
ceptualized from an individual human actor to a broader
socio-technical decision unit that includes both human and
algorithmic elements. Delegated action means that human
behavior may change even without a conscious change in
attitude. Thus, influence can occur without persuasion.
Conceptualizing synthetic WOM
The previous discussion shows how AI fundamentally
affects every aspect of WOM and highlights the need
to conceptualize synthetic WOM as a distinct form of
WOM, alongside traditional WOM, electronic WOM, and

influencer marketing. In doing so, we follow the approach
of Babić Rosario et al. (2020), who define electronic WOM
as organized around four core components: source, mes -
sage, channel, and receiver.
At the same time, AI-enabled WOM cannot be under -
stood solely by revisiting these components, as it is closely
tied to AI involvement and the resulting synthetic nature
of the communication. Kaplan and Haenlein ( 2019, p. 17)
define AI as “ a system’ s ability to interpret external data
correctly, to learn from such data, and to use those learnings
to achieve specific goals and tasks through flexible adapta -
tion.” Applied to WOM, this implies a form of participa -
tion that goes well beyond the use of digital tools. Because
AI systems can interpret external data, learn from it, and
adapt their actions toward specific goals, they can engage
with WOM in ways that resemble mechanical, thinking, and
feeling tasks traditionally performed by humans (see also
Huang & Rust, 2021). AI can interpret consumption-related
communication, learn from patterns across large volumes
of WOM, and adapt its outputs to achieve goals. Through
these capabilities, AI can influence who or what serves as a
source of WOM, shape the form and structure of the mes -
sage, intervene through channels that reorganize or generate
content, and act as a receiver that processes WOM.
We label this new form of AI-enabled WOM as synthetic
WOM, where the term synthetic clarifies the nature of WOM
that emerges from AI involvement. Merriam-Webster
defines synthetic as “ devised, arranged, or fabricated for
special situations to imitate or replace usual realities .” In
synthetic WOM, this definition highlights that WOM may
resemble conventional consumption-related communication
while no longer relying on the usual realities that underpin
WOM, such as human consumption, human expression, and
direct interpersonal interpretation. AI enables WOM com -
munication that can imitate, substitute for, or reorganize the
roles of the source, message, channel, and receiver, even
when these roles are no longer grounded in direct human
experience. Based on the concepts above and prior concep-
tualization of electronic WOM, we propose the following
definition:
Synthetic WOM is consumption-related communica -
tion in which AI participates in creating, mediating,
evaluating, and/or acting on the message.
In the definition above, the word “creating” implies what
the source does. In synthetic WOM, the source that creates
WOM may be a human, an AI system, or a hybrid arrange -
ment in which AI assists in shaping the content. The message
itself consists of consumption-related content (e.g., reviews,
recommendations, or simply a picture portraying the act
or experience of consumption) that resembles traditional
WOM but may be generated, edited, or transformed through
AI technologies. The word “mediating” indicates the role
of the channel. In these environments, AI-enabled digital
channels do more than simply transmit information. They
can curate, structure, summarize, synthesize, or even re-
imagine what is presented to consumers. The phrase “evalu-
ating and acting on” suggests the receiver’ s capacity. The
receiver may be a human consumer who interprets the com-
munication, or an AI system that analyzes WOM, presents
the results to a human, and potentially makes consumption
decisions on the human consumer's behalf.
Regarding this definition, it should also be noted that the
conjunctions “and/or” indicate that in synthetic WOM, AI
involvement does not need to occur across all three compo-
nents or roles of “creating”, “mediating”, and “evaluating
and acting on”. Participation in any one of these is sufficient
for the communication to qualify as synthetic WOM (e.g.,
the WOM is AI-generated, but the mediating channel is
non-AI, and the recipient is a human consumer). By specify-
ing these elements, the definition maintains continuity with
established WOM frameworks while illustrating how AI
reshapes the roles of source, message, channel, and receiver
in contemporary consumption-related communication.
Degree of syntheticity in synthetic WOM
Although AI may serve as a receiver of WOM by filter -
ing, summarizing, or even acting on WOM content on
behalf of consumers, consumer evaluation remains central
when humans are exposed to, reflect on, or rely on WOM
in their decision-making. In these settings, consumers will
likely assess the degree of syntheticity of the WOM they
are exposed to. Such syntheticity assessments toward the
content can be activated, for example, by the WOM con -
tent itself. Certain features may appear suspiciously artifi -
cial, such as overly polished visuals, unnaturally consistent
positivity, repetitive phrasing, or a lack of contextual and
experiential detail. When such cues surface, consumers
may question whether the content reflects automated gen -
eration or genuine human contribution. Another factor that
may prompt consumers to assess the syntheticity of WOM
content is the broader development of the digital environ -
ment. AI technologies have become affordable and user-
friendly, enabling ordinary users to create a large volume
of diverse content. This has led to a substantial increase in
content online, and consumer skepticism about how content
is generated has grown significantly (Wahid, Mero, et al.,
2025). Thus, when encountering online content, consumers
often take a precautionary step by assessing whether it is
synthetic.

Higher perceived syntheticity does not necessarily
imply negative judgments or decisions. Empirical mar -
keting research has shown that individuals differentiate
between objects that are produced (or served) by automated
or mechanical processes and those that stem from human
action, not because one is inherently superior, but because
both machine contributions and human involvement carry
different interpretive implications (Brüns & Meißner, 2024;
Fuchs et al., 2015; Mende et al., 2019). Non-human out -
puts are often treated as procedural or instrumental, whereas
human-generated objects and opinions can often be under -
stood to reflect subjective experience, situational judgment,
or personal choice (Longoni et al., 2019).
These distinctions suggest that the machine-generated
nature of content shapes how information is interpreted rather
than uniformly signaling lower value (Kirk & Givi, 2025).
Consequently, the influence of the degree of syntheticity on
consumer decision-making is likely to vary across contexts,
depending on what consumers seek from the WOM and
how they intend to use it (e.g., when considering a medi -
cal treatment or a high-cost financial product, consumers
may place greater weight on whether WOM reflects real use
rather than simulated or machine-generated evaluation; and
when searching or choosing a low-cost everyday item, con-
sumers may rely on synthetic WOM without closely con -
sidering the extent of machine involvement). Considering
the potential significance of the synthetic nature of WOM
content for consumers, we introduce the concept of degree
of syntheticity, which we define as follows1:
The degree of syntheticity is consumers’ perception
of how much consumption-related content reflects
machine-driven rather than human-driven processes.
The degree of syntheticity is determined by four key per -
ceptual dimensions that signal machine-generated content
in different ways. Specifically, consumers may infer syn -
theticity from the narrative of the WOM content, the voice
through which it is expressed, the appearance of entities
presented, and the consumption experiences depicted. Col -
lectively, narrative syntheticity, voice syntheticity, appear -
ance syntheticity, and consumption syntheticity capture
the extent to which consumers perceive WOM content as
machine-generated (see Fig. 1).

Sound syntheticity captures the extent to which the audi-
tory elements of WOM content are perceived as machine-
generated rather than human-produced. This dimension
includes spoken language, music, and sound effects con -
veyed through audio or audiovisual WOM formats. Human
voices typically exhibit natural variation in tone, pacing,
pitch, and rhythm, as well as hesitations, breaths, and minor
imperfections that reflect embodied speech. By contrast,
synthetic voices may sound overly smooth or consistently
modulated, with limited variation and no spontaneous
irregularities. For music and sound effects, assessments of
syntheticity may be less immediate for consumers with low
musical skills and often rely on recognition or recall (e.g.,
when consumers notice sounds that sound overly generic
or widely associated with AI-generated media). Through
these auditory cues, consumers may form impressions
about whether the soundscape reflects human expression
or machine-driven production, shaping perceptions of the
degree of syntheticity in WOM content.
Appearance syntheticity  applies to WOM that depicts
human actors. It reflects the extent to which the entity
depicted in WOM content is perceived as a virtual character
or a deepfake of an existing person rather than a real human.
This dimension is salient in synthetic WOM because AI-
generated content can convincingly simulate human faces
and bodies. AI systems may introduce entirely new per -
sonas (e.g., virtual influencers) or fabricate realistic repre -
sentations of real individuals. As a result, consumers may
question whether the appearing entity is algorithmically
produced or genuinely human. This doubt often emerges
when visual cues subtly diverge from expected human
patterns. For example, testimonial videos on social media
may depict a person reviewing a product or service. These
videos may appear realistic initially, yet subtle irregulari -
ties (e.g., slightly mismatched lip movements, unnaturally
smooth skin texture, or rigid facial expressions) may raise
doubts about whether the person is real or a deepfake. In
these situations, consumers may question whether a human
truly appears in the communication or whether the content
merely simulates human presence.
Another form of ambiguous human appearance arises
when the individual appears recognizable but is contextu -
ally implausible. For instance, a video may depict a famous
influencer giving a testimonial for an obscure traditional
remedy or an unknown household product. While the face,
voice, and mannerisms resemble those of a real person, con-
sumers may question whether the individual in the video
is actually the influencer herself or a deepfake. The doubt
does not originate from technical flaws alone, but from a
mismatch between the perceived recognizability of the per-
son and the nature of the product, under review. In such
cases, the appearance is human and familiar, yet contextual
implausibility casts doubt on whether a real human is pres -
ent or the content merely simulates human appearance. In
the context of synthetic WOM content, consumers assess
the appearance of syntheticity by weighing visual realism,
motion consistency, and contextual fit. These inferences
inform broader judgments of the degree of syntheticity in
WOM.
Consumption syntheticity  captures the extent to which
WOM content conveys a sense of simulated usage rather
than one rooted in actual human use. It is of relevance, for
example, in the context of reviews or product endorsements
(e.g., influencer marketing). In purely textual WOM, con -
sumers may infer simulated consumption when descriptions
feel generic or oddly detached from situational detail, such
as travel reviews that list features or emotions in a formu -
laic way without concrete markers of lived experience (note
that this part of consumption syntheticity differs from nar -
rative syntheticity because it evaluates the synthetic nature
of consumption, not the synthetic nature of storytelling). In
multimodal WOM, discrepancies across formats may fur -
ther heighten the perceived syntheticity of consumption. For
instance, a written account of using a skincare product may
read as personal and human. At the same time, the accom -
panying image depicts skin textures or lighting that appear
digitally altered, prompting doubts about whether the con -
sumption described in the text actually occurred. In such
cases, judgments of consumption syntheticity arise from
how different elements of the message align or conflict in
conveying real use.

in dynamic or highly visual content. In video-based
WOM, consumption can appear simulated when motion,

Overall degree of WOM syntheticity
The overall degree of syntheticity in synthetic WOM does
not require all dimensions to be present simultaneously.
Different WOM formats activate different subsets of syn -
theticity perceptions depending on the cues available in the
content. For instance, a static image of a virtual influencer
wearing a branded handbag contains no spoken or written
narrative and no vocal expression. In this case, consumers
may assess syntheticity primarily through appearance and
consumption syntheticity, whereas narrative and voice syn-
theticity remain absent. By contrast, a narrated video review
that includes voice and storyline but no visible human figure
allows consumers to assess narrative syntheticity, voice syn-
theticity, and consumption syntheticity, whereas appearance
syntheticity cannot be evaluated. These examples illustrate
that the degree of syntheticity is not a fixed checklist of
attributes. It is a flexible perceptual construct formed from
whichever synthetic cues are available in a given WOM
message. The dimensions of narrative, voice, appearance,
and consumption syntheticity, therefore, constitute forma -
tive (rather than reflective) dimensions of the underlying
construct.
In the future, assessing the degree of syntheticity in
WOM content will likely become increasingly challenging.
On the one hand, consumers are becoming more familiar
with prompt writing and content refinement (e.g., carefully
crafted prompts enable AI-generated narratives in WOM
to sound more personal, nuanced, and humanlike). On the
other hand, rapid advances in generative AI continue to
improve the realism of synthetic text, images, audio, and
video, reducing overt cues of machine-generated content
(Hartmann et al., 2024; Heitmann et al., 2025; Wahid, Han,
et al., 2026). As these developments blur the perceptual
boundary between human-made and machine-generated
WOM, judgments of syntheticity are likely to rely on sub -
tler and more inferential cues.
Future research directions
In this article, we conceptualize synthetic WOM and intro -
duce the notion of degree of syntheticity. These contribu -
tions establish a foundational understanding of how AI
participates in consumption-related communication. How -
ever, our conceptualizations represent only the fundamental
aspects of synthetic WOM. As synthetic WOM continues
to develop rapidly in practice, academic research needs to
investigate how these new forms of communication oper -
ate, how consumers and firms respond to them, and how
they reshape digital marketplaces. To advance this agenda,
we propose five directions that can guide future research on
synthetic WOM.
When does the degree of syntheticity matter?
Our conceptualization treats the degree of syntheticity as a
perceptual construct that shapes interpretation rather than as
a uniform reduction in value. Future research should there -
fore identify when a higher degree of syntheticity decreases
WOM influence, when it amplifies it, and when it becomes
largely irrelevant. One factor influencing this may be deci -
sion goal orientation. Research shows that consumers pur -
sue different goals when evaluating WOM (Babić Rosario
et al., 2020). Sometimes they seek experiential insight (e.g.,
understanding how a product feels in everyday life or how
others respond to it emotionally). In contrast, in other situ -
ations, they pursue functional goals (e.g., reducing search
costs or comparing alternatives efficiently). Future studies
may examine whether consumers discount highly synthetic
WOM when experiential understanding is central but rely
on it more when the decision goal emphasizes efficiency.
A second interesting characteristic in this context is social
visibility, which relates to the extent to which a decision
is observable by others (Fisher & Price, 1992). Future
research may test whether synthetic WOM is more accept -
able in private decisions but less persuasive when consump-
tion choices are publicly visible and socially evaluated.
Future research may also move beyond treating the
degree of syntheticity as a single continuum and examine
how its formative dimensions produce distinct outcomes.
For example, narrative syntheticity may matter more when
consumers seek experiential insight; voice syntheticity may
matter more in testimonial formats; appearance syntheticity
may dominate in influencer-like contexts; and consumption
syntheticity may drive judgments in product-trial claims.
Researchers may therefore test whether specific syntheticity
profiles (e.g., high narrative syntheticity but low consump -
tion syntheticity) differentially shape perceived diagnostic -
ity, trust, and persuasion. This configurational approach can
clarify not only whether syntheticity matters, but which type

AI-exclusive social media and AI-to-AI WOM: What
happens when AI talks to AI?
Emerging platforms designed for interactions among AI
agents suggest a future in which consumption-related com -
munication may occur directly between them. One of these
platforms is Moltbook. Although reports suggest that many
Moltbook agents may not be fully autonomous and may still
be guided by humans (Li, 2026; Schmelzer, 2026), the plat-
form nonetheless signals the emerging potential for WOM
among AI systems. In such environments, WOM may cir -
culate primarily between AI agents that gather, interpret,
and exchange WOM on behalf of users. Future research
may investigate how WOM dynamics evolve when AI
agents become the primary communicators. For instance,
scholars may examine how AI agents evaluate credibility
signals in WOM, how they weigh conflicting information,
and whether influence structures emerge among AI agents in
ways that resemble or diverge from human social networks.
In this context, a related question concerns how AI agents
represent and negotiate the preferences of the humans they
serve. Researchers may examine how AI agents translate
human goals, constraints, and preferences into WOM-based
decision rules, and how these representations influence the
WOM information that agents generate, transmit, or priori -
tize. Studies may also investigate how markets change when
purchasing decisions increasingly rely on AI-to-AI com -
munication based on WOM signals (e.g., when AI agents
collect reviews, compare options, and communicate recom-
mendations to other systems responsible for purchasing or
product selection). By studying these emerging configu -
rations, future research can deepen understanding of how
influence, credibility, and persuasion operate in environ -
ments where WOM communication no longer occurs exclu-
sively among humans but increasingly involves interactions
among AI agents.
AI agents as WOM receivers
AI assistants and autonomous agents increasingly process
WOM on behalf of consumers by reading reviews, aggre -
gating ratings, summarizing opinions, and generating rec -
ommendations. In these situations, WOM may influence
consumption outcomes even when humans never directly
encounter the original communication. Future research may
examine how AI agents interpret WOM signals and whether
their evaluation criteria differ from those used by human con-
sumers. For example, AI systems may prioritize aggregated
metrics (e.g., rating distributions, sentiment scores, or sta -
tistical consistency across large sets of reviews). In contrast,
human consumers often rely more on narrative richness,
emotional cues, or perceived similarity with other consum -
ers. Understanding these differences can clarify how influ -
ence operates when WOM is first interpreted by machines
rather than by humans.
From a firm perspective, the emergence of AI agents as
recipients of WOM raises new strategic questions about how
companies should produce, structure, and manage WOM
signals in environments where AI systems increasingly
interpret consumption-related information. Future research
may investigate how firms can optimize WOM-related con-
tent, such as product information, for AI interpretation, for
example, by structuring reviews, highlighting verifiable
consumption experiences, or ensuring that key product attri-
butes are clearly expressed in ways that AI systems can eas-
ily detect and process. Scholars may also explore how firms
monitor and respond to WOM as AI agents increasingly
mediate consumer decision-making, including whether
companies adjust their review management strategies, repu-
tation management practices, or content design to influence
AI-driven recommendation systems. As AI agents become
more central to information processing and purchasing deci-
sions, understanding how firms strategically interact with
this new layer of WOM reception will become increasingly
important for both marketing practice and theory.
The dark side of synthetic WOM
One potential dark side of synthetic WOM is that AI-gener-
ated content may unrealistically inflate consumers' expec -
tations of consumption. AI can produce highly polished
images, videos, and narratives that portray idealized prod -
uct experiences or lifestyles that are difficult or impossible
to achieve in reality. For example, synthetic WOM may
depict flawless skin after using cosmetics, unrealistic body
standards associated with fashion or fitness, or exaggerated
travel and lifestyle experiences that appear effortless and
perfect. Such portrayals may lead consumers to form expec-
tations that real consumption cannot satisfy. Future research
may investigate how exposure to synthetic WOM that pres-
ents idealized consumption scenarios influences consumers’
perceptions of product performance, post-purchase satisfac-
tion, and broader mental and emotional well-being. Schol -
ars may also explore whether repeated exposure to highly
curated or artificial consumption narratives contributes to
feelings of inadequacy, social comparison, or dissatisfaction
with one’s own lifestyle. The question of how AI can create
or amplify customer vulnerability has recently emerged as
an active area of research (Hermann et al., 2024).

The misuse of identities and reputations in synthetic
WOM is also another dark side of synthetic WOM. Advances
in AI make it possible to create deepfake representations of
individuals who appear to recommend or review products
that they have never used. Such content may damage repu -
tations, mislead consumers, or distort market competition.
Future research may investigate how consumers attribute
responsibility when harmful synthetic WOM appears and
whether blame is directed toward the content creator, the
firm that benefits from the communication, the platform host-
ing the content, or the AI technology involved. Researchers
may also examine how firms and platforms should respond
to harmful synthetic WOM, including whether corrective
communication or content-verification mechanisms effec -
tively reduce the negative consequences.
Regulation and governance of synthetic
WOM
The emergence of synthetic WOM also raises important
questions about how platforms, firms, and regulators should
govern AI-enabled communication related to consump -
tion. Synthetic WOM varies in its degree of syntheticity,
may involve AI agents as recipients, may circulate among
AI agents in AI-exclusive environments, and may lead to
harmful outcomes in certain contexts. Future research
should therefore examine how governance mechanisms can
address these evolving configurations of communication.
For instance, scholars may examine how disclosure poli -
cies, labeling systems, or detection tools should be designed
when different dimensions of syntheticity are present in
WOM content. Research may also examine whether gov -
ernance approaches should differ depending on the com -
munication context, such as when synthetic WOM targets
human audiences versus when it is interpreted primarily by
AI agents that filter and summarize WOM before it reaches
consumers.
A final crucial point is the institutional arrangements
and enforcement mechanisms required to manage synthetic
WOM environments. Platforms increasingly rely on auto -
mated detection systems and user reporting processes to
identify problematic content. Yet, these mechanisms face
challenges when synthetic WOM becomes highly realistic
or when the severity of harm varies across product catego -
ries. Future research may explore how platforms and regula-
tors determine appropriate responses once synthetic WOM
is detected. For example, some content may warrant label -
ing or reduced algorithmic amplification, whereas more
harmful cases may require removal of the content or suspen-
sion of accounts responsible for producing it. Research may
also examine how these governance approaches influence
the behavior of firms that produce or benefit from synthetic
WOM, including whether clearer rules and enforcement
mechanisms encourage responsible use of AI in consump -
tion-related communication. Understanding how gover -
nance systems differentiate between acceptable and harmful
synthetic WOM can help maintain trust in digital informa -
tion environments while allowing beneficial uses of AI-
enabled communication to continue.
Conclusion
The WOM literature has long examined how consumers,
brands, and digital channels interact in shaping the diffusion
of consumption-related communication in the marketplace
(Babić Rosario et al., 2020; Hennig-Thurau et al., 2004;
Kozinets et al., 2010). In parallel, research on influencer
marketing has increasingly sought to clarify the intricate
relationships among influencers, brands, and platforms,
highlighting how influence emerges from the coordinated
dynamics within this digital ecosystem (Libai et al., 2025).
Building on these, the evolving landscape of digital commu-
nication suggests that AI is becoming another key partici -
pant in this environment. Alongside consumers, influencers,
brands, and digital channels, AI systems are beginning to
take part in creating, mediating, evaluating, and acting on
WOM. In response to this development, this article intro -
duces the concept of synthetic WOM and clarifies how AI
may influence the source, message, channel, and receiver
elements of WOM. By articulating this perspective, we
aim to offer a foundation for examining how the growing
presence of AI may reshape influence processes in digital
markets. We hope this article serves as a starting point for
scholars seeking to better understand this major evolution
the field is likely to face in the future.
