ABSTRACT
Asglobalmarkets transition from linear consumptionsystems toward circular, sustainability-orientedbusiness models, environmental responsibilityhasemerged
as a crucial driver of corporate legitimacy and competitive advantage. This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of how green marketing and sustainable
packaginginfluence consumerpurchase decisions, with a specific focusonthe mediatingpsychologicalmechanismsofenvironmentalawareness andgreen trust.
Drawingoncontemporaryempiricalresearch,the studyconceptualizeseco-friendlycommunicationandpackaging designnotmerelyaspromotionaltools,butas
strategic signals that shape consumerperceptions ofbrand authenticity and value. The conceptual model is robustly grounded in the Theory of Planned Behavior
andtheCommitment-TrustTheoryofRelationshipMarketing.Adoptingaquantitativecross-sectionaldesign,thestudyreliesonsurveyevidencefrom412active
consumers in Bangladesh. The measurement instrument demonstrated strong internal consistency, with reliability testing yielding high Cronbach's alpha values
for all constructs: Consumer Purchase Decision (0.892), Environmental Awareness (0.876), Green Trust (0.852), Green Marketing (0.845), and Sustainable
Packaging (0.814). Multiple linear regression analysis confirmed the predictive validity of the proposed model, which collectively explained 63.6% of the
variance in consumer purchase decisions (R² = 0.636, p < 0.001). The findings indicate that while green marketing and sustainable packaging exert significant
direct positive effectsonpurchase behavior, the psychologicaldimensionsplayaneven morecriticalrole.Notably, greentrust emerged asthestrongest predictor
of consumer choice, emphasizing that transparent, credible, and verifiable sustainability claims are essential for overcoming consumer skepticism and reducing
the perceived risks associated with greenwashing. Furthermore, environmental awareness significantly strengthened the efficacy of these marketing stimuli.
Ultimately, this article contributes to the literature on sustainable consumer behavior by quantitatively demonstrating that functional green practices achieve
optimal market outcomes only when anchored by consumer ecological literacy and trust-based brand relationships. Practical, evidence-based implications are
providedformanagers,marketers,andpolicymakersstrivingtofostersustainable consumptionwithinincreasinglyeco-consciousandskepticalmarkets.

1. Introduction
The contemporary business environment is undergoing a profound and irreversible transformation in how consumers evaluate brands, products, and
overall corporateresponsibility. In earliermarketingeras, competitiveadvantagewas largelyshapedbythemastery ofthetraditionalmarketingmix—
optimizing product performance, price, place, and promotion to maximize shareholder value and market share (Kotler & Keller, 2016; Kotler &
Armstrong, 2021). In the current marketplace, however, this paradigm is no longer sufficient. Consumer choice is now increasingly governed by a
complexmatrixofethical,environmental,andsocialconsiderations.
Escalating global crises—such as accelerating climate change, pervasive plastic pollution, rapid resource depletion, and a rising collective eco-
anxiety—have fundamentally altered consumer expectations. Today’s buyers are no longer passive recipients of goods; they actively assess not only
whatacompanysells,butalsotheethicalfootprintofhowresponsiblyitsources,produces,packages,communicates,anddistributesitsproductsacross

Authentic green marketing transcends simple eco-friendly advertising; it requires the deep integration of environmental responsibility into the core
DNA of a business. This includes sustainable product development, lifecycle-based pricing models, low-emission distribution networks, transparent
communication, verifiable eco-labeling, and purposeful brand positioning. It represents a shift from linear "take-make-dispose" models toward
strategies aligned with theprinciples of a circulareconomy, where long-term ecologicalbalance is valued alongside economic profitability. Parallel to
thisshift,sustainablepackaginghasemergedasapowerful,physicalexpressionofabrand'senvironmentalcommitment.
Because packaging is frequently the first tangible point of sensory interaction between the consumerand the product, it serves as a critical non-verbal

consumer perceptions of product quality, brand responsibility, and overall purchase suitability. In highly saturated markets, innovative sustainable
packagingactsasakeydifferentiatorthatvisuallysignalsabrand'sdedicationtoreducingenvironmentalharm.
Despite the growing global momentum toward sustainability, consumer responses to green marketing cues remain highly complex and frequently
paradoxical. The widespread, unethical practice of greenwashing—where organizations exaggerate, fabricate, or intentionally misrepresent their
environmental initiatives—has cultivated a high degree of consumer skepticism. When companies utilize vague buzzwords or unsubstantiated eco-
labels, they erode market confidence. As a result, the mere presence of green marketing campaigns and sustainable packaging does not automatically

The effectiveness of these sustainable strategies is highly contingent upon underlying psychological mechanisms. Specifically, it depends on
environmentalawareness—whetherconsumers possesstheecologicalliteracyrequiredtounderstandtheimpactoftheirchoices—andgreen trust—the

article investigates how the interplay of external market stimuli (green marketing and sustainable packaging) and internal psychological factors

2. Literature Review
2.1 Green Marketing and Ethical Brand Equity
Green marketing refers to the holistic planning, implementation, and communication of business activities designed to satisfy consumer needs while
actively minimizing harmful impacts on the natural environment (Polonsky, 1994; Peattie, 2001). Over the last two decades, the concept has evolved
from a niche strategy to a mainstream corporate necessity(Ottman, 2011; Dangelico & Vocalelli, 2017). It extends far beyond superficial advertising,
encompassing sustainable product design, responsible sourcing of raw materials, ethical pricing strategies, verified eco-labeling, and transparent
corporate communication (Papadas et al., 2017). When implemented authentically and integrated into the firm's core strategy, green marketing
fundamentally strengthens ethical brand equity by communicating that the organization is deeply committed to long-term ecological and social value
generation,ratherthanpurelyshort-termfinancialprofit(Leonidouetal.,2013;Linetal.,2017).
Prior studies consistently demonstrate that ethical and environmentally responsible marketing exerts a profound, positive influence on consumer
attitudes, brand loyalty, and ultimate purchase decisions. Genoveva and Samukti (2020) reported that strategic green marketing bolsters brand image
and significantly lowers consumer hesitation during the purchase process. Building on this, Nayak et al. (2026) emphasized the critical role of ethical
advertisinginenhancingbrandcredibility, particularlyamongyounger,highlycriticaldemographics.Furthermore,inhighlyconnecteddigitalmarkets,
greenmarketingmessagescanproliferaterapidlythroughonlineplatformsandsocialmedianetworks.However,thishyper-visibilityequallyintensifies
the necessity for evidence-based communication (Trivedi et al., 2018). Consumers are demonstrably more likely to respond positively and develop
brand attachment when environmental claims are specific, verifiable, third-party certified, and strictly consistent with the brand's operational realities
(Schmucketal.,2018).
2.2 Sustainable Packaging in the Circular Economy
Sustainable packagingrepresents one of the most tangible, highly visible, and functional dimensions of a brand's environmental responsibility. Within
the contemporary framework of a circular economy, packaging is conceptually redesigned to eliminate waste, mandate reuse or recycling, and
drasticallyminimize thecarbonfootprint throughoutthe product'sentirelife cycle (Geissdoerferet al., 2017;Korhonenet al.,2018).The utilizationof
materials such as biodegradable paper, compostable bioplastics, post-consumer recyclable containers, and refillable formats serves as an immediate
communicationtoolatthepointofsale,clearlydifferentiatingaproductfromconventional,resource-depletingalternatives(Prakash&Pathak,2017).
The psychological and behavioral influence of packaging on consumer decision-making is paramount because packaging simultaneously synthesizes
informational data, aesthetic appeal, functional convenience, and environmental symbolism (Magnier & Crié, 2015). Lutfie, Syafrina, and Hidayat
(2017) argued that eco-packaging can profoundly manipulate product perception, often leading consumers to infer higher overall product quality—a
phenomenonknownasthe"haloeffect."Imranetal.(2024)furthersubstantiatedthis,demonstratingthateco-friendlypackagingsignificantlyimproves
purchase intention in the competitive beverage sector. Nevertheless, scholars caution that sustainable packaging must remain practical, economically
accessible, and conceptually clear. Magnier and Schoormans (2015) found that if consumers fail to comprehend the specific environmental benefit of

2.3 Environmental Awareness and Eco-Conscious Consumption
Environmentalawarenessrefers toaconsumer'scognitiveunderstandingofpressingecologicalcrisesandtheirperceivedmoralobligationtoengagein
environmentally responsible behavior (Kollmuss & Agyeman, 2002). It functions as the foundational psychological trigger that determines whether
consumers actively seek out, recognize, and value green marketing cues. According to the widely applied Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) (Ajzen,
1991), consumer behavior is determined by attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. In the context of sustainability, extensive
research demonstrates that heightened environmental awareness directly strengthens positive attitudes toward green products and amplifies the moral
motivationrequiredtodeviatefromhabitual,non-greenpurchasingpatterns(Paul,Modi,&Patel,2016;Han,2015).
Crucially, environmentally aware consumers are cognitively equipped to interpret sustainable packaging and nuanced green marketing campaigns as
meaningfulindicatorsofcorporatevalues,ratherthandismissingthemassuperficialproductfeatures(Joshi&Rahman,2015).Prihandonoetal.(2020)
highlighted that green marketing tools, particularly eco-labels and carbon footprint metrics, become significantly more effective when the target
audiencepossessestheecologicalliteracyrequiredtounderstandtheirrelevance.Similarly,Mahmoudetal.(2022)notedthatenvironmentalawareness
acts as a moderating variable that interacts with willingness to pay and green packaging to finalize the purchase decision. However, literature also
heavilydocumentsan"attitude-behaviorgap"(or"greengap"),whereinhighlyawareconsumersoftenfailtopurchasegreenproductsduetosituational
barriers like high premiums, lack of availability, or deep-seated consumer habits (Carrington, Neville, & Whitwell, 2010; ElHaffar et al., 2020).
Therefore, while environmental awareness intensifies the effect of green marketing, marketers must simultaneously lower transactional barriers to
convertthisawarenessintoaction.
2.4 Green Trust and the Challenge of Greenwashing
Green trust refers to a consumer's willingness to rely on a brand, product, or service based on the deeply held belief that its environmental claims are
credible, honest, transparent, and genuinely capable of delivering the promised ecological value (Chen, 2010). In markets saturated withsustainability
claims, trust is an absolute prerequisite because consumers possess "information asymmetry"—they cannot directly or easily verify the hidden
environmental performance of a supply chain or product life cycle (Atkinson & Rosenthal, 2014). Instead, they are forced to depend on brand
communication, third-party certification, and overarching corporate reputation. When green trust is successfully cultivated, consumers feel confident
thattheireconomicchoiceslegitimatelyalignwiththeirpersonalenvironmentalvalues,therebysolidifyingbrandloyalty(Martinez,2015).
Conversely, "greenwashing"—the corporate practice of disseminating exaggerated, vague, deceptive, or entirely fabricated environmental claims—
represents the most severe barrier to the growth of sustainable consumption (Delmas & Burbano, 2011). The proliferation of greenwashing has
generated widespread consumer skepticism, severely diluting the effectiveness of genuine green marketing efforts (Skarmeas & Leonidou, 2013).
Nyilasy, Gangadharbatla, and Paladino (2014) documented a "boomerang effect," revealing that when consumers detect a discrepancy between a
brand's green advertising and its actual environmental performance, their perception of the brand drops lower than if the brand had never made green
claims at all. Addressing this risk, Chen and Chang (2012) argued that "green perceived value" only increases purchase intention when "green
perceived risk" is actively mitigated through the establishment of deep green trust. Contemporary research continuously supports this paradigm,
indicating that green trust serves as the indispensable psychological bridge required to convert positive environmental attitudes and awareness into
actual,repeatedpurchasedecisions(Nguyenetal.,2019;Testaetal.,2020).

3.1 Theoretical Foundations
Theconceptualframework ofthisstudyis anchored ina dual-theory approach, integratingtwo majorbehavioralandrelationalparadigms:The Theory
of PlannedBehavior (TPB)(Ajzen, 1991)andtheCommitment-Trust Theory ofRelationship Marketing(Morgan& Hunt,1994).Bycombining these
theories,thisstudyaddressesboththeinternalcognitiveprocessesoftheconsumerandtherelationaldynamicsbetweentheconsumerandthebrand.
TheTheoryofPlannedBehavior(TPB)positsthathumanactionisguidedbythreecoreconsiderations:behavioralbeliefs(attitudes),normativebeliefs
(social pressures), and control beliefs (perceived behavioral control). In the context of this study, green marketing and sustainable packaging are
conceptualized as external, informationalstimuli.These stimuli influencethe consumer's cognitive evaluation, ultimately shaping theirattitude toward
environmentally responsible products. Furthermore, environmental awareness serves as a cognitive amplifier; it strengthens the consumer's ability to
decode green marketing stimuli, assign personal relevance to them, and recognize their own perceived behavioral control in mitigating environmental
harmthroughtheirpurchasingchoices.
The Commitment-Trust Theory complements the cognitive focus of TPB by emphasizing the critical importance of confidence, risk reduction, and
shared values in fostering long-term consumer-brand relationships. In contemporary sustainability markets, where the deceptive practice of
"greenwashing" is prevalent, cognitive approval of a product's green attributes is often insufficient to trigger a purchase. Consumers may hold strong
pro-environmental attitudes, but they remain highly unlikely to execute a purchase if they doubt the authenticity of a brand's environmental claims.

6. Discussion
The findings demonstrate that eco-conscious consumption is shaped by a combination of external brand actions and internal consumer psychology.
Green marketing and sustainable packaging both contribute positively to purchase decisions because they communicate environmental responsibility
and help consumers identify brands that align with their values. However, their effects are not equally powerful when compared with psychological
factors.GreenTrustemergedasthestrongestpredictor,suggestingthatconsumersaremorelikelytopurchaseeco-friendlyproductswhentheybelieve
thebrand'senvironmentalclaimsaregenuine,transparent,andreliable. Thisresult isparticularlyimportantinthecontext ofgreenwashing.Consumers
are increasingly exposed toenvironmental claims, butnot all claims are credible. When brands providevague or unsupported sustainabilitymessages,
consumers may become skeptical and avoid purchase. By contrast, transparent information, recognized eco-certifications, responsible packaging
choices, and consistent corporate behavior can strengthen trust and reduce perceived risk. Therefore, green trust does not simply support purchase
behavior;ittransformssustainabilityfromamarketingmessageintoacrediblevalueproposition.
Environmental Awareness also played a significant role. Consumers who understand ecological issues are more likely to value biodegradable
packaging, recyclable materials, eco-labels, and ethical advertising. Awareness helps consumers interpret sustainability signals and connect them to
broader environmental outcomes. This finding supports the view that consumer education is essential for the success of green marketing strategies.
Withoutsufficientawareness,consumersmayoverlookorundervalueenvironmentallyresponsibleproductfeatures.
7. Managerial and Policy Implications
7.1 Managerial Implications
For managers, brand strategists,and marketing practitioners, the findings of this research offer profoundinsightsinto modern consumerbehavior. The
central message is clear: sustainability must transcend superficial advertising and be fully integrated into the core identity, operational processes, and
long-term vision of the brand. Treating environmental responsibility as a temporary promotional gimmick or a reactive public relations strategy is
increasingly counterproductive in today’s market. Companies must heavily invest in credible, demonstrable environmental initiatives. This requires a
shift toward responsible sourcing of raw materials, rigorous lifecycle assessments (LCA) to monitor the ecological footprint of product development,
and the active pursuit of recognized third-party certifications (e.g., Fair Trade, FSC, Rainforest Alliance). Marketing communication strategies must
evolve from vague, emotive messaging to highly specific, evidence-based storytelling. Information regarding supply chain transparency, ethical labor
practices,andcarbonreductiongoals shouldbeconsistentandseamlesslyintegratedacrossalltouchpoints,fromphysicalretailenvironmentstodigital
platformsandsocialmediacampaigns.
Furthermore, managers should recognize that sustainable packaging is not merely a logistical necessity but a powerful, highly visible strategic
communicationtool. Withintheframeworkof acirculareconomy, thephysicalpackageacts asasilentsalesperson atthe criticalmomentof purchase.
Therefore,thepackagingdesignmustserveadualpurpose:itmustfunctionallyreduceenvironmentalharm(e.g.,utilizingbiodegradable,compostable,
or highly recyclable materials) while simultaneously communicating those exact benefits to the consumer in an easily digestible manner. Simple,
recognizable eco-labels, clear and accessible disposal instructions, verified certification marks, and innovative technologies like QR codes linking to
detailedsustainabilityreports cansignificantlyenhanceconsumerunderstanding.Bybridgingthe gapbetween complexcorporatesustainabilityefforts
and everyday consumer comprehension, brands can foster deep-seated trust and loyalty. Conversely, brands must rigorously avoid any exaggerated,
unsubstantiated, or misleading claims—a practice that risks severe reputational damage. Managers must champion authenticity, choosing to
communicatemeasurable,verifiedactionsandtangibleecologicaloutcomes.
7.2 Policy Implications
Policymakers, regulatory bodies, and industry associations play an equally critical role in shaping and safeguarding the integrity of sustainability
markets.Thewidespreadissueofgreenwashing—wherecompaniesintentionallyexaggerateormisrepresenttheirenvironmentalperformance—creates
market confusion and unfairly disadvantages businesses that genuinely invest in sustainable practices. To address this, policymakers must implement
andenforcerigorous, standardizedeco-labelingguidelines.Afragmentedlandscapeofunregulated,self-declared eco-labelscurrentlydilutesconsumer
trust. The establishment of clear, universally recognized, and scientifically backed certification frameworks is essential. Furthermore, stricter
monitoring and auditing mechanisms for corporate environmental claims must be instituted, accompanied by significant financial and operational
penaltiesforcompaniesfoundguiltyofdeceptivegreencommunication.
Clear and decisive regulations not only protect consumers from exploitation but also support responsible firms by leveling the playing field. When
authentic sustainability efforts are shielded from the noise of deceptive competitors, ethical brands can thrive. Beyond regulation, government bodies
and environmental NGOs should actively invest in nationwide public awareness and educational campaigns. By elevating the baseline ecological
literacyofthegeneralpublic,consumersbecomeempoweredtoevaluateenvironmentalclaimscritically,differentiatebetweenauthenticandsuperficial
sustainability,andultimatelymakeinformed,responsiblepurchasedecisionsthatdrivemarkettransformation.
