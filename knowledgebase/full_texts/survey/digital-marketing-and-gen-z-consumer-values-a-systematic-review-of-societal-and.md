Abstract:
The great transformation brought on by a 'Third Wave' of digital marketing has changed marketing
from a separate business process into an underlying sociocultural structure that employs
algorithmic personalization and generative artificial intelligence. A systematic review of the
academic literature published from 2018 to 2026 is conducted in this paper, which demonstrates
how much of an impact the new range of digital tools has had on Generation Z (born 1997-2012).
As 'Digital Natives,' these consumers are  heavily reliant on peer networks and electronic Word -
of-Mouth (eWOM) because of their experience with increasingly blurred lines between the
physical and digital worlds. Despite the current academic focus on how AI-based personalization
and influencer dynamics have shifted generational value priorities, a large 'knowledge gap' exists
regarding all of these factors. By employing the Content Marketing Theory (Value Exchange) and
combining that with Behavioral Economics, the author examines how digital market ing uses
cognitive heuristics as manipulative techniques to disrupt collective behavior and decision -
making. The paper show that while digital marketing achieves some successes by providing a
platform for equitable information distribution and enabling relating -based discovery, it
ultimately creates a major 'ethical-consumption gap. The paper concluded that digital marketing
has transformed into a key  force shaping powerful social and marketing culture and  fosters
strategic shift from just selling stuff to creating meaning and ultimately dismantling the traditional
vertical product funnel, result ing to  a horizontal cultural distribution system.  In addition , it
constitutes a very cost-efficient and successful option for selling products or services online. It is
recommended that organizations and brands should ditch those fake influencer deals and focus on
digital marketing contents that promotes acceptable social and cultural values.

Keywords:  Consumer Values, Gen Z, Digital Marketing, Social Change.

Introduction
At the start of the 21st century, there was a major change in the way people buy things and talk to
one another. Marketing was high on the broadcast side of the scale, and the main way that people
sent out messages to their customers was through print, radio, TV, and the like. Marketing has now
turned into a big digital ecosystem and continues to grow each day with new technologies and
ways for companies and individuals to market themselves. While this growth and change has
occurred, it has been accompanied  by the continued growth of the Social Web. The evolution of
digital marketing through the "Web 2.0" continues at a rapid pace, which has caused digital
marketing to now become the primary driver of culture in today's society.

The "First Wave" of digital marketing was focused on transactional methods, so the introduction
of social media marks the beginning of a new era - the "Second Wave" of digital marketing is
marked by the introduction of interactivity into marketing tactics. Consumers are no longer passive
recipients; they are now active participants. Now that we have reached the "Third Wave" in 2026,
characterized by algorithmic personalization and the use of generative artificial intelligence,
marketing has become something other than just advertisements; it is now an ever-present, unseen
infrastructure that helps dictate the types of information that we receive, whom we interact with,
and, ultimately, what cultural values we hold.
As digital marketing is affecting all age demographics around the world, the impact that digital
marketing has on Generation Z (those born from 1997 to 2012) is very different from its impact
on the other age groups and very fundamental to them as individu als. Gen Z are truly the first
digital natives; they do not remember a time prior to the introduction of smartphones and
algorithms. For them, the line between their "physical self" and their "digital persona" really does
not exist. Gen Zs are perceived as  being familiar with the use of digital technology, the internet,
and social media from a very young age.
Shaji (2024) added that Gen Z can be found between analog and digital forms of life and serve as
a bridge between their childhoods in the analog world and their adulthood in the digital world.
However, there are some important reasons for considering this generation when conducting a
study on consumer values; those include: Gen Z’s media consumption trends: namely, their
preference for short -form videos (i.e., TikTok/Reels) and the influence of peers on consumer
decisions—i.e., through reverse socialization  of older generations (traditional consumers).
Furthermore, the value -identity connection between value and identity can be seen through the
way that Gen Z purchases products; Gen Z not only purchases products, but they also purchase
(i.e., through purchas ing products, consumers can demonstrate their value) the meaning, ethics,
and sense of belonging they feel from the purchase of a product.
Digital marketing, therefore, is the primary means through which this generation constructs its
cultural identity. Reportedly, Gen Z has the highest levels of anxiety and depression since the 'baby
boomer' generation (i.e., 20%-25% higher compared to any prior generation).
The Z Keepers, despite expressing their preference for ethical and authentic practices, face many
issues in the online marketing world, which impacts the Generation Z culture on a large scale. The
contradiction lies between their commitment to helping the environment and their vulnerability to
"ultra-fast fashion" promotional tactics. In addition, algorithms and psychological "scarcity"
principles used by marketers can often override their ethical beliefs, causing them to buy products
at an accelerated pace  through spur-of-the-moment purchasing. Internet marketing tools have a
primary purpose of maintaining users' interest; however, the echo chamber effects they create
impact far more than just brand experience; in fact, they limit the multiculturalism and s ocial
isolation experienced by many of today's teens.
Despite years of academic and industry research, there are currently no comprehensive, systematic
reviews that create an integrated link between the numerous different types of academic literature
on digital marketing. Much of the focus of the current academic literature on digital marketing has
been limited to the economic impact of different types of digital marketing (i.e., how to improve
click-through rates) or the psychological effects of using social media (i.e., how much time a user
spends on social media platforms is related to depression). There currently does not exist a
comprehensive, systematic meta -analysis of the literature on digital marketing which combines
the two separate strands of academic literature that address digital marketing and the  measurable
effects of digital marketing on cultural change.

Consumers Values
Consumer values refer to the deeply held beliefs, principles, and standards  that guide a person’s
behavior and choices in the marketplace   Customers purchase goods or services primarily because
of the value they receive. This realization has led marketers and marketing departments of
businesses to focus on consumer value creation in order to maintain customer loyalty and attract
new clients through current ones in order to maximize profits. Because of this, Albert and Peter
(2008) pointed out that comprehe nding and generating consumer value has emerged as a crucial
requirement for sustained corporate success. Adetayo et al. (2024) state that value generation refers
to crafting a product or service that fulfills customer requirements. This indicates the fact  that
every business success rests upon its ability to deliver value to customers through product/service
offerings.
Therefore, businesses’ concerted marketing efforts are focused on the issue of value delivery to
customers significantly. According to Iyanna et al. (2012), the importance that companies place
on providing value to customers indicates that their exchange r elationship with customers is
predicated on giving up value in order to receive value in return. Rashmi et al. (2023) found that
consumers’ values are used to determine behavioral intention in patronizing online fashion retail
organizations; the ability of  the commodity to communicate value to the customers determines
whether they will purchase a product or not. Kumar, back in 2018, as mentioned in Godwill et al.
(2020), said that to keep a business going, the business has to do something for its customers. This
means the business must give its customers something they really want or need. At the time, the
business gets something good from its customers, which is making a profit from the things it sells
to them.
This is how a business can keep going by giving value to its customers and getting value back
from them in the form of profit. Customer orientation, value creation and economic sustainability
are linked together as Adetayo et al., 2024 stated that, my perspective on customer value is based
on the notion of reciprocity, a strategic exchange in which long-term success depends on both the
company and the client. Three perspectives are used to observe customers: the value exchange
model, value as a communication instrument, and strategic customer orientation.

Social Change
Social change refers to the noticeable change in the social structure, cultural symbols, rules of
conduct, or social value systems over a period. It is not a mere change in the conduct of individuals,
but a basic change in the operation of the society and its mode of organization. From the Industrial
Revolution to the Digital Age, social change has been the engine of human history, driven by a
complex interplay of technology, ideology, and collective action. Changes in society can be
seldom attributed to a single factor or event. There are three main sources of social change as
technical innovation, environmental shifts, and ideology and social movements.
Social change is the shift in the values, behavioral patterns, and power structures of the society
brought about by our always -on connectivity. It is not just about a ‘viral’ post; it is about how
digital tools reshape how we communicate, consume, and advocate for causes. Social change has
an impact on evolving marketing trends, as Ankita (2025) stated that these changes influence the
way people live and think; marketers need to cope with this changing perception of the consumer.

Theories
Content Marketing Theory (Value Exchange)
With a strong emphasis on the value exchange notion, Joe Pulizzi is credited with codifying,
popularizing, and promoting "content marketing" as a unique strategic discipline. The creation of
the Content Marketing Institute (CMI) in 2007 is his most importa nt contribution. CMI quickly
became the leading global resource for content marketing education and training for enterprise
brands. Under his leadership, CMI launched Content Marketing World, the largest content
marketing event in the world, and Chief Content Officer Magazine. He has spoken at hundreds of
events globally, always emphasizing the core philosophy of providing value to customers before
asking for anything in return. At the heart of effective content marketing lies the "value exchange"
theory. This theory emphasizes the creation and distribution of valuable, relevant, and consistent
content to attract and retain an audience. The core theoretical underpinning is the value exchange:
brands offer valuable information or entertainment, and in return,  they earn attention, trust, and
ultimately, business.
According to Value Exchange Theory, brands foster audience loyalty by providing regular, expert-
led content that addresses audience issues. In contrast to interruptive advertising, this tactic draws
customers by providing real utility a concept that dates back to the Michelin Guide but is today
essential for online presence. Offering value guarantees that material is discovered by search
engines rather than getting lost in the clutter in a congested online environment. Brands build the
trust required to cultivate enduring connections by putting the needs of users first. In the context
of society and culture, this theory explains how the line between "commercial message" and
"cultural artifact" has blurred as digital marketing is no longer just a business tool, it is a cultural
force that shapes how we learn, what stories we tell, and how we define our personal values.

Behavioral Economics & Psychology Principles
Behavioral Economics & Psychology Principles is not just one big idea from a single author; it is
more like a team effort that mixes psychology and economics together. This research looks into
how our thoughts, feelings, social interactions, and mental pro cesses affect the money choices
made by people and organizations. In digital marketing, it's all about getting the hang of how
people make decisions online, even when it seems a bit off but is actually pretty predictable. The
field's got a bunch of big names, but three stand out, born March 5, 1934, and passed away March
27, 2024. Daniel Kahneman, an Israeli -American psychologist, is famous for his pioneering
research on how we make decisions and judge things. He snagged the Nobel Memorial Prize in
Economic Sciences in 2002, sharing it with Vernon L. Smith. His work really stands out for
blending psychology with economics, especially when it comes to how people make choices when
they're not sure what's going to happen. In his well -known book "Thinking, Fast and Slow" from
2011, Daniel Kahneman brings together years of research to show how our brains work with two
different systems. The first is fast and instinctive, which he calls "System 1," and the second is
slower.
Finally, behavioral economics and psychology dig into how our biases, feelings, and mental
shortcuts mess with the choices we make This theory makes sense because it shows how digital
marketing can really change what society thinks is normal and how people act Digital marketing
isn't just about pushing products; it's reshaping how we make choices together

Theories Triangulation (Behavioral Economics and Psychology Principles, and Content
Marketing (Value Exchange)
Mixing behavioral economics and psychology with content marketing ideas about trading value is
a smart and powerful way to do digital marketing. These two ideas help marketers to make stuff
that is useful and taps into what makes people tick, making their messages hit harder and getting
people to listen. When digital marketers smartly blend psychological nuggets from behavioral
economics into their content marketing game plan, they are basically increasing the quality or
depth of what they are giving in ord er to get a better response, more trust, or higher engagement
from the audience.

The Underpinning Theory
Behavioral economics & psychology acts as a "grand theory" because it explains why digital
marketing works on a fundamental human level and how it reshapes societal norms. It also
provides a causal explanation for how digital marketing physically and mentally alters human
behavior and, by extension, society.
