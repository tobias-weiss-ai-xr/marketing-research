Abstract
The paper presents two empirical studies focusing on the psychosocial deter -
minants of purchasing green personal care and household care products. These
products comprise natural components, free from pesticides, fertilizers, and ge -
netically modified organisms, and are packaged in an environmentally friendly
manner to minimize their overall ecological impact. Two different samples of
adult Italian consumers participated in the cross-sectional studies by completing
online questionnaires, receiving a total of 766 responses (68% women, mean age
of 37 years). A two-wave prospective survey design tested a relational model
based on the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB), with perceived environmental
and health values serving as background factors, and the temporal stability of
intentions acting as a key moderator of the relationships between intentions and
purchasing behaviours. The TPB-based processes underlying purchase intentions
and behaviours were found to be largely similar for both green personal care
and household care products. Results suggest that consumers with favourable at -
titudes toward purchasing green products for personal and home care, who feel
a sense of control over their purchasing behaviours and receive support from
relevant others, will demonstrate both strong intentions and actual purchasing
behaviours, particularly when their intentions remain stable over time. Environ -
mental and health values are significant motivators, especially the health-related
ones, and their impacts are largely mediated by TPB constructs. Significant impli -
cations for marketing green personal and household care products can be drawn
from the main findings.

Introduction
Cosmetics, personal care, and household cleaning products, which contain compo -
nents like phthalates and use single-use plastic containers, are among the most toxic
for the planet. They are also seen as one of the main sources of microplastic pollution
worldwide (Praveena et al., 2018). In the last decades, the environmental awareness
of the public has been reflected in the increasing consumption of green products, and
the rising consumer preference for a healthy lifestyle has motivated industry to align
and invest in green personal and home care products (Ghazali et al., 2017; Zollo et
al., 2021). An analysis by Sdrolia and Zarotiadis ( 2019) yielded over 50 definitions
of green products. They argue that “green is a product (tangible or intangible) that
minimizes its environmental impact (direct and indirect) during its whole life cycle”
(Sdrolia & Zarotiadis, 2019, p. 171), by saving energy and/or resources and reduc -
ing or eliminating the use of toxic substances, pollutants, and waste. Green personal
care products encompass skin, hair, and oral care, as well as deodorisers and hygiene
goods comprising natural components without pesticides, fertilizers, or genetically
modified organisms. Green home care products include dishwashing detergents, floor
cleaners, glass cleaners, laundry detergents, and toilet cleaners. Whenever possible,
green products should also be packaged in an environmentally friendly way to lessen
their direct and indirect ecological impact during their life cycle. Ecocert, the Soil
Association, ABCERT, BDIH, Cosmebio, EU Ecolabel, and ICEA are among the
leading green international certification bodies.
The global market of green personal care products is forecast to increase by USD
12.37 billion, at a Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) of 9.3% between 2024
and 2029 (Technavio Plus, 2025). In the case of household green cleaning products,
the world trade grew from USD 5.14 billion in 2023 to USD 5.49 billion in 2024. It
is expected to continue expanding at a CAGR of 7.4%, reaching USD 8.49 billion by
2030 (Research and Markets, 2024). Also in Italy, the context in which the research
reported in this paper was conducted, consumers increasingly prioritize green per -
sonal care products, reflecting a spreading awareness and concern for sustainability
and health (Statista, 2024). Further, according to a survey by R5 Living in March

The socio-psychological literature on the determinants of green purchase decisions
has focused mainly on organic foods (e.g., Scalco et al., 2017; Zhang & Dong, 2020).
Research on the antecedents of consumer intentions to purchase green personal care
products has grown in recent years in Western countries (e.g., Delistavrou et al., 2022;
Delistavrou, & Tilikidou, 2022; Yeon Kim & Chung, 2011; Zollo et al., 2021), but
especially in emerging countries, such as Malaysia (e.g., Ghazali et al., 2017; Zainudin
et al., 2022), Indonesia (Askadilla & Krisjanti, 2017; Chin et al., 2018), Pakistan (e.g.,
Ali et al., 2023; Atta et al., 2021), India (e.g., Halan et al., 2024), Taiwan (e.g., Hsu et
al., 2017), South Africa (e.g., Bevan-Dye & Synodinos, 2025; Dlamini & Mahowa,
2024; Shimul et al., 2022), Vietnam (e.g., Ngo-Thi-Ngoc et al., 2024; Nguyen et al.,
2024; Nguyen-Viet & Nguyen, 2024; Phu et al., 2024). Less attention has been paid to
intentions to purchase green home care products (e.g., Arli et al., 2018).
Almost all the studies mentioned above used the Theory of Planned Behaviour
(TPB; Ajzen, 1991) as their theoretical reference model, either in its original form
or in an extended one. According to a recent systematic review aiming to assess
the theories and the constructs involved in predicting and explaining green purchase
intention (Patiño-Toro et al., 2024), TPB is the most widely used. There are several
reasons for this spread, starting from the fact that TPB is a social cognitive model,
suitable for explaining voluntary behaviour in different contexts (Nguyen et al.,
2024). Other models, such as the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) (Davis et
al., 1989), are less general. For example, TAM is appropriate for studying behaviours
related to using a technological product or an innovation. Another model, which is
referred to in the literature, is the Value Belief-Norm (VBN) (Stern, 2000; Kaiser et
al., 2005), linking a person’s ecological worldview and environmental values with
the Norm-Activation Theory (NAT) (Schwartz, 1977). Some scholars have proposed
to integrate the whole TPB or some of its components (e.g., attitude or intention) and
VBN to predict sustainable behaviors, such as the purchase of sustainable clothing or
local and seasonal foods (e.g., Carfora et al., 2021; Pasquariello et al., 2024). Another
interesting branch of research is based on the Theory of Consumption Values (TCV)
(Sheth et al., 1991), which proposes that different aspects of perceived value can be
identified and may influence consumers’ behaviors. Recently, in the domain of green
products, some studies integrated TCV with both NAT and the Theory of Reasoned
Action (TRA; Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975), a model preceding the TPB (e.g., Chae et al.,
2024; Roh et al., 2022). In sum, all these alternative or complementary models either
refer to constructs already present in the TPB or integrate with it.
This paper presents two studies examining the determinants of purchasing behav-
iour for green personal care (Study 1) and home care (Study 2) products among Ital-
ian consumers.
Both studies assumed the TPB as the theoretical framework. TPB has its roots in
the expectancy-value model of attitude theory (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975) and in the
social cognition tradition. It is a general theory designed to predict and explain volun-
tary human behaviour in specific domains (e.g., health, consumer behaviour, environ-
mentally sustainable behaviour) (Ajzen, 1991; Hagger & Hamilton, 2025). In brief,
the TPB proposes that a given behaviour is guided by the intention to perform it. Intention,

in turn, is a function of three factors: attitude toward behaviour, subjective norm, and
perceived behavioural control (PBC). Attitude refers to people’s evaluative judgment of
behaviour and reflects the most important behavioural beliefs that executing a behaviour
will lead to a desirable or undesirable outcome. Subjective norm is a function of normative
beliefs representing the individual’s perceptions about expectations of significant others,
such as family members or friends. Finally, PBC refers to beliefs concerning access to the
means and opportunities to effectively execute the behaviour and to the ability to perform
it. PBC, since it may reflect actual control, can predict specific behaviour directly.
TPB focuses on deliberate, goal-oriented actions guided by conscious self-regulatory
processes. However, the TPB does not claim that human behaviour is “rational.” Atti-
tudes, subjective norms, and PBC reflect the information people have in relation to the
performance of a given behaviour, but this information may be inaccurate and incomplete,
based on irrational premises, and biased by self-serving motives and emotions. Impor-
tantly, even if inaccurate, biased, or irrational, the beliefs produce attitudes, intentions,
and behaviours consistent with these beliefs. It is in this sense that behaviour is said to
be reasoned or planned (Ajzen, 2011). Secondly, some argued that TPB overlooks auto-
matic, unconscious processes influencing behaviour. However, as we pointed out earlier,
the TPB can incorporate additional factors like past behaviour or habits (Hagger & Ham-
ilton, 2025; Hagger, 2016). Hagger et al. (2018) found in a meta-analysis that past behav-
iour predicts health behaviour both directly and indirectly via TPB constructs. The direct
effects suggest unconscious influences such as implicit cognitions or behavioural scripts.
This aligns with dual-process theories, which propose that behaviour results from both
deliberate reasoning and automatic, non-conscious processes (Deutsch et al., 2017).
TPB assumes that its constructs (attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioural
control) should account for the relationships between variables that represent socio-
structural, contextual, and dispositional factors, intentions and behaviours. These vari-
ables, which Ajzen called background factors (Ajzen, 2011, 2020), represent salient
sources of information that individuals account for when forming their beliefs con -
cerning their future behavioural performance. For example, socio-structural variables
related to barriers to performing the behaviour (such as lack of facilities, low access to
certain kinds of products, low financial resources) should be reflected in individuals’
evaluations of their capacity to perform behaviour, that is, their perceived behavioural
control. The theory constructs should mediate the associations between background fac-
tors, intention and behaviour. In this way, TPB constructs can explain the mechanisms
underlying the observed associations between socio-structural and intrapersonal factors
and behaviour (Hagger & Hamilton, 2025).
In our two studies, we considered, as background factors, two consumers’ per -
ceived values of green products concerning health and the environment. Following
previous studies (e.g., Ghazali et al., 2017; Halan et al., 2024), we hypothesized them
to be related to attitudes toward the purchase of these products.
The studies adopted a prospective design with data collected in two waves (Time
1 and Time 2, one month later). Further, to deepen the understanding of the deter -
minants of purchase behaviour and improve the TPB’s predictive power, the role
of the temporal stability of intentions (Conner & Norman, 2022) in moderating the
relationship between intention and future purchase behaviour was also examined.

Theoretical Framework and Hypotheses
Perceived Value of Green Products
Zeithaml (1988) defined perceived value as an overall assessment of the utility of
a product/service based on the perception of what is received (benefits) and what
is given (costs). It is commonly termed as the ratio between quality and price (e.g.,
Sweeney & Soutar, 2001). Perceived values are important criteria that guide con -
sumers’ choices (Butler et al., 2016), and they are supposed to opt for a particular
product with a higher perceived value (Yadav & Pathak, 2017). However, consumers’
purchasing and consumption experiences involve various dimensions of value, not
just consideration of quality and price. For example, Sweeney and Soutar (2001) sug-
gested that consumers also take into account the utility derived from affective states
that a product generates (emotional value) or the perception of the product’s ability to
enhance social self-concept (social value), and proposed four distinct dimensions of
perceived value of a product (emotional, social, quality/performance, and price/value
for money). Consumer perceived values depend on the various contexts that deter -
mine the distinctive properties of different products (e.g., durable goods, consumer
goods, services), and it has been suggested that consumers perception of values in
any given context should be measured through a specific tool developed to assess
perceived values in that context. For example, Sangroya and Nayak (2017) proposed
a multidimensional scale to assess green perceived values of consumers, specifically
in the context of green energy. Roh et al. ( 2022) and Chae et al. ( 2024) adapted this
scale to the consumption of organic food. In these scales, perceived values were seg-
mented into functional, social, emotional, conditional, and epistemic components,
each one reflecting the net benefit perceived by consumers from green energy or
organic food, matching their environmental and sustainable objectives.
In the domain of personal care products, Ghazali et al. (2017) proposed five dimen-
sions of consumers’ perceived values (i.e., environment, health, hedonism, safety,

On the one hand, perceived environmental value is related to concerns about
the negative impact of chemicals present in conventional products for personal and
home care on the environment; on the other, to the benefits of green products deemed
environmentally friendly. Previous studies have highlighted that consumers consider
environmental benefits when purchasing eco-friendly products (e.g., Yeon Kim &
Chung, 2011), and environmental perceived value was reported to have a positive
relationship with consumer green purchase intention (Yadav & Pathak, 2017). More-
over, Ghazali et al. ( 2017) and Halan et al. ( 2024) found that perceived environ -
mental value was a significant antecedent of attitudes toward the purchase of green
personal care products. Capitalizing on this evidence, we proposed that:
H1 Consumer perception of the environmental value of green personal and house -
hold care products will be positively associated with attitude toward their purchase.
Perceived health value involves protecting and enhancing health, and past research
has shown that health benefits are among organic consumption’s predominant
motives. For example, many consumers think organic foods are healthier than con -
ventional products because they are free from pesticides and other chemical residues
(e.g., Xie et al., 2015). It was also found that perceived health benefits significantly
influence attitudes toward purchasing organic cotton apparel (Han & Chung, 2014).
Ghazali et al. (2017) and Halan et al. (2024) found that health value was a significant
antecedent of attitude toward the purchase of green personal care products. Hence, it
is likely that the perceived health value of green personal or home care products will
have similar relations with attitudes toward the purchase of these products, and we
proposed that:
H2 Consumer perception of the health value of green personal and household care
products will have a significant positive relation with attitude toward purchasing
these products.

Regarding the relationships between perceived environmental and health values and
the other constructs of the TPB, namely subjective norm and PBC, we adopted an

previous studies (e.g., Canova et al., 2023), we may expect that individuals who

Theory of Planned Behaviour and Green Products
The TPB has been applied to explain a variety of green behaviours, including organic
food (e.g., Canova et al., 2020; Scalco et al., 2017), energy saving (e.g., Canova &
Manganelli, 2020), sustainable clothing (e.g., Carfora et al., 2021), purchase of eco-
labelled products (e.g., Gidaković et al., 2024), and many other pro-environmental
behaviours (e.g., de Leeuw et al., 2015; Yuriev et al., 2020). Concerning the specific
product categories of green personal/home care products, TPB has provided evidence
of its ability to predict intentions to purchase green cosmetics (Ali et al., 2022; Aska-
dilla & Krisjanti, 2017; Ngo-Thi-Ngoc et al., 2024; Nguyen et al., 2024; Phu et al.,
2024; Shimul et al., 2022), green beauty products (Bevan-Dye & Synodinos, 2025),
green skin care products (Chin et al., 2018; Hsu et al., 2017), green body lotions
and shampoos (Yeon Kim & Chung, 2011), green personal care products in general
(Delistavrou et al., 2022; Ghazali et al., 2017; Halan et al., 2024; Zainudin et al.,
2022), environmentally-friendly household care products (Arli et al., 2018). Attitude
was found to be a predictor of intentions in all those studies. Subjective norm was
associated with intention in most cases (e.g., Askadilla & Krisjanti, 2017; Delistavrou
et al., 2022; Halan et al., 2024; Hsu et al., 2017; Nguyen et al., 2024; Ngo-Thi-Ngoc
et al., 2024; Shimul et al., 2022; Yeon Kim & Chung, 2011; Zainudin et al., 2022),
while Ghazali et al. (2017) found no significant relation between subjective norm and
intention. PBC was related to intentions in most studies (Askadilla & Krisjanti, 2017;
Delistavrou et al., 2022; Ghazali et al., 2017; Halan et al., 2024; Hsu et al., 2017;
Yeon Kim & Chung, 2011; Zainudin et al., 2022) but was linearly independent in
the studies by Shimul et al. (2022) and Nguyen et al. (2024). A limitation of all these
studies could be that they considered purchasing intention as the outcome variable of
the hypothesised models. Furthermore, none of them employed a prospective design
with two-wave data collection, nor considered purchase behaviour in the future. As
mentioned above, the present two-study paper aimed to overcome these limitations.

Intention Temporal Stability
Some meta-analyses considering studies that applied TPB in pro-environmental
behaviours (e.g., Yurev et al., 2020) or health-related behaviours (e.g., Hagger et al.,
2018) reported that the explained variance in intentions was higher (around 50%)
than that in behaviours (around 30%). This disjunction between intention and behav-
iour has been named the intention–behaviour gap. Several methodological factors
can be associated with either narrowing or widening the intention–behaviour gap. For
example, more reliable measures of intention and behaviour or a focus on their rela -
tionships disattenuating for measurement error, may be associated with narrowing
the gap (Conner & Norman, 2022). However, in the extant literature, a key approach
to understanding this gap has been to focus on its potential moderators, i.e., on factors
associated with changes in the magnitude of the intention-behaviour relationship.
Moderators help identify the conditions under which strong vs. weak relationships
might be expected. Some moderators have been identified in various studies and dis-
cussed in several reviews (e.g., Rhodes et al., 2022; Sheeran & Webb, 2016), provid-
ing useful insights. Intention temporal stability has received much attention.
Ajzen (1996, p. 389) argues that “to obtain accurate prediction of behaviour, inten-
tions (and PBC) must remain reasonably stable over time until the behaviour is per -
formed”. Intentions measured before behaviour performance may change because
of new information or unforeseen obstacles, resulting in reduced predictive power.
Temporal stability of intention has been indicated as an important mechanism through
which intention better predicts behaviour, and the moderating role of temporal sta -
bility has been addressed in several studies. (e.g., Conner & Norman, 2022; Conner
et al., 2023; Gibson et al., 2021). Based on theoretical considerations and empirical
proof, we expected that:
H7 The relationship between intention and future purchase of green personal/home
care products will be stronger when intention temporal stability is high vs. low.

green purchase and consumption in various ways. First, one of them considers a
category of green products (home care products) whose purchase intentions and

toward achieving sustainable development goals (United Nations, 2024).
Method
Procedure and Participants
For both Study 1 and Study 2, two waves of data collection were organised (Time
1 and Time 2), with a lag interval of one month. Structured questionnaires were
designed and distributed via the Qualtrics platform between October and December
2023. They were identical except for the target purchase behaviour: green products
for personal care in Study 1 and green products for home care in Study 2. At Time 1,
participants answered constructs’ measures of the extended TPB model (health and
environmental perceived values, attitude, subjective norm, PBC, and intention) along
with socio-demographic variables. One month later, at Time 2, the behavioural inten-
tion was collected again with self-reported behavioural measures.
About 100 master’s students from the School of Psychology at Padua University
were engaged in data collection as part of their internship or research experience
assignments. They were presented with the general aim of the research project and
asked to involve ten of their friends, relatives, or acquaintances, inviting five of them
to connect to the online questionnaire designed for Study 1 and the other five to
that related to Study 2. The same person could not participate in both studies. To be
eligible for participation, people were required to be of legal age (i.e., equal to or
greater than 18 years old). Along with the questionnaire link, participants received
an instruction letter in which they were made familiar with the aim of the study, the
estimated duration of the task (about 15 min), and the possibility of withholding
their consent to participate at any time. They were also assured that participation was
voluntary and that all answers would remain confidential and treated as a group. The

instruction letter also stated that the research had two phases, and they would receive
another very short questionnaire a month later, which would have taken less than
5 min to complete. The questionnaires were paired thanks to self-generated alphanu-

first page of the questionnaire.
In the questionnaires, the target behaviour was made explicit as “the purchase
of green products for personal/home care.” Furthermore, the following definition of
green products was proposed: “Green products for personal/home care are defined as
those products whose components mostly come from organic farming, which does
not use synthetic fertilisers and pesticides. Petroleum derivatives (e.g., paraffin, form-
aldehyde, and dyes of synthetic origin), sewage sludge, or ionising radiation must not
be used in the production of these goods. The packaging of these products bears, for
example, the “EU Ecolabel” certification label.” A list of products was also presented
to help participants better understand the behaviour investigated. As an example, in
Study 1: shampoo, soap, and deodorants; in Study 2: products for laundry by hand
or with the washing machine, detergents for cleaning the house, and detergents for
cleaning dishes.
In Study 1, 543 people accessed the questionnaire link, but complete usable data
was obtained from 475 individuals (response rate: 87.5%). Among them, 374 also
filled in the second questionnaire (final response rate: 68.9%). In Study 2, 570 poten-
tial participants got into the questionnaire; among them, 510 provided usable data
(response rate: 89.5%), and 392 also completed the second one (final response rate:
68.8%). Only those who completed both questionnaires constituted the final sample
for data analysis.
Attrition analyses were conducted via multivariate analysis of variance
(MANOV A), chi-square test, and t-test comparing the participants included in the
final samples and the dropouts ( n = 101 in Study 1 and n = 118 in Study 2) who
skipped the second questionnaire. Regarding psychosocial variables (i.e., perceived
health and environmental values, attitude, subjective norm, PBC and intention) mul-
tivariate main effects were not significant (personal care: F6,468 = 1.27; p =.27; home
care: F6,503 = 0.57, p =.76). For the socio-demographic variables (age, gender, educa-
tion level, employment condition, geographical region of residence, parental status,
marital status, and family income), t-tests and chi-square tests showed no significant
difference (all ps ≥ 0.06) 1. This suggests that our final groups of respondents were
reasonably representative of the initial ones.

We used four items introduced by the statement “Green products for personal/house-

Perceived Health Value
This was assessed with three items: “Green personal/home care products:” (a)

Attitude Toward Behaviour
Participants were presented with the statement: “To purchase green personal/home

undesirable–desirable, useless–useful, harmful–beneficial). The response scales were

should buy green personal/home care green products in the next month;” and “Most

and hygiene products in the next month.” The response scale ranged from 1 (strongly

Intention stability between Time 1 and Time 2 was assessed as 4 minus the mean

Data Analysis
Analyses were performed using MPLUS 8.5, SPSS 28, and the PROCESS macro
(Hayes, 2022). All responses to the items measuring the variables included in the
model were mandatory; thus, there were no missing values. Preliminary, to check
common method variance (CMV) bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003), Harman’s single-fac-
tor test was conducted using exploratory factor analysis. CMV is present if a single
factor emerges or if a single factor explains most of the covariance among the mea -
sures. For the adequacy of the measurement models, we conducted confirmatory fac-
tor analyses (CFA) using the robust maximum likelihood method (MLR) applied to
covariance matrices. The measurement models of the two studies included six latent

Discussion
This research, adopting a two-wave prospective survey design, tested an extended
TPB model, with perceived environmental and health values as background factors
and intention temporal stability as a moderator of the relationships between inten -
tions and future purchasing behaviours of personal and household care products, of
which the latter is still underinvestigated in the literature. The TPB-based processes
were similar for both target behaviours, although some differences emerged, which
will be discussed below.
In both studies, perceived environmental and health values explained about a third
of attitude variance, and the hypotheses concerning the relations between these values
and attitude (H1 and H2) were supported, in line with previous research (e.g., Ghazali
et al., 2017; Halan et al., 2024). Perceived health value showed the strongest associa-
tion with attitude. Moreover, it showed a direct link to intention in Study 1, an unex-
pected association with PBC in Study 1, and with subjective norm in Study 2. The
relationship with PBC indicates that the higher the health value attributed to green
personal care products, the more our participants perceive the purchasing behaviour
as under their volitional control, and it is likely to assume that they are motivated to
overcome possible obstacles to get them. The association between perceived health
value and subjective norm tells us that the higher the health value attributed to green
home care products, the more our participants relied on the opinions of their impor -
tant referents, who likely share the same values. That is, those who perceived green

Ghazali et al. ( 2017) suggested that the choice of green products for personal care
can be driven more by personal factors (such as skin types of allergic reactions or
preference for various product functions) rather than by peers or family members.
PBC was a direct predictor of future purchasing behaviour only in the case of
household care products (supporting H6b), indicating that people did not perceive
this purchase behaviour under their full volitional control (Ajzen, 1991), and this can
be explained by the difficulty that people may have in finding and recognizing these
products in retail locations. As we pointed out earlier, the market for these products,
although growing, still has lower volumes than that for green personal care products.
Intentions predicted future behaviour in both studies, confirming the assumptions
of the TPB (H3). Moreover, as hypothesized and consistent with previous studies
on the intention-behaviour gap, more stable intentions were stronger predictors of
future purchasing behaviour (H7). Previous studies that investigated this effect have
covered especially health behaviours, from physical activity (e.g., Conner & Godin,
2007; Conner et al., 2016) to COVID-19 protection behaviours (e.g., Conner et al.,
2023; Gibson et al., 2021). To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to exam-
ine the moderating effect of the temporal stability of intention within domain pur -
chase behaviour.
According to Ajzen (2020), all the predictive power of background factors, such
as perceived values, on intentions should be mediated by the TPB constructs. In other
words, perceived values should have no direct links to intentions and behaviours. Our
findings indicate that the indirect effects of perceived environmental value on inten -
tions and behaviours were mediated by attitude and intention, while perceived health
value demonstrated a direct association with the intention to buy green products for
personal care; thus, the mediation via attitude was partial. The indirect effects of per-
ceived health value on intentions and behaviours involve attitude (in both studies),
PBC (in Study 1), subjective norm (in Study 2), and intention itself (in both studies).
These findings show that both perceived environmental and health values are impor-
tant motivators for consumer green choices and that their effects are mediated by

Though the analysis of sociodemographic variables was not among the purposes
of this research, having considered gender and age as control variables in the regres-
sion analyses revealed some interesting insights about their associations with the
purchase of green products. For personal care products, women more than men
expressed a positive attitude and a stronger intention to buy them. For home care

higher intention to buy them. These last results can be attributed to the greater famil-
iarity that women and older people have with household care, consistent with gender
roles and social expectations. The gender differences aligned with extant literature
about differences between men and women in environmental behaviours. A recent
review by Zhao et al. ( 2021) showed that women declared a more positive green
consumption intention and purchased green products more frequently.
In conclusion, the results of this research suggest that consumers with favourable
attitudes toward the purchase of green products for personal and home care, who
have a sense of control over their purchase behaviours, and whose relevant others
are perceived to be agreeable with these behaviours, will exhibit purchase intentions.
Intentions will have a higher chance of being translated into concrete purchasing
behaviours when the intentions themselves are stable over time. Attitudes and other
TPB constructs, in turn, convey the health and environmental values attributed to the
products considered.
Limitations and Future Research
Despite its strengths (a two-wave prospective design, adherence to the compatibility
principle of measures, and a comparison between different types of green products
rarely investigated), our research, has some limitations that offer opportunities for
future research (Clarke et al., 2024). First of all, it focused on the role of inten -
tion stability and on perceived values as possible antecedents of attitude within the
TPB framework. Consequently, our approach was mainly confirmatory, since we did
not consider models alternative to the TPB. Although this is common in psychol -
ogy (Sanbonmatsu et al., 2015), it is not ideal and should be overcome by more
complex research designs testing simultaneously alternative models. Second, the two
interviewed convenience samples primarily consisted of young adult female con -
sumers with medium-high education and family income levels, living in a culturally
homogeneous and specific geographical Italian area. Future studies should explore
representative samples of Italian consumers to enhance the generalizability of results
and further investigate the impact of sociodemographic variables on green products
purchasing decisions. Third, although the study employed a prospective measure of
behaviour, its correlational design only allowed for inferring relationship directions
based on the reference theory. Longitudinal and experimental designs are necessary
to confirm the direction of causal relationships. Fourth, the self-reported nature of
all variable measurements may be subject to social desirability or approval biases.
Additionally, the contemporaneous collection of the second intention measure and
behaviour at Time 2 may have led to an overestimation of intention stability’s moder-

ating effect on the intention-behaviour relationship. However, previous studies (Con-
ner & Godin, 2007; Norman et al., 2022) have shown this moderating effect even
when behaviour was assessed later than the second intention measure. Fifth, while
we found evidence supporting the hypotheses, the analyses testing them were not
constrained by a detailed preregistered analysis plan. Since this increases the chances
that these findings are false positives, they should be considered tentative unless and
until stronger evidence emerges. Sixth, our key findings were supported by results

is still scope for testing additional variables influencing intentions and behaviours,
such as product knowledge (e.g., Nguyen-Viet & Nguyen, 2024), willingness to pay
higher prices (e.g., Hsu et al., 2017), trust (e.g., Canova et al., 2020; Ngo-Thi-Ngoc et
al., 2024), and scepticism about product quality and certifications (e.g., Nguyen-Viet
& Nguyen, 2024).
Practical Implications
Our findings offer valuable insights into the marketing of green personal and home
care products. Attitude emerged as the primary determinant of purchase intention,
suggesting to producers and retailers that fostering positive attitudes toward these
products could increase consumers’ purchase intentions and behaviours. To generate
favourable attitudes, marketing strategies should highlight and inform consumers of
both environmental and health benefits. Concerning perceived environmental value,
marketing campaigns can also create awareness of environmental issues, educating
consumers on how green products mitigate negative environmental impacts, such as
reducing toxic chemicals, plastic pollution, and non-biodegradable packaging, as well
as avoiding animal testing, can increase perceived environmental value (Bevan-Dye
& Synodinos, 2025). Simultaneously, emphasizing health benefits by showcasing
products that meet toxicity, hypoallergenic, and dermatological standards certified by
accredited laboratories can enhance perceived health value.
Our results also indicate that family, relatives, and friends can significantly influ -
ence intentions to purchase green products. Marketing campaigns could target these
social networks to strengthen sustainable buying practices. Furthermore, given that
perceived behavioural control significantly influences intentions and behaviour, it is
crucial to establish optimal sales conditions that enhance the accessibility and afford-
ability of green products. Policymakers and retailers should consider strategic prod -
uct placement within stores, attractive packaging, competitive pricing, and increasing
overall product availability. Strengthening intention stability is a key factor to reduce
the intention-behaviour gap, as our research shows that stable intentions are more
likely to translate into behaviour. However, studies are needed to identify effective
methods for enhancing intention stability (Conner et al., 2023). Potential strategies
might include encouraging individuals to prioritize specific intentions over compet -
ing ones and protecting intentions from conflicting goals (Conner et al., 2006; Conner

& Godin, 2007). These approaches could help reduce the intention-behaviour gap,
although more research is required in this area (Conner et al., 2023). By implement-
ing these multifaceted strategies, producers, retailers, and policymakers can effec -
tively promote the adoption of green personal and home care products, contributing
to more sustainable consumer behaviours. The key lies in creating a positive attitude,
leveraging social influence, enhancing perceived control, and fostering stable inten -
tions among consumers.
Conclusion
The present research has proved the validity of TPB in understanding the consumers’
intentions and purchasing behaviours of green personal/home care products in the
Italian context. Attitudes toward behaviour emerged as the most significant deter -
minant of intentions, followed by PBC and subjective norm. The findings have also
supported the inclusion of perceived environmental and health values as background
factors, and the testing of their indirect effects, mediated by TPB constructs, on inten-
tions and behaviours. Overall, perceived health values showed a more important role
than environmental ones in the process of intention formation and purchasing deci -
sions. Finally, the stability of intention strengthened the relationship between inten -
tions and behaviours. Industry practitioners and retailers should provide clear and
genuine information about the benefits of their green products and ensure the authen-
ticity of their organic product claims regarding health and environmental friendliness.
They should also build consumer loyalty toward the consumption of these products
through, for example, increasing availability and keeping fair prices.
