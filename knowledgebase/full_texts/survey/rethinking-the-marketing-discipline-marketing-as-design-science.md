Abstract
Persistent challenges in the academic marketing field hinder the full realization of its value for both research and prac ‑
tice. Addressing these issues requires broadening the methodological repertoire and reconsidering the field’s underlying
purpose. We argue that design science, with its focus on creating and evaluating solutions to real‑world problems, offers
a valuable complementary paradigm that enriches and extends the existing research traditions in marketing. Viewing
marketing through a design science lens shifts the emphasis from analyzing how things currently are to systematically
developing how they should be. Providing a scientific answer to this normative question requires an abductive research
process grounded in a pragmatic epistemology and supported by a creation‑oriented methodology in which researchers
iteratively design, create, and test marketing interventions. Building on this foundation, we offer a conceptual reframing
of marketing as a design‑oriented discipline aimed at enabling the scientific development of purposeful interventions.
The manuscript further advances a theoretical contribution by outlining the structure, ingredients, and “recipe” of design
theory in marketing. We also discuss the key challenges that arise when positioning marketing as a design science and
what adopting such a perspective requires from the field. Methodologically, we introduce practical guidance to increase
the feasibility and publishability of marketing as a design science. By embracing a design‑oriented approach, marketing
scholars can move beyond diagnosing practitioner problems and shift from offering advice to creating actionable solutions
that genuinely advance the field.
Keywords Design science · Marketing paradigm · Abductive approach · Indigenous theory of marketing design

The academic marketing landscape is both challenging
and complex, currently characterized by several persis ‑
tent problems. To be specific, marketing science faces four
interrelated problems: (1) losses of domain expertise, (2)
detachment from real‑world phenomena, (3) methodologi ‑
cal monocultures, and (4) detachment from the discipline’s

required. Some scholars argue that the marketing discipline
requires revisions to its underlying theories, models, and
frameworks (Kalaignanam et al., 2021; Varadarajan et al.,
2022). Other scholars claim that the marketing discipline
faces “an urgent need for a rethinking of its fundamental
purpose” (Webster & Lusch, 2013, p. 389). We concur with
Zeithaml et al.’s ( 2020, p. 32) prescription that revisions
are probably not enough, because “the marketing discipline
is at a crossroads. Marketing scholars can continue on the
well‑worn road of largely testing or extending theories by
borrowing from allied disciplines, or we can challenge our‑
selves to make a significant difference in the lives of manag‑
ers, public policy officials, and/or consumers.”
We thus contend that revisions alone are insufficient;
both purpose and methodology must be expanded to allow
for a distinctly different approach alongside established
routes. Specifically, we argue that the scientific marketing
landscape can and should be more diverse, which would
improve marketing science (Tellis et al., 1999) and cre ‑
ate room for different types of contributions. We therefore
advocate for a comprehensive exploration of marketing as a
design science, alongside other methodological approaches.
Design science entails the design and validation of solution
proposals to real‑world problems (Wieringa, 2009; Hevner
et al., 2004). Its goal is “to consider a situation, imagine a
better situation, and act to create that improved situation”
(Dorst, 2015, p. vii). This resonates with the broader ambi ‑
tion within marketing science to generate knowledge that
contributes to better practice, in line with the wider expecta‑
tion that science should not only advance understanding but
also generate solutions for everyday life (UNESCO, 2024).
Within a design science approach, the challenges encoun‑
tered in marketing practice serve as the point of departure.
From that starting point, multiple solutions are created,
explored and tested, resulting in academic knowledge that
includes practical guidance on what works and what does
not. This approach thus aligns with the diagnosis of various
marketing scholars that marketing academics should rely on
managerial sensibility to shape research questions and that
research motivations should reflect a managerial perspective
(Gulati, 2007; Kohli & Haenlein, 2021). Vargo (2022), for
instance, emphasizes that the essence of marketing is inher‑
ently normative, highlighting the need for scholars to engage
with what should be done in the market rather than merely
observing what is. Similarly, Kotler (2020) characterizes mar‑
keters as “value engineers,” underscoring the problem‑solv‑
ing capacity that marketing should possess. Consequently, we
note calls for the design of impactful interventions that can
make a significant difference in the lives of practitioners and
consumers (Madan et al., 2023; Zeithaml et al., 2020; Agar‑
wal et al., 2025), for applications of design science research
in marketing (Halstrick, 2023), for the application of more
abductive reasoning and creativity by marketing scholars
(Arens, 2022), and for moving marketing perspectives from
a reactive or administrative mode to a market‑driving and
entrepreneurial mode (Miles et al., 2011). We propose that
by adopting design science, marketing scholars can acquire
new, relevant knowledge and frameworks, which would
tackle the loss of domain expertise (problem 1; Fehrer, 2020).
Because design research starts with actual field problems, it
can address detachment from real‑world phenomena (prob‑
lem 2). Embracing design science will require the adoption of
different methodologies and creative solution‑finding tactics,
which can be achieved through cross‑fertilization of research
methods (problem 3). Finally, the core purpose of design sci‑
ence is practical relevance, so its use can refine the purpose of
the marketing discipline (problem 4). Marketing as a design
science could therefore contribute to finding solutions to the
problems outlined by Fehrer (2020).
To create room for a different approach that complements
existing ones, it is essential that marketing journals embrace
a broader scope of approaches and methodologies. This
would create more room for creative, imagination‑based
techniques that allow researchers to explore how typical
marketing situations could be, rather than how they cur ‑
rently are. To facilitate this pathway, we address four ques‑
tions in this manuscript: (1) What does a design approach
offer that other research approaches do not, (2) How does
marketing as design science influence the purpose, struc ‑
ture, and validation criteria of research, (3) What does mar‑
keting as design science imply for theory generation, and (4)
What are the challenges of marketing as design science? In
the following sections, we address these questions sequen ‑
tially. The value of this manuscript stems from its combined
conceptual, theoretical, and methodological contributions.
At the conceptual level, we present an alternative scientific
view of marketing science, focused on creating better mar ‑
keting practices. Reframing marketing as a design‑oriented
discipline means facilitating and enabling the scientific
design of purposeful interventions in order to increase the
practical relevance of marketing science. This is accompa ‑
nied by certain obstacles and mismatches between market ‑
ing science and design science, which are also discussed in
this manuscript. At the theoretical level, we explain what a
design theory in marketing science looks like and identify
the ingredients and the “recipe” of such a theory, by intro ‑
ducing a framework for cultivating indigenous theories of
marketing design. We identify five foundational premises of
marketing as design science. At the methodological level,
we offer concrete guidance, such as a design‑oriented pub ‑
lication scheme and a Design Science Reporting checklist,
with which we aim to increase the feasibility and publish ‑
ability of marketing as a design science. Together these con‑
tributions facilitate marketing as design science.

The unique value of a design approach
This section addresses our first research question: What
does a design‑oriented approach provide that alternative
approaches cannot? What makes a design approach unique
is the combination of (1) adopting a pragmatic episte ‑
mology, focusing on the design of solutions, (2) using
abductive reasoning instead of inductive and deductive
reasoning, and (3) the use of alternative research tech ‑
niques, such as those oriented toward creative thinking,
which enable different ways of seeing and acting, and con ‑
sequently lead to different kinds of solutions. Below, we
discuss these three elements.
Design science draws on a pragmatic epistemology
(Goldkuhl, 2011; Brendel et al., 2021; Hevner et al., 2004).
Pragmatism is a philosophical perspective that consid ‑
ers practical consequences and real‑world effects central
to both meaning and truth (Hevner, 2007). In pragmatism,
something is valuable because it works in practice or helps
achieve a goal. Accordingly, pragmatism evaluates ideas
in terms of their practical consequences and their util ‑
ity in addressing real‑world problems. From a pragmatic
perspective, knowledge about what is possible (i.e., pro ‑
spective knowledge rooted in possibilism) is inherently
connected to normative considerations of what is desirable
(Goldkuhl, 2011). In design science, the value of research
is not only determined by practical usefulness, but lies in
the combination of methodological rigor and practical rel ‑
evance (Hevner, 2007). The knowledge that results from
this interplay is directly linked to improvement and action
(Goldkuhl, 2011). Design science is therefore always ori ‑
ented toward creating solutions, which is why it both tack ‑
les problems for stakeholders, produces a unique type of
knowledge, and contributes to closing the academic–practi‑
tioner gap (Stange et al., 2022). These components of added
value from design science arise because understanding a
problem is essentially different from knowing how to solve a
problem. Whereas understanding a problem equips scholars
to inform practice, knowledge of how to solve that prob ‑
lem arises through the systematic creation and evaluation
of alternative solutions (Sutton et al., 2021). Both informing
and creating are essential dimensions of scholarly contri ‑
bution, yet neither can be assumed to follow automatically
from the other. Solutions must be studied in their own right,
not just as results obtained from investigating phenomena.
The process of studying solutions to problems is closely
linked to abductive reasoning. Abduction is a creative form
of inference through which new theories are generated
(Timmermans & Tavory, 2012). In fact, abductive reason ‑
ing is seen as the “lifeblood” of the design approach (Dong
et al., 2015). With abductive reasoning, we know the value
we aim to create for others, but we do not yet know what
to create or how to achieve it (Dorst, 2011). Consequently,
the outcome of abductive reasoning is the development of
an artifact (the ‘what’) along with its underlying working
principles (the ‘how’). In abductive reasoning, the design
activity thus begins with hypothesized desired outcomes
and proceeds by studying forms and structures to real ‑
ize them (Cramer ‑Petersen et al., 2019). Where abductive
reasoning truly forms the central heart of the design disci ‑
pline, we see an advocacy for a more abductive approach
emerging in other disciplines, including the social, busi ‑
ness, and management sciences (Arens, 2022; Timmermans
& Tavory, 2012; Kovács & Spens, 2005; Dubois & Gadde,
2002; Sætre & Van de Ven, 2021).
As the third pillar, design‑based research draws on tech ‑
niques from established research paradigms (The Design‑
Based Research Collective, 2003), but extends this repertoire
with creation‑oriented methods, which are solution‑focused,
constructive research methods that involve iteratively
designing, creating, and testing interventions to generate
actionable solutions. Designing and constructing effective
solutions for complex problems often requires techniques
grounded in creative thinking (Cross, 2011; Kolko, 2010;
Henseler et al., 2023). In fact, creativity “gives design its
special qualities” (Nelson & Stolterman, 2012, p. 4). These
creative techniques enable the researcher to reframe the
problem and, as a result, arrive at solutions from a different
perspective (Dorst, 2010). Additionally, creative techniques
help stretch the bounds of possibility and move beyond
established and imaginable paths. In the end, “design is
about bringing things into the world that have not existed
before; it is about creating the not-yet-existing” (Nelson &
Stolterman, 2012, p. 127). The creation of what does not yet
exist is stimulated when researchers employ techniques that
activate imagination, support thinking beyond established
frames, and enable the generation of genuinely novel alter ‑
natives; precisely the essence of creative techniques. Cre ‑
ative techniques are therefore not employed because they
are enjoyable or simply associated with design, but because
they are epistemologically necessary (Cross, 2006).
By integrating pragmatism, abductive reasoning, and cre‑
ation‑oriented methods, researchers move beyond generat ‑
ing insights and recommendations from research to creating
novel solutions through research, by employing a process
of abductive experimentation (Kistruck & Slade Shantz,
2022). Design science is an approach that results in action-
able artifacts, where artifacts are seen as enablers (Galle,
2018). For example, scholars who approach marketing
from a design science perspective will not study the drivers
and barriers to customer loyalty, but will instead design an
intervention that stimulates it. Or, a design‑oriented scholar
will not investigate the factors influencing green consumer
behavior, but will design a retail environment that enables

design, yet it does not imply that one can directly shape such
behavior, given the gap between attitudes and actions (John‑

to realize a desired outcome constitutes a research process
in its own right, and it is precisely this process that a design‑
oriented approach defines. This also directly illustrates the
complementarity of approaches: while one approach can
identify the drivers and barriers, that knowledge can then be
used by a design approach to develop various solutions and
investigate which solution is most effective. It is therefore
essential to consider the potential synergies that arise from
combining a design‑oriented approach with other method ‑
ological traditions (Sutton et al., 2021), as such integration
can yield a more comprehensive and generative foundation
for advancing marketing science.
By embracing design science, researchers not only extend
their methodological repertoire but also adopt a fundamen ‑
tally different paradigm. For the purposes of this discussion,
the term paradigm refers to the researcher’s perspective,
encompassing how research is designed, how meaning is
attributed to data, and how conclusions are interpreted from
that data (Panakaduwa et al., 2025). The main point here is
that the study of solutions requires an alternative scientific
approach, constituting a different paradigm from that used
in the study of existing phenomena (Baskerville et al., 2009;
Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Iivari, 2007; Maedche et al., 2019;
V om Brocke et al., 2020). As a paradigm, design science has
been distinguished by several scholars as clearly different
from another paradigm that dominates much of the research:
the behavioral paradigm (Hevner et al., 2004; Sutton et al.,
2021; Goldkuhl, 2011; Halstrick, 2023). Since paradigms
are often best understood by clarifying what they are not or
how they differ from other approaches, the key differences
between these two paradigms are summarized in Table 1.
These differences should not be read as a contrast between
opposing views, but rather as complementary orientations. It
is particularly important to emphasize the difference in pur‑
pose. Within a behavioral paradigm, the primary aim lies in
developing understanding. This understanding can certainly
inform practice and support organizations in formulating
their own solutions, yet without making the investigation of
those solutions its core objective. Design science explicitly
centers its purpose on the systematic creation and examina‑
tion of alternative solution pathways. In the following sec ‑
tion, we will explore this in more depth.
To highlight the complementarity of these two para ‑
digms, we illustrate this with a vignette on green consumer
behavior. A substantial stream of behavioral research has
documented that consumers who report positive attitudes
toward sustainability nonetheless often fail to purchase
sustainable products, a discrepancy known as the attitude–
behavior gap (Johnstone & Tan, 2015). A design‑oriented
scholar takes this behavioral knowledge as an inspirational
foundation and uses the knowledge on barriers to green
consumption to design an artifact: for instance, by design ‑
ing a new food storage system in the kitchen to facilitate
sustainable food‑consumption behavior (Bhamra et al.,
2011) or by designing different signs in a grocery store
which direct consumers toward ecological instead of ‘nor ‑
mal’ products (Kristensson et al., 2017). Where behavioral
research answers the question of why the attitude–behavior
gap exists, design research answers a different question:
How can that gap be closed? Whereas the design scientist
actively designs an intervention, behavioral science pro ‑
vides insights into why that intervention might work, or,
in other words, the underlying mechanisms through which
it works. To illustrate this, Demarque et al.(2015) designed
descriptive norm stimuli that were shown to increase the
purchase of eco‑products in an online French grocery store,
drawing on descriptive norm and nudging theory (Thaler
& Sunstein, 2008; Cialdini & Trost, 1998) as the under ‑
lying working mechanisms. We return to this same green
consumer behavior example throughout this manuscript to
make the structure, ingredients, and recipe of marketing
design theory concrete.
Although paradigms are complementary, they are, of
course, also distinct. Conducting academic research from a
different paradigm affects not only the research design but
also the perception and execution of the study. According
to Kuhn ( 1970), a paradigm does not reflect any specific
topic or research interest, but rather the behavior of the
researcher. It is not about what scholars are studying but
rather why and how they study it. As Kuhn states: “a theory
of matter is not the sort of topic on which the members of
even a single community must necessarily agree. The need
for agreement depends on what it is the community does”
(Kuhn, 1970, p. 180). Their shared behaviors might mani ‑
fest as (1) symbolic generalizations that they employ and
about which they exhibit shared understanding; (2) shared
commitments to beliefs that help determine what constitutes
an acceptable explanation and what does not, (3) shared val‑
ues, and (4) shared exemplars (Kuhn, 1970). It is plausible

Kuhn defines values as “the good‑making qualities of
theories” (Preston, 2008, p. 39). The scientific market ‑
ing community has often placed considerable emphasis on
developing generalizable insights and enduring theoretical
contributions (Sheth & Sisodia, 1999; Stremersch et al.,
2023). This orientation assumes that the outcomes of scien‑
tific inquiry should ideally extend beyond the specific con ‑
texts in which they were produced. Design science does not
necessarily reject this aspiration, but approaches it in a fun‑
damentally different way. Scholars working from a design
perspective often find that knowledge is closely tied to the
context and situation in which artifacts are developed and
evaluated, and that the designs themselves may be tempo ‑
rary or subject to change as circumstances evolve (Müller
& Thoring, 2010; Ashton, 2007). The context‑bound and
potentially ephemeral nature of design outcomes warrants
further elaboration.
Context bound
Scientists operating from a design perspective recognize
that every outcome reflects the context, and that generaliz ‑
ability by definition has boundaries (Schaathun, 2025). To
demarcate the context‑bounded character of their science,
design scientists use the so‑called CIMO logic to present
the outcomes of their scientific conduct (Denyer et al.,
2008): For a certain field problem in context [C], we can
apply this intervention [I] that, through these mechanisms
[M], will probably lead to this outcome [O]. The CIMO
logic operates like a recipe, and the emphasis on context
emphasizes that context dependency is standard for such
recipes. In the context of marketing science these reci ‑
pes are seen as “normative marketing guidelines” (Vargo,
2022, p. 138). However, the context‑dependency of these
recipes is not widely embraced by marketing schol ‑
ars, despite the call to clearly identify contextual factors
(Sheth, 2011; Stremersch et al., 2023).
Ephemeral
Designers know that their designed artifacts are ephemeral:
in the future, new technologies will become available, and
new societal contexts or constraints will emerge, which
will require scientists to rethink their designed solutions.
A designed artifact being replaced by a new one represents
common sense in the world of the designer and offers a sign
of (technological) progress (Gregor & Hevner, 2013). Ulti‑
mately, design is about creating change, which reveals the
difference between what currently is and what could be in the
future. Thus, design can be understood as an “intervention ‑
ist discipline” (Dalsgaard, 2014, p. 148): the intervention is
an artifact, undertaken to change an existing situation into a
preferred one. Both the existing situation and the preferred
situation change over time, so any designed intervention is
impermanent. Recognizing marketing as a design science
also requires accepting the transient nature of its scientific
contributions, in contrast to the discipline’s emphasis on
law‑like generalizations (Sheth & Sisodia, 1999). Breaking
away from law‑like generalizations and totalizing theories
could contribute to a renewal of the marketing discipline
and foster greater innovation in the development of market‑
ing thought (Helkkula & Arnould, 2022).
Not everything in design is context‑bound and ephem ‑
eral. Certain design principles, such as symmetry or sim ‑
plicity, are not. However, design science focuses on the
scientific development and evaluation of artifacts, which
are inherently situated in a particular temporal and contex ‑
tual setting (Weigand et al., 2021). Although the design of
artifacts may draw on principles that appear timeless, the
artifacts that emerge from the design process are not them ‑
selves timeless. While design science draws on established
theoretical knowledge, the artifacts produced are inherently
contingent upon the time and context in which they are cre‑
ated (Hevner et al., 2004).
Paradigmatic core of marketing as design
science: Purpose, structure, and validation
criteria
Operating from a different paradigm requires a different
process and distinct rules and standards for scientific prac ‑
tice (Kuhn, 1970). Marketing theories rooted in different
paradigms thus differ in their purpose, structure, and valida‑
tion criteria (Hunt, 2010), as elaborated in this section.
Purpose
The purpose of academic research within the design‑oriented
paradigm differs fundamentally from that of most marketing
science: rather than primarily generating understanding and
advising practice on the basis of such insights, design‑ori ‑
ented marketing science seeks to develop and test actionable
solutions to practical marketing problems. While other para‑
digms start from the current situation ( what is), design sci‑
ence starts from a future‑oriented perspective ( what ought
to be) (Simon, 1969). Design theory makes explicit “what
would be desirable and how the existing situation could be
changed into the desired situation” (Blessing et al., 1998, p.
43). As a result of this difference in purpose, design science

produces a different type of knowledge: design knowledge,
defined as the knowledge “of how things can and should be
constructed or arranged (i.e. designed), usually by human
agency, to achieve a desired set of goals” (V om Brocke et
al., 2020, p. 2). This type of knowledge is acquired uniquely
through abductive reasoning. A distinct research purpose
thus affects the type of knowledge that will result from the
research efforts, as well as the ways the resulting knowledge
types can be shared with others (Gregor & Hevner, 2013).
Therefore, we propose an alternative publication scheme
for marketing science conducted within the design‑oriented
paradigm, as presented in a later section of this manuscript.
Structure
Design‑oriented marketing science is structured differently,
both in its process and its outcomes. This difference becomes
apparent at the outset of the research process, particularly in
the formulation of the research question. In design research,
research questions are typically framed as “How can…?”
(Henseler & Guerreiro, 2020) or “How might we…?” ques‑
tions (IDEO, n.d.), reflecting the discipline’s consistent
emphasis on developing multiple solutions to a problem or
a class of interrelated problems (Van Aken & Andriessen,
2011), under the assumption that developing multiple solu ‑
tions ultimately leads to better solutions. These interrelated
problems are often ill‑defined and ill‑structured (Archer,
1979; Simon, 1969) or characterized as “wicked” (Rittel &
Webber, 1973; Buchanan, 1992), which means that there is
no easy or clear cut solution. Wicked problems are origi ‑
nally defined by Horst Rittel as “a class of social system
problems which are ill‑formulated, where the information is
confusing, where there are many clients and decision mak ‑
ers with conflicting values, and where the ramifications in
the whole system are thoroughly confusing” (Churchman,
1967, pp. B‑141). The absence of a clear ‑cut solution for
such problems necessitates designing a solution, which
explains why design science is intrinsically linked to wicked
problems, also known as the “wicked problems theory of
design” (Buchanan, 1992). Many problems in the world are
inherently complex, ill‑defined and ill‑structured, and these
problems are thus not unique to the discipline of design,
which underscores the inherently interdisciplinary character
of design as a field concerned with addressing such com ‑
plex challenges. From that perspective, virtually all mar ‑
keting problems can be considered “wicked,” considering
that the marketing domain by definition is embedded in a
wider social system, in which many stakeholders operate on
the basis of different values and perspectives, and various
disruptive forces operate in a context of high uncertainty.
In fact, “many real‑world problems combine a marketing
issue for one stakeholder with financial, human resource,
social, cultural, or even moral issues for other stakehold ‑
ers” (Steenkamp et al., 2026, p. 6). In turn, typical market ‑
ing problems are poorly structured, in the sense that they
can be viewed from multiple points of view, the underlying
goals are often ambiguous, and they can prompt contradic ‑
tory solutions, such as due to conflicting algorithms (Clark,
2020). A design science approach offers particular effective‑
ness for these types of problems. The wicked character of
problems does not mean that every problem–solution com ‑
bination is idiosyncratic though. In other fields that tackle
wicked problems, such as design and engineering, patterns
of solutions frequently emerge (Alexander et al., 1977),
with substantial impacts on progress in the related field.
The complex background of the research process affects
its structure. For example, researchers should start by inves‑
tigating the stakeholders affected by the marketing problem
(Hillebrand et al., 2015). Design thinkers would label this
step “empathizing,” reflecting a “putting people first” prin ‑
ciple (Brown & Katz, 2011). This approach is more complex
than it might seem, because various stakeholders express
conflicting needs and viewpoints. Design scientists antici ‑
pate discovering paradoxes during this exploration phase
and created reframing strategies to overcome them (Dorst,
2015; Gaim & Wåhlin, 2016). Only once the problem, its
context, and its stakeholders have been explored, reframed,
and defined, do design scientists begin to study solutions.
This part of the research phase is never linear; it develops as
a “knowledge funnel” (Martin, 2010) that moves from mys‑
tery to artifact in undefinable and intuitive ways. The ide ‑
ation phase is the start of a creative process that finally leads
to a prototype, or “marketing artifact” (Brown, 2002, p. 9).
It is precisely this ideation phase that makes design research
distinctive and different from other forms of research. By
employing a variety of creative techniques, multiple solu ‑
tion directions are conceived and explored, turning the
search for a suitable solution into a genuine research pro ‑
cess. Whereas the design process itself is complex and itera‑
tive, the outcome of that process, the designed artifact, can
be simple in the sense that it serves a single, straightfor ‑
ward purpose: whether it constitutes a workable solution to
the problem at hand. Artifacts always do something; they
always have a specific function (Iivari, 2007). To ensure the
artifact is fulfilling this function, many iterations usually are
necessary (Peffers et al., 2007; Hevner et al., 2004). To sum
up, a typical structure for academic work adopting a design
orientation thus consists of three phases: (1) an exploration
of the problem space, in which the complex nature of the
problem, its context, and the needs of different stakehold ‑
ers are examined; (2) an exploration of the solution space,
where the actual design work takes place in the search for
a suitable solution; and (3) artifact evaluation, in which
the designed intervention is assessed for its effectiveness.

Within this process, the primary value of design science lies
in broadening the solution space by employing various cre‑
ative approaches, allowing multiple solution directions for
complex problems to be designed and investigated.
Returning to the green consumer behavior vignette intro‑
duced earlier, this three‑phase structure can be made con ‑
crete. Bhamra et al. ( 2011) present a case study in design
for sustainable behavior that focuses on refrigerators and
freezers, the product category responsible for the largest
share of household energy consumption (around one‑fifth
of the total). In phase one they explore the problem space
by different observation studies and a questionnaire to study
kitchen user profiles, food‑shopping unpacking and food
preparation behavior, and factors influencing decision‑mak‑
ing. Based on this study routine refrigerator and freezer use
patterns could be identified that informed the design pro ‑
cess. In the second phase multiple design strategies were
used to design different solutions. Finally, a reflection on the
solutions takes place based on interviews. What makes this
a genuinely design‑oriented approach is that Bhamra et al.
(2011) did not rely on questionnaires alone, but conducted
actual product‑in‑use observations, filming consumers with
audio‑visual equipment as they used their refrigerators and
freezers in everyday life. This approach surfaced striking
findings that self‑report alone would likely have missed:
most of the time the refrigerator door was left open, for
instance, was spent not retrieving food but rearranging
items to make room for new groceries. Their observations
further revealed that many modern kitchens have a built‑
in refrigerator or freezer positioned next to the oven, an
unfavorable placement, combined with insufficient storage
space elsewhere in the kitchen, so that consumers end up
buying a second refrigerator and placing it somewhere else
in the house. This is therefore not a matter of user behavior
that could be solved with a nudge toward buying a more
eco‑friendly refrigerator, but a kitchen‑design and systemic
problem for which a new storage design is a more effective
solution. As is often the case, a design‑oriented approach
here led to a reframing of the research question: rather than
asking how consumers could be encouraged to purchase
more eco‑friendly refrigerators, the question became how
kitchen design and user behavior could be adapted so that
people themselves could use their existing refrigerator in a
more eco‑friendly way.
Validation criteria
The validation criteria for a design‑oriented approach differ
from those typically applied in marketing science, as design
science is concerned with possible worlds rather than the
existing world (Schaathun, 2025), implying that theories
emerging from design are evaluated differently and cannot
be tested in the same way as theories about existing phe ‑
nomena. Design theories do not aim to explain or predict
phenomena; instead, they provide solutions. Offering and
testing solutions differs fundamentally from formulating
and testing hypotheses, which primarily describe how the
world is expected to work. However, a designed artifact can
be viewed as a kind of hypothesis about what might work in
practice. The validation criteria for design‑oriented market‑
ing science concentrate on the effectiveness and usefulness
of the designed marketing artifact, so the evaluation is not
based on “true or false” but rather on “good or bad” out ‑
comes (Rittel & Webber, 1973). Usefulness is, of course,
always an important virtue, but it plays out differently
depending on the paradigm. In explanatory and predictive
theory building, the outcome of the academic process is
evaluated against criteria such as truth, explanatory and pre‑
dictive power, and generalizability, with usefulness follow‑
ing only as an indirect result. In design science, by contrast,
the outcome of the academic process is assessed directly
against usefulness, making it a direct rather than an indirect
result. The key evaluative question for the design scientist
is: does the marketing artifact work? The answer depends
on its utility for users, who might be marketing practitio ‑
ners, customers, or other relevant stakeholders. They must
believe the artifact works. Reflecting the emphasis on solv‑
ing field problems, actors in the field should perceive that
the artifact itself is solving their problem. In turn, knowl ‑
edge produced by design science can be ‘tool‑like’ if the
practitioner finds it usable (Worren et al., 2002), or else
‘warning‑like’ if the artifact is not usable or is usable only in
certain conditions. Accordingly, pragmatic validity, defined
as “the extent to which goals or intended consequences can
be achieved by producing certain actions or using particular
instruments” (Worren et al., 2002, p. 1228), is a core consid‑
eration. Validity then depends on the truth of utility claims
related to the design and the artifact, as judged by actors in
the field (Offermann et al., 2010). Within design science,
utility assessments examine the usefulness of an artifact,
whereas validity assessments evaluate the extent to which
the artifact actually achieves its intended purpose.
Implications of marketing as design science
for theory generation
What does marketing as design science mean for theory
generation? Although theories are equally indispensable in
design science and, for example, in behavioral science, the
roles they fulfill within these paradigms differ in important
ways. Design theories typically include a prescriptive state‑
ment indicating how to do something (Kuechler & Vaish ‑
navi, 2008); in contrast, behavioral theories refer to what is

(analysis); how, why, when, and where something is (expla‑
nation); and/or what will be (prediction) (Gregor, 2006).
Design theories primarily emerge from synthesis rather than
analysis, as they focus on the creation and integration of
artifacts and solutions (Buchanan, 1992). In this section,
following Hevner et al. ( 2004), Goldkuhl (2011), Sutton et
al. (2021) and Halstrick (2023), we contrast design science
with the behavioral paradigm, as we did in Table 1. We do
so because the behavioral paradigm, as advanced by the
Ford Foundation’s Behavioral Sciences Program (Pooley,
2016), represents a common approach within marketing sci‑
ence, and because contrasting paradigms helps clarify the
essence of the design paradigm.
Within a behavioral paradigm, scientific progress under
the hypothetico‑deductive approach starts with theory
development, followed by the acquisition of meaningful
data, data analysis, and a learning process (Bagozzi, 1980;
Burgess & Steenkamp, 2006). Although not all behavioral
academic marketing research is explicitly theory‑based, the
‘theory‑first’ paradigm still dominates current academic
marketing research (Golder et al., 2023). With a design
paradigm, however, theory development does not come
first. Instead, theory evolves through the scientific process
and is formulated toward the end (Kuechler & Vaishnavi,
2008). We depict this difference graphically in Fig. 1, with
the added caution that, despite what it shows, scientific
processes are almost never linear. Furthermore, the scien ‑
tific processes adopted in these paradigms address distinct
entities: behavioral science concerns the study of phenom ‑
ena, whereas design science focuses on solving problems.
Phenomena are not the same as problems. Phenomena are
observable events or situations that can be studied and ana‑
lyzed. Problems are undesired states of affairs, such as a dis‑
crepancy between how things are and how they should be.
The knowledge created through a design‑oriented para ‑
digm can complement the knowledge created through the
behavioral paradigm. Design science produces prescriptive
(means–ends) knowledge instead of descriptive (cause–
effect) knowledge (Avdiji & Winter, 2019; Drechsler &
Hevner, 2022; Gregor & Hevner, 2013; March & Smith,
1995). Yet descriptive knowledge generally is often needed
to create prescriptive knowledge. Therefore, behavioral sci‑
ence and design science research are not either–or options in
the long run, but rather complementary.
An intuitive way to leverage the complementarity
between the two paradigms is to conduct academic research
from a behavioral perspective first, followed by academic
research from the design perspective. However, the reverse
scenario is also possible. For example, the invention of the
steam engine by engineers preceded the development of
knowledge in thermodynamics and thus a comprehensive
understanding of its mechanisms. Intriguingly, the steam
engine had been in existence for almost a century before
the field of thermodynamics began to emerge. In market ‑
ing, we can point to other examples of a design perspective
preceding a behavioral perspective. For example, psychol ‑
ogy theories in general and emotions in particular had been
acknowledged and applied long before advertising scien ‑
tists explicitly studied such applications (French, 1909;
Scott, 1903); it was not until late in the 20th century that the
psychological mechanisms were thoroughly detailed. By
2002, Daniel Kahneman had been awarded a Nobel Prize

To generate different kinds of knowledge through the
design‑oriented paradigm, the marketing domain should not
merely import external design theories. Rather, it requires
indigenous marketing design theories, especially consider ‑

theory development” (Hunt, 2020, p. 8). Therefore, we
propose a framework for cultivating indigenous theories of
marketing design that feature foundational pillars of theory
development. In indigenous theory development within
marketing design, it is the marketing scholars, and not the
designers, who undertake efforts to create solutions for
problems experienced by marketing practitioners. To clarify
the essence of this form of indigenous theory development,
Fig. 2 juxtaposes the design‑oriented paradigm, based on
an abductive model, with an inductive realist model, as out‑

science. Although Fig. 2 depicts both models along the same
seven steps to enable direct comparison, this shared struc ‑

evaluates foundational premises, the abductive pragmatist
model develops and evaluates a concrete artifact together
with practitioners in the field, a difference reflected in their
respective final theory proposals, one aimed at understand ‑

When marketing scholars, not designers, are design ‑
ing solutions to marketing problems, the marketing design
theory generation should start with the identification of the
problem (step I). Scholars explore this problem as part of
the reality of the practitioner, then identify what is already
known about the problem, drawing not only on the theo ‑
retical solutions proposed by other scholars but also on
existing practices and solutions already in use in the field
(step II). Such insights can be imported from domains
other than marketing, and from practice itself, because they
do not form the core of the theory but rather establish an
inspirational foundation for the design process. Theories
that serve as sources for creative ideas and foundations for
developing design principles are also called kernel theories
(Hevner, 2007; Möller & Halinen, 2022). After reviewing
and gathering input from existing literature, the design pro‑
cess takes place (step III). A designer or design team applies
design methods to develop an artifact, ideally in co‑design
with practitioners and other stakeholders in the problem
that initiated the effort (step IV). After this process of (co)
design, the artifact gets evaluated, on the basis of the extent
to which it works and solves practitioners’ problems (step
V). This evaluation results in a theory proposal (step VI).
The core of this theory proposal is the artifact itself. The
theory has scientific truth if it shows that the artifact actually
works: Does it do its job in solving the problem? Although
kernel theories can be useful for studying artifacts (e.g., to

to build stronger connections (Overdiek & Warnaby, 2020).

In domains that typically embrace solution‑oriented design
science (such as engineering or medicine), effective net ‑

and the practitioners learn from scientists just as much as
scientists learn from practitioners. Knowledge production
and knowledge exchanges are bidirectional. For market ‑
ing scholars, the relationship with practitioners is much
more one‑sided (Ojansivu, 2023). For marketing as design
science to work, these relationships must become bidirec ‑
tional, which requires scholars to invest in a reciprocal rela‑
tionship from the start, that is, in the research design phase.
When formulating the research question and a methodologi‑
cal approach, academic needs should be balanced with prac‑
titioners’ needs (Young & Freytag, 2021).
Steps III–V of the scientific process illustrate that design
theories have a distinct architecture; captured by Aristotle’s
four causes, which stem from his investigations in metaphys‑
ics and natural philosophy (Müller ‑Merbach, 2005; Nelson
& Stolterman, 2012). The designer’s efforts (step III) estab‑
lish an answer to the question, “What made it happen?” or
Aristotle’s efficient cause (Fig. 3). The design of the arti ‑
fact (step IV) is characterized by the general idea behind
the design, or the formal cause. This general idea can take
different forms, such as a tool, a recipe, a model, or a con ‑
struction rule. Then it gets shaped by different components
or ingredients, which form the material cause, or “What is
it made of?” The result is an artifact that can be evaluated in
relationship with its purpose or goal (step V). The purpose
or goal answers the question, “What is it for?” or the final
cause of Aristotle (Gregor, 2009). Such Aristotelian cau ‑
sality not only underpins the foundational pillars of design
theory, but also indicates that the distinctive anatomy of
design theories is shaped by an underlying logical structure.
This logical structure is shaped by a ‘technological rule,’ a
concept introduced by the philosopher Bunge ( 1967). For
marketing design theory, that rule always entails something
like, “In the context of situation Z, marketing scholars have
designed artifact X, which helps marketing practitioners
achieve purpose Y .” This rule or recipe, which is also for ‑
mulated as the CIMO logic discussed earlier, constitutes the
central outcome of the theory development process.

possible interventions. The formal cause is the general idea
underlying the artifact, for example a choice‑architecture
intervention that changes the default or salience of options
at the point of purchase, rather than an informational cam ‑
paign aimed at changing attitudes. The material cause con ‑
sists of the concrete ingredients from which the artifact is
built, such as the specific shelf position chosen, the wording
and framing of the descriptive‑norm message, or the default
setting in an online assortment. The final cause, or purpose,

Taken together, the material presented above allows us to
formulate a set of foundational premises that guide the gen‑
eration of indigenous marketing design theory. These are:
Premise 1 Marketing design theory begins with a practitio‑
ner‑experienced problem and follows a people‑
first principle throughout the process.
Where marketing science often starts by identifying a gap
in the current literature, a design approach starts by iden ‑
tifying the problem from the context of the practitioner
(Fig. 2, step I). Because marketing problems can typically
be approached from multiple stakeholder perspectives and
often involve ambiguous or conflicting goals, this first step
requires exploring and reframing the problem space together
with the relevant stakeholders, guided by a people‑first prin‑
ciple that puts their needs and perspectives at the center of
the process. The starting point is therefore always what a
situation currently is and, more importantly, what it ought
to become from the perspective of the relevant stakeholders.
Premise 2 Marketing design theory is generated by creating
the ‘not‑yet‑existing’ through research.
Design is fundamentally about bringing something new
into the world, that is, about creating what does not yet
exist. This act of creation is driven by abductive reason ‑
ing, in which the design process begins with a hypothesized
desired outcome and searches for a viable artifact capable
of bringing it about. This process of generating and explor ‑
ing multiple candidate solutions requires creation‑oriented
methods and creative, lateral‑thinking techniques, which are
not incidental to the process but epistemically necessary for
reframing the problem and reaching solutions that would
not otherwise be found. The resulting artifact, together with
the working principles that make it function, is itself the out‑
come of this design process. Kernel theories, whether drawn
from marketing or other disciplines, can inform and inspire
this act of creation, but the theoretical core of a marketing
design theory is the newly created artifact itself.
Premise 3 Marketing design theory emerges through bidi ‑
rectional co‑design with practitioners.
If design‑oriented research aims to bring about real change
in marketing practice, that change can only take hold if
it is developed together with the practitioners who will
ultimately have to adopt and sustain it. The actual design
process (Fig. 2, steps III–V) therefore requires intensive,
reciprocal collaboration between scholars and practitioners,
in which practitioners are not merely a source of problems
and data but active partners in shaping and evaluating the
artifact itself.
Premise 4 A marketing design theory’s architecture follows
Aristotle’s four causes and is expressed as a tech‑
nological rule.
The designer’s efforts (efficient cause), the general idea
behind the artifact (formal cause), its concrete ingredients
(material cause), and its purpose (final cause) together con‑
stitute the theory’s architecture (Fig. 3). The result is cap ‑
tured in a technological rule: “In the context of situation Z,
marketing scholars have designed artifact X, which helps
marketing practitioners achieve purpose Y .” This rule is
also reflected in the CIMO logic so characteristic of design
science: for a certain field problem in context [C], we can
apply this intervention [I] that, through these mechanisms
[M], will probably lead to this outcome [O].
Premise 5  A marketing design theory is validated by
whether the artifact demonstrably works.
Theory development is completed only once the artifact
has been evaluated with and by practitioners (Fig. 2, step
V), and its scientific truth is established through pragmatic
validity: evidence that it achieves its intended purpose for
those who use it. Because artifacts and the contexts in which
they operate continue to evolve, a marketing design theory
is inherently context‑bound and may need to be revised or
replaced over time.
Challenges of marketing as design science
Advancing toward design theories in marketing science
entails several substantive challenges. Highlighting these
challenges constitutes the fourth and final question that
underpins this manuscript. Design science is powerful

1 3
   25  Page 12 of 19
AMS Review           (2026) 16:25
because it aims to improve real ‑world situations, but pre ‑
cisely for that reason, research becomes more complex, con‑
text‑dependent, and methodologically challenging. In this
section, four categories of challenges are addressed: meth ‑
odological, evaluation, ethical, and publication challenges.
Methodological challenges
Design science is often conducted by a combination of
research techniques (Panakaduwa et al., 2025; The Design‑
Based Research Collective, 2003). Often methods are
employed that rely heavily on creativity (Baskerville et al.,
2016; Cross, 2006). This is not only time‑consuming and
difficult to standardize, but also challenging to replicate
due to the inherently uncertain and unpredictable nature of
creative thinking processes (Kryssanov et al., 2001). Addi‑
tionally, the problem‑solving methods required for practical
problems and the solution‑justification methods required
for knowledge problems differ in design science (Wieringa,
2009). Furthermore, artifacts are context‑dependent and this
context is ever‑changing (Brendel et al., 2021). As a result
of all these factors, reproducibility is a challenging concept
in design science. It is now well established that many stud‑
ies are difficult to reproduce, and this has led to a repro ‑
ducibility crisis across disciplines (Baker, 2016); however,
design science faces additional, unique challenges in this
regard because of the issues stated above (Brendel et al.,
2021). Due to the turbulent, iterative, and creative nature of
design science, there is an increased need to optimize aca ‑
demic rigor in design science research. At the same time, it
has been argued that this very academic rigor may constrain
creativity in design science research projects (Baskerville et
al., 2016), thereby creating a paradoxical tension between
methodological discipline and the generative openness
required for innovative design. In this complex dynamic,
transparency is the key word, and recently there have been
calls for the design science community to provide greater
transparency in the process, underpinned by the open design
movement (Hevner et al., 2024).
Evaluation challenges
How can the effectiveness of a design in practice be demon‑
strated? Success criteria are often multidimensional, mean ‑
ing that no single measure can determine whether a design
is successful. Each design, within its specific context, there‑
fore requires its own criteria to define success (Venable et
al., 2016). However, in practice, design science evalua ‑
tions often rely more on intuition than on clearly defined
metrics, which may reduce the reliability of the scientific
process (Prochner & Godin, 2022). Moreover, a distinction
must be made between validation of the artifact itself during
the design process and evaluation of its implementation, as
these may yield different outcomes (van Zeeland, 2023).
This difference is also called ex ante versus ex post evalua ‑
tion (Venable et al., 2016). In many cases, only an ex‑ante
evaluation is conducted, whereas it is the ex‑post evaluation
that actually determines the real value creation of the design:
an artifact that cannot be successfully implemented is not a
design that creates value. The effects of a design may also
vary over time, with short‑term, proximal results differing
from long‑term, distal impacts after implementation (Rossi
et al., 1999; Blessing & Chakrabarti, 2009). In addition, the
involvement of multiple stakeholders can introduce further
complexity, particularly when they hold conflicting defini ‑
tions of success (Kensing & Blomberg, 1998). The evalua‑
tion of artifacts in real‑world contexts is further complicated
by the simultaneous interaction of numerous variables,
which cannot be entirely isolated (Blessing & Chakrabarti,
2009; March & Smith, 1995). Consequently, results tend to
be highly context‑dependent, making it difficult to establish
rigorous cause–effect relationships. This implies that more
complex evaluation methods must be employed, such as
realist evaluation (Pawson & Tilley, 1997), in order to arrive
at a comprehensive understanding. A related challenge is
that the knowledge acquired through evaluation is inad ‑
equately applied: even actionable knowledge will not make
complex problems simple (The Design‑Based Research
Collective, 2003).
Ethical challenges
Several ethical challenges are inherent in marketing as
design science. Foremost among these is the concern that
certain interventions may be manipulative. Marketing
already maintains a complex, often ambivalent relation ‑
ship with nudging, persuasion, and manipulation toward
overconsumption (Pitt, 2020), and this tension is intensified
in design science due to its strong emphasis on designing
interventions. It is possible to design marketing interven ‑
tions that fall on the darker side of marketing, for instance,
interventions aimed at increasing consumers’ impulse pur ‑
chases, even when such strategies are known to diminish
well‑being in certain cases by inducing feelings of regret
(Hartanto et al., 2026). In these cases, the interventions
are demonstrably effective, yet simultaneously potentially
harmful to society. Another ethical challenge concerns
power dynamics: designing for others versus designing with
others (co‑design) not only carries significant methodologi‑
cal implications, but also raises important ethical consid ‑
erations (Pols, 2017). One way to address this challenge
in marketing is through design tools, such as the Wheel
of Power, which assist in revealing and analyzing power
dynamics between participants (van Hest et al., 2026).

Publication challenges
Because design science typically produces context‑specific
solutions, questions arise as to whether a design addresses
a single case, generates generalizable knowledge, or falls
somewhere in between (Wieringa, 2014). Results are
sometimes perceived as “consulting” rather than science
(Gregory, 2010), and for this reason, the term design sci ‑
ence is sometimes approached with skepticism (Blessing
& Chakrabarti, 2009). Researchers in design science often
combine the roles of designer and implementation partner,
creating potential biases due to compromised scientific inde‑
pendence (Blessing & Chakrabarti, 2009; Kensing & Blom‑
berg, 1998). The field is also criticized for “solutionism,”
rushing to actionable quick fixes without fully addressing
root causes (Richterich, 2024; Blythe et al., 2015). Another
point of criticism is that the line between science and activ‑
ism can sometimes be thin (Bashiri et al., 2025). Design sci‑
ence is thus often seen as too practical and context‑specific,
which makes publishing challenging (Hevner et al., 2004;
Gregor & Hevner, 2013). Top marketing journals favor the‑
ory testing over artifact creation, creating publication bar ‑
riers for marketing as design science. Additionally, values,
purpose, structure, validation criteria, and theory‑building
processes differ fundamentally when marketing science
is approached from a design perspective, requiring manu ‑
scripts in this paradigm to take a distinct form. Journals
play an important role in showcasing both understanding‑
focused and problem‑solving research (Kohli & Haenlein,
2021), yet a paradox prevails: the type of research most
valued by academia seems least compelling to practitioners
(Kuusela et al., 2014). For marketing as design science to
be accepted, journals must recognize that rigor is defined
differently and adapt requirements to the nature of design‑
oriented research (Helkkula & Arnould, 2022; Stange et al.,
2022).
A multi‑study publication scheme can support academic
acceptance and rigor in marketing as design science, yet the
publication schemes normally suggested in design science
follow a single‑study approach (Gregor & Hevner, 2013;
Stange et al., 2022), which does not do justice to the meth ‑
odological richness of a full design process. We therefore
present a multi‑study approach, which has several advan ‑
tages over single‑study schemes: it accommodates human
factors and multiple perspectives, allows iterative design
processes, and supports methodological rigor across phases.
Furthermore, leading marketing journals nowadays increas‑
ingly favor a multi‑study approach.
A multi‑study publication scheme might include: (1)
an exploratory study of the problem space to map stake ‑
holder perspectives and identify barriers and dilemmas,
(2) a study exploring possible interventions and applying
design methods, ideally with co‑design, and (3) an evalua ‑
tion study assessing the artifact against multiple criteria to
inform theory (see also Table 2). Each study can employ dif‑
ferent methodologies appropriate to its goals, enabling rel ‑
evance and rigor to coexist. Rigor is then instantiated across
all phases of the design process: exploration of the problem
space, exploration of the solution space, and evaluation of
the resulting artifact.
Study 1 maps the problem space and the issues that dif ‑
ferent stakeholders face (what makes the problem complex).
It uses mostly qualitative methods, including interviews and
focus groups, and its goal is to identify a design challenge
that frames subsequent work. Study 2 explores the solu ‑
tion space ( which interventions are possible ), producing
an artifact intended to solve the problem. This study might
begin with a literature review to identify kernel theories that
inform potential solution directions. The research methods
for this study should involve actual design methods and
creative problem‑solving techniques, including analogous
modeling, what‑if thinking, visual and construction tech ‑
niques, and role play. All of these methods involve lateral
thinking, in which the researcher uncovers new perspectives
to create innovative solutions. This is an essential stage of
the research process, as it involves exploring multiple pos ‑
sible solution directions for a complex problem. Study 3
evaluates the artifact (is the designed intervention effective),
using both quantitative and qualitative measures, often in
an experimental design. Additional iterative cycles may
create more studies within the compendium. By adopting
an approach that makes the various studies visible, room is
created for diversity in focus and methodology, room that is
necessary not only to honor the design‑oriented character of

Discussion and conclusion
If you wanted to learn more about the human body, you
might consult a human biologist to gain an explanation of
how it functions, or you could visit a physician who can
offer guidance on how to improve its functioning. Current
marketing science often resembles the human biologist; we
propose reshaping the discipline to more fully integrate the
perspective and contributions of the physician. That is, it
remains crucial to examine the current functioning of the
marketing field while also exploring its potential future
operations. In a design‑oriented paradigm, relevance for
the real world is the very purpose of academic research. In
turn, we argue that marketing as design science will be par‑
ticularly relevant for increasing research devoted to social
marketing, sustainable marketing, and marketing for a bet ‑
ter world, that is, research streams that imagine marketing
as a discipline that can contribute to improving things and
serve the greater good (Madan et al., 2023; Nenonen, 2022).
But, more broadly, a design approach is relevant to any form
of marketing in which a situation has arisen that can be
improved, and through that improvement, value is created
for the stakeholders involved.
We thus propose that a broader adoption of the design
paradigm can contribute to the development of marketing
science. First, marketing as design science will produce
different types of theories and models, which will enrich
marketing science in general and establish a more diverse
knowledge landscape. Second, embracing marketing as
design science will help scholars bridge the gap between
science and practice. There is a difference between offer ‑
ing solution‑oriented advice based on research insights and
creating actionable solutions through research itself, and
because design science is focused on the latter, it brings
scientific work much closer to the needs of practice. We
should not forget, in this regard, that a design approach is
not new to the marketing field. In practice, design think ‑
ing has long been applied as an innovation approach by
organizations across industries. Over time, design think ‑
ing has matured from an isolated, pilot‑level practice into a
standard organizational capability applied at scale (Kolko,
2015). Academia, however, could learn more from this rich
practical tradition and could play a more active role in fur ‑
ther developing and substantiating it. We stress that design
science does not per se mean applied science (Stremersch,
2021); designing a solution that is relevant to practice can
still be methodologically rigorous and advance scientific
knowledge. By embracing a design science methodology,
academic discourses can provide both practical relevance
and impacts on the development of scientific knowledge.
Gregor and Hevner (2013) offer a practical guide for how to
create such a win–win scenario.
Despite its promise as a valuable complement to the cur‑
rent scientific landscape, marketing as design science has
yet to gain widespread adoption and acceptance. Several
factors plausibly explain this lack of adoption, which we
characterize as three related mismatches between market ‑
ing design science and the discipline’s prevailing norms.
The first is a values mismatch: following Kuhn’s (1970)
observation that a paradigm shift requires a community’s
shared values to align with the new paradigm, marketing
science has traditionally valued generalizable, enduring
theoretical contributions (Stremersch et al., 2023), whereas
design knowledge is, as we have argued, context‑bound and
ephemeral. Compounding this is a validation mismatch:
design science evaluates artifacts by whether they work in
practice rather than by statistical or theoretical proof that
they exist, and because leading marketing journals continue
to favor theory testing over artifact creation, design‑oriented
work risks being dismissed as “mere” consulting (Gregory,
2010) rather than being recognized as rigorous science.
Alba ( 2012) made a similar observation about consumer
research, noting how what began as a reasonable apprecia ‑
tion for theory as a result of the theory‑oriented hypo‑deduc‑
tive approach, hardened into a rigid requirement, sidelining
exploratory work despite its scientific value. Underlying
both is an incentive mismatch: doctoral training, tenure cri‑
teria, and journal rankings in marketing continue to reward
theory‑first, generalizable contributions, giving individual
scholars little professional incentive to pursue a paradigm
that is, by its own logic, temporary and context‑specific.
Good designs are often complex in the underlying problem
they take on, yet surprisingly simple in the solution they
arrive at; this beauty of simplicity may help counter aca ‑
demic scholarship’s tendency to value complexity over sim‑
plicity, a tendency that is itself reinforced by an incentive
structure that rewards theoretical elaboration over parsimo‑
nious, workable solutions.
Overcoming these mismatches will require sustained
effort across the field, but this manuscript itself takes a
first step by making marketing design science more leg ‑
ible and actionable to the discipline. As outlined at the out ‑
set of this manuscript, the four persistent problems facing

With this manuscript, we have tried to establish what ele ‑
ments are needed to implement marketing as design sci ‑
ence and addressed four main questions: (1) What does a
design approach offer that other research approaches do
not, (2) how does marketing as design science influence
the purpose, structure, and validation criteria of research,
(3) what does marketing as design science imply for theory
generation, and (4) what are the challenges of marketing as
design science? Regarding the first question, we identified
three characteristics that make a design approach unique,
namely its pragmatic epistemology, its reliance on abduc ‑
tive reasoning, and its use of additional research techniques
that often draw on creative, lateral thinking. We also diag ‑
nosed a conflict in shared values, or what Preston ( 2008)
calls ‘the good‑making qualities of theories’: whereas the
scientific marketing community predominantly values gen ‑
eralizable and timeless knowledge, such qualities are virtu ‑
ally unattainable within design science. Instead, knowledge
in design science is context bound and ephemeral. The sec‑
ond question pertains to creating awareness and understand‑
ing of the essence marketing as design science. We made
explicit how the purpose, structure, and validation criteria
in design science differ from those in other paradigmatic
approaches, which is important in the context of managing
academic expectations. In addressing the third question, we
examined what adopting design science implies for the pro‑
cess of theory development and showed that the abductive
mode of reasoning inherent to design science fundamentally
alters the nature of this process. We also linked the process
of theory development to Aristotle’s four causes, thereby
revealing not only the fundamental pillars of a design theory
but also the underlying ‘recipe’ from which such a theory
is constructed. Finally, we constituted foundational prem ‑
ises that guide indigenous marketing design theory. Taken
together, this can be seen as a framework for cultivating an
indigenous theory of marketing design. In addressing the
final question, we outlined several challenges associated
with marketing as design science, including methodologi ‑
cal, evaluation, ethical, and publication challenges. With
respect to the latter, we proposed a new multi‑study publi ‑
cation scheme for marketing as design science. Through this
manuscript, we aim to support marketing scholars in design‑
ing solutions and to foster the acceptance of these solu ‑
tions (or artifacts) as foundational components of emerging
marketing theories within the academic community. Such
acceptance, however, also depends on the methodological
and evaluative rigor with which these solutions are reported
and assessed. Developing shared reporting standards, com ‑
parable to those already established for other research tradi‑
tions (e.g., CONSORT for randomized trials, PRISMA for
systematic reviews), could help marketing journals embrace
marketing as design science by further strengthening the
methodological and evaluative rigor expected of manu ‑
scripts that employ creation‑oriented methods. As a first
step in this direction, we outline a design science reporting
checklist for marketing design science (Table 3) that builds
directly on the concepts and structure discussed throughout
this manuscript. Authors could use this checklist to report
their design process transparently, and reviewers and editors
could use it to benchmark the methodological and evalu ‑
ative rigor of manuscripts that employ creation‑oriented
methods. We present this checklist as a starting point rather
than a validated instrument; we invite future research to test,
refine, and where necessary revise it across a broader range
of design‑oriented marketing studies.

doing science. Embracing the design‑oriented paradigm
undoubtedly will feel awkward at first, rather like wearing
inverted lenses (Kuhn, 1970, p. 122). Choosing between
