Abstract
Luxury hotel marketing increasingly promotes sustainability, yet how this communication aligns with the
experiential and symbolic foundations of luxury remains insufficiently understood. While prior research has often
framed luxury and sustainability as co nflicting concepts, limited attention has been paid to how sustainability is
strategically embedded and communicated in service -based luxury contexts from a managerial perspective.
Addressing this gap, the present research examines how sustainability is in tegrated into the marketing
communications of Swiss luxury hotels, with a focus on underlying motivations, practices, and challenges. A
qualitative multiple–case study approach is adopted, triangulating semi‑structured interviews with hotel managers,
academic literature, industry reports, and online travel reviews. Findings show that sustainability is incorporated
through environmental, socio‑cultural, and managerial practices and is communicated mainly via dedicated online
channels, destination‑based experiences, and collaborations with local actors. The study identifies implicit versus
explicit communication strategies, and key challenges related to measurement, greenwashing concerns, and
audience alignment. By mobilizing signaling theory and the Online Communication Model, the research reveals
how sustainability communication operates through complementary visible and embedded signals, allowing hotels
to convey sustainability while preserving exclusivity and experiential value.

Keywords:  sustainable luxury; hospitality marketing; experiential luxury; case study methodology; destination
ecosystem; signaling theory.

1. Introduction
Communicating sustainability in luxury hospitality raises a fundamental tension. Luxury brands rely on
symbolic value, exclusivity, and experiential richness, while sustainability communication is often
associated with transparency, accountability, and res ponsibility-oriented claims. In the context of
luxury hotels, this tension becomes particularly salient, as marketing communication must
simultaneously preserve the experiential and emotional foundations of luxury while responding to
growing expectations regarding sustainable practices (e.g., Shrivastava et al., 2025).

Existing research on sustainable luxury has primarily examined this tension from the perspective of
consumers, focusing on attitudes, intentions, and perceived value. While these studies provide
important insights into how sustainability influences luxury consumption, they offer limited
understanding of how sustainability is strategically integrated into luxury service -based contexts such
as hospitality. As a result, the communication processes through which luxury hotels articulate, frame,
and operationalize sustainability remain underexplored (Athwal et al., 2019).

In this study, luxury is described as a set of high -quality, high-priced goods or services that trigger the
hedonic and eudaimonic happiness of customers. These two outcomes – and the underlying experiences
– are achieved by signaling authenticity, aesthetics, exclusivity (Holmqvist & Diaz Ruiz, 2020; Ko et al.,
2019), as well as sustainable practices (Batat et al., 2025). The literature explains that the first type of
happiness relates to pleasure and enjoyment, while the second requires meaning and purpose (Batat,
2022). Indeed, the term “eudaimonia” means “well -being”, and, in this context, it refers to choices and
actions that are congruent with personal values and the true self (the “daimon”), contributing to its
growth (Ryan, 2001). This implies that the decision of high -end travelers to stay in green luxury hotels
can be a consequence of their pursuit of euda imonic happiness and wellbeing, since the hotel’s
(markedly) sustainable practices are supposedly in line with their identity and values (Jain et al., 2025).
Following this description, by extension, the new generation of luxury hospitality providers consists of
five-star hotels that offer the highest levels of comfort, service, maintenance, and décor
(Hotelleriesuisse, 2020), rooted in both individua l and collective well -being (Batat et al., 2025). Thus,
luxury services are described as extraordinary, exclusive experiences (Wirtz et al., 2020) that offer not
only pleasurable moments, but also well-being and self-realization (Iloranta, 2019).

Scholars argue that marketing communication is essential for luxury brands, enabling them to
emphasize intangible benefits and build an emotional connection with prospective customers, as brand
status depends significantly on the imaginary and perceptions of consumers (Kyrousi et al ., 2022).
Furthermore, incorporating sustainability into marketing, driven by consumer demand, can provide
luxury hotels with a competitive edge, especially among customers who exhibit “green” behaviors or
have an interest in “slow travel” (Rishi et al ., 2015). Findings suggest that luxury hotel messages
emphasizing sustainability, rather than customer service, tend to drive higher booking intentions,
especially among clients who care about environmental issues (Amatulli et al., 2021).

The importance of sustainability in luxury hotel marketing also makes it valuable to examine how it is
communicated, especially given the apparent paradox between (pro-self) luxury services and pro-social
behavior (Alghamin & Ndubisi, 2022; Athwal et al., 2019). Additionally, scholars highlight the challenge
of balancing the exclusivity expected of luxury brands with the digital accessibility demanded by newer
consumers, such as Millennials (Oliveira & Fernandes, 2022). Although this topic holds signific ant
research potential, luxury marketing communication is still described as “an uncharted research area”
(Kyrousi et al., 2022, p. 1), with a similar consideration can be made about sustainable luxury, for which
there has been a call for more studies (Pereira et al., 2021; Athwal et al., 2019).

This study addresses the research gap by examining how sustainability is integrated into the marketing
communications of luxury hotels in Switzerland, along with the motivations behind this choice.
Switzerland provides a relevant context due to its strong commitment to be positioned as a sustainable
tourism destination (Switzerland Tourism, 2024a). This gap is particularly relevant given the growing
prominence of sustainability in tourism and hospitality markets. Recent research indicates a growing,
yet amb ivalent, interest in sustainable travel experiences, combining heightened environmental
awareness with increasing fatigue toward a lack of “sustainability in action” (Booking.com, 2024).

To achieve our research objective, we opted for a qualitative case study, triangulating evidence from
multiple sources: semi-structured interviews with luxury hotel managers, academic literature, industry
reports, as well as online travel reviews from two hotel groups and four five -star hotels. Findings were
then used to create a framework illustrating the reasons (i.e., “why”) and activities (i.e., “how”) involved
in integrating sustainability into luxury hotel marketing communications. The theoretical sig nificance
of this study lies thus in the exploration of marketing communication practices within luxury hotels
that invest in sustainability. Leveraging on signaling theory (Shahid et al., 2024), it contributes to the
literature (e.g., Amatulli et al., 2021; Pereira et al., 2021; Sahin et al., 2020) by investigating whether the
promotion of experiential luxury within hospitality can be successfully performed and how it should be
integrated. This research is relevant because, on the one hand, the concept of luxury is changing – from
“more and more” to “less is more” – because of a variety of factors, including demand (Liu et al., 2023).
On the other hand, the subjectivity and inconspicuousness of experiential luxury (Iloranta, 2019) can
open the door for a full integration of sustainability principles within the understanding of luxury as a
concept – something which was anticipa ted in broader business research (e.g., Hennings et al., 2013)
but never explored (to the best of our knowledge) from a hotel -manager viewpoint. Consequently, the
practical significance of this study lies in the exploration of reasons, challenges and solutions for the
fruitful integration of sustainability in  luxury hotel marketing by comparing diverse hotels on this
subject; an activity that managers do not necessarily have the resources to perform. Indeed, there is an
important difference between acting sustainable, knowing how to act, and knowing how to
communicate that act – a topic of interest for luxury hotels that have integrated sustainability in their
business model (e.g., Wurst, 2023).

2. Theoretical background
2.1. Aligning sustainability and luxury: a signaling theory perspective
Although for many years luxury and sustainability have been perceived as opposites (Alghamin &
Ndubisi, 2022), the pressure of accountability and a heightened sensitivity toward sustainable
purchasing from the demand side have led to a reconsideration of t he relationship between these two
concepts (e.g., Jestratijevic et al., 2020). In this regard, scholars identify numerous communal elements.
The functional dimension of luxury includes quality and durability; the latter is at the core of
sustainability, while craftsmanship and authenticity create the foundation for high quality products and
services (Hennings et al., 2013).

From a marketing perspective, personal values and intimate self -disclosure – that is, revealing one’s
engagement in sustainability – matter for successful promotion of sustainable luxury (Shan & Xu, 2025).
Indeed, this phenomenon occurs because high -end products and services are identitarian and reflect
one’s personal orientation (Hennings et al., 2013). This personal, emotional dimension arguably explains
the rising importance of meaningful experiences over conspicuous brand names. Within the
phenomenon of quiet luxury, simplicity allows wealthy customers to send discreet signals that can only
be captured by their own kind (Eckhardt et al., 2015) – since showing off to the masses is not a concern
for them (Taylor et al., 2025). Eventually, simplicity – in contrast to abundance and conspicuousness –
is part of sustainability and thus creates an additional point of contact between these two concepts,
paving the way to the promotion of sustainable luxury brands (e.g., Isla m et al., 2022). Furthermore,

customers opting for voluntary simplicity are arguably interested in human and environmental well -
being – thus, taking a holistic approach to consumption desires (Cristini et al., 2022). Pressure from
customers has been identified as an important driver for luxury hotels to start communicating about
sustainability (Mariani, 2022).

Signaling theory helps framing the alignment between luxury and sustainability for a unique value
proposition (Shrivastava et al., 2025), and its appropriate communication (e.g., Zhang et al., 2026). This
theory examines contexts in which two parties face an information imbalance. In such situations, the
actor possessing the information – that is, the sender – must determine which elements to disclose to
the other party – that is, the receive r. Signals are typically defined as actions or characteristics that,
whether intentionally or unintentionally, shape others’ beliefs or transmit information within a market
setting. While the sender decides how to formulate and transmit the signal, the rec eiver is responsible
for evaluating and interpreting the information conveyed ( Osburg et al., 2024). Brands can use visible
cues to communicate otherwise unobservable qualities and shape consumer judgments. For instance,
established luxury labels may reduce consumers’ reluctance toward repurposed goods by signaling
reliability and craftsmanship.  High-priced sustainable luxury can function as markers of prosocial
commitment, enabling consumers to display altruistic or status -oriented motives. Accordingly, some
consumers may choose sustainable luxury not only for environmental or social reasons but  also for the
prestige associated with these signals (Ye, 2025).

2.2. Sustainable luxury marketing: a supply viewpoint
The concept of sustainable luxury dates back to Bendell and Kleanthous (2007), who described it as
brands that prioritize sustainability as a core value and apply it concretely throughout their supply
chains, respecting both people and the planet. Engaging younger audiences, such as Generations Y and
Z, who show strong interest in sustainability (Brydges et al., 2022), requires clear communication on
these efforts (e.g., Pencarelli et al., 2020). Since the introduction of the concept, research has explored
this theme extensively, with Athwal et al . (2019) categorizing studies related to sustainable luxury
marketing into three thematic groups: consumer perspectives, cross -cultural aspects, and
organizational issues and activities. The latter mainly consists of the efforts in communicating properly
about sustainable initiatives to avoid “greenwashing” accusations, overcome challenges arising from
possible conflict between luxury imaginary and eco -friendly activities, and ensure supply chain
transparency – including information about local sourcing.

The concept of “green marketing” finds its origins in the late 1980s (Peattie & Crane, 2005), and refers
to business practices aimed at creating, marketing, pricing, and delivering products with a minimal
impact on the environment (Tiwari et al ., 2011). The phenomenon of “greenwashing” occurs when
enterprises engage in symbolic communication about pro-environment actions, that are not backed by
genuine actions (Ruiz -Blanco et al ., 2022). Recent studies highlight regulatory efforts to combat
greenwashing, as vague and misleading sustainability claims have eroded public trust (Peng et al., 2024).
These efforts include sanctions and clearer guidelines that define deceptive claims, aiming to improve
disclosure transparency.

Even when firms adopt sustainable practices, they may hesitate to promote them for fear of being seen
as insincere or exaggerated, which could harm their reputation (Chan, 2013). This phenomenon, known
in the literature as “green hushing”, consists in unde r-communicating the hotels’ sustainable actions
(Shen et al ., 2020). In the hospitality sector, customers may suspect that certain environmental
messages are only meant to cut operational expenses, such as encouraging guests to reduce energy
consumption or reuse their towels (Majeed & Kim, 2022). Thus, to mitigate  “greenwashing” concerns,
Majeed and Kim (2022) suggest implementing appropriate green marketing activities and independent
certifications.

For luxury hotels, sustainable marketing is particularly challenging due to the potential for
greenwashing accusations as well as perceived conflicts between luxury and eco-friendly services (Peng
& Chen, 2019). This dilemma affects the broader luxury sector, from watchmaking to tourism. Yet,
scholars argue that sustainability can align with the luxury essence, as both highlight quality, creativity,
and preservation. For this reason, luxury groups consider sustainability as a key success factor. Yet, they
may still limit their communication on the topic to appeal to a broader audience (Kapferer & Michaut -
Denizeau, 2020). In the tourism sector, sustainability arguably represents a key component for business
success (Amatulli et al., 2021). Addressing this claim, a study by Amatulli et al. (2021) shows, for instance,
that sustainability-focused communication, as opposed to service-focused, leads to a higher willingness
to book a luxury hotel room – thus, providing strong support for hoteliers to engage in sustainable
marketing activities.

In addition, an evolving view of luxury supports the integration of sustainability. Modern concept of
luxury emphasizes non -material, experiential values such as time, space, tranquility and cultural
authenticity – over material opulence (Spode et al ., 2019). This perspective aligns well with green
initiatives, as providing unique, immersive experiences in nature or meaningful cultural connections
enhances the perception of luxury while supporting environmental responsibility. In this sense,
sustainable luxury emphasizes quality over quantity and caters to travelers seeking self -fulfillment and
authenticity.

Engaging in sustainable practices should also involve efforts toward supply chain transparency, with
companies clearly communicating – for example, on their websites (Suluo et al., 2023) – about sourcing
and suppliers (Jestratijevic et al ., 2020). In the hospitality context, marketing research shows that
effective communication on locally sourced products is important for restaurants, and advertising
messages focused on community -benefits and preservation can be enticing for customers (Kim  et al.,
2022). By extension, strong connections with local suppliers and culinary heritage arguably creates a
unique selling proposition for luxury hotels, adding authenticity through storytelling and encouraging
guests to further explore local sources  (Franco et al ., 2022). Millennials and Gen Z members, who
account for 30% of new customers in the luxury goods market since 2019, are particularly receptive to
this type of communication and arguably driven by “authentic, purpose -driven, transparent brands”
(Bharwani & Mathews, 2023, p. 231).

2.3. Digital marketing for luxury tourism and hospitality
Digital marketing encompasses “all marketing efforts that use an electronic device or internet”,
including the use of channels such as website, email, social media, and search engines (Desai, 2019, p.
196). The range of available digital tools, for both suppliers and consumers, has made digital marketing
highly interactive, user-centered, and measurable, driven by both supply and demand factors (Piñeiro -
Otero & Martínez-Rolán, 2016). Taking a communication perspective, the key pillars and processes of
digital marketing activities have been outlined within the Online Communication Model (OCM), which
underlines the importance of aligning material (software and hardware) with human resources within
a market context to maximize the appeal of online content to audiences that bring their own values and
preferences also in the online environment (Mele & Cantoni, 2018). These OCM elements can be
expressed in terms of content and functionalities, technical (accessibility) tools, managers, users, and
external environment (Figure 1). Following the model, managers should refrain from merely “targeting”
prospects or clients. Instead, they are encouraged to foster meaningful dialogue through active listening
– supported by digital analytics – and by delivering carefully tailored conten t that integrates the
promotion of sustainable practices (Kalbaska et al ., 2018). These features make the model arguably
suitable to guide the investigation on sustainability integration in luxury hotel marketing.

For the luxury industry, digital marketing is an inevitable business shift, which is expected by
Millennials, baby boomers and even older generations; a dynamic that comes with benefits (Oliveira &
Fernandes, 2022) and several challenges, including the ten sion between exclusivity and (digital)
accessibility as well as creating a seamless customer experience across all touchpoints (Abtan et al.,
2016). Indeed, research shows that when luxury goods are perceived as too democratic – that is,
accessible and mass -produced —, they lose their appeal as symbols of conspicuous consumption,
making them less attractive (Shukla et al., 2022). However, luxury’s traditional focus on conspicuous
consumption is now evolving to coexist with the emergence of inconspicuous behavior and interest in
social connections (Shukla et al., 2025), where greater accessibility, due to globalization and ICT, allows
luxury to appeal to a wider socioeconomic audience. New values, especially among younger consumers,
increasingly include personal fulfillment, intrinsic experiential value, self -directed pleasure, and
sustainability (Atkinson & Kang, 2022). Indeed, Generation Z, as an emerging consumer segment in the
luxury sector, has been recognized as a force that has pushed toward increased accessibility,
digitalization, and sustainability withi n the industry. Compared to the preceding generations, their
interest in social media, ethical consumption, and experiences over possession has driven luxury brands
toward a stronger focus on sustainable participation and digital presence, with virtual pla tforms
becoming an opportunity for enhanced personalization and interactivity (Ye & Kim, 2024).

Other elements that characterize the luxury industry, making it unique, include its reliance not only on
marketing but also on consumer perception, with much of its brand equity built within consumers’
imaginary (Kyrousi et al ., 2022). In luxury advertising, the goal is often less about selling products
directly than creating an aspirational image, which can be undermined by sales growth or over-exposure
in the media (Kapferer & Bastien, 2009). The object of luxury has changed, with “traditional” categories
like clothing, watches and jewelry, now joined by the rise of “experiential luxury”, including hotels,
restaurants, or spas (Guido et al., 2020) – which is arguably a manifestation of the social megatrend that
prioritizes experiences over possessions (Lehmann, 2020).

Research on digital marketing for luxury hospitality and tourism has investigated the importance of
websites for luxury hotels (Baloglu & Peckan, 2006), customer segmentation (Zhang et al., 2022; Huh et
al., 2019; Wu & Yang, 2018), and repurchase intentions (Peng & Chen, 2019a). A literature review
identifies four macro groups in luxury tourism marketing research: consumer consumption and
behavior, service marketing, and brand management (Iloranta, 2022). A n analysis of the literature on
luxury hotels – conducted by Chu et al. (2016) – has revealed that marketing and technology are the
most frequently researched themes, and that there is a noticeable lack of qualitative research addressing
the “how” questions. In a qualitative study, Kapoor and Kapoor (2021) explain, for insta nce, that social
media marketing can be beneficial for luxury hotels by helping them build a strong social footprint,
engage with current or potential customers and enhance brand recall through authentic storytelling.
Given the time -intensive nature of soc ial media management, luxury hotels tend to contract external
agencies to perform the job for them. Other qualitative research shows that sustainability initiatives in
luxury hotels can positively impact customer satisfaction online, including on social me dia platforms
(Pereira et al ., 2021). The reason(s) and the process behind their integration remain, although,
underexplored in scientific research, suggesting a significant area for further academic inquiry.

The integration of sustainability into luxury hotel marketing has emerged as both a necessity and a
challenge, given the historical perception of luxury and sustainability as contradictory concepts. Recent
shifts in consumer values – particularly among you nger, environmentally conscious travelers – have
prompted luxury hotels to align their offerings with principles of well -being, authenticity, and
environmental responsibility. However, despite growing interest, the literature reveals a significant gap
in understanding how sustainability is effectively communicated in luxury hospitality contexts. While
prior research has explored sustainable luxury from consumer and organizational perspectives, little is

3. Methodology
To explore the reasons (“why”) and methods (“how”) behind the integration of sustainability in luxury
hotel marketing communications, we opted for an in -depth qualitative case study. This research
approach was considered appropriate for our purpose since i t had already been successfully applied in
previous research on sustainability and hospitality (Gutiérrez -Martínez & Duhamel, 2019). Qualitative
research is well-suited to provide “rich, in-depth knowledge from multiple viewpoints”, while shedding
light on processes and reasons underlying tourism phenomena (Jennings, 2012, p. 309). This research
followed a multiple -case study design, and the unit of analysis (i.e., “the case”) was the integration of
sustainability in luxury hotel marketing. The rationale fo r multiple-case design was literal replication,
through the selection of cases that would provide exemplary outcomes in relation to the focus of enquiry
(Yin, 2018). Developing convergent evidence, data triangulation was obtained through multiple sources
of evidence: semi-structured interviews, online travel reviews (OTRs), academic literature, and industry
reports.

To select interview participants, we used maximum variation sampling (Saunders et al ., 2019), a
purposive technique allowing the identification of “information -rich cases for the most proper
utilization of available resources” – which results in a list of (very diverse) participants with relevant
information and experience for the phenomenon of interest (Etikan et al., 2016, p. 2). In Switzerland, at
the time of the study, we found a total of 57 five -star (or 5 -star superior) hotels holding the
“Swisstainable” label which measures and certifies sustainable practices undertaken by accommodation
providers and offers guidance to customers (Switzerland Tourism, 2024b). Through professional
contacts, we secured participation from representatives of 4 l uxury hotels and 2 luxury hotel groups
(each representing multiple hotels in Switzerland). This corresponded to approximately 10% of the
original pool of hotels. These varied in terms of characteristics, sustainability efforts, and locations
(Table 1). As performed in previous research (Pereira et al., 2021), we conducted one interview per hotel
with one or two key informants each. Following the recommendation from Eisenhardt (1989), multiple
investigators (3) were involved in designing the interview instruments and protocols.

Interview questions were structured according to a theoretical framework, namely the OCM, which
describes the characteristics, actors, and processes involved in online communication, summarized as
content, accessibility tools (e.g., user interface), manage rs, users, and the external environment (e.g.,
outside environment, trends, and competitors) ( Figure 1 ). These components are then part of an
iterative process of evaluation and design. The external environment has an influence on the
expectations and behavior of visitors as well as on the decisions of the managers (Cantoni et al., 2003).
The original framework was designed for online communication via websites; however, its
characteristics allow for application across diverse platforms. In this study, its use as a guiding structure
is justified by its clear mapping of online comm unication actors, objects, and contexts, as well as its
relevance for examining the integration of sustainable practices into marketing activities (e.g., Kalbaska
et al., 2018) – specifically, for what concerns the responsibility of managers to include the dimensions
of sustainability into promotional content and the importance of persuading users of the benefits
associated with sustainable practices. Finally, interview questions were adapted from the literature,
previous qualitative research on sustainable practices in luxury hotels (Pereira et al ., 2021), and an
industry report on effective marketing for eco -conscious hotels (Cendyn , 2024). Consequently, after
formal introductions, interviewees were asked about the integration of sustainable practices, online
communication strategies, their knowledge of eco -conscious customers and their perceptions, as well
as marketing performance e valuation activities. The relationship between the interview and the OCM
is outlined in Table 3 (Annex).

All semi-structured interviews – except one written response from a manager (#lux1) who could not be
available for an online or on -site interview – were conducted online between March and May 2024, in
German, French, or English depending on participant pre ference. With explicit consent from the hotel
managers, all conversations were recorded to ensure accurate transcription. Furthermore, anonymity
was guaranteed by assigning participant codes and by making properties unidentifiable. The duration
of the interviews ranged from 30 to 45 minutes. The semi-structured format was chosen for its flexibility
to explore topics in depth, follow -up on unexpected answers, and let interviewees express their
viewpoints “without the constraints of scripted questions” (Tracy , 2020a, p. 158).

Interview data were triangulated with academic literature, industry reports, and OTRs extrapolated
from the Customer Experience Platform (TrustYou , n.d.) which aggregates OTRs about hotels, from
over 220 sources, into categories (e.g., sustainability) and sub -categories (e.g., sustainable sourcing)
through automated semantic analysis. The number of categories varies depending on whether a certain
topic is mentioned in the reviews with the tool tracking the frequency of these mentions; a single review
may include mentions across multiple categories or sub -categories. For data triangulation, the
“sustainability” category and the sub -categories “excursi on” (within “location”) and “biking” (within
“sporting activities”) were pre -selected for analysis, given the importance of natural attractors in
Switzerland (Mele & Egberts, 2023). Combining an inductive approach – which is common in qualitative
research (Tracy, 2020b) – this selection was confirmed following an exploration of the data provided by
the Customer Experience Platform (TrustYou, n.d.).

The “sustainability” category captures those mentions related to socio -economic and environmental
dimensions, such as the local sourcing of food and beverage. The “excursion” sub -category contains
references to tours and activities that highlight the aesth etic and experiential value of the destination,
including slow-mobility options, like biking and waterside walks, which are important for sustainable
mobility (Maltese et al ., 2017). The selection of this sub -category was supported by the idea that
sustainable luxury hotels should promote responsible activities that respect and value the destination’s
cultural and natural resources (Luxonomy, 2023). Over a two -year range (01. 06.2022-31.05.2024), we
investigated whether the topic of sustainability emerged from the OTRs for the sampled luxury hotels.

OTRs were analyzed for two main reasons. First, as part of data triangulation, the purpose was to
strengthen the validity of the findings by cross-verifying claims made by hotel managers with real-world
guest experiences and perceptions. The combination of  interviews with destination stakeholders with
customers viewpoints expressed in OTRs had been also done in past tourism research to investigate the
creation and marketing of an experience (e.g., Heimtun  & Haug, 2022). Second, this data source
provided unfiltered, spontaneous feedback from guests, offering insights on sustainability perceptions
by actual customers – who also consider user -generated content as an instrumental source of
information, where s ustainability-related considerations can be reported (Pacheco et al ., 2024).
Therefore, OTRs complemented the managerial viewpoint and helped assess whether marketing
messages would resonate with the audience.

Within data triangulation, industry reports (or guides) were meant to bring insights produced by
organizations, agencies, and businesses related to the luxury sector. Anytime it was applicable, their
purpose was to validate or challenge findings from inter views and academic literature, as done in
previous tourism research (e.g., Bramwell & Meyer, 2007). Furthermore, we integrated them to
contextualize the findings within a wider hospitality landscape, while highlighting trends or best
practices that would not necessarily emerge from interviews. A total of 6 industry-produced documents
(reports or guides) were included in the data triangulation process (Annex, Table 4).

To summarize, this study aims to explore how sustainability is incorporated into the marketing
strategies of luxury hotels in Switzerland, and to understand the motivations, methods, and challenges
associated with this integration. Our unit of analysis (i.e., “the case”) was the integration of sustainable
efforts in the marketing activities of luxury hotels, which functioned as “the context” in the case study
design. Data triangulation strengthened our findings and helped to delineate content themes in the
corpus. First, a within-case analysis provided familiarity with the data and allowed for the identification

4. Findings
Data triangulation from interviews, scientific articles, industry reports, and OTRs, revealed three
macro-themes: (1) integration of sustainability practices, (2) sustainability marketing, and (3)
communication challenges. Each theme is supported by eviden ce from multiple sources. Extracts from
the sampled OTRs offered examples of the language and wording used by clients. Findings from the
Customer Experience Platform (TrustYou, n.d.) ( Table 2 ) indicate that while sustainability -related
mentions are present in the sampled OTRs, their absolute frequency is substantially lower compared to
mentions of other categories, such as hotel, food, and room reviews. To provide a context, over a seven-
year period, an average of only 10% of the comments on TripAdvisor’s top 10 green hotels mentioned
sustainability elements (Yi et al., 2016).

This outcome can be explained by different elements. First, previous academic research indicates that
only one out of three luxury segments places emphasis on sustainability. For most customers, however,
top priorities remain cuisine, design, and facilitie s when selecting a 5 -star hotel (Mele et al., 2024).
Moreover, sustainability may now be perceived as a standard expectation rather than a distinctive
feature worth mentioning in reviews (e.g., Philip, 2022). From these premises, the following subsections
explore the integration of sustainability i n luxury hotels, its role in marketing, and the associated
communication challenges. Information from interviews is either presented as direct quotation or
summarized; in both cases the property number is indicated for transparency. Findings from the
interviews were triangulated with academic literature, industry reports, and extracts from OTRs.
References are provided throughout to allow a clear distinction between sources.

4.1. Integration of sustainability practices
Sustainable practices at the sampled properties addressed the natural environment, sustainable
management, and societal aspects. One interviewee (#lux3) underlined efforts focused solely on
environmental sustainability and hotel management, whereas the oth ers also emphasized their
investments in social sustainability. These commitments were certified via national labels – namely, the
“Swisstainable” certificate – and international ones, like Green Globe (2024). Research shows that green
certifications enhance the effectiveness of advertising for luxury hotels by making their offered services
more tangible in a high-involvement context (Sahin et al., 2020).

Interviews revealed that sustainability principles extended beyond processes or products (e.g., recycling
rates and gastronomic offerings) to include hotel infrastructure initiatives, like offsetting CO2 emissions
through geothermal energy and heat recover y. This approach to integrating sustainability in luxury
hospitality aligns with previous research, which highlights the importance of preserving natural
ecosystems not only for diversity and abundance, but also to enhance location attractiveness (Pereira
et al., 2021). Indeed, the latter influences hotel selection and significantly impacts guest movements
(Shoval et al., 2011). Activities at the destination and experiences related to its heritage also emerged
from the interviews, as reported in this extract:

“[…] I would say that almost all year round, we regularly have packages that allow clients to enjoy a
fairly local experience. For example, we have packages where the client, in addition to their overnight
stay, gets a guided city tour by electric bike or can go for a walk through the vineyards. There are also
small electric tuk -tuks. So, there's a driver and then six seats in the back.” [#lux3, translated from
French]

4.2. Sustainability marketing
Sustainability marketing and communication, as revealed through interviews, are managed internally,
sometimes by a dedicated team of people (#lux 1, 4 -6), with support from the marketing and HR
departments (#lux5). External parties contribute by offering e nergy-saving training for employees
(#lux3), copywriting and public relations to spread sustainability messages (#lux2), and gathering
detailed information and insights on the topic (#lux1). This aligns with a broader trend of luxury brands
establishing dedicated teams to handle sustainability marketing communications, addressing concerns
about unsustainable and irresponsible practices (Nallaperuma et al., 2022).

Findings suggest that the primary channels for communicating sustainability to customers include the
hotel’s website dedicated section, social media platforms, and newsletters. Other methods mentioned
by interviewees include the hotel’s magazine (#lux3), interviews with local actors (#lux 4, 6), initiatives
like sustainable guest programs and university lectures (#lux1). Specific marketing activities performed
at the properties include showcasing sustainable features on websites and other platforms, launchi ng
online campaigns aimed at eco -conscious audiences through dedicated channels (#lux1, 5 -6), and
maintaining a blog focused on sustainability efforts (#lux1). Research highlights the value of blogs in
enhancing Search Engine Optimization, brand awareness,  and customer engagement (Almeida &
Magalhães, 2022). Social media platforms, like Facebook and Instagram, are particularly relevant for
connecting with younger luxury consumers (Oliveira & Fernandes, 2022; Achille et al., 2018), as they
enable the sharing of sustainability-related stories and partnerships, including collaborations with local
suppliers (#lux 1, 5-6). The platform LinkedIn can also be occasionally used for these communications
(#lux3).

Hotel managers can go beyond the online promotion of sustainable practices at their properties  by
informing guests about (sustainable) experiences or activities to be done at the destination . Indeed,

sustainability issues (#lux3). One interviewee (#lux6) estimated that at least 30-35% of their guests were

representative (#lux2) declared that they would not collect data on client perceptions of sustainability,
while another (family hotel, #lux5) noted minimal interest among their clientele (91% Swiss, 42–48 years

customer segmentation – for which demographics and travel purpose (leisure vs. business) were rather

4.3. Communication challenges
Numerous obstacles have been identified through the interviews when it comes to the communication
or promotion of sustainable practices by luxury hotels. First, a portion of the clientele does not seem to
be interested in or sensitive to sustainability issues (#lux 3-4). According to an interviewee, this applies
especially to visitors from the Middle East and India (#lux4):

The most eco-conscious travelers are Europeans, like those from Germany or even the Swiss ones […].
The least [eco-conscious] are guests from India […], Middle East, for whom [this] is really not a
priority. [#lux4, translated from French]

Second, different customers have diverse expectations in terms of sustainability, which increases the
complexity of a communication strategy on the topic (#lux2). Challenging points include
communicating the match between sustainability and luxury without making it appear as a
contradiction, towel re-use, and the lack of products that are out of season (#lux6). Another interviewee
(#lux5) also mentions that social issues – which are part of sustainability – are difficult to communicate;
staff turnover and employees’ integration of sustainability values add another layer of complexity to the
matter (#lux2). Finally, the representatives of one hotel group (#lux1) underline that the greatest
challenge is communicating sustainability appropriately for the refere nce audience(s). The timing,
amount, and depth of the communication content must be chosen according to the recipient. Indeed,
it must not sound like lecturing; it should be rather understood as added value to the experience.
Furthermore, as reported by the representative of one property (#lux3), the right balance must be found
between marketing communications and the actions undertaken by the hotel, to avoid any
greenwashing critique.

The interviews reveal that measuring the success of sustainability -related communication is
challenging. Indeed, because of its complexity, none of the luxury hotel representatives confirmed the
use of a measurement system to assess the impact of sustainab ility messages on consumer behavior.
One interviewee (#lux2) mentioned customer satisfaction surveys as a starting point, while the
representatives of one hotel group (#lux1) highlighted that persistence and repetition were key for the
success of any communication effort:

That is very difficult to measure. It simply takes a long breath and repeated communication in
exchange – according to the motto “Constant dripping wears away the stone”.  [#lux1, translated from
German]

The lack of a measurement system corresponded in most cases to a lack of information, from the
managers’ perspective, on the possible changes brought by sustainable marketing activities. One
interviewee (#lux3) mentioned the lack of data on the subject fro m the OTAs’ and social media
platforms; other hotel managers describe sustainability as a topic for hoteliers rather than guests (#lux
5-6). Finally, the representatives from a luxury hotel group (#lux1) reported one change in consumer
behavior that they could observe:

[…] 10 years ago, for example, we would have received complaints if we did not provide small shampoo
bottles in the bathrooms instead of a big soap and shampoo suspenders (which is almost normal today)
[…]. The same applies to the change of towels and bed linen, which are only changed every 3 days, unless

The information provided by the interviewees was triangulated with other data sources, including the
sustainability-related mentions and subcategories extracted through the Customer Experience Platform
(TrustYou, n.d.). The platform revealed that some guests expressed their negative views about the
scarcity of sustainable -certified food (#lux2) or the presence of plastic bathroom bottles for the
shampoo (instead of refillable containers) (#lux3). These are just examples indicating that, at least for a
part of the customers, sustainability is an important matter (and not only for the hoteliers). This
observation is in line with the results from research about luxury guest segmentation (Mele et al., 2024)
and industry insights about the importance of sustainable practices for eco -conscious travelers
(Cendyn, 2024). Furthermore, interview data reveal that interest in sustainability may also be expressed
indirectly by clients:

In fact, I would say that the main interest – which is not an explicit request [about sustainability] – is
something that clients really like: [that is], having in their dish […] a lot of local products. Our teams

Addressing the challenge of the diverse expectations in terms of sustainability raised by one interviewee
(#lux2), in one OTR we indeed found a guest appreciating sustainably sourced local products, while
expecting the temperature in the heated bathtub to be higher:

[Great] location, bedding, common areas, outdoor bath, quality of the breakfast (with local products).
Quality of the cuisine […]. […] The temperature of the heated bathtub would benefit from 3 extra

The barriers mentioned so far share as a common denominator the difficulty in matching the luxury
realm of 5 -star hotels with eco -friendly practices, combining indulgence and responsibility, high -end
comfort and environmental consciousness (Glion, 2024). T his challenge is recognized in academic
research (e.g., Amatulli et al., 2021). Yet, a paradigm shift is taking place, with sustainability becoming
a competitive element for luxury hotels; “high quality, superior durability, and deeper value” represent
a solid base “for the design and marketing of products that preserve fund amental social and
environmental values” (Hennings et al., 2013, p. 25). As reported by one hotel manager, sustainability
can be a differentiator for certain markets:

I believe most of our guests consider it important. [...] And I think in Switzerland and Germany,
sustainability is probably a widely recognized topic. [#lux6, translated from German]

5. Discussion and conclusion
This study analyzed the integration of sustainability in luxury hotel marketing, by taking the case of
Swiss hotels. In line with the research objective, the OCM (Cantoni et al., 2003) served as the theoretical
framework guiding data collection within the case study methodology. Overall, findings from data
triangulation show that luxury hotels can integrate marketing communications about sustainable
practices by assigning the ta sk to an internal team; employee training is essential for the consistent
application of sustainable principles and their communication in off - and on-line environments, like
the hotel’s website and social media channels. Despite the numerous barriers that  hotel managers
encounter in the promotion of sustainable practices – e.g., fear of greenwashing, guests’ diverse
expectations in terms of sustainability – their implementation underscores, on the one hand, the

significant relevance that sustainable living has for both companies and consumers (Zuniga, 2024). On
the other hand, by valuing the local ecosystem (e.g., farms, vineyards, local products), luxury hotels
contribute to the preservation of the uniqueness an d attractiveness of the destination, including the
pull factors that appeal to present and future guests. In other words, the location becomes a source of
sustainable competitive advantage for entrepreneurs, which is “hard to imitate and substitute for”
(McKeever et al., 2015, p. 62).

From a consumer perspective, these activities appear to be especially relevant to two types of guests,
who (clearly) stood out from the interviews: the Anglo -Saxon and corporate guests, including MICE
events. The former come from a public system where sust ainability transparency is high (Navarro -
Galera et al., 2017). In terms of business travel, the relevance of a sustainable travel policy for companies
can be a product of several factors, including public accountability, especially for large organizations
that are “Publicly Limited Companies”, customer press ure, organizational culture, as well as the
recruitment and retention strategy. Indeed, “a business travel policy that shows that the organization
cares about their staff and the environment can help to attract the best talent to the organization”
(Roby, 2014, p. 30).

From a psychological perspective, the destination -based experiences identified through the interviews
(e.g., farm picnics, vineyard tours) can become transformative because they potentially allow guests to
reconnect with nature, support local communities, and experience authenticity (Teoh et al ., 2021).
These experiences may arguably fulfill eudaimonic needs – such as meaning, purpose, and congruence
with one’s values (Hamby et al., 2023) – rather than just hedonic pleasure. This can be especially relevant
for the “quiet luxury” segment, where customers seek discreet, meaningful and identity -relevant
experiences over conspicuous consumption (Cho & Cho, 2025). Using these findings, we have
elaborated an explanatory model of sustainability integration in luxury hotel marketing. The framework
illustrates the relationships between drivers and activities, which are then operationalized through
explicit and implicit signaling mechanisms for strategic content diversification (Figure 2).

5.1. Theoretical implications
Our research findings offer three main theoretical contributions. First, guided by the OCM (Kalbaska
et al., 2018), this qualitative study identifies drivers and activities involved in marketing sustainability
for luxury hotels ( Figure 2 ). Therefore, it brings novel insights into hospitality research, where the
investigation of the communication-side of sustainable practices is scarce (Kim et al., 2019). Specifically,
we show that managers actively frame sustainability as an experiential and value -enhancing attribute
rather than a moral or functional claim. This perspective contributes to the literature (Schuck et al.,
2026; Grigorescu & Ion, 2022) by repositioning sustainable luxury as a result of a strategic value
proposition design and narrative construction, rather than the sole reflection of consumer psychological
states.

Second, our findings expand past communication research on sustainable luxury (Osburg et al., 2022)
by advancing that sustainability communication in luxury hotels can be integrated as a dual -layer
signaling system (e.g., Kapoor et al., 2026). While prior research has highlighted the paradox between
luxury and sustainability (Alghamin & Ndubisi, 2022), our findings suggest that this tension is not
resolved through segmentation alone (e.g., Hamilton & Wilson, 2025), but through the coex istence of
differentiated signaling practices that operate at different levels of visibility and interpretation. On the
one hand, explicit sustainability signals (e.g., certifications) are deployed in contexts where
transparency and accountability are expected, like corporate and MICE marke ts (e.g., Accor, 2025). On
the other hand, implicit sustainability signals are embedded within the symbolic and experiential
dimensions of luxury – such as craftmanship, local sourcing, and authenticity – allowing sustainability
to be communicated without disrupting the core luxury narrative via signal incongruence ( Osburg et
al., 2022). Indeed, acquiring or experiencing artistic productions, including luxury meals (Michael &
Fusté-Forné, 2022) – as nonutilitarian objects – is a powerful signal of social status and skill (e.g., Steel,
2013; Bird & Smith, 2005). This dual -layer si gnaling system arguably enables brands to maintain
symbolic coherence while addressing heterogeneous audience expectations – thus, having multiple,
coexisting signals that are strategically orchestrated within a single brand identity.

Third, our findings nuance prior research highlighting the “openness of emerging markets to
sustainable luxury”, describing it as a global phenomenon (Osburg et al., 2024: 728). Specifically, our
interviews suggest that cultural differences remain influential, shaping how customers perceive

sustainability in luxury hospitality – underscoring the importance of cultural adaptation in this context.
These contributions are in line with the proposed framework ( Figure 2 ), where the key challenges
identified through our data triangulation point at diverse perceptions of sustainability within the
customer base – up to a lack of interest – and the scarcity of resources (including knowledge) to properly
segment guests.

Supported by our findings, the above -mentioned theoretical implications lead to an advancement in
the conceptualization of a luxury brand, as new a construct that is not only “capable of inspiring a deep
connection, or resonance, with the consumer” (Ko et al ., 2019, p. 406), but it also embedded (i.e.,
connected) into the natural and socio -cultural ecosystem that produced and supported it. This
symbiotic embeddedness with the place (e.g., Hahn & Tampe, 2020) is a characteristic that brings value
(McKeever et al., 2015) and resources to offer authentic experiences, while underlining craftsmanship
and artisanship at premium prices; a series of attributes that mirror the diverse facets of sustainability
(e.g., Hennings et al ., 2013) and make it possible for brands to communicate about sustainable
experiences without explicitly mentioning the concept in their communications (see extract about local
produce in section 4.3).

5.2. Managerial implications
In terms of managerial implications, following research results, we suggest, first, luxury hotel managers
to invest in digital marketing, especially social media, because of their role in the decision -making
process (Zha et al., 2023) and – like all luxury advertisement – their dream-making power (Kapferer &
Bastien, 2009). Second, digital content should convey sustainable features implicitly, while highlighting
instead the derived hedonistic benefits for hotel guests (Font et al., 2018). This strategy would avoid
conflicts arising from audiences that do not understand or have no interest in sustainability.
Furthermore, it would contour the complications of making explicit environmental claims, which can
be subject to criticism or  strict regulations (e.g., Publyon , 2024). Finally, we suggest luxury hotel
managers adopt a destination-centric marketing strategy that emphasizes the co-creation of sustainable
experiences with local stakeholders. This would involve forging partnerships with local actors (e.g.,
farmers) to design meaningful guest experiences and highlighting the destination’s cultural and natural
assets in the marketing material, as part of the value proposition. Such activities will arguably be
appreciated also by those guests that are pushed by eudaimon ic motivations, since “eudaimonic well -
being can result from restorative experiences perceived as meaningful to tourists” ( Wattanacharoensil
et al., 2024, p. 5).

5.3. Limitations and future research
This research is not exempt from limitations. First, the results of this qualitative study are based on 10%
of the original pool of luxury hotels. Therefore, generalizations should be made with caution. Yet,
findings are arguably applicable to geographical ly and culturally close destinations in Europe, like
Austria and Germany. Second, OTRs were explored and categorized using a tool for automated semantic
analysis. Despite its quality, some reviews with content nuances related to sustainability might have
been ignored and thus excluded from the data triangulation. In addition to addressing these limitations,
future research should extend the sample by including 4 -star hotels and investigate the integration of
the so-called “regenerative” practices, which are meant to improve the local ecosystem.
