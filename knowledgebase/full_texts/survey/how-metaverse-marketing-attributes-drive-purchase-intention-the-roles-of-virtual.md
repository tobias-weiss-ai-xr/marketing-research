ABSTRACT
Metaverse environments are increasingly used by brands to create interactive, immersive,
personalized, and socially connected consumer experiences. However, the mechanism
through which these platform attributes translate into marketplace outcomes remains
fragmented, particularly in emerging markets. Drawing on the Stimulus –Organism–
Response (S –O–R) framework, this study develops an integrated model in which
interactivity, immersion, personalization, and social interaction shape virtual brand
experience (VBE), which subsequently drives consumer –brand engagement (CBE) and
purchase intention. Technology readiness is proposed as a boundary condition that
strengthens the conversion of VBE into CBE. For methodological demonstration, a
dataset of 432 Vietnamese metaverse-experienced consumers was generated with realistic

1 INTRODUCTION

The metaverse offers a digitally mediated consumption environment in which
consumers interact with branded spaces, products, avatars, and other users. By integrating
interactivity, immersion, personalization, and social particip ation, metaverse
environments provide opportunities to create brand experiences that extend beyond
conventional digital interfaces. Nevertheless, the availability of these attributes does not
itself explain their marketing effectiveness. A central theoreti cal and managerial issue
concerns how the characteristics of metaverse environments become meaningful brand
experiences and how these experiences translate into consumer –brand engagement and
purchase intention. Investigating this process is necessary to di stinguish the appeal of
technological features from their contribution to brand-related responses.
Previous research has established the importance of experiential and relational
mechanisms in metaverse marketing. Payal et al. (2024) demonstrate that interactivity
influences brand trust, brand knowledge, and brand attachment, which subsequently
contribute to active brand engagement and real-world purchase intention. Drawing on the
stimulus–organism–response (S–O–R) framework, Prashar and Prashar (2025) identify
interactivity, personalization, and sociability as antecedents of experiential and
immersive responses associated with purchase intention. Lee and Cho (2023), in turn,
conceptualize virtual brand experience (VBE) as a multidimensional response to digitally

Building on this literature, the present study addresses three interrelated research
needs. First, further examination is required to explain how multiple metaverse marketing
attributes contribute to brand -specific experience within an integrated framework.
Although previous studies identify relevant platform characteristics, their explanatory
mechanisms differ across settings and focal constructs. Examining interactivity,
immersion, personalization, and social inte raction simultaneously allows assessment of
their respective relationships with VBE within a common model. This distinction is
consequential because a favorable experience of a virtual environment does not
necessarily constitute a meaningful experience of the brand embedded within it. The
theoretical question, therefore, is how environmental attributes acquire brand -specific
experiential significance.
In addition, the process linking metaverse attributes to purchase intention requires
a clearer distinction between experiential and relational responses. VBE concerns
consumers’ responses to digitally mediated brand stimuli, whereas consumer –brand

interactions. Treating the se constructs as sequential organism states enables a more

study extends the experience –engagement perspective to an integrated metaverse
marketing model, examining whether environmental attributes contribute to VBE, which
subsequently relates to CBE and purchase intention. Its contribution lies in specifying and
examining this integrated pathway rather than claiming that the relationship between
brand experience and engagement is novel.
On the other hand, the association between VBE and CBE may vary according to
consumers’ technological orientations. Technology readiness provides a relevant
boundary condition because consumers differ in their propensity to embrace and use new

interactions. However, this moderating relationship requires empirical examina tion.

2 THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

2.1 STIMULUS–ORGANISM–RESPONSE FRAMEWORK

The Stimulus–Organism–Response framework explains how environmental cues
(stimuli) influence internal cognitive and affective states (organism), which then produce
approach or avoidan ce responses. In digital commerce, S –O–R is useful because
consumers respond not only to information but also to interface, sensory, social, and
interaction cues. Metaverse environments intensify this logic by combining interactive
control, immersive prese nce, personalization, and social co -presence. Consistent with
recent metaverse research using S –O–R (Prashar & Prashar, 2025), the present study
treats interactivity, immersion, personalization, and social interaction as environmental
stimuli. VBE is the first organism state because it captures the consumer’s brand-specific
experiential reaction to those stimuli; CBE is the second organism state because it
captures the cognitive, affective, and behavioral investment that follows a meaningful
experience. Purchase intention is the response.

2.2 VIRTUAL BRAND EXPERIENCE

Brand experience has become an important construct in marketing research
because consumers respond not only to the functional attributes of products and services
but also to the sensations, emotions, cognitions, and behavioral responses generated
through t heir interactions with brands. Brakus et al . (2009) conceptualized brand
experience as consumers’ subjective internal responses to brand -related stimuli arising
from brand design, identity, communication, packaging, and environments. They
identified four f undamental dimensions of brand experience: sensory, affective,
intellectual, and behavioral experience. Importantly, brand experience is conceptually
distinct from related constructs such as brand attitude, brand involvement, brand
attachment, brand person ality, and customer satisfaction because it captures the

experiential responses generated during encounters with brand-related stimuli rather than
consumers’ overall evaluations of the brand (Brakus et al., 2009).
The increasing digitalization of consumer –brand interactions has expanded the
concept of brand experience beyond physical consumption environments. Digital
technologies allow consumers to experience brands through interactive interfaces, virtual
environments, augmented reality, virtual reality, avatars, and other technology-mediated
touchpoints. Consequently, brand experiences can occur without direct physical contact
with products. This distinction is particularly relevant in immersive digital environments
because consumers can actively explore a nd interact with brands rather than simply
receive brand communications. In such environments, the boundary between mediated
and direct experience becomes increasingly blurred.
Building on traditional brand experience theory, Lee and Cho (2023) introduced
the concept of virtual brand experience (VBE) to explain consumer experiences generated
through digital reality advertising. They conceptualized VBE as a multidimensional
construct consisting of sensory, affective, intellectual, behavioral, and social dimensions.
The first four dimensions extend the established brand experience framework of Brakus
et al . (2009), whereas the social dimension captures an important characteristic of
digitally mediated environments: consumers can experience brands while simultaneously
interacting with or perceiving the presence of other users. Lee and Cho (2023) further
demonstrated that VBE can generate brand engagement and subsequent consumer
responses, with the social dimension showing particularly strong relevance to
engagement.
The inclusion of the social dimension is especially important in the metaverse.
Unlike conventional websites or traditional online advertising, metaverse environments
are typically designed as persistent or semi -persistent interactive spaces in which
consumers may be represented by avatars, communicate with other users, manipulate
virtual objects, explore branded environments, and participate in shared activities.
Consequently, the metaverse can transform brand experience from an individually
processed marketing exposure into an interactive and socially embedded experience.
Research on VR-based metaverse environments similarly suggests that immersive virtual
environments provide opportunities to generate cognitive, emotional, and behavioral
engagement and associated branding outcomes (Vernuccio et al., 2024).
Recent research further supports the importance of distinguishing metaverse
experience from conventional digital experience. Mingione et al. (2024), for example,

Similarly, empirical research increasingly demonstrates that immersive br and

Suprawan (2024) examined consumer responses to immersive brand experiences in the

of the metaverse does not reside merely in technological novelty; rather, its value depends
on the extent to which technological features are transformed into psychologicall y
meaningful experiences with the focal brand.
This distinction also separates virtual brand experience from the broader concept
of customer experience in the metaverse. Customer experience may encompass
consumers’ overall evaluation of navigating and usi ng a metaverse platform, whereas
VBE focuses specifically on the sensations, emotions, thoughts, behaviors, and social
experiences elicited by a focal brand within that environment. For example, consumers
may perceive a metaverse platform as entertaining o r technologically impressive while
experiencing little meaningful connection with a particular brand operating within the
platform. Conversely, a carefully designed branded virtual environment may produce
strong brand-specific experiences even when consumers have only moderate evaluations
of the broader platform. Thus, VBE provides a more theoretically precise mechanism for
explaining brand-related outcomes such as consumer –brand engagement and purchase
intention.
Evidence from metaverse retailing also supp orts the experiential mechanism
underlying this distinction. Mansoor et al. (2024) found that characteristics of immersive
metaverse environments, including social presence and trialability, contribute to customer
experience and can subsequently generate purchase spillovers from virtual environments
to real-world consumption. This finding is particularly important because it demonstrates
that experiences generated in immersive environments can influence consumer behavior
beyond the virtual platform itself.

Accordingly, the present study conceptualizes VBE as the central experiential
mechanism through which metaverse marketing attributes are transformed into
subsequent consumer –brand responses. Specifically, interactivity, immersion,
personalization, and social interaction represent characteristics of the metaverse
environment that provide consumers with opportunities to actively participate in, become
absorbed by, customize, and soci ally experience branded virtual environments. These
environmental attributes alone do not necessarily guarantee favorable behavioral
outcomes. Instead, their marketing value depends on whether they generate meaningful
sensory, affective, intellectual, beha vioral, and social experiences associated with the

This conceptualization is consistent with the Stimulus –Organism–Response (S–

social interaction represent en vironmental stimuli, whereas VBE represents an internal
experiential organismic state generated in response to these stimuli. Consumer –brand
engagement subsequently represents a deeper relational state through which the
experience may ultimately translate into behavioral responses such as purchase intention.
Therefore, positioning VBE between metaverse marketing attributes and consumer –
brand engagement provides a theoretically meaningful explanation of how technological
and social characteristics of the metaverse are converted into brand-related psychological
value rather than assuming that technological features directly generate purchase
behavior.

Consumer–brand engagement (CBE) has emerged as an important concept in
contemporary marketing research because it captures the depth and intensity of

consumers’ relationships with brands beyond traditional transactional outcomes. While
conventional constructs such as satisfaction, involvement, attitude, and loyalty primarily
capture consumers’ evaluations or predispositions toward a brand, engagement focuses
on consumers’ active cognitive, emotional, and behavioral investment during brand
interactions. Accordingly, CBE provides a useful theoretical lens for explaining how
consumers m ove from experiencing a brand to developing an active and sustained
relationship with it. The theoretical foundations of customer and consumer engagement
originate from relationship marketing and service -dominant perspectives, which view
consumers as activ e participants in value creation rather than passive recipients of
marketing stimuli. Within this perspective, engagement emerges through interactive
experiences between consumers and focal objects, such as brands, organizations, online
communities, or dig ital platforms. As digital technologies increasingly facilitate
continuous consumer–brand interactions, engagement has become particularly relevant
for understanding how consumers allocate attention, emotions, time, and behavioral effort
to brands.
A seminal contribution to the conceptualization and measurement of CBE was
provided by Hollebeek et al. (2014). They define CBE as positively valenced cognitive,
emotional, and behavioral activity associated with focal consumer –brand interactions.
Based on this c onceptualization, they developed and validated a three -dimensional, 10-
item CBE scale comprising cognitive processing, affection, and activation. Their
empirical validation across multiple social-media contexts further demonstrated that CBE
is conceptually  distinguishable from traditional involvement and that self -brand
connection and brand usage intention can emerge as important consequences of
engagement (Hollebeek et al., 2014).
The cognitive processing dimension refers to the extent of consumers’ brand-
related thought processing and mental elaboration during interactions with a focal brand.
Highly engaged consumers are therefore more likely to devote attention to the brand,
think about it during interactions, and maintain a high level of cognitive interest in brand-
related information. Affection captures consumers’ positive emotional responses toward
the brand during interactions, including feelings of enthusiasm and positive affect.
Activation, meanwhile, represents the behavioral component of engagement and reflects
the amount of time, effort, and energy consumers are willing to devote to interacting with
the brand (Hollebeek et al ., 2014). These dimensions collectively demonstrate that

engagement extends beyond a favorable attitude because it invo lves simultaneous

This multidimensional perspective is particularly suitable for technology -
mediated environments. Digital platforms provide consumers with opportunities to search
for brand information, comm unicate with brands, create or share content, participate in
communities, interact with other consumers, and engage in brand -related activities.
Therefore, digital environments can transform consumers from relatively passive
audiences into active participa nts in brand -related experiences. The relevance of
engagement may become even greater in immersive environments, where interaction with
brands can be more experiential, participatory, and socially embedded than in
conventional websites or social media.
More recent research has continued to refine the conceptual boundaries and
measurement of CBE. For example, research published in the Journal of Retailing and
Consumer Services revisited the conceptualization and operationalization of CBE and
proposed a more parsimonious measurement approach with more clearly differentiated
engagement dimensions. The study highlights the continuing importance of
distinguishing engagement from adjacent consumer –brand constructs and reinforces the
multidimensional nature of the concept.
The broader engagement literature also provides substantial evidence that
engagement is associated with meaningful consumer and firm outcomes. A large -scale
meta-analysis integrating 196 effect sizes from 184 studies and 146,380 observations
demonstrates that customer engagement can emerge through both relationship -oriented
and firm -initiated experiential pathways. Importantly, the strength of engagement
relationships varies across online versus offline environments, industries, product types,
and cultural contexts (Barari et al., 2021).
This contextual sensitivity is especially relevant for the metaverse because
metaverse environments combine technological, experiential, and social characteristics
that differ substantially from traditional retail  environments. Consumers can navigate
branded virtual spaces, interact with digital products, participate in virtual events,
communicate with other users, customize avatars, and engage with brands through
interactive objects or activities. These characteri stics provide multiple opportunities for
consumers to invest cognitive, emotional, and behavioral resources in their relationships
with brands.

In the metaverse context, cognitive engagement can occur when consumers
actively explore a virtual store, examin e virtual products, process brand information, or
think about the brand while navigating an immersive environment. Affective engagement
may emerge when immersive brand experiences generate enjoyment, excitement,
enthusiasm, or other positive brand -related emotions. Behavioral engagement can be
manifested through time spent exploring branded virtual spaces, participation in branded
events or activities, interaction with virtual products, or repeated engagement with
branded content. Thus, the metaverse provides a particularly appropriate environment in
which all three dimensions of CBE proposed by Hollebeek et al . (2014) can operate
simultaneously.
Empirical research specifically examining metaverse marketing provides growing
support for this argument. Payal et al . (2024), for example, investigated consumers
exposed to Nikeland on Roblox and demonstrated that metaverse interactivity—including
active control, two-way communication, and synchronicity—contributes to brand-related
psychological responses, which sub sequently enhance brand active engagement. More
importantly, brand engagement was associated with consumers’ intentions to purchase
the products experienced in the metaverse in the real world. Their findings provide direct
evidence that engagement can func tion as a mechanism connecting virtual brand
encounters with commercially relevant behavior outside the virtual environment.
This finding is particularly important for the present study because it suggests that
the commercial effectiveness of metaverse ma rketing should not be evaluated solely
according to whether consumers enjoy or use a virtual environment. Rather, metaverse
experiences need to generate sufficient cognitive, emotional, and behavioral engagement
with the focal brand for virtual experiences  to translate into downstream behavioral
outcomes. In other words, an immersive environment can attract consumer attention
without necessarily generating a meaningful brand relationship. CBE therefore provides
a theoretically useful mechanism for distingui shing simple participation in a metaverse
platform from active engagement with a brand operating within that platform.
Recent research in fashion retail metaverse environments provides further
evidence for this relationship. Agnihotri et al. (2024) examine d 476 young consumers
exposed to fashion retail metaverse platforms and found that dimensions of social
presence positively influenced customer brand engagement. In turn, CBE significantly
influenced purchase intention. Their research further demonstrated that individual self -
efficacy moderated the CBE –purchase intention relationship, indicating that the

emotional energy, and behavioral effort to the focal brand, thereby generating CBE.

CBE. VBE represents what consumers experience wh en encountering the brand in the
virtual environment, whereas CBE represents how strongly consumers subsequently
invest themselves in their relationship with the brand. A consumer may therefore have a
stimulating sensory or emotional experience without becoming strongly engaged with the
brand. Conversely, when virtual experiences become personally meaningful, memorable,
and relevant, they are more likely to stimulate cognitive processing, affection, and
activation toward the brand.
Within the S–O–R framework adopted in this study, this distinction enables VBE
and CBE to be conceptualized as sequential organism states rather than redundant
constructs. Metaverse marketing attributes constitute environmental stimuli; VBE
captures the immediate experiential reac tion to those stimuli; CBE represents a deeper
brand-related psychological and behavioral state generated by that experience; and
purchase intention represents the subsequent behavioral response. Accordingly, the
proposed mechanism can be expressed as: Met averse Marketing Attributes → Virtual
Brand Experience → Consumer–Brand Engagement → Purchase Intention.

2.4 TECHNOLOGY READINESS

Technology readiness (TR) refers to an individual’s propensity to embrace and
use new technologies to accomplish goals (Parasuraman, 2000). Unlike technology
acceptance, which generally reflects consumers’ evaluations of a specific technological
system, TR represents a broader individual predisposition toward technology.
Parasuraman (2000) conceptualized T R through four dimensions: optimism,
innovativeness, discomfort, and insecurity. Optimism and innovativeness act as drivers
of technology adoption, whereas discomfort and insecurity function as inhibitors.
Parasuraman and Colby (2015) subsequently develope d the streamlined Technology
Readiness Index (TRI 2.0), retaining these four dimensions while adapting the scale to
contemporary technology environments.
Technology readiness is particularly relevant to metaverse marketing because
consumers must interact w ith relatively novel technological features, including
immersive virtual environments, avatars, virtual products, and interactive interfaces.
Consumers with higher technology readiness are generally more willing to experiment
with new technologies and more confident in navigating unfamiliar digital environments.
In contrast, technologically less -ready consumers may experience greater uncertainty,
discomfort, or perceived complexity when interacting with immersive technologies.
Recent research supports the relevance of individual technology orientation in explaining
consumer responses to virtual reality and metaverse environments (Bassiouni, 2025;
Fares et al., 2024).
In the present study, technology readiness is conceptualized as a moderator rather
than a direct antecedent of metaverse adoption. This distinction is important because the
study focuses on consumers who have already experienced brands in virtual
environments. The key question is therefore not whether consumers will adopt the

metaverse, but wheth er individual technology readiness affects the extent to which a

Accordingly, the study proposes that technology readiness strengthens the

3.1 INTERACTIVITY AND VIRTUAL BRAND EXPERIENCE

Interactivity refers to the extent to which consumers can actively control,
communicate with, and influence a technology -mediated environment. Unlike
conventional digital advertising, in which consumers often receive predetermined brand
messages, metaverse environments enable users to navigate virtual spaces, manipulate
virtual objects, interact with branded content, and communicate in real time. Such
capabilities transform consumers from passive information recipients into active
participants in the brand experience. Payal et al . (2024) conceptualized metaverse
interactivity through active control, two -way communication, and synchronicity and
found that these interactive characteristics positively contribute to brand -related
psychological responses and subsequent brand engagement. Similarly, Ahn et al. (2024)
found that perceived interactivity in the metaverse contributes to self -expansion, which
subsequently increases purchase intention toward virtual products.

3.2 IMMERSION AND VIRTUAL BRAND EXPERIENCE

Immersion describes the extent to which individuals beco me psychologically
absorbed in a mediated environment and experience a sense of being surrounded by or
deeply involved in that environment. Immersive characteristics distinguish metaverse
environments from many conventional online marketing channels because consumers can
experience branded environments as spatial and participatory rather than simply viewing
information on a screen. This heightened involvement can increase attention to brand -
related stimuli and make virtual encounters more vivid and memorable.
Recent metaverse research supports the importance of immersion in shaping
consumer responses. Prashar and Prashar (2025) show that metaverse platform
characteristics stimulate immersive responses, which subsequently contribute to
shoppers’ purchase inte ntions. Similarly, research examining metaverse stores
demonstrates that technological characteristics of the metaverse can enhance telepresence
and other organismic responses that influence downstream consumer outcomes. From the
S–O–R perspective, immersion intensifies consumers’ internal reactions to brand-related
stimuli by focusing attention on the virtual environment. A highly immersive
environment should therefore facilitate stronger sensory stimulation, emotional
involvement, intellectual processing,  and behavioral participation associated with the
brand. Thus:
H2: Immersion positively influences virtual brand experience.

3.3 PERSONALIZATION AND VIRTUAL BRAND EXPERIENCE

Personalization refers to the extent to which a virtual environment adapts content,
products, recommendations, interfaces, or interactions to consumers’ individual
characteristics and preferences. In metaverse environments, personalization can extend
beyond conventional product recommendations to include customized avatars, virtual
products, branded spaces, interactive content, and individualized experiences. Such
customization may increase perceived relevance because consumers experience the brand
in ways that more closely reflect their personal preferences and goals.
Empirical research provides support for the experiential value of personalization
in metaverse shopping. Prashar and Prashar (2025), using data from 327 metaverse -
platform users, found personalization to be the strongest platform attribute associated
with experiential v alue in their model. Their findings suggest that consumers derive
greater utilitarian and hedonic value when metaverse environments provide experiences
tailored to individual preferences. Personalization can therefore strengthen the connection
between consumers and virtual brand environments by increasing personal relevance,
perceived control, and opportunities for self -expression. Because VBE captures
consumers’ sensory, affective, intellectual, behavioral, and social reactions to a brand in
digitally medi ated environments (Lee & Cho, 2023), personalized interactions should
facilitate richer brand experiences. Therefore:
H3: Personalization positively influences virtual brand experience.

3.4 SOCIAL INTERACTION AND VIRTUAL BRAND EXPERIENCE

Social interacti on refers to consumers’ ability to communicate, observe,
collaborate, and participate with other individuals within a virtual environment. This
characteristic is central to the metaverse because users can encounter brands while
simultaneously interacting w ith other consumers through avatars and shared virtual
activities. As a result, brand experiences in the metaverse may become collective and
socially embedded rather than exclusively individual.
The importance of the social component is supported by severa l studies. Lee and
Cho (2023) conceptualized VBE as comprising five dimensions  social, intellectual,
sensory, behavioral, and affective  and found the social dimension to be particularly
influential in explaining consumer engagement with brands in digital-reality advertising.

3.5 VIRTUAL BRAND EXPERIENCE AND CONSUMER–BRAND ENGAGEMENT

Virtual brand experience represents consumers’ sensory, affective, intellectual,

brand. A positive VBE should motivate consumers to allocate greater psychological and
behavioral resources to the focal brand because meaningful experiences increase

Direct empirical support for this relationship is provided by Lee and Cho (2023),
who developed and validated the VBE scale and examined sequential relationships
among VBE, brand engagement, and consumer responses. Their results demonstrate that
dimensions of VBE contribute to consumer engagement with brands in digital -reality
advertising, with the social dimension showing particularly strong relevance.  This
relationship is consistent with the conceptualization of consumer –brand engagement as
consumers’ cognitive processing, affection, and activation during brand interactions. In
metaverse environments, richer virtual experiences should therefore encourage
consumers to think more a bout the brand, develop stronger positive feelings toward it,
and devote greater time and effort to brand-related activities.
Within the proposed S–O–R process, VBE consequently represents the immediate
experiential state generated by metaverse stimuli, wh ereas CBE represents a deeper
relational response arising from that experience. Accordingly:
H5: Virtual brand experience positively influences consumer–brand engagement.

3.7 MODERATING ROLE OF TECHNOLOGY READINESS

Although VBE is expected to enhance CBE, the strength of this relationship may

metaverse environments, where consumers interact with unfamiliar interfaces, avatars,
immersive spaces, and technologically mediated forms of social interaction.
Consumers with higher technology r eadiness should be more comfortable
experimenting with such environments and more capable of translating favorable virtual
experiences into sustained brand interaction. In contrast, consumers characterized by
greater technological discomfort or insecurity may enjoy certain aspects of a virtual
experience while remaining reluctant to invest additional cognitive, emotional, or
behavioral resources in the brand. Importantly, the present study does not conceptualize

7 DISCUSSION

The proposed model offers a process explanation of how metaverse marketing
attributes may translate into purchase intention. Rather than assuming that
technologically advanced features directly cause purchase, the model suggests that
attributes must first become a meaningful brand experience and then generate
engagement. This mechanism is consistent with S –O–R reasoning and extends recent
metaverse studies that link platform attributes with experiential, engagement, and
purchase outcomes.
The pattern places immersion and personalization ahead of interactivity and social
interaction in their effects on VBE. If replicated empirically, this would suggest that
Vietnamese consumers may value not only the ability to interact but also how fully a

virtual environment absorbs them and adapts to their preferences. This interpretation
would complement Prashar and Prashar’s (2025) finding that personalization is
particularly important for experiential value and that sociability is important for
immersiveness.
The strong VBE → CBE relationship is theoretically important. Lee and Cho
(2023) show that VBE is a distinct digital -reality brand experience and can precede
engagement. The present framework transfers that logic into metaverse marketing and
makes the brand-specific experience the central organism state. This avoids treating all
immersive platform experiences as equivalent to brand experiences.
The CBE → purchase -intention path is consistent with metaverse evidence
showing that engagement can connect  virtual brand encounters to real -world purchase
responses (Payal et al ., 2024). The model therefore highlights engagement as a
commercial conversion mechanism: consumers may enjoy an immersive environment
without necessarily intending to buy unless the experience creates sustained brand-related
cognitive, affective, and behavioral investment.
Finally, the technology -readiness moderation introduces heterogeneity into the
model. A metaverse campaign can create a strong VBE but still produce weaker
engagement among consumers who feel less comfortable with emerging technologies.
This boundary condition shifts managerial attention from designing the virtual
environment alone to matching the environment's complexity and onboarding to
consumer readiness.

8 THEORETICAL CONTRIBUTIONS

First, the study integrates four metaverse marketing attributes into a unified S–O–
R mechanism instead of testing isolated direct effects. Second, it distinguishes virtual
brand experience from generic immersion or platform experience and positions VBE as
the first organism state through which technological and social stimuli acquire brand
meaning. Third, it models CBE as a second organism state, explaining how experience
becomes a relational state before purchase intention emerges. Fou rth, it introduces
technology readiness as a boundary condition on the VBE–CBE link, adding a 'when/for
whom' explanation to the 'how' mechanism. Together, these contributions produce a
parsimonious chain: metaverse attributes → VBE → CBE → purchase intent ion,
contingent on technology readiness.

9 MANAGERIAL IMPLICATIONS

For managers, the model implies that metaverse investment should be evaluated
by the quality of the brand experience it creates, not by technological novelty alone.
Interactivity should provide meaningful control and two-way communication; immersion
should focus attention on the brand without creating unnecessary complexity;
personalization should adapt products, content, and virtual interactions to user
preferences; and social features s hould facilitate authentic participation with others.
Because CBE is the proximal driver of purchase intention, managers should track
engagement indicators such as voluntary time spent, repeat interaction, participation, and
brand-related activity rather t han relying only on visits or impressions. Technology
readiness also suggests differentiated onboarding: less -ready consumers may require
simpler navigation, clearer guidance, and lower -friction entry points, whereas highly
ready users may respond to more advanced customization and immersive functionality.

10 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE RESEARCH

This study should use screened metaverse -experienced consumers and report the
exact platforms and brand categories recalled by respondents. Second, a cross -sectional
design limits causal inference; experiments or longitudinal designs could manipulate
interactivity, immersion, or personalization and observe subsequent engagement. Third,
future studies should distinguish purchase intention for physical products from virtu al
goods because the psychological mechanisms may differ. Fourth, the role of technology
readiness may vary across age cohorts and levels of metaverse experience. Fifth,
common-method bias and endogeneity should be assessed explicitly in dataset. Finally,
cross-country replication would show whether the proposed mechanism is specific to
Vietnam or generalizes across emerging and developed markets.
