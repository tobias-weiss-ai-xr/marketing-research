 Competition among Islamic educational institutions has increased
alongside public demands for service quality, program relevance,
transparency, and institutional credibility. This condition requires
educational marketing to be managed as an integral part of educational
services rather than merely as a promotional activity. This study aims
to analyze the application of the 7P marketing mix in Islamic
educational institutions and its integration with Islamic values. The
study employed a qualitative library re search approach with
descriptive-analytical analysis. Data were obtained from relevant
scholarly literature on service marketing, educational marketing,
Islamic educational management, and Islamic marketing. The findings
indicate that product, price, place, promotion, people, process, and
physical evidence provide a strategic framework for strengthening
institutional attractiveness, service quality, and competitiveness. Their
implementation needs to be guided by honesty, trustworthiness,
justice, public benefit, transparency, and social responsibility. The
integration of these principles strengthens public trust, institutional
credibility, and sustainable educational management. This study
concludes that Islamic values can serve as an ethical and evaluative
foundation for implementing the 7P marketing mix in Islamic
educational institutions.

1. INTRODUCTION
Educational institutions are service organizations that create value through the provision of
educational services. Unlike product marketing, which focuses on the characteristics of physical
products, educational service marketing involves service experiences, human resource competence,
learning processes, facilities, institutional reputation, and relationships with students and the
community. Therefore, educational marketing requires a comprehensive approach rather than relying
solely on promotional activities. Increasing competition among educational institutions has given
parents and prospective students more choices when selecting educational institutions. Their
considerations include program quality, educational costs, location, reputation, educator competence,
facilities, service systems, and information available through digital media. This condition requires
educational institutions to understand community needs and develop marketing strategies oriented

toward service quality. The marketing mix concept provides a framework for managing these aspects
through seven elements: product, price, place, promotion, people, process, and physical evidence [1].
In Islamic educational institutions, however, marketing has a broader dimension because institutions
provide educational services while carrying Islamic values, identity, and mission. Islamic educational
products may include academic programs, religious development, tahfiz, language programs, skills
development, and character education. Thus, Islamic educational marketing should communicate
educational value and quality accurately rather than focus solely on increasing student enrollment.
Previous studies have examined the application of the 7P marketing mix in various Islamic
educational institutions. Ibrahim et al. reported that the implementation of the 7P marketing mix at
Phatnawitya School in Thailand involved educational program development, facilities, innovation,
segmentation, and communication with the community and social media [2]. Ibadiyah, Hermawan, and
Mukhlis found that the 7P strategy could be applied in Islamic boarding school-based education through
program management, pricing, location, promotion, human resources, service processes, and physical
evidence [3]. Abadi and Hidayatulloh examined the relationship between the marketing mix and brand
image as a strategy for marketing Islamic educational institutions [4]. More recent studies have also
discussed the application of the 7P framework in Islamic education. Maisah et al. identified educational
programs, costs, location, promotion, teaching staff, services, and facilities as components of the 7P
marketing mix in Islamic educational marketing [5]. Budiarti and Amin reported that the seven
elements of the 7P framework formed part of the marketing strategy of an Islamic boarding school in
Blitar in responding to competition and maintaining institutional sustainability [6]. Furthermore, Zebua
and Us examined the application of the 7P marketing mix in Islamic higher education institutions in
Indonesia and identified program development, service -value-based pricing, human resource
competence, service processes, facilities, and digital promotion as important components of educational
marketing strategies [7].
Despite the growing body of research on the 7P marketing mix in Islamic education, previous
studies tend to emphasize the technical application of marketing elements. The conceptual integration
of the seven elements with Islamic ethical principles remains insufficiently discussed. Islamic
marketing is grounded in values such as honesty, trustworthiness, justice, transparency, public benefit,
and social responsibility. These principles are particularly important in educational services because
institutions communicate promises concerning programs, costs, services, facilities, and educational
outcomes to students and the community. Alzalook, Lubis, and Mansor emphasized that Islamic
marketing should be integrated with ethical principles, justice, honesty, and the protection of consumer
interests [8]. In the context of education, these principles require institutions to provide accurate
program information, establish reasonable educational costs, deliver services in accordance with
institutional commitments, and treat students and the community fairly. From a maslahah perspective,
Islamic educational marketing should also generate benefits for students, families, communities, and
educational institutions rather than merely increase institutional attractiveness [9]. Consequently,
Islamic educational marketing can be understood as a process of creating, communicating, and
sustaining educational value while responding to community needs and maintaining consistency with
Islamic principles.
Based on this gap, this study focuses on a conceptual synthesis of educational service
marketing and Islamic educational marketing to explain how the seven elements of the 7P marketing
mix can be integrated with Islamic values. The study aims to: (1) examine the concept of the 7P
marketing mix in educational service marketing; (2) identify the application of each 7P element in
Islamic educational institutions based on the existing literature; and (3) formulate the integration of the
7P strategy with Islamic values in strengthening service quality, public trust, and institutional
competitiveness. The novelty of this study lies in proposing a conceptual framework that connects the
7P marketing mix, Islamic ethical principles, and institutional outcomes. Unlike previous studies that
primarily describe the application of individual marketing elements, this study positions Islamic values
as an evaluative foundation for guiding each element of the 7P marketing mix. The resulting conceptual
synthesis places educational marketing beyond institutional attractiveness by connecting marketing
practices with service quality, public trust, institutional reputation, and sustainability.

2. Method
This study employed a qualitative library research design. Library research is a research
method conducted through the systematic exploration, reading, and critical analysis of literature
sources relevant to the research topic. Literature sources may include scholarly journal articles,
academic books, documents, and other relevant written materials without conducting field research
[10]. The research procedure was conducted chronologically through several stages. First, literature
identification was carried out by determining the research focus and identifying relevant sources
related to the 7P marketing mix, service marketing, educational marketing, Islamic educational
management, and Islamic marketing. Second, literature acquisition involved collecting relevant
scholarly articles, academic books, and other traceable academic sources. Third, literature selection
was conducted by examining the relevance, academic credibility, and contribution of each source to
the research topic. Fourth, literature reading and recording involved critically reading the selected
sources and systematically recording relevant concepts, arguments, and research findings. According
to Zed, as cited in Rahayu, library research involves collecting literature materials, reading and
recording relevant information, and subsequently processing and analyzing the materials for research
purposes [11].
The literature selection was based on several criteria. First, the sources had to be directly
related to service marketing, educational marketing, the 7P marketing mix, Islamic educational
management, or Islamic marketing. Second, the sources had to originate from scholarly journals,
academic books, or other identifiable academic publications. Third, the sources had to provide
theoretical arguments, conceptual explanations, or research findings relevant to the research focus.
Fourth, the sources had to contribute to the development of the conceptual synthesis rather than being
included merely to fulfill the required number of references. Publications from 2020 to 2026 were
prioritized to capture recent developments, while selected classical sources were used as theoretical
foundations for service marketing and the marketing mix. The data acquisition process involved
collecting, reading, and documenting relevant information from the selected literature. The
information was organized according to the seven elements of the marketing mix: product, price,
place, promotion, people, process, and physical evidence, as well as relevant Islamic values,
including honesty, trustworthiness, justice, transparency, maslahah, and social responsibility [12].
Data analysis was conducted through four stages: literature identification, thematic
classification, comparative analysis, and conceptual synthesis. Thematic classification involved
grouping information according to the seven elements of the 7P marketing mix and relevant Islamic
values. Comparative analysis was conducted by examining similarities, differences, and
complementary arguments across the selected literature. Conceptual synthesis was then performed
by integrating the findings into a coherent argument concerning the application of the 7P marketing
mix in accordance with the characteristics and values of Islamic educational institutions. The final
stage involved formulating the research findings and conclusions based on the results of the
conceptual synthesis. This analytical process was intended to develop a comprehensive conceptual
understanding of how the 7P marketing mix can be integrated with Islamic values in the management
and marketing of Islamic educational institutions.
3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1 Marketing Mix 7P as an Educational Services Marketing Framework
The Marketing Mix approach has been widely applied in the business sector and has
increasingly been adopted within the field of education. The 7P framework, consisting of Product,
Price, Place, Promotion, People, Process, and Physical Evidence, provides a comprehensive
perspective for examining educational marketing practices and developing appropriate marketing
strategies for educational institutions (A. Kusumawati, 2019). [13]. The literature indicates that
educational institutions operate within a service context in which value is experienced through
programs, interactions, processes, human resources, and institutional environments. Educational
marketing therefore cannot be reduced to promotional activities because prospective students and
parents evaluate the overall educational experience, including the relevance of programs,
affordability, accessibility, personnel competence, service processes, and physical facilities. The 7P

Islamic educational marketing. Similarly, research on Islamic boarding school-based education

services according to community needs. The application of the 7P framework in Islamic educational
institutions has also expanded beyond school-level marketing. Maisah et al. identified the seven

educational programs, costs, location, promotion, human resources, processes, and physical evidence

further demonstrates that the seven elements are visible in institutional marketing activities, including
digital communication and institutional presentation [15]. Thus, the literature supports the use of 7P
as a comprehensive analytical framework for Islamic educational service marketing.

3.1.1 Product: Educational Products Based on Values and Community Needs
A product refers to anything offered by an organization to fulfill customers’ needs and
expectations. In the context of marketing, a product reflects the value perceived by consumers and is
developed according to the organization’s capabilities and the pur chasing power of the target
market.[16]. Product in educational marketing refers to the educational value offered by an
institution. In contrast to conventional products, educational products consist largely of experiences,
programs, learning outcomes, institutional services, and relationships.  In Islamic educational
institutions, the product dimension may include academic programs, religious education, tahfiz
programs, language development, character education, extracurricular activities, and skills
development [17]. Research on Islamic educational institutions demonstrates that program
differentiation can become an important component of institutional positioning. Islamic schools
frequently communicate distinctive programs as part of their institutional identity and branding.
Studies of Islamic schools show that flagship programs, religious character development,
and educational quality can function as elements of institutional differentiation [18]. The product
therefore needs to correspond with the actual needs of students, parents, and the wider community
rather than being designed merely as a promotional attraction. The literature also indicates that
educational products need to demonstrate consistency between institutional promises and actual
service delivery. Marketing that communicates educational programs that are not adequately
supported by institutional capacity may weaken institutional credibility. Therefore, product
development should involve curriculum relevance, educator competence, learning quality, student
development, and institutional values [19]. Within Islamic education, product development should
also reflect the institution's religious identity and educational mission. From an Islamic perspective,
the product dimension is connected with maslahah because educational programs should provide
meaningful benefits to students and society. Islamic marketing literature emphasizes that marketing
should be conducted within an ethical framework that protects consumer interests and promotes
fairness and honesty  [8]. Consequently, Islamic educational products should be developed on the
basis of educational quality and social benefit rather than merely market attractiveness.
3.1.2 Price: Fairness, Affordability, and Transparency
According to Kotler and Gary Armstrong (2010), price represents the amount customers are
required to pay for a product or service. It also reflects the overall value customers exchange for the
benefits they receive from the offered product or service. [20]. “Price is an amount of money that

serves as a medium of exchange for obtaining a product or service. Price can also be understood as
the determination of a product’s value in the minds of consumers (Nana, 2015: 109).” [21]. Price in
educational services includes tuition fees, registration costs, program fees, and other financial
obligations borne by students or their families. Educational price decisions therefore have
implications for accessibility and perceptions of insti tutional fairness. The service marketing
literature emphasizes that pricing is part of the overall service value perceived by customers [22].
Research on educational marketing demonstrates that price needs to be considered together
with the benefits and quality of services offered. In Islamic educational institutions, the price
dimension can include affordability, transparency of payment structures, scholarships, and clarity
regarding additional costs [23]. A transparent pricing policy enables parents and students to
understand the actual financial commitment before choosing an institution. Islamic marketing
principles provide an ethical foundation for this dimension. Alzalook, Lubis, and Mansor emphasize
honesty, fairness, consumer protection, and ethical conduct as important principles of Islamic
marketing. These principles indicate that educational institutions should avoid misleading
information regarding costs and should communicate financial obligations clearly. The principle of
fairness also means that financial policies should consider the educational mission and social
responsibilities of the institution. Consequently, pricing strategies may be connected with scholarship
programs, financial assistance, installment arrangements, and other mechanisms that support access
to education. The price dimension should therefore be understood as part of value creation rather
than simply as a mechanism for generating institutional income.
3.1.3 Place: Accessibility and the Educational Environment
Place refers to the location where services are delivered to customers. In healthcare service
marketing, the selection of a strategic and accessible location is an important consideration because
it can facilitate public access to information and make healthcare services more easily reached by the
community.[24]. Place concerns how and where educational services are delivered. In education, this
dimension includes geographical accessibility, institutional location, transportation, learning
environments, and increasingly digital accessibility. Educational institut ions need to consider
whether students and parents can access the institution and its services conveniently. Research on
educational marketing shows that location remains relevant in institutional selection because
accessibility can affect parents' perceptions of convenience and educational service value [25]. For
Islamic schools, the physical environment may also contribute to institutional identity. A safe,
comfortable, clean, and religiously appropriate environment can strengthen the educational
experience. Digitalization has expanded the meaning of place. Educational information, registration
services, consultation, learning resources, and institutional communication can now be accessed
through digital platforms. Juhaidi's study of Islamic higher education institutions in Indonesia
demonstrates that social media has become an important channel through which institutions
communicate their products, services, personnel, and institutional activities [26]. Therefore, place in
Islamic educational marketing should be understood as a combination of physical accessibility and
digital accessibility. The institution's location remains important, but digital channels increasingly
complement physical service delivery.
3.1.4 Promotion: Informative and Non-Misleading Communication
Promotion is an important component in determining the effectiveness of a marketing
program. Within the service marketing mix, promotional activities serve as a strategic means of
communicating the value and market positioning of a service to its target customers. [27]. Promotion
refers to a form of communication between sellers, buyers, and other parties within a distribution
channel that aims to shape consumer attitudes and influence their purchasing behavior (McCarthy &
Perreault, as cited in Tampubolon, 2018). Promotion represents the fourth element of the marketing
strategy used by educational institutions to strengthen their institutional image. It plays an important
role in supporting the effectiveness of marketing programs because the quality of an educational
service alone does not necessarily attract public interest. When prospective users are unfamiliar with

In educational service marketing, promotional activities serve to introduce the products or

promotion is increasingly connected with institutional branding. Sapranto and Latifah, for example,

of Islamic educational institutions that emphasize marketing communication as a mechanism for
communicating institutional excellence [32]. Digital promotion has become particularly significant.
Research on Islamic higher education demonstrates that Facebook and Instagram are used to
communicate marketing mix elements and institutional information [33]. However, the same study
indicates that the representation of 7P elements in social media content can be uneven. This finding
suggests that digital promotion should be strategically planned rather than limited to frequent posting.
Islamic marketing ethics provide an important limitation on promotional practices. Alzlaook, Lubis,
and Mansor discuss the problem of misleading advertising and emphasize consumer protection in
marketing practices [34]. In the context of Islamic education, this means that promotional content
should accurately represent programs, facilities, achievements, costs, and institutional capabilities.
Promotional communication should therefore emphasize information, transparency, and institutional
accountability.
3.1.5 People: Human Resources as the Face of the Institution
Within the service marketing framework, the People element encompasses individuals who
contribute to the delivery and experience of a service, including employees and other service users.
The quality of service is closely associated with the competence and interaction of those involved in
its delivery. Well-trained and capable human resources can enhance positive experiences among
service users while strengthening their trust in the institution (Moersidi, 2024). [35]. People represent
one of the most important dimensions of educational services because education is delivered through
human interaction. Teachers, principals, administrative staff, counselors, and other educational
personnel directly influence students' e xperiences of institutional quality. Human resource
management encompasses a range of organizational practices designed to address employee-related
matters and coordinate the roles of managers and other personnel. These practices support the
effective management of human resources and contribute to the achievement of the organization’s
established goals.[36]. The service marketing literature emphasizes the central role of employees in
service delivery because customers often evaluate service quality through their interactions with
personnel [37].
In education, teacher competence, responsiveness, communication, professionalism, and
ethical conduct become visible representations of institutional quality. Research in Islamic education
similarly places educators and personnel within the marketing process. The 7P studies conducted in
Islamic educational institutions identify teachers and educational staff as essential components of
service delivery [38]. The people dimension is therefore not merely a promotional element but part
of the substantive educational service. Islamic values further strengthen the importance of people.
Amanah requires educational personnel to fulfill their responsibilities, while shiddiq requires honesty
in communication and professional conduct. Justice requires fair treatment of students, while

3.1.6 Process: Effective and Equitable Educational Services
Process refers to the procedures through which educational services are delivered. In
educational institutions, this includes registration, admission, learning activities, assessment,
communication with parents, administrative services, complaint handling,  and graduation
procedures. The literature indicates that service processes influence perceptions of educational
quality because students and parents experience the institution through repeated administrative and
educational interactions [39]. A clear and consistent process can reduce uncertainty and strengthen
perceptions of institutional reliability. Research on Islamic educational marketing confirms that
process is one of the seven elements applied in educational institutions. In Islamic higher education,
process is also visible in digital communication and institutional service systems. From an Islamic
perspective, process should be guided by justice, transparency, responsibility, and trustworthiness.
Admission procedures, financial administration, assessment, and student services should therefore
be implemented consistently and fairly. This means that the ethical quality of a process is as
important as its administrative efficiency.
3.1.7 Physical Evidence: Tangible Evidence of Service Quality
Physical evidence or tangible evidence refers to the physical elements that support the
delivery and perception of educational services. According to Ratnasari and Aksa (2011, p. 53),
physical evidence is a dynamic component of the marketing mix that may change according to the
needs and development of the service environment. In educational institutions, physical evidence
includes classrooms, libraries, laboratories, places of worship, sanitation facilities, technological
infrastructure, learning platforms, and other facilities that support the educational process. Effective
marketing management should ensure that these facilities provide value and satisfaction to students,
parents, and other stakeholders. Therefore, Islamic educational institutions need to provide adequate,
quality, and accessible facilities that are consistent with the services offered. Physical evidence
should not merely serve as a promotional symbol but should demonstrate the institution's
commitment to educational quality, service excellence, and the fulfillment of the needs of its
educational community. [40].
Physical evidence refers to tangible elements that support the delivery and perception of
educational services. These include classrooms, libraries, laboratories, religious facilities, sanitation,
technology infrastructure, websites, learning platforms, and other institutional facilities. Because
educational services contain many intangible elements, physical evidence provides students and
parents with visible indicators of institutional preparedness. The service marketing literature
recognizes the importance of physical environments in shaping service experiences [41]. Studies of
Islamic educational institutions demonstrate that facilities and institutional environments form part
of the marketing mix. Physical facilities may also support school branding because the visual
environment communicates institutional identity and educational standards. However, physical
evidence should not be separated from actual service quality. Attractive buildings cannot substitute
for weak educational services. Therefore, facilities should function as tangible support for
educational quality rather than as purely promotional symbols.

3.3 Digitalization and Changing Patterns of Islamic Educational Marketing
Digitalization refers to the process of converting printed materials, audio recordings, and
video content into digital formats. This process facilitates the preservation and management of
documents in digital form, supports reproduction, and contributes to the development of digital
library collections. The implementation of digitalization requires various supporting resources,
including computers, scanners, trained operators, source media, and appropriate software (Sukmana,
2005). Similarly, Lasa Hs describes digitalization as a process of transforming printed documents
into electronic documents that can be stored, managed, and accessed digitally. [44]. The development
of digital communication has significantly enhanced the way people interact by providing faster and
more efficient opportunities to establish connections with individuals across different parts of the
world. [45]. Digitalization has changed the way educational institutions communicate with
stakeholders. Websites and social media allow institutions to disseminate information about
programs, achievements, personnel, facilities, and institutional activities more rapi dly than
traditional promotional media. Dwivedi's study shows that social media marketing in higher
education is connected with faculty student relationship quality and institutional brand performance
[46].
In Islamic higher education, Juhaidi found that Facebook and Instagram are used to
communicate all seven elements of the marketing mix, although their representation is not
necessarily balanced. Recent research also demonstrates the increasing importance of social media
in Islamic higher education marketing communication. Utami, Fahmi, and Prastya examine social
media use by Islamic universities in Muslim countries and emphasize its role as a marketing
communication tool. More recent research focusing on Is lamic higher education in Indonesia
similarly examines engagement and brand-post content on Instagram and X [47]. Digitalization
therefore functions as an enabling mechanism for the 7P framework rather than constituting an
additional eighth element. Digital platforms can strengthen promotion, facilitate access,
communicate educational products, display physical evidence, support service processes, and create
interaction between institutions and stakeholders. Nevertheless, digital marketing requires ethical
controls. Institutional content should be accurate, responsible, and consistent with the values
communicated by the institution. In Islamic educational marketing, the credibility of digital
communication depends on consistency between institutional claims and actual educational services.

promotional framework.
3.5 Synthesis of the Islamic Value-Based Marketing Mix 7P Model
Based on the comparative analysis of the literature, the seven marketing elements can be
synthesized with Islamic values into an integrated educational marketing framework. Product is
evaluated through educational relevance, quality, and benefit. Price is evaluated through fairness,
affordability, and transparency. Place is evaluated through accessibility and suitability of the
educational environment. Promotion is evaluated through truthfulness and responsible
communication. People are evaluated through competence, amanah, professionalism, and equitable
treatment. Process is evaluated through efficiency, consistency, justice, and accountability. Physical
evidence is evaluated through the extent to which facilities genuinely support educational service
quality. The framework indicates that Islamic values should not be placed separately from marketing
activities. Instead, they should function as principles that guide the implementation of each element.
This interpretation is consistent with Islamic marketing literature, which positions ethical principles
as integral to marketing practice rather than as additional promotional attributes.
Recent Islamic educational research also supports the integration of values, marketing,
branding, and competitiveness. Rachman et al. demonstrate the relevance of reputation management
and branding image in strengthening competitive advantage among Indones ian Islamic higher
education institutions. Arsyadana and Indrioko similarly identify institutional identity, leadership
networks, alumni, and digital media as components of Islamic higher education branding. At the
school level, recent studies indicate that 7P marketing can be integrated with school branding and
educational excellence. Asrulla et al. describe the integration of educational excellence and 7P
marketing in building the branding of an Islamic school. Awalliyah and Bahar also identify 7P, digital
branding, alumni networks, and Islamic values as components of competitive marketing strategies in
Islamic boarding school education [48]. Thus, the conceptual contribution of this study lies in
connecting three dimensions: the operational dimension of the 7P marketing mix, the ethical
dimension of Islamic values, and the institutional-outcome dimension consisting of service quality,
satisfaction, trust, reputation, competitiveness, and sustainability.
3.6 Managerial Implications
The literature synthesis produces several implications for managers of Islamic educational
institutions. First, marketing should be integrated into institutional management rather than assigned
exclusively to promotional activities. Educational programs, human resources, service procedures,
facilities, and communication should convey a consistent institutional value proposition. Second,

Islamic values should be translated into observable managerial practices. Honesty should be reflected
in accurate information, amanah in fulfillment of institutional commitments, justice in fair treatment,
transparency in financial and administrative communication, and maslahah in the consideration of
educational benefits for students and society. Third, digital marketing should be managed
systematically. Social media should communicate substantive educational value rather than merely

actual educational quality. Research on Islamic educational branding indicates that institutional
identity becomes stronger when branding is supported by distinctive programs, service quality,
leadership, and consistent institutional values [49].
Finally, managers should evaluate marketing success through broader indicators than student
enrollment. Relevant indicators include service quality, satisfaction, trust, institutional reputation,
student loyalty, stakeholder engagement, and institutional s ustainability. Research in higher
education shows that service quality, satisfaction, trust, and loyalty are interconnected components
of long-term institutional relationships [50]. The literature therefore suggests that the implementation
of 7P in Islamic educational institutions should be viewed as a continuous process of value creation,
communication, service delivery, and relationship development. This approach places marketing
within the broader mission of educational management and Islamic social responsibility.
4. CONCLUSION
This library research shows that the 7P marketing mix provides a comprehensive framework
for understanding and managing educational service marketing in Islamic educational institutions.
Product, price, place, promotion, people, process, and physical evidence represent interconnected
dimensions of educational service delivery rather than independent promotional instruments. The
literature further indicates that Islamic values provide an ethical and evaluative foundation for
implementing each element of the 7P framework. Honesty, trustworthiness, justice, transparency,
maslahah, and social responsibility can guide institutions in developing educational programs,
determining costs, providing accessible services, communicating institutional information, managing
human resources, implementing service processes, and developing physical facilities. The conceptual
synthesis developed in this study connects the 7P marketing mix and Islamic values with educational
service quality, satisfaction, public trust, institutional reputation, competitiveness, and sustainability.
Digitalization functions as an enabling mechanism that expands the communication and delivery of
educational services without constituting an additional marketing -mix element. The principal
contribution of this study is therefore a value-based interpretation of educational marketing in which
Islamic values operate as an evaluative foundation for the entire marketing mix. The proposed
framework remains a conceptual synthesis of the literature and has not been statistically tested in this
study. Future empirical research may examine the framework through quantitative, qualitative, or
mixed-method approaches in different types of Islamic educational institutions.
