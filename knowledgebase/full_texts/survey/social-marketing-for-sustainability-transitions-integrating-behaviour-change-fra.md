ABSTRACT

Sustainability has emerged as a defining challenge for contemporary societies, requiring not only technological
innovation and policy reform but also profound changes in everyday behaviors, organizational routines, and social
norms. The primary objective o f the paper is to develop and justify a conceptual framework that embeds social
marketing within sustainability strategies. Drawing on an integrative systematic literature review and conceptual
synthesis across social marketing, behavior change science, an d sustainability transitions, the paper proposes a
three-dimensional framework comprising: (1) behavioral diagnosis and audience segmentation, (2) value co -
creation and stakeholder engagement, and (3) feedback and systemic reinforcement. The framework is designed to
explain how pro -sustainable behaviors can be adopted, maintained, and scaled in ways that support longer -term
socio-technical transitions. The results synthesize evidence showing that sustainability -oriented interventions are
most effective when they move beyond generic awareness -raising and instead address heterogeneous behavioral
barriers, involve stakeholders in co -design processes, and create reinforcing feedback loops aligned with
infrastructures, institutions, and social norms. Behavioral diagnosis enables targeted intervention design by
recognizing differences in motivation, capability, opportunity, and social context. Value co -creation enhances
relevance, legitimacy, and equity by engaging employees, communities, and cross -sector partners in shaping
solutions. Feedback and systemic reinforcement support long -term maintenance by making outcomes visible,
leveraging social influence, and embedding behaviors within supportive policy and service environments. The
paper contributes theoretically by linking micro -level behavior change mechanisms with meso - and macro-level
sustainability transition dynamics. Practically, it offers organizations and policy actors a structured blueprint for
operationalizing sustainability goals through social marketing as a strategic capability rather than a communication
tool. The article concludes by outlining directions for future empirical research to test the framework across
industries, cultural contexts, and sustainability domains, with particular attention to s calability, ethics, and long -
term impact.

RESUMO
A sustentabilidade emergiu como um desafio definidor das sociedades contemporâneas, e xigindo não apenas
inovação tecnológica e reformas políticas, mas também mudanças profundas nos comportamentos quotidianos, nas
rotinas organizacionais e nas normas sociais. O principal objetivo do artigo é desenvolver e fundamentar um quadro
conceptual que integre o marketing social nas estratégias de sustentabilidade. Com base numa revisão sistemática
integrativa da literatura e numa síntese conceptual que articula contributos do marketing social, da ciência da
mudança comportamental e das transições para  a sustentabilidade, o artigo propõe um quadro tridimensional
composto por: (1) diagnóstico comportamental e segmentação dos públicos, (2) cocriação de valor e envolvimento
das partes interessadas, e (3) feedback e reforço sistémico. Este quadro foi conceb ido para explicar como os
comportamentos pró -sustentáveis podem ser adotados, mantidos e ampliados de forma a apoiar transições
sociotécnicas de longo prazo. Os resultados sintetizam evidências que mostram que as intervenções orientadas para
a sustentabilidade são mais eficazes quando ultrapassam a mera sensibilização genérica e passam a abordar
barreiras comportamentais heterogéneas, a envolver as partes inter essadas em processos de cocriação e a criar
ciclos de feedback reforçadores alinhados com infraestruturas, instituições e normas sociais. O diagnóstico
comportamental permite a conceção de intervenções direcionadas, ao reconhecer diferenças de motivação,
capacidade, oportunidade e contexto social. A cocriação de valor reforça a relevância, a legitimidade e a equidade,
ao envolver colaboradores, comunidades e parceiros intersetoriais na construção de soluções. O feedback e o
reforço sistémico apoiam a manute nção a longo prazo, tornando os resultados visíveis, aproveitando a influência
social e incorporando os comportamentos em ambientes de políticas públicas e de serviços favoráveis.  O artigo
contribui teoricamente ao articular mecanismos de mudança comportam ental ao nível micro com dinâmicas de
transição para a sustentabilidade nos níveis meso e macro. Do ponto de vista prático, oferece às organizações e aos
decisores políticos um guia estruturado para operacionalizar objetivos de sustentabilidade através do marketing
social como uma capacidade estratégica, e não apenas como uma ferramenta de comunicação. O artigo conclui
delineando orientações para futuras investigações empíricas destinadas a testar o quadro proposto em diferentes
setores, contextos culturais  e domínios da sustentabilidade, com especial atenção à escalabilidade, à ética e ao
impacto de longo prazo.

INTRODUCTION

Sustainability has become a central priority for organizations, governments, and civil society as they
confront intertwined global challenges such as climate change, resource depletion, and persistent social inequality
(IPCC, 2023; United Nations, 2015). Beyond being an environmental agenda, sustainability increasingly functions
as a governance and innovation imperative: institutions are expected to redesign products, services, and business
models to create value while reducing negative externalities across the "triple bottom line" (Bocken et al., 2014).
Yet evidence from transition studies suggests that technological progress and policy targets alone rarely deliver
rapid, durable change unless they are matched by widespread shifts in everyday practices, market routines, and
social norms (Geels, 2002; Markard et al., 2012). In other words, sustainability is not only a technical challenge—
it is also a behavioral and cultural one.
In this context, social marketing—classically defined as the application of marketing principles to influence
voluntary behaviors for individua l and societal benefit —offers a potentially powerful pathway to accelerate
sustainability transitions (Andreasen, 1994; Kotler & Zaltman, 1971). Unlike commercial marketing, which
primarily optimizes exchange for private gain, social marketing explicitly t argets public value by diagnosing
barriers to behavior change (e.g., convenience, costs, habits, social pressure) and designing interventions that make
sustainable choices easier, more rewarding, and more socially supported (McKenzie -Mohr, 2011). This approach
aligns with contemporary behavior change science, which emphasizes that sustainable action depends not only on
attitudes and intentions but also on capability, opportunity, and motivation shaped by context (Ajzen, 1991; Michie
et al., 2011). Recent wo rk further argues that to address "wicked" sustainability problems, social marketing must
move beyond isolated, downstream campaigns toward upstream and systems -oriented strategies that reshape
institutions, infrastructures, and choice environments (Domegan et al., 2016; Peattie & Peattie, 2009).
Although research on corporate sustainability and environmental management has grown substantially, a
persistent gap remains in explaining how social marketing can be systematically integrated into sustainability
frameworks to influence sustainable behavior at scale—especially in ways that connect individual -level behavior
change to broader transition dynamics in markets and governance (Geels, 2002; Loorbach, 2010). This paper
responds to that gap by proposing a con ceptual integration of social marketing —particularly systems social
marketing—with corporate sustainability practice and policy, clarifying mechanisms through which behavior
change interventions can support innovation diffusion and long -term sustainability transitions (Domegan et al.,
2016; Markard et al., 2012).

1 LITERATURE REVIEW

Sustainability and Innovation
Sustainability scholarship increasingly frames innovation as a necessary condition for achieving
environmental stewardship, social equity, and long‑term economic viability. Rather than treating sustainability as
a "compliance" topic, many streams of resear ch argue that firms must innovate in technologies, processes, and
business models to reduce environmental burdens while maintaining (or even improving) competitiveness (Bocken
et al., 2014; Porter & van der Linde, 1995). This perspective is reinforced by w ork on sustainability transitions,
which shows that deep decarbonization and circular resource use typically require structural changes in production–
consumption systems rather than just incremental efficiency improvements (Geels, 2002; Markard et al., 201 2).
Accordingly, sustainability -linked innovation spans eco‑innovation, circular -economy initiatives, renewable -
energy adoption, and service-based models intended to dematerialize consumption (Geissdoerfer et al., 2017).
From an organizational viewpoint, the strategic rationale for sustainable innovation is also shaped by
stakeholder expectations and institutional pressures. Stakeholder theory suggests firms face demands not only from
shareholders but from customers, communities, employees, NGO s, and regulators—groups increasingly attentive
to transparent conduct and sustainability performance (Freeman, 1984). In parallel, institutional theory explains
why sustainability practices diffuse across industries through mimetic and normative pressures  (e.g., the adoption
of environmental management systems and sustainability reporting) and why sustainability can become a taken -
for-granted "rule of the game" with in a sector (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). These pressures matter because

Social Marketing: Beyond Promotion to Behavioral Change
Social marketing emerged as a field focused on using marketing principles to encourage behaviors that
deliver societal benefits, with early foundational work positioning it as a method for planned social change (Kotler
& Zaltman, 1971). Later definitions emphasized that social marketing is not simply communication or persuasion,
but a disciplined process of diagnosing behavioral problems, understanding audience barriers and motivations, and
designing interventions that make the desired behavior more likely to occur voluntarily (Andreasen, 1994).
Historically, social marketing has been applied extensively in areas such as public health, safety, and civic
participation—domains where behavioral outcomes are clearly defined and can be measured (Andreasen , 2002;
Truong, 2014; Vrablikova et al., 2024).
A key reason social marketing can be effective is its commitment to audience orientation and segmentation
(recognizing that "the public" is not homogeneous), and its explicit attention to "exchange" and "competition"—
that is, what people must give up to adopt a behavior and what rival behaviors, conveniences, or social rewards
compete with the intended change (Andreasen, 1994). This is particularly relevant for sustainability behaviors that
are often shaped by  everyday friction (time, effort, money), habits, and social influence. Social marketing ,
therefore, aligns naturally with behavioral science frameworks that explain why people adopt (or do not adopt) pro-
social and pro-environmental behaviors. The Theory of Planned Behavior, for instance, highlights that intentions
and behavior are influenced by attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control—constructs directly
relevant to sustainable c onsumption and daily practices (Ajzen, 1991). Social Cognitive Theory similarly
emphasizes observational learning, self -efficacy, and reciprocal interactions between individuals and their
environments—factors that help explain  how sustainable behaviors can spread through communities and social
networks (Bandura, 1986).
More recent approaches to behavior change stress that interventions must address not only motivation but
also capability and opportunity. The Behavior Change Wheel and COM‑B model (Capability –Opportunity–
Motivation–Behavior) offer a practical structure for designing interventions by linking behavioral diagnosis to
policy categories and intervention functions (Michie et al., 2011). In sustainability contexts, community -based
social marketing has been influential because it emphasizes removing practical barriers, leveraging social norms,
prompting commitments, and providing feedback —tools well -suited to recycling, energy saving, and mobility
behaviors (McKenzie-Mohr, 2011). Marketing research has  also produced integrative frameworks for shifting
consumer behavior toward sustainability (e.g., by activating social influence, aligning with identity, and reducing
perceived sacrifice), further supporting the idea that marketing tools can be repurposed for public value (White et
al., 2019).
Despite these strengths, scholars argue that social marketing must avoid being reduced to promotional
campaigns and instead confront the structural drivers of unsustainable consumption. Critical perspectives note that
focusing only on individual responsibility can obscure systemic constraints (infrastructure, pricing, availability)
and can unintentionally reproduce inequities if interventions assume equal resources and opportunities across
groups (Gordon, 2011; Peattie & Peattie, 2009). This critique has fueled an evolution from "downstream" behavior

Bridging Sustainability and Social Marketing
Linking sustainability and social marketing requires treating sustainability challenges not merely as
information deficits, but as shared behavioral and systemic problems in which individuals, organizations, and
institutions co -produce outcomes. Sustainability transition theories underscore that durable change depends on
shifts across multiple levels—niche innovations, incumbent regimes, and broader landscape pressures —implying
that behavioral interventions need to connect with innovation diffusio n and institutional change (Geels, 2002;
Markard et al., 2012). Social marketing contributes by offering a structured approach to designing interventions
that address behavioral barriers and enable the adoption of sustainable innovations —particularly when combined
with diffusion thinking (e.g., relative advantage, compatibility, complexity), which explains how new practices and
technologies spread through social systems (Rogers, 2003).
This bridge becomes stronger when social marketing is understood not just as messaging but as an approach
to designing value propositions, environments, and relationships that support sustainable choices. Service -
dominant logic and co -creation perspectives in marketing emphasize that value is co -created with stakeholders
rather than delivered unilaterally—an idea consistent with participatory sustainability governance and community
engagement (Vargo & Lusch, 2004). In practical terms, co -design processes (e.g., living labs, community pilots,
participatory workshops) can help en sure that sustainability interventions align with local constraints, build
legitimacy, and foster  social ownership —conditions associated with stronger implementation and reduced
resistance.
A significant development at this intersection is the emergence of systems social marketing, which explicitly
applies systems thinking to social marketing problems. Rather than focusing only on individual behavior, systems
approaches analyses feedback loops, institutional incentives, market structures, and infrastructure —helping
practitioners identify leverage points for scalable sustainability outcomes (Domegan et al., 2016). Systematic
reviews in this area suggest growing interest in macro and systems approaches that can influence policy, reshape
markets, and support collective action—features directly relevant to sustainability transitions (Flaherty et al., 2020;
Kopac et al., 2025 ). For example, promoting low -carbon mobility is unlikely to succeed through individual
persuasion alone; it typically requires coordinated i nterventions involving service design, pricing, urban
infrastructure, and norms around convenience and status. Systems social marketing provides a conceptual bridge
for integrating these elements into a coherent strategy.
Overall, the intersection of sustainability and social marketing points toward a "whole-system" practice:
organizations can align sustainability innovation strategies with behavioral insights, stakeholder co-creation, and
upstream policy engagement. This i ntegration is significant for reducing the implementation gap between
sustainability commitments (e.g., circular economy goals, net-zero targets) and the everyday behaviors and routines
that ultimately determine whether such commitments translate into measurable impact.

2 METHODS

The objective of this article is to develop and justify a conceptual framework that integrates social marketing
into sustainability strategies by specifying three design dimensions —(1) behavioral diagnosis and audience
segmentation, (2) value co-creation and stakeholder engagement, and (3) feedback and systemic reinforcement —
and explaining how these dimensions can support the adoption, maintenance, and scaling of pro -sustainable
behaviors. The fra mework is intended to bridge the gap between sustainability commitments and the behavioral
and systemic mechanisms required for implementation at scale.
To achieve this objective, the study employed an integrative systematic literature review combined with
conceptual synthesis. A structured search strategy was developed to capture interdisciplinary scholarship across
social marketing, pro-environmental behavior change, and sustainability transitions. The review process followed
widely used principles for tr ansparent evidence identification and reporting, informed by the PRISMA 2020
guidance for systematic reviews (Page et al., 2021) and established review procedures in management and social

3 RESULTS

Drawing on the proposed Conceptual Framework, the results are presented as a three-part synthesis showing
how sustainability-oriented social marketing programs can be designed and strengthened through: (1) behavioural
diagnosis and segmentation, (2) value co-creation and stakeholder engagement, and (3) feedback and systemic
reinforcement. Across the literature on pro-environmental behavior, social marketing, and sustainability transitions,
a consistent pattern emerges: sustainability outcomes are more likel y when interventions move beyond generic
awareness-raising toward context-specific design, shared ownership, and mechanisms that maintain behavior over
time (Andreasen, 1994; Geels, 2002; McKenzie-Mohr, 2011)

Behavioral Diagnosis and Audience Segmentation
The first set of results highlights that "sustainable behavior" is not a single behavior, but a family of actions
with different barriers, benefits, and social meanings (Steg & Vlek, 2009; Stern, 2000). As a consequence, programs
that treat the public as one undifferentiated target audience risk misaligning the intervention "offer" with what
specific groups actually need to change (Andreasen, 1994). In sustainability contexts —recycling, energy
conservation, sustainable mobility, reduced consumption —behavior is shaped by a mix of attitudes, norms,
perceived control, habits, and context, which vary across demographic and cultural settings (Ajzen, 1991; Stern,
2000).
A central result of applying the framework is that effective diagnosis tends to cluster barrie rs into four
practical segmentation logics (these are presented as conceptual "segments" that can be used as a coding scheme
in empirical work):
• Cost–benefit segment (economic exchange dominated): These audiences respond to interventions where
the personal "exchange" is clear: saving money, avoiding fees, or gaining tangible benefits. Sustainability
action is more likely when the perceived net benefit is immediate and credible (Andreasen, 1994; White et
al., 2019). In this segment, behavior change often hinges on pricing signals, convenience, and information
that reduces uncertainty about payoffs.
• Capability and confidence segment (skills/self-efficacy-dominated): Here, the barrier is not values but how
to act: uncertainty, low self-efficacy, or a lack of practical know-how. Behavior change theory suggests that
increasing capability—skills, routines, perceived competence—can be decisive (Bandura, 1986; Michie et

Value Co-Creation and Stakeholder Engagement
The second set of results shows that sustainability interventions become stronger when organizations shift
from unilateral messaging to co -created value propositions with stakeholders. This aligns with service -dominant
logic, which views value as co -created through interaction rather than delivered as a finished product (Vargo &
Lusch, 2004). In sustainability and social marketing, co-creation is not only an ethical preference—it is a practical
mechanism for increasing relevance, legitimacy, and feasibility.
Applying the framework suggests that co-creation operates through three engagement arenas:
• Internal co -creation (em ployees as implementers and citizens): Employees influence sustainability
outcomes both operationally (process compliance) and socially (norms and advocacy). When employees
participate in designing sustainability initiatives—rather than merely receiving instructions—organizations
can reduce resistance and strengthen implementation fidelity. This logic aligns with stakeholder theory:
employees are not only internal resources but also  key stakeholders with agency and tacit knowledge
(Freeman, 1984). It also reduces the risk that sustainability remains a "communications layer" detached
from daily work.
• Customer/community co -creation (context fit and shared ownership): Community -based ap proaches
emphasize that sustainable behaviors are embedded in local constraints; interventions must therefore be
designed with people, not simply for them (McKenzie -Mohr, 2011). Co -creation formats —community
workshops, pilot programs, local champions —help identify the "real barriers " and support culturally
resonant solutions. This also addresses a known critique of downstream -only approaches: focusing solely
on individual responsibility can overlook structural inequities and context differences (Gordon, 201 1;
Peattie & Peattie, 2009).
• Cross-sector co-creation (policy, infrastructure, and legitimacy): Many sustainability behaviors depend on
systems outside a single organization’s control (transport infrastructure, waste services, energy systems).

across public, private, and civil society sectors (Domegan et al., 2016). Transition governance literature

1983; Freeman, 1984). Conceptually, co-creation functions as both a design tool and a legitimacy mechanism.

Feedback and Systemic Reinforcement
The third results cluster concerns what happens after initial adoption. A typical pattern in behaviors change
research is that one-time adoption is easier than long-term maintenance, especially when habits, convenience, and
social reinforcement favor the old behavior (Bandura, 1986; Michie et al., 2011). Therefore, sustainability programs
require feedback loops that make progress visible, sustain motivation , and embed the behavior into social and
material systems.
Applying the framework suggests that reinforcement works through three complementary feedback
channels:
• Performance feedback (visibility of outcomes): Sustainability outcomes are often delayed or invisible (e.g.,
emissions avoided). Feedback mechanisms —dashboards, progress reports, "before/after" comparisons—
make impact tangible and reinforce perceived behavioral control, a key construct in the Theory of Planned
Behavior (Ajzen, 1991). Community-based social marketing emphasizes feedback as a practical method for
sustaining change and building a sense of efficacy (McKenzie-Mohr, 2011).
• Social feedback (peer influence and norm formation): Social learning processes strengthen behaviors when
people observe peers benefiting  from, participating in , or endorsing the action (Bandura, 1986). In
organizational and community settings, visible participation can shift descriptive norms ("people like us do
this"), which in turn supports maintenance and diffusion (Rogers, 2003; White et al., 2019). Digital
platforms can amplify this channel by showcasing part icipation and progress, but designers must consider
equity and privacy so that reinforcement mechanisms do not exclude low -access groups or create
surveillance concerns (Gordon, 2011; Peattie & Peattie, 2009).
• System feedback (alignment with infrastructure and rules): The most substantial reinforcement occurs when
sustainable behavior is supported by the "system": defaults, service design, incentives, and policy. Systems
social marketing highlights that behaviors persist when feedback loops are embedded in institutions and
environments—not just in communications (Domegan et al., 2016; Flaherty et al., 2020). This resonates
with sustainability transition theory: when niche practices become stabilized through infrastructure and
institutional support, they are more likely to scale and reshape the dominant regime (Geels, 2002; Markard
et al., 2012).

Integrated Result: A Practical Pathway from Adoption to Transition Support
Synthesizing the three dimensions yields an overarching result: sustainability-oriented social marketing is
most likely to contribute to transition-level impact when interventions follow a sequence of (a) diagnostic precision,
(b) shared ownership, and (c) reinforcement architecture. In transition terms, behavioral diagnosis improves the fit
between niche practices and user contexts; co -creation increases legitimacy and reduces resistance within the
regime; and reinforcement mechanisms support diffusion and institutionalization, increasing the likeli hood that
sustainable practices persist and scale (Geels, 2002; Loorbach, 2010). This implies that "successful" social
marketing for sustainability should be evaluated not only by short -term behavior metrics but also by indicators of
institutional embeddin g: new routines, stable partnerships, supportive infrastructures, and lasting norm change
(Domegan et al., 2016; Markard et al., 2012).

4 DISCUSSION

This paper advances both theoretical and practical implications by positioning social marketing as a missing
"behavioral engine" in many sustainability strategies. Sustainability and transition scholarship has repeatedly
shown that sustainable innovation does not diffuse automatically: new practices and technologies often struggle
against entrenched routines, incumbent infrastructu res, and regime -level lock -ins (Geels, 2002; Markard et al.,
2012). The proposed framework clarifies how social marketing can operationalize sustainability ambitions by
translating broad principles (e.g., circularity, low-carbon living, responsible consumption) into specific, measurable
behaviors, and by specifying the mechanisms through which those behaviors can be adopted, maintained, and
scaled (Andreasen, 1994; McKenzie-Mohr, 2011). In doing so, it connects micro-level behavioral change to meso-
and macro-level dynamics emphasized in sustainability transitions research.
The first theoretical contribution is the integration of behavioral diagnosis and segmentation into the
sustainability transition conversation. Much sustainability discourse still relies on generalized awareness and
attitudinal change . Yet,  evidence suggests that pro -environmental behavior is shaped by heterogeneous
determinants—attitudes, norms, perceived control, habits, and contextual constraints —which vary across groups
and settings (A jzen, 1991; Stern, 2000; Steg & Vlek, 2009). Social marketing 's emphasis on audience insight,
exchange, and competition analysis provides a structured way to identify what different segments must "give up"
(time, money, convenience, status) and what they r eceive in return (benefits, identity alignment, social approval)
(Andreasen, 1994). The framework , therefore, shifts sustainability interventions away from one -size-fits-all

From a managerial perspective, the integrated approach suggests that organizations can improve both

constraints, and may exacerbate inequities if interventions assume equal access to time, money, and infrastructure

involving affected groups, making trade -offs explicit, and identifying system -level fixes (Domegan et al., 2016).

communities—especially in contexts where greenwashing concerns are salient.

CONCLUSION

Integrating social marketing into sustainability str ategies offers a promising and practical pathway to
mobilize stakeholders, influence pro-sustainable behavior, and embed sustainability into organizational culture and
public policy. The central argument developed in this paper is that sustainability ambitions often remain abstract—
expressed through targets, principles, or reporting commitments —unless they are translated into concrete
behavioral pathways that people can adopt and sustain in real-life contexts. Social marketing contributes precisely
this mis sing link: a structured approach to understanding why behaviors occur, what competes with desired
behaviors, and how interventions can be designed to make sustainable action more feasible, meaningful, and
socially reinforced (Andreasen, 1994; McKenzie-Mohr, 2011).
The proposed conceptual framework consolidates this contribution into three interconnected dimensions—
behavioral diagnosis and segmentation, value co-creation and stakeholder engagement, and feedback and systemic
reinforcement—each of which is nec essary to move from short -term behavioral adoption to longer -term
stabilization and scaling. Behavioral diagnosis and segmentation address heterogeneity: sustainability behaviors
differ across contexts, and individuals and groups face distinct barriers shaped by attitudes, norms, perceived
control, and opportunity structures (Ajzen, 1991; Michie et al., 2011). Value co-creation strengthens relevance and
legitimacy by involving employees, communities, and partners in shaping interventions, thereby helping avoid the
pitfalls of one -way persuasion and supporting  solutions that fit lived constraints and social realities (Vargo &
Lusch, 2004; Domegan et al., 2016). Feedback and systemic reinforcement, finally, emphasize that behavior change
must be maintained through visible progress, social proof, and alignment with infrastructures and rules—conditions
that connect behavioral interventions with the broader system dynamics emphasized in sustainability transitions
research (Geels, 2002; Markard et al., 2012).
From a practical standpoint, the framework offers a usable blueprint for organizations and policy actors
seeking to operationalize sustainability goals. It suggests that effective sus tainability-oriented social marketing
should go beyond communications and incorporate intervention designs that restructure environments, reduce
friction, and create reinforcing loops that normalize sustainable choices. For organizations, this supports not only
compliance and reputation but also innovation adoption, capability development, and long -term competitiveness
in markets increasingly shaped by stakeholder and regulatory expectations. For public policy and community
initiatives, the framework highlights the importance of combining behavioural insights with upstream measures—
such as service design, infrastructure provision, and institutional incentives —to ensure that sustainable behaviors
are possible, equitable, and scalable (Domegan et al., 2016; Gordon, 2011).
Future empirical research should test and refine the proposed framework across industries, sustainability
domains, and cultural contexts to validate effectiveness and scalability. Priority areas include longitudinal studies
that distinguish sh ort-lived campaign effects from sustained practice change . These comparative studies  identify
which combinations of segmentation, co -creation intensity, and reinforcement mechanisms deliver the strongest
outcomes, and evaluations that incorporate equity an d ethical considerations alongside behavioral metrics.
Additional work should explore how organizations can integrate these principles into governance routines (e.g.,
strategy, innovation management, HR, procurement) so that social marketing becomes an emb edded capability
rather than a project -based activity. Ultimately, advancing sustainability transitions requires not only better
