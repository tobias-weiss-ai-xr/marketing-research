1. Introduction
1.1. Trust as a resource condition of the nonprofit form
The 2030 Agenda assigns nonprofit organizations a delivery role that neither markets nor
states can absorb, and it assigns that role to a sector whose resource base is voluntary in every
component. A sector unable to sustain public confidence cannot sustai n the delivery, which makes
trust a precondition of sustainable development outcomes rather than a communications concern
adjacent to them. A nonprofit organization converts voluntary contributions into mission outputs
without offering contributors an enforceable claim on the result. The contributor cannot verify most
of what happens after the transfer. Willingness to give therefore rests on an expectation about future
conduct, making trust the sector’s operating currency rather than a reputational bonus (Burt, 2014).
Becker (2023) identifies five trust -forming mechanisms, namely mission promise, reputation,
transparency and accountability, performance, and use of contributions, yet the list specifies what is
signaled and omits when, how often, and in what sequence. Those signals now travel almost entirely
through owned digital channels, so digital marketing in this sector is not a promotional layer added
to program  work but the primary way the organization transports evidence  to everyone who
assesses it. Emp irical work on charity social media marketing separates awareness, interaction,
timeliness, informativeness, customization , and advertisement as distinct dimensions that reach
brand trust and donation intention through  different routes (Bilgin and Kethuda,  2022). Of these
dimensions, timeliness carries an explicitly temporal claim, and it is also the one nonprofit practice
manages least deliberately: organizations plan what they publish far more carefully than how  they
publish it.
Sector-level data complicate the narrative of decline. Chapman et al. (2020) analyzed Edelman
Trust Barometer responses from thirty-one countries over nine years and found no general crisis of
trust in nonprofits despite repeated high-profile scandals. The aggregate is stable; variance sits at the
level of the individual organization,  where managerial decisions are made. Becker et al. (2020)
sharpen the point with a multilevel design covering 4,072 dyads from 1,686 individuals and 102
German nonprofits, reporting that individual con text contributes as much as individual trust
evaluation while organizational context explains far less than anticipated. If organizational attributes
explain little in a static cross -section, the explanatory work lies in how those attributes are enacted
over time.
The informational environment raises the stakes of that timing. Systematic evidence covering
2015 to 2024 identifies digital instruments, including blockchain -based recording and social
platforms, as the principal vector for expanding disclosure (Nurdiani et al., 2025). Disclosure capacity
has expanded faster than disclosure discipline. An organization can now publish more, faster , and to
more audiences, so gaps in its publication record become visible sooner and to more observers.
1.2. Consequences of trust loss
Trust loss in the nonprofit setting is not a reputational inconvenience that a marketing remedy
can fix. Clarke (2021) reviews the 2018 Oxfam scandal and the responses of the organization, peer
international nongovernmental organizations, a nd United Kingdom regulators, and argues that the
episode marked a structural shift in accountability expectations rather than an isolated failure. A
systematic review of seventy -one articles covering three decades of scandal research treats
prevention and response as distinct managerial problems (Chapman et al., 2022). The consequences
cascade through the resource base: contributions contract, volunteer participation weakens,
partnerships lapse, and access to beneficiaries narrows. The volunteer and benefi ciary components

1.3. Research gap and problem statement
The literature has produced a dense catalog  of trust determinants but a thin ac count of how
they coordinate . Transparency has been reviewed as a self -standing construct with its own
antecedents and reporting logics (Ortega-Rodriguez et al., 2020), governance and accountability as a
separate stream linked to the preservation of social  mission (Eguzo, 2020), and perceptual
determinants have been estimated on population data, with financial accountability, transparency,
familiarity and institutional trust each carrying an independent effect (Farwell et al., 2019). None of
these streams specifies what happens when a determinant previously enacted at a stable interval is
enacted late, irregularly, or not at all. La Torre et al. (2020) come closest, arguing for a shift from
accounting-based accountability toward accountability-based accounting and insisting on timeliness
as a condition of dialogue. The nonprofit trust literature has not made timeliness a modeling
parameter.
The purpose of this paper is to construct a conceptual model that explains the formation,
maintenance, destruction , and restoration of public trust in nonprofit organizations as a single
temporally ordered process, and to specify how that model is executed through digital marketing.
The model demarcates trust from adjacent constructs, describes the ecosystem that produces i t,
reduces the documented determinants to four operational domains, justifies trust rhythm as the
temporal parameter, formalizes the asymmetry of recovery, and specifies indicators, channel -level
practices, and testable propositions.
Three research questions follow. RQ1 asks how the documented determinants of trust interact
rather than accumulate. RQ2 asks why the regularity of organizational signals carries more weight
than their intensity. RQ3 asks why restoring trust takes longer than its initial acquisition.
1.4. Contribution and structure
The paper contributes on three levels. The theoretical contribution is the construct of trust
rhythm, which converts a managerial intuition about consistency into a parameter with frequency,
amplitude, and predictability. The integrative contribution is an architecture that assigns dispersed
determinants to four domains and connects each to an observable signal and an empirical proxy. The
applied contribution is a monitoring indicator set, a mapping of the domains onto digital marketing
channels and their cadence, and a crisis protocol with time thresholds. Across all three levels, the
contribution to the sustainability agenda is to convert accountability from a declared value into an
inspectable schedule, which subsecti on 5.1.1 maps onto named Sustainable Development Goal
targets and indicators.
2. Literature review
2.1. The construct of public trust and its theoretical frames
Public trust in a nonprofit organization is a collective expectation that the organization will
continue to act in accordance with its declared mission under conditions of incomplete information
and voluntary exposure to loss. The defining feature is orientation toward the future. Subsection 4.1
sets out the demarcation from reputation, image, loyalty, and satisfaction in full.
The theoretical frames applied to nonprofit trust divide over whether trust is calculated or
socially constituted. Stakeholder theory links accountability to preserving social mission across
claimants (Eguzo, 2020), while attr ibution theory explains how the perceived locus, stability, and
controllability of a failure shap e organizational forgiveness (Hou et al., 2020). Against both, Keating
and Thrandardottir (2017) challenge the rational model embedded in the accountability ag enda,
arguing that it misrepresents the social character of the donor relationship. If trust responds to social
signals rather than verification alone, then the level of trust reflects the pattern of signa ling rather
than the volume of disclosed data.
Adjacent domains correct the tendency toward institutional isolation. A systematic review of
seventeen studies on social trust in emergency and disaster relief distributes trust -building factors
across local government, social media, social capital and communi ty participation (Khankeh et al.,
2023). A scoping review of 194 quantitative studies on trust and public health identifies
socioeconomic position, perceived institutional performance, and the information environment as

2.2. Determinants of trust and the communication environment
Financial transparency occupies the largest shar e of the evidence base. A systematic review
shows that, absent binding disclosure legislation, nonprofits converge on voluntary disclosure
policies whose scope they define themselves, and links that architecture to broader sustainability
commitments (Orteg a-Rodriguez et al., 2020). Later synthesis covering 2015 to 2024 identifies
digital instruments as the emerging carrier of transparency and recommends board diversity and
cross-sector collaboration as structural enablers (Nurdiani et al., 2025), a recommendation qualified
by evidence that the relationship between board composition and web transparency is conditional
rather than linear (Benito-Esteban et al., 2024). Analy zing data from 3,853 Canadians, Farwell et al.
(2019) report that perceptions of financi al accountability, transparency , and familiarity, together
with institutional trust, significantly shape trust in charitable nonprofits. Familiarity matters here
because it is not an attribute of a single disclosure act but the residue of repeated exposure .
Accountability is not a monotonic good. Keating and Thrandardottir (2017) show that
intensified accountability demands can displace the relational basis of giving, so formal compliance
may substitute for relational signaling. La Torre et al. (2020) argue  from the reporting side for
accountability-based accounting supported by more timely non -financial information. Satia (2024)
completes the determinant set, concluding that accountability mechanisms deliver integrity gains
only when combined with political  will and participatory arrangements, placing the burden on
sustained behavior rather tha n the formal existence of a framework. Governance research reaches
an equivalent conclusion by tracing accountability arrangements to the protection of social mission
(Eguzo, 2020), while Burt (2014) frames the same variables from the fundraising side.
Communication is where the evidence base thins. The nonprofit literature reviewed here
contains no study isolating the effect of social platforms on organizational trust,  and the closest
treatment classifies social media as one of four trust -building factors in disaster relief (Khankeh et
al., 2023). The absence is consequential, because the channel that carries most disclosure also carries
most allegations. Nonprofit -sector research nevertheless supplies part of the design logic, and it is
the appropriate starting point. Saxton and Guo (2011) show that web -based accountability practice
separates into disclosure, which publishes evidence, and dialogue, which sustains contac t, and that
organizations perform the first far more readily than the second; that split is the one the four-domain
architecture below reproduces as openness against communication. Waters and Jamal (2011) find
that nonprofit social media output is dominate d by one -way informational updates issued at
irregular intervals, which establishes , based on nonprofit data,  that cadence is unmanaged rather
than merely under -theorized. Lovejoy and Saxton (2012) classify that output into information,
community, and acti on, showing that the three serve different relational functions and therefore
cannot substitute for one another within a single posting schedule. These studies establish, using
nonprofit data, that the pattern of contact is irregular and largely unmanaged;  they do not offer  a
design logic for sequencing it.
Adjacent research in professional services supplies that design logic, and the transfer is
deliberately narrow. Avagimyan (2026b) models omnichannel brand communication through
segmentation, personalization, and retention design, treating message sequence rather than message
volume as the unit of analysis. Andreasyan (2026e) specifies lifecycle messaging in which contact
cadence predicts relationship persistence. Balaian (2026b) examines trust signals, offers, and funnel
design as determinants of engagement conversion, the closest available operationalization of a trust
signal as a designed and repeatable arti fact, and positioning research ties a stated value proposition
to the revenue model that must sustain it (Andreasyan, 2026c).
Exactly one claim is carried across from that body of work: a signal acquires evidentiary
weight from its position in a sequence. No conversion metric, funnel structure , or revenue logic
transfers with it, and three boundary con ditions limit even that narrow claim. The beneficiary of a
nonprofit service is not the party that funds it, so a message recipient cannot be treated as a revenue
source. Withdrawal is a neutral event in a commercial market but a transmitted signal in a nonprofit
ecosystem, because the exit of a donor or a partner is itself observed by the remaining groups.
Moreover, commercial cadence is bounded by audience tolerance for advertising, whereas a
regulatory calendar bounds nonprofit cadence  that the organizat ion does not control. These
conditions are stated here, at the point of transfer , and their consequences for the scope of the
framework are taken up again in subsection 5.3.
What that literature does not supply is a unit of analysis that spans the gap betw een contacts.
Digital marketing practice in the nonprofit sector is organized around the campaign, and its
governing metrics, namely reach, engagement, cost per acquired donor , and conversion to a

2.3. Measurement, violation, and repair
Measurement remains a weakness in trust research. Studi es often substitute confidence
vocabulary for trust and ignore social capital (Wilk & Platt, 2016), relying on implicit single -item
indicators rather than validated multi -item instruments (Larson et al., 2018). The public health
scoping review reports that only three of 194 studies addressed trust repair or maintenance (Kalulu
et al., 2025). Nonprofit research is better served on the instrument side, through multinational
barometer data (Chapman et al., 2020) and multilevel survey designs (Becker et al., 20 20), yet it
inherits the same weakness over time. Recent work narrows that gap by validating a
multidimensional measure of donor -perceived transparency, which provides an instrument for one
of the four domains modeled below (Dethier et al., 2024).
The viol ation literature is more mature. The systematic review of nonprofit scandals
synthesizes three decades of work into a model of causes and consequences. It separates prevention
from response (Chapman et al., 2022), and the Oxfam case shows how a single epis ode reallocates
accountability expectations across an entire organizational field (Clarke, 2021). On repair, Hou et al.
(2020) show that a higher locus of causality, higher stability , and higher controllability make
forgiveness harder to obtain. For a temp oral model, this implies that attribution forms early, before
corrective evidence can be assembled, which is why the interval between the triggering event and
the response carries disproportionate weight.
Professional services research offers measurement architecture the nonprofit field has not
imported. Multi-touch attribution with incrementality testing addresses the inferential problem that
arises when a shift in public trust is attributed to a specific disclosure act (Andreasyan, 2026d), and
a complementary protocol sets the evidentiary standard required before claiming an outcome
(Avagimyan, 2026a). Retention research treats a continued relationship as the observable
consequence of accumulated confidenc e (Avagimyan, 2026d), and bottleneck diagnosis isolates the
single constraint governing system performance (Balaian, 2026a). The analytical move follows: an
organization does not need to improve all four domains at once, only to identify which one has broken
its established rhythm.
2.4. Synthesis and statement of the gap
Four deficits emerge from this review. The first is temporal. The determinants of trust have
been cataloged in detail . At the same time,  the sequence, frequency , and predictability with whi ch
organizations enact them have not been modeled, even though timeliness is treated as a condition of
accountability in the reporting literature (La Torre et al., 2020) and familiarity operates as a
determinant in perceptual studies (Farwell et al., 2019) . The second is structural. Trust is measured
as a scalar attached to an organization, whereas evidence on stakeholder heterogeneity and on the
weakness of organizational context in cross -sectional designs (Becker et al., 2020) suggests uneven
distribution across groups observing from different positions. The third is evidentiary. Repair and
maintenance remain almost unstudied relative to violation, with only three of one hundred ninety -
four studies in the most recent scoping review addressing them directly  (Kalulu et al., 2025). The
fourth is operational. Determinants have been cataloged for the organization, not for the channel that
carries them, so no reviewed account states which digital marketing practice enacts which
determinant, at what interval, or how a failure of that interval would be detected.
These deficits define the contribution of the framework developed below. The model reassigns
the documented determinants to four operational domains, attaches a temporal parameter to each,
and derives the destruction -and-repair sequence from the attribution logic established in the crisis
literature (Hou et al., 2020; Chapman et al., 2022).

3. Methodology
3.1. Research design
This study examines the structure of relations among trust determinants that have already
been estimated; therefore,  primary measurement is not the relevant instrument. Researchers have
estimated determinants of nonprofit trust in large population samples and synthesized them  in
systematic reviews (Farwell et al., 2019; Ortega -Rodriguez et al., 2020; Nurdiani et al., 2025). What
those estimates do not carry is an architecture specifying how the determinants relate to one another
in time. The design is therefore conceptual by construction rather than by constraint.
The framework was built through framework synthesis, in four moves. An initial scaffold was
derived from the mechanisms proposed i n the conceptual literature, principally the five trust
mechanisms distinguished by Becker (2023) and the causal attribution logic applied to crisis
recovery by Hou et al. (2020). Evidence extracted from the corpus was coded against that scaffold.
Codes that did not fit were retained , and the scaffold was revised to absorb them, which is how the
temporal parameter entered the model. The revised structure was converted into propositions s o
each could be contradicted by observation. Framework synthesis was pr eferred over meta-analysis
because the reviewed studies measure trust through incompatible instruments (Wilk & Platt, 2016;
Larson et al., 2018).
The unit of analysis is the organization as a collective bearer of obligations toward its
stakeholder ecosyste m. Neither an individual executive nor an individual program serves as the
locus, although both generate signals the ecosystem attributes to the organization. The choice
matters for the crisis section, as the perceived locus of causality determines whether a failure is read
as personal or organizational (Hou et al., 2020).
3.2. Source selection and coding protocol
We retrieved records from Scopus - and Web of Science -indexed journals through database
queries and citation tracking. The window opens in 2020 because the disclosure environment shifted
with the diffusion of digital reporting instruments documented for 2015 to 2024 (Nurdiani et al.,
2025) and because the empirical study of institutional trust expanded sharply after that date (Kalulu
et al., 2025). Eight earlier works were admitted, either because later studies adopt their construct
definitions and measurement instruments without revision or because they remain the standing
empirical account of nonprofit online accountability practice: Saxton and Guo  (2011), Waters and
Jamal (2011) and Lovejoy and Saxton (2012) on nonprofit web and social media practice, Burt (2014)
on trust as a condition of giving, Wilk and Platt (2016) and Larson et al. (2018) on measurement
practice, Keating and Thrandardottir (2017) on the critique of the accountability agenda, and Farwell
et al. (2019) on the perceptual determinants of trust in charities. We retained peer-reviewed articles
and academic book chapters in English; we excluded conference abstracts, unreviewed preprin ts,
non-indexed outlets, and descriptive case reports without a theoretical frame, with one exception: a
working paper with a registered identifier that we retained for its systematic coverage of governance
and accountability (Eguzo, 2020).
This paper reports the retrieval and screening procedure so  the corpus can be reconstructed.
Eleven search strands structured the retrieval, covering the conceptualization of public trust, the
theoretical frames applied to it, financial transparency and voluntary disclos ure, accountability and
reporting practice, leadership ethics and governance quality, program effectiveness, media and
platform communication, crisis communication, measurement instruments, violation and repair, and
cross-cultural comparison. Queries combi ned a construct term, namely trust, public trust , or
confidence, with a sector term, namely nonprofit, charity, nongovernmental organization, or third
sector, and, in the communication strands, with a channel term, namely digital marketing, social
media, or online disclosure. Searches ran on titles, abstracts , and keywords from January 2020.
Because the design is a purposive framework synthesis rather than an exhaustive systematic review,
retrieval was iterative across the eleven strands and continued until additional records returned only
determinants already represented in the coding frame. For that reason, no PRISMA flow count is
reported: the corpus was assembled to populate a coding frame, not to establish the prevalence of a
finding, and a screening co unt would imply  an exhaustiveness this design does not claim. The
retained corpus comprises 40 peer-reviewed sources: 26 are nonprofit, governance, and public-trust
studies coded for determinants and measurement practice, and 14 are studies of communicatio n
sequence and cadence in adjacent professional -services settings, admitted under the boundary
conditions set out in subsection 2.2. The full source -to-domain coding table is provided as
supplementary material, so that the corpus and every coding decision can be inspected directly. Two

Coding proceeded in two passes. The first pass extracted every organizational attribute

We applied five comparison vectors to each coded item: the stakeholder group addressed, the
channel carrying the signal, the periodicity at which the signal is normally emitted, the observability
of the underlying result, and the reversibility of a violation. The last two carry most of the analytical
weight. Observability separates domains whe re the ecosystem can verify a claim from those where
it must extend credit, and reversibility distinguishes failures that admit correction from failures that
do not (Chapman et al., 2022).
3.3. Limitations of the method
Four limitations bound the claims th at follow. The framework rests on secondary evidence
and generates no primary measurement, so the propositions in subsection 4.4 are stated but not
tested. The conclusions inherit the co rpus’s coverage bia s, which concentrates in high -income
settings with developed regulatory infrastructure, and national context conditions the relationship
between institutional and organizational trust (Farwell et al., 2019; Chapman et al., 2020). The coding
rule that assigns attributes to domains by signal class is defensi ble but not unique: a rule organized
by stakeholder group rather than by signal class would produce a different and equally arguable
partition, so the four-domain architecture is offered as reproducible rather than as uniquely correct.
A single reviewer conducted screening and coding, so no inter -rater agreement statistic is available,
which limits the claims of subsection 4.2 more tightly than a multi -coder design would. To keep the
procedure open to inspection in the absence of such a statistic, the supplementary material lists every
extracted attribute, the domain to which it was assigned, and the signal class that produced the
assignment, so that any individual coding decision can be reproduced or contested.
4. Framework development
This section derives everything from the coded corpus through  framework synthesis. The
statements are theoretical propositions rather than empirically observed outcomes: none has been
estimated on data collected for this study, and subsection 4.4 states , for each,  the observation that
would falsify it. The language of the section should be read accordingly.
4.1. Demarcation of the construct and the trust ecosystem
Public trust in a nonprofit organization is defined here as the collective expectation, held
under incomplete infor mation, that the organization will act in future accordance with its declared
mission. Everything analytically distinctive about the construct follows from its tense. Reputation is
an accumulated judgment of what an organization has already done; trust is an advance against what
it has not yet done. An organization with an unblemished record can hold weak trust if its future
conduct has become unpredictable, and one with a damaged record can hold usable trust if its
corrective trajectory is legible.
Loyalty and satisfaction sit closer to behavior than to expectation: the first describes repeated
giving, and the second the assessment of a completed interaction. Neither anticipates. Becker (2023)
treats reputation as one of several mechanisms feeding trust, wh ich supports the separation while
showing how often the two are used interchangeably (Table 1).

Because the evidentiary bases differ, aggregate trust behaves as a vector rather than as a
scalar, and an organization can hold the confidence of its principal donors while losing the confidence
of the general public. Local ruptures are therefore diagnostically informative, because a single score
reports as a modest decline what the vector shows as a rupture in one group. Publ ic trust also has
structural priority over donor trust, because familiarity and general institutional standing enter
donor evaluation as inputs (Farwell et al., 2019). This is why organizational context explains less in
cross-sectional designs than practit ioners expect (Becker et al., 2020): the organization is assessed
through a filter it does not own.
4.2. The four domain architecture and the rhythm of trust
The coding procedure reduced the documented determinants to four domains. Openness
covers the voluntary release of information about operations, finance, and decisions, and its signal is
the disclosure itself, verifiable against accounts; separating this disclosure function from the dialogue
function carried by communication follows the division establ ished in web -based nonprofit
accountability practice (Saxton & Guo, 2011). Accountability covers accepting consequences for
results, and its signal is submission to external judgment rather than the content of any single report.
Communication covers the ma intained flow of contact through social platforms, media and direct

An organization reporting quarterly for three years has established a quarterly expectation,

The mechanism linking a missed interval to trust decline is inferential: audiences treat silence
as withheld information, and trust falls before any adverse  outcome is confirmed. This aligns with
the finding that intensified accountability can damage trust (Keating & Thrandardottir, 2017),
because each mandated report creates another interval that can be missed . It frames timeliness in
the reporting literature (La Torre et al., 2020) as conformity to expectations  rather than speed.
4.3. Destruction of trust and the asymmetry of recovery
Triggers of trust destruction can be ordered by the speed and depth of the damage they inflict:
diversion of organizational funds to personal use ranks first, followed by falsified reporting,
concealment of material information, silence during an unfolding crisis, and overstatement of
achievement. The first two do not merely reveal a failure of performance; they invalidate the
evidentiary channel, meaning the organization’s own reporting, through which every other claim
reaches the ecosystem. Once reporting is known to be falsified, subsequent reports cannot restore
confidence, because the same mechanism produces them. This is why financial violations inflict
damage disproportionate to the sums involved, and why the scandal literature treats prevention and
response as separate problems (Chapman et al., 2022). The Oxfam case extends the same logic to the
field level (Clarke, 2021).
Recovery requires more time and evidence than initial acquisition. Three factors produce the
asymmetry: audiences shift to heightened scrutiny, early attribution to internal causes blocks
forgiveness (Hou et al., 2020), and restoring regul arity requires several completed intervals. An
organization can publish a great deal in one week. It cannot publish three quarters of consistent
reporting in one week (Fig. 3).

4.4. Measurement and propositions
Direct measurement of public trust proceeds through population survey instruments, and the
multinational barometer series enables longitudinal comparison at the sector level (Chapman et al.,
2020). Their weakness at the organizational level is cost and cadence, as few organizations can field
a survey often enough to detect a change while it is still correctable. Stability of contribution flow,
the share of repeat donors, volunteer retention, partnership duration, beneficiary demand , and
referral behavior each register the behavioral consequence of confidence without asking about it,
and they map onto the domains asymmetrically: repeat donation tracks openness and accountability,
while volunteer retention and beneficiary demand track communication and demonstrated
performance (Table 2).
Two measurement requirements follow. The measurement must be longitudinal, because the
model’s central variable is variance in an interval and a point estimate cannot express it. It must also
be disaggregated by stakeholder group, because a scalar conceals the vector structure established in
subsection 4.1. Neither requirement is exotic, and both are absent from most reported practice,
where trust is measured through single -item proxies and the vocabulary of confidence rather than
through validated instruments (Wilk & Platt, 2016; Larson et al., 2018).

4.5. The trust rhythm method in nonprofit digital marketing
The framework becomes practice as a five-step method. The first step is an inventory of actual
intervals: for each domain , the organization records the dates of the last twelve enactments and
computes the mean interval and its variance, turning  the current rhythm into a measured quantity
rather than an intention. The second step fixes the baseline cadence at a level the organization can
hold in its worst quarter, and predictive scoring under capacity constraints supplies the arithmetic
for setting that floo r against available staff time (Avagimyan, 2026c). The third step assigns each
domain to the channels that carry it and writes the resulting cadence into one editorial calendar. The
fourth step monitors interval variance as a leading indicator, ahead of th e behavio ral proxies in
subsection 4.4, and a scoring approach that compresses heterogeneous relational signals into a single
decision-ready index offers a workable form for that monitor (Andreasyan, 2026a). The fifth step
attaches the crisis protocol of s ubsection 4.3 to a latency threshold agreed before any triggering
event.
This method makes three tasks tractable. Detection moves earlier, because a widening interval
is observable while trust is still correctable and before any adverse outcome is confirme d. Indicator
interpretation becomes safer, because separating rhythm from volume separates the domains that

4.6. An illustrative application of the interval inventory
The first step of the method underpins the rest, and it is worth showing in full. The values used
below are illustrative and are constructed to demonstrate the computation rather than reported from
an observed organization. The procedure itself, however, operates entirely on dates that nonprofit
organizations already hold, so any organization can substitute its own record without collecting new
data.
Consider a mid -sized service delivery nonprofit observed over eight quarters. For the
openness domain, the analyst records the publication date of each disclosure document released in
that window; for accountability, the date on which each statutory return was filed; for
communication, the date of each post on the principal maintained platform; and for dem onstrated
performance, the date on which each outcome claim was released with supporting evidence. Suppose
the openness intervals, measured in days between consecutive publications, are 91, 88, 94, 90, 92,
148, 61 and 89. The mean interval is 94 days, whic h reads as a stable quarterly rhythm and would
satisfy any audit of publication frequency. The standard deviation is 24 days, and it is carried almost
entirely by a single pair of values: one interval of 148 days followed by a compensating interval of
61. Under a volume metric , nothing has happened, because the same number of documents was
published across the window. Under the framework, the organization missed an expectation it had
itself established and then published twice in quick succession to restore the count, which is precisely
the pattern P2 predicts will register as a negative signal before any adverse outcome is confirmed.
Suppose the same organization records a communication standard deviation of four days
against a mean interval of seven, an accountability filing latency that never exceeds two days against
its own prior record, and a performance interval whose variance cannot be computed because only
two outcome claims were released across the eight quarters. Two conclusions follow directly, and
neither requires a survey. The domain currently constraining trust is openness, because it combines
an established expectation with a visible break ; the editorial calendar and any additional staff time
belong there rather than in the weekly posting schedu le, which  is already stable. Demonstrated
performance, in turn, is not underperforming but unmeasurable, and that is itself the finding: a
domain enacted twice in two years has no rhythm to violate, and the first task there is to establish an
interval rather than to protect one. This is the diagnostic move the method exists to make, and it is
available from a spreadsheet of dates.

5. Discussion
5.1. Theoretical contribution and position relative to existing accounts
The framework advances by reorganizing a settled body of findings around a variable the field
has not modeled. Determinant-based accounts explain trust levels through the presence and strength
of organizational attributes and perform well in cross -sectional estimation (Farwell et a l., 2019).
They perform poorly in cases where  trust declines while every attribute remains formally in place.
The rhythm construct resolves that class: if the operative signal is the interval rather than the act, a
decline without an event is expected rather than anomalous.
This position also reinterprets a result treated as discouraging. Becker et al. (2020) report that
organizational context explains far less of public trust than anticipated once individual context is
modeled under a static reading that suggests limited managerial leverage. Under the present reading,
it suggests a specification problem, because organizational context was measured as attributes held
at a point in time, which is the operationalization the rhythm argument predicts will underp erform.
The claim is testable and could be false. If temporal measures of organizational conduct add no
explanatory power over static attribute measures, the framework loses its principal justification.
Stakeholder reasoning supplies the ecosystem structure and the vector treatment of aggregate
trust (Eguzo, 2020). Attribution theory supplies the destruction -and-repair sequence, and
specifically the finding that locus, stability,  and controllability govern the availability of forgiveness
(Hou et al., 2020). Signaling logic supplies the inferential mechanism that converts a missed interval
into negative information. The critique advanced by Keating and Thrandardottir (2017) remains
unresolved, because each additional mandated disclosure creates another  interval that can be
missed. This mechanism is inconsistent with regulatory reasoning that treats disclosure volume as
monotonically beneficial. A rule raising mandatory reporting from an annual to a quarterly cycle, for
instance, quadruples the number of deadli nes an organization can visibly miss, and can therefore
lower observed trust even where every individual report is accurate in content. Reporting research
pointing toward accountability-based accounting is, by contrast, consistent with the mechanism (La
Torre et al., 2020).
The relation to existing digital marketing frameworks is additive rather than competitive.
Engagement and funnel models optimize the amplitude of a single contact through targeting, creative
quality, and personalization, and they evaluat e that optimization inside a campaign window. The
framework advanced here leaves those mechanics intact and adds the parameter they treat as
residual, namely the variance of the interval between contacts. The two are separable and therefore
testable agains t one another, since holding volume and creative treatment constant while
manipulating interval variance isolates the rhythm effect. Where charity social media research
identifies timeliness as a distinct dimension of practice (Bilgin and Kethuda, 2022), t he framework
specifies what it means: conformity to a self-established interval rather than speed.
One boundary condition deserves statement. The framework assumes an ecosystem capable
of observing intervals, which presupposes a minimum level of public visibility. Below that threshold,
in small localities or closed donor networks, trust is transmitted through direct relational contact
rather than through published signals, and the model should not be applied without modification.
The framework connects to t he sustainability agenda through governance rather than
environmental performance, and the connection is specific enough to be stated at the level of
individual targets rather than as a general alignment. Subsection 5.1.1 sets out that mapping.

5.2. Practical implications
The first implication reverses a common managerial priority. Organizations facing weak trust
typically increase the volume of what they publish. The model recommends stabilizing the interval
first and expanding volume second, at a cadence the organization can sustain during its worst quarter
rather than its best. A commitment to publish quarterly and keep it beats a commitment to publish
monthly and miss two months. Th is holds for all four domains and most acutely for accountability,
where the reporting calendar is visible to regulators and funders at once.
The second implication concerns the composition of disclosure. Publishing failures alongside
results serves a signaling function distinct from its ethical one, because an organization that reports
only favo rable outcomes gives the ecosystem no basis to distinguish selection from performance.
Demonstrated performance depends on evidence observable independently of the organization’s
own account, which is why external verification carries more weight than internal narrative (Ortega-
Rodriguez et al., 2020; La Torre et al., 2020).
The third implication is channel design. Because most disclosure now travels through digital
channels, maintaining cadence is an operational , not rhetorical,  problem. Nonprofit social media
output divides into informational, community -building, and action -oriented content that serve
distinct relational functions, so a disclosure calendar must  be designed as a sequence rather than
assembled as a series of independent posts (Lovejoy & Saxton, 2012). Evidence from Meta platform
campaigns indicates that staged warming sequences outperform single-exposure contact, supporting
the prediction in P1 that distributed regular contact outperforms concentrated intensity (Demydova,
2025a).
Automation resolves the resource constraint that makes cadence fragile in small
organizations. A reporting convention that fixes in advance which evidentiary standard a publish ed
claim must meet keeps amplitude stable when frequency is automated (Balaian, 2026c), and an audit
sequence that ranks  interventions after a diagnostic pass determines which domain enters the
calendar first (Andreasyan, 2026b). Scheduling and templating of routine contact then protect the
interval when staff time contracts. The claim for the nonprofit setting is not that automation
increases giving but that it decouples the reliability of routine communication from staff availability,
which is the most frequent cause of a missed interval.
The fourth implication cautions agains t instruments that raise engagement without raising
evidence. Gamified mechanics raise customer lifetime value in online service markets through
extrinsic reward (Demydova, 2025b), wh ich, in a nonprofit context, affects the loyalty and
satisfaction constructs demarcated in subsection 4.1 rather tha n the expectation of future mission
conformity. Gamification can therefore lift repeat giving while leaving trust unchanged, which
corrupts the indicator profile if repeat donation is used as the sole proxy. Automated content
generation carries the parallel risk, because a system that protects the schedule without adding
evidence sustains frequency while degrading amplitude. Practitioners are most likely to lose the
distinction between a maintained interval and a maintained signal .

5.3. Limitations and directions for further research
The framework is untested. Every proposition in subsection 4.4 states a relationship the
reviewed literature makes plausible, and none has been estimated on data collected for this purpose.
Two further limitations qualify the argument. The corpus concentrates in high -income jurisdictions
with functioning reporting infrastructure, so the interval mechanism may not transfer to settings
where publication is irregular by default , and audiences hold no stable expectation to violate. The
repair sequence rests on a thin evidentiary base, with only three of one hundred ninety -four studies
in the most recent scoping review addressing repair or maintenance directly (Kalulu et al., 2025).
The communication domain rests partly on transfer from commercial research. Subsection 2.2
states three boundaries limiting that transfer, and one  has a visible consequence for the framework
itself. Because a regulatory calendar bounds nonprofit cadence the organization does not control, the
accountability row of Table 4 carries a statutory calendar rather than a number. Whether the narrow
claim carried across, namely that a signal acquires evidentiary weight from its position in a sequence,
holds in a setting where the recipient of the message is not the source of the revenue remains an open
empirical question, and the experimental design proposed below is the direct test of it.
Four research directions follow. Panel measurement of disclosure timing matched to periodic
trust assessment would test P1 and P2 and requires nothing beyond archival collection of publication
dates paired with an existing survey series. Experimental manipulation of interval variance, holding
disclosure volume constant, would isolate the rhythm mechanism from t he volume mechanism, and
standard A/B protocols from digital marketing field research offer a ready design template. Event
study designs with measured response latency would test P5, and comparative longitudinal case
work across regulatory regimes would establish the boundary conditions identified in subsection 5.1.
Testing any of these requires validated multi -item instruments rather than the single -item proxies
that dominate (Wilk & Platt, 2016; Larson et al., 2018).

A pilot of the Trust Rhythm Method is specified here so that the first test does not require new

nonprofit organizations in one jurisdiction, stratified by budget size, observed over eight consecutive
quarters. The collected variable is a set of dates: publication dates for disclosure documents, filing

6. Conclusions
This paper reorganizes the fragmented determinants of nonprofit public trust, namely
openness, accountability, communication, and demonstrated performance, around a single temporal
parameter: the rhythm with which these domains are enacted. The central claim is that audiences do
not merely register the presence of trust -building signals but calibrate their expectations to the
organization’s own prior cadence, so that deviation from an established interval, rather than the
absolute frequency of disclosure, is what erodes confidence.
Three findings follow from this architecture. First, aggregate trust is not a  single score but a
vector distributed unevenly across nine stakeholder groups, so an organization can retain donor
confidence while losing public confidence without either shift showing up  in a composite metric.
Second, trust destruction is hierarchical: violations that corrupt the evidentiary channel itself, such
as fund diversion or falsified reporting, cause disproportionate and durable damage because they
invalidate every subsequent claim the organization makes. Third, restoration is structurally slowe r
than initial acquisition, because it requires several completed intervals under heightened scrutiny
rather than a single corrective act.
These results answer the paper’s three research questions . Determinants interact through a
shared temporal schedule r ather than accumulating independently, which answers RQ1 and is
carried by P1 and P3. Regularity is proposed to outweigh intensity because a missed interval
functions as withheld information, which answers RQ2 and is stated as P2. Restoration lags
acquisition because attribution forms before corrective evidence can be assembled, which answers
RQ3 and is stated as P6. Read against the Sustainable Development Goals, a disclosure rhythm that is
inspectable and consistent is the operational expression of accountable institutions under SDG 16, of
durable partnerships under SDG 17, and of a stable volunteer and staff base under SDG 8, as
subsection 5.1.1 sets out target by target.
The paper contributes an indicator set pairing behavioral proxies with the domains they track,
a mapping of those domains onto the digital marketing channels that carry them, a response
sequence for violations, and six propositions, each  with a falsification route. Its principal limitation
is that the framework remains untested. All six propositions await empirical confirmation, the repair
sequence rests on an especially thin evidentiary base, and t ransferring communication-design logic
from commercial to nonprofit contexts requires further scrutiny given the structural difference
between payers and beneficiaries in the two settings. Future research should prioritize panel
measurement of disclosure timing matched to periodic trust assessment; the interval inventory
illustrated in subsection 4.6 and the eight -quarter observational pilot outl ined in subsection 4.5
together offer a feasible starting point using data organizations already hold. The practical claim is
narrower than the usual prescription and more demanding: trust is not accumulated through
disclosure and is not defended through volume. It is held only while it is continuously reproduced.
