Abstract: Digital transformation requires vocational education to provide learning experiences that are
increasingly relevant to the needs of modern industry. This study aims to examine the integration of digital
technology in Teaching Factory –based vocational lea rning to strengthen the Vocational High School Center of
Excellence Program. This research employed a Systematic Literature Review method guided by the PRISMA
framework. Data were obtained from Scopus and Google Scholar through Publish or Perish, covering publications
from 2017 to 2026. From 369 initially identified documents, 67 articles were selected as the final corpus after the
identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion stages. The findings show that publications related to Teaching
Factory, digital technology, and the Vocational High School Center of Excellence increased markedly during
2023–2025, with Indonesia as the dominant research context. The identified digital technologies span six domains,
ranging from classroom -level ICT and digital  marketing tools to simulation -based and Industry 4.0 -oriented
technologies, and are pedagogically integrated through six recurring patterns centered on authentic production -
based learning, project - and work-based learning, teacher capacity -building, and d igital entrepreneurship. The
reviewed literature reports several potential contributions of digital technology integration, including stronger
school–industry alignment, student competency development, teacher pedagogical readiness, Teaching Factory
management, school product commercialization, and the identity of Vocational High School Centers of
Excellence as reference schools. However, this review also identifies several challenges, including uneven digital
integration, limited infrastructure, teacher readiness, industry collaboration, quality control, and limited evaluation
of learning impact. Building on these findings, this review proposes a preliminary Digital Teaching Factory model
comprising seven interlocking components (curriculum, pedagogy, techn ology, teacher capacity, industry
partnership, production and management, and quality assurance and evaluation), offered as a direction for future
empirical validation rather than as a finalized framework.

Keywords: Teaching factory, Digital technology, Vocational education

Introduction

The development of digital technology has brought substantial changes to education, including vocational
education, and Vocational High Schools (SMK) can no longer rely solely on conventional practical training to
prepare a workforce that meets industry needs. Industrial shifts toward automation, digital marketing, simulation,
and data-driven work systems demand that SMK graduates possess technical competencies, digital skills, and
work readiness aligned with the needs of the business and industrial sectors (Yoto et al., 2019; Yondri et al., 2020;
Mazlan et al., 2025a, 2025b, 2025c). Meeting this demand requires more than mastering theory and basic skills:
vocational learning must give students authentic exposure to work processes, industry standards, real -world

problem-solving, quality control, and technology relevant to their field (Roll & Ifenthaler, 2021; Vilppola et al.,
2022; Ramanda et al., 2025).

One widely used approach for meeting this demand is the Teaching Factory, which transforms the school into a
learning space that mimics an industrial environment so that students engage directly in production processes,
services, problem-solving, and produ ct development rather than learning concepts alone (Sugianto & Casmudi,
2022; Setiadi et al., 2025; Cholis et al., 2025; Elisabet et al., 2025). This role is increasingly at stake, however, as
production-based learning that remains disconnected from digita l technology risks falling behind an industry
already reshaped by simulation, automation, and data -driven tools (Riemann et al., 2020; Nanda et al., 2025).
Teaching Factories therefore need to evolve from physical production spaces into digital learning ecosystems that
integrate work practices, technology, entrepreneurship, school management, and industry needs (Yondri et al.,
2020; Muhardono & Meliza, 2023; Shofiana & Tohirin, 2025).

This context is increasingly relevant with the introduction of the Vocational High School Center of Excellence
Program, which aims to strengthen the quality of vocational education through curriculum alignment, improved
learning quality, stronger link -and-match initiatives, industry collaboration, teacher competency development,
and enhanced graduate work readiness (Ahmanda et al., 2022; Sholihah, 2022; Tessa & Humaedi, 2023). The
Teaching Factory is a relevant strategy for these objectives (Akyuwen et al.,  2023; Elisabet et al., 2025), but
strengthening Centers of Excellence through this approach is insufficient if it focuses solely on production: digital
technology must also be incorporated into strategies for learning, management, marketing, evaluation, a nd
industry collaboration (Septianingsih, 2023; Hastuti et al., 2025; Prihantoro et al., 2025).

Although Teaching Factories, digital technology, and Centers of Excellence in Vocational High Schools all hold
important roles in vocational education, the three are often still discussed separately. Studies on Teaching
Factories generally focus on the imp lementation of production -based learning, TeFa management, facility
readiness, or industry relations (Baharsyah & Irfan, 2024; Muharam et al., 2024; Prihantoro et al., 2025). On the
other hand, studies on digital technology in vocational education often di scuss the use of learning media, ICT,
simulations, or the digitization of learning without always linking them to Teaching Factories (Riemann et al.,
2020; Vilppola et al., 2022; Nanda et al., 2025). Meanwhile, discussions regarding Vocational High Schools  of
Excellence (SMK Pusat Keunggulan) are largely focused on policy, industry -education alignment (link and
match), curriculum, and industry partnerships (Asngad et al., 2023; Nuryanti et al., 2025; Sulistyawati & Mulyono,
2024). This situation reveals a s pecific fragmentation: many studies document the digital tools and technologies
used in vocational settings, while comparatively few offer a synthesis of the instructional design or pedagogical
strategies through which such tools can be meaningfully integr ated into Teaching Factory -based learning. This
situation highlights the need for a study that maps these three literatures onto one another and synthesizes their
combined findings into an actionable model, an outcome this review works toward in the form o f the integrative
Digital Teaching Factory model presented in the Conclusion.

To interpret the patterns that emerge from this body of literature, this review draws on four complementary
theoretical lenses: activity theory (Engeström, 2001), situated learning theory (Lave & Wenger, 1991), the
TPACK framework (Mishra & Koehler, 2006),  and the concept of boundary crossing (Akkerman & Bakker,
2011). These lenses are applied in the Discussion section to interpret the thematic patterns generated through the
synthesis procedure described in the Method, providing a conceptual basis for under standing digital technology
integration in the Teaching Factory as more than an inventory of individual tools and interventions.

A systematic review of the integration of digital technology in Teaching Factory -based vocational learning is
crucial as it can provide a more comprehensive understanding of the direction of vocational education
development. Through a Systematic Literature Review (SLR), previous studies can be mapped to determine how
digital technology is utilized, how it is pedagogically integrated, its contributions to strengthening Center of
Excellence vocational high schools (SMK), as well as the challenges and future d evelopment directions (Roll &
Ifenthaler, 2021; Vilppola et al., 2022; Mazlan et al., 2025a, 2025b, 2025c). In this way, the SLR functions as
more than a summary of prior research: it also identifies conceptual and practical gaps that can form the basis for
developing a digital technology-based Teaching Factory model (Yondri et al., 2020; Nanda et al., 2025).

Given this urgency, this article is designed to examine the integration of digital technology into Teaching Factory–
based vocational learning and its implications for strengthening the Vocational High School Center of Excellence
Program. The primary focus of this article is to understand how digital technology can strengthen the Teaching
Factory as a more authentic, adaptive, and industry-relevant vocational learning strategy. Through this study, it is
hoped that a more comprehensive understanding of the re lationship between the Teaching Factory, digital

Method

Research Design

This study employs the Systematic Literature Review (SLR) method to identify, evaluate, and synthesize prior
research related to the integration of digital technology in Teaching Factory-based vocational education. The SLR
method was chosen because it is suitable for mapping literature trends, identifying thematic patterns, pinpointing
research gaps, and formulating directions for future research based on published academic evidence. SLR is an
approach used to systematically collect and interpret previous research so that the literature review process is not
conducted randomly but through clear, transparent, and accountable stages (Kitchenham & Charters, 2007;
Snyder, 2019). Methodologically, this study adheres to the PRISMA framework to ensure the article s election
process is conducted systematically, transparently, and traceably. PRISMA is used to describe the stages of
identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and the final selection of articles for analysis. The PRISMA
framework helps researchers  report the study selection process more clearly through flowcharts and structured
reporting criteria (Moher et al., 2009; Page et al., 2021). This study does not aim to directly test the effectiveness
of an intervention but rather to map and interpret tre nds in literature. Accordingly, the analysis was conducted
using two approaches: descriptive mapping and thematic synthesis. Article screening, eligibility assessment, data
extraction, and quality appraisal were conducted by a single reviewer (the first author); no second reviewer
independently verified these stages, and no review protocol was registered in a public registry (e.g., PROSPERO)
prior to conducting this review, and no second -reviewer check was subsequently performed on any stage. The
single-reviewer process may therefore have increased the risk of selection and coding subjectivity, and both points
are acknowledged as limitations of the review process.

Data Sources and Search Strategy

The research data sources were drawn from two databases: Scopus and Google Scholar. Scopus was used to obtain
international articles related to Teaching Factory, Learning Factory, Work -Based Learning, TVET, Industry 4.0,
and digital technology in vocationa l education. Google Scholar was used to expand the scope of the literature,
particularly national articles discussing vocational high schools (SMK), Centers of Excellence for Vocational
High Schools (SMK Pusat Keunggulan), Teaching Factory/TeFa, digital te chnology, ICT, and Industry 4.0. The
search on Google Scholar was conducted using the Publish or Perish application. Both the Scopus and Google
Scholar searches were executed on 27 April 2026. The use of more than one data source was employed to broaden
the scope of articles and reduce the risk of limited search results from a single database. In a systematic literature
review (SLR), search strategies must be designed systematically to ensure that the retrieved articles align with the
research questions and that the search process can be traced back (Tranfield et al., 2003; Booth et al., 2016). The
publication year range was limited to 2017–2026 to ensure that the analyzed articles remain relevant to the latest
developments regarding the digitization of vocational education, Teaching Factory, Learning Factory 4.0, Industry
4.0, and Centers of Excellence for Vocational High Schools. The Scopus search was intentionally structured

Keyword Selection

Keywords were developed based on the three main research focuses: Teaching Factory–based vocational learning,
digital technology integration, and the Vocational High School Center of Excellence Program. When selecting
keywords for a systematic literature r eview (SLR), it is essential to consider main terms, synonyms, and related
terms to ensure search results are not overly narrow while remaining relevant to the study’s focus (Kitchenham &
Charters, 2007; Booth et al., 2016).  On Scopus, the search was conducted using the following Boolean
combination: (("teaching factory" OR "learning factory" OR "production -based learning" OR "work -based
learning" OR "industry -based learning" OR "factory -based learning") AND ("vocational education" OR
"vocational training" OR "vocational learning" OR "vocational school " OR "vocational high school" OR
"technical and vocational education and training" OR TVET) AND ("digital technology" OR "digital
transformation" OR "technology integration" OR "technology -enhanced learning" OR "educational technology"
OR ICT OR "Industry 4.0" OR "smart factory" OR "digital manufacturing" OR "digital simulation" OR "virtual
reality" OR "augmented reality"))

On Google Scholar via Publish or Perish, the search combined the following terms as alternatives within a single
query field: "Teaching Factory" OR "TeFa" OR "Learning Factory" OR "SMK" OR "Vocational High School"
OR "Digital Technology" OR "Technology Integration" OR "ICT" OR "Industry 4.0", restricted to the 2017–2026
publication-year range through Publish or Perish’s built-in date filter.

Because Publish or Perish retrieves records by querying Google Scholar’s full -text index rather than a fielded
metadata database, these terms could not be constrained to title, abstract, or keyword fields in the same way as
the Scopus Boolean string above;  this is a platform-level constraint rather than a difference in search intent, and
it is acknowledged here as a limitation of the Google Scholar search channel relative to Scopus. No additional
result-count cap was applied beyond this date filter: all 330 records returned by the query were carried forward to
the identification stage, consistent with the identification-stage count reported below. These keywords were made
flexible because national articles often use a mix of Indonesian and English terms, such as Teaching Factory,
TeFa, SMK, SMK Center of Excellence, digital technology, ICT/TIK, and Industry 4.0.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion and exclusion criteria are used to ensure that the analyzed articles align with the research focus. In a
systematic literature review (SLR), inclusion and exclusion criteria are essential for maintaining consistency in
article selection and ensur ing that included articles are truly relevant to the research questions  (Kitchenham &
Charters, 2007; Page et al., 2021).

Article Selection Process Using PRISMA

The article selection process was conducted through the stages of identification, screening, eligibility, and
inclusion. These stages followed the PRISMA principles, which emphasize transparent reporting of the study
selection process, ranging from the num ber of articles identified, articles excluded, reasons for exclusion, to the
final articles analyzed (Moher et al., 2009; Page et al., 2021). During the identification stage, 369 documents were
identified, comprising 39 documents from Scopus and 330 documents from Google Scholar via Publish or Perish.
No registries were used in this study. Prior to screening, 1 duplicate article and 112 documents that did not meet
the initial criteria were excluded. No articles were excluded via automated tools. Following this stage, 256 articles
proceeded to the screening stage. During the screening phase, 163 articles were excluded because they did not
align with the research focus, leaving 93 articles to proceed to the search and eligibility assessment phase.

Data Extraction

Once the final articles were selected, data extraction was conducted to gather key information from each article.
Data extraction in a systematic literature review aims to ensure that information from the analyzed articles is
recorded consistently and can be used to answer research questions (Kitchenham & Charters, 2007; Booth et al.,
2016). The extracted data included author names, year of publication, article titles, country or research location,
research methods, keywords, database sources, citations, types of digital technology,  forms of pedagogical
integration, contributions to the Center of Excellence for Vocational High Schools, as well as research challenges
or gaps. This data was then grouped according to the five research questions. For RQ1, the data was analyzed
based on year of publication, country, database, and keywords. For RQ2, the data focused on digital technology
and digitalization domains. For RQ3, the data was analyzed based on pedagogical integration patterns. For RQ4,
the data focused on contributions to strengt hening the Center of Excellence Vocational High School. For RQ5,
the data was analyzed based on challenges, gaps, and future research directions.

Article Quality Assessment

Article quality assessment was conducted to ensure that the articles included in the synthesis possess academic
relevance and validity. In systematic reviews, quality assessment is necessary so that the synthesis is not based
solely on the number of articl es but also considers the appropriateness of the focus, clarity of methods, and the
articles’ contribution to the research questions (Tranfield et al., 2003; Snyder, 2019). This appraisal was carried
out qualitatively by the first author against four indicators (topic relevance, methodological clarity, availability of
findings, and contribution to the research questions) rather than through a standardized quantitative checklist (e.g.,
MMAT or CASP); this is acknowledged as a methodological limitation, and n o per-article numeric scores were
generated. For reference, these four indicators broadly parallel core domains used in standardized instruments
such as the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT): clarity of the research question, appropriateness of the
design to that question, adequacy of the reported data and findings, and coherence between aims and conclusions;
however, this review applied them through the qualitative scoring rule described below rather than through the
MMAT checklist itself, because the corpus mixes empirical, evaluative, community-service, and conceptual study
types (see the classification below) that are not uniformly compatible with MMAT's category -specific criteria.
Articles most relevant to the Teaching Factory, vocational education, d igital technology, and the Center of
Excellence Vocational High School (SMK) were used as the primary sources for the synthesis. Meanwhile,
articles with more limited relevance were still utilized as contextual support if they helped explain the
strengthening of the Center of Excellence Vocational High School (SMK) Program. This topical/methodological
appraisal is conceptually distinct from the source-tier information (Scopus quartile and SINTA accreditation level)
reported for transparency in the Appendix,  which reflects the standing of the publishing outlet rather than the
methodological quality of the individual study. To make this appraisal more transparent and operational, each
article was scored qualitatively against the four indicators above using a t hree-point decision rule (fully met = 1,

Data Analysis Techniques

Data analysis was conducted using two techniques: descriptive mapping and thematic synthesis. Descriptive
mapping was used to address RQ1 by examining publication trends based on year, country, and keywords. The
results of the mapping are presented in tabl es and diagrams to make publication patterns easier to interpret.
Meanwhile, thematic synthesis was used to address RQ2 through RQ5. The synthesis involves reviewing the titles,
abstracts, keywords, methods, results, and discussions of the articles, then g rouping the findings based on main
themes. Thematic synthesis enables researchers to systematically identify patterns, relationships, and meanings
across a body of published literature (Thomas & Harden, 2008), drawing on principles of thematic analysis mor e
broadly (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Coding followed a consistent sequence across all themes: each reported
technology, practice, or outcome was first labeled by technology type, then by its primary instructional or
institutional function, then by the specific pedagogical or managerial use described in the source article, and finally
by the reported outcome or challenge associated with it; categories in Tables 2–4 were formed by grouping codes
that shared the same function-level label. RQ2 groups digital technology and digitalization domains identified in
the corpus, RQ3 groups patterns of pedagogical integration, RQ4 groups reported contributions relevant to
strengthening Vocational High Schools of Excellence, and RQ5 groups challenges, evidence gaps, and directions
for future development. Because several primary studies report both a classroom-level practice and its institutional
payoff within the same account, RQ3 and RQ4 necessarily draw on a partially shared set of studies; to keep the
two research question s analytically distinct, each shared study is cited for two different reported facts, a
pedagogical mechanism under RQ3 and an institutional-level outcome under RQ4, and no single reported finding
is used as evidence for both. In the Discussion, these them atic findings are further interpreted through four
complementary interpretive theoretical lenses: activity theory (Engeström, 2001), situated learning theory (Lave
& Wenger, 1991), the TPACK framework (Mishra & Koehler, 2006), and the concept of boundary c rossing
(Akkerman & Bakker, 2011). These lenses were used post hoc to aid interpretation and to help explain the patterns
identified through thematic synthesis, rather than as frameworks that guided the original coding process.

Synthesis and Reporting of Findings

The synthesis results are organized according to the sequence of the research questions. RQ1 is reported through
a mapping of publication trends and thematic focuses, while RQ2 through RQ5 are reported through a narrative
synthesis supported by thematic tables. The reporting of SLR results must be structured systematically so that the
relationship between research questions, the selection process, data extraction, analysis, and findings can be
clearly traced (Page et al., 2021; Snyder, 2019). Therefore, the  reporting maintains a direct connection between
the data, categories, and research questions, ensuring that the entire discussion remains within the scope of the
article: the integration of digital technology into Teaching Factory -based vocational learnin g to strengthen the
Vocational High School Center of Excellence Program.

Digital Technology and Digitalization Domains Integrated into Teaching Factory–Based Vocational Learning

Based on an analysis of 67 articles, the integration of digital technology into Teaching Factory -based vocational
learning does not appear in a uniform form. Some articles explicitly mention digital technologies such as ICT,
digital marketing, digital products, mobile simulators, virtual reality, Learning Factory 4.0, Industry 4.0, learning
management systems, and ISO-based digital systems. However, other articles discuss technology more indirectly,
for example, through the strengthening of industry -based curricula, production-based learning, the development
of Teaching Factories, or the enhancement of teacher and school capacity. Overall, the digital technology and
digitalization domains found in the article corpus are classified into the typology presented  in Table 2, which
organizes the corpus into six categories rather than reporting each article’s technology use individually.

ICT and digital competencies constitute the most frequently cited domain in the corpus, appearing on both the

ICT competencies are treated as a crucial component of work -based vocational teacher training (Vilppola et al.,

as an integral part of vocational teachers’ professional competencies.

Digital marketing, digital business, and digital products form the second major category, closely aligned with the

integral part of production, entrepreneurship, and marketing activity rather than merely a learning m edium.

Pedagogical Integration of Digital Technology into Teaching Factory, Learning Factory, Production-Based
Learning, Work-Based Learning, or Industry-Based Vocational Learning

Analysis results indicate that the integration of digital technology in Teaching Factory and industry -based
vocational learning is carried out through several pedagogical patterns. RQ3 is concerned specifically with the
classroom- and workshop-level mechanisms of this integration, that is, how digital technology is embedded into
the teaching–learning process itself, which is analytically distinct from RQ4, presented afterward, which examines
the institutional-level outcomes of this same integration for the SMK PK program. These patterns are important
because digital technology functions here as an integral part of instructional design rather than as a tool alone. In
the corpus of articles, digital technology is utilized to enhance authentic learning experien ces, project -based
learning, production -based learning, industry collaboration, teacher training, program evaluation, and school
entrepreneurship. Of these, the first three approaches discussed below (authentic production -based learning,
Project/Problem-Based Learning, and Work -Based Learning) constitute direct classroom - and workshop -level
pedagogical integration, whereas the remaining three (teacher training and capacity building,
evaluation/management/quality assurance, and entrepreneurship/commercializa tion) function as supporting
implementation mechanisms that shape the conditions under which classroom-level integration occurs, rather than
being pedagogical techniques in the narrow sense. Where a study is cited under both RQ3 and RQ4, each research
question draws on a distinct fact reported in that study: RQ3, below, reports what the intervention does inside the
classroom or workshop, while RQ4, presented afterward, reports what that same intervention is found to contribute
at the institutional level; no single reported finding is used as evidence for both questions.

The first pedagogical pattern embeds authentic, production -based tasks directly into the workshop sequence, so
that students move through the same production, service, and quality-control steps found in industry rather than a
simplified classroom simulatio n. This is achieved by scheduling fieldwork practice alongside production -based
coursework (Elisabet et al., 2025), sequencing workshop stages around a simulated industrial production cycle
(Setiadi et al., 2025), embedding a mobile QR -EFI simulator direct ly into problem-based task design (Nanda et
al., 2025), and adapting the same production -based structure to specific vocational tracks such as the laundry -
industry subject (Nastiti, 2022) and general Teaching Factory practice (Purwaningtias & Chamami, 2025 ). Read
together, these accounts describe a classroom -level mechanism: technology and authentic task design working
together so that the resemblance to an industrial work system extends to the workshop itself, and not to its stated
goals alone. What these same interventions are separately found to achieve at the school or program level is
addressed under RQ4.

The second pattern structures learning around projects, problems, and product development rather than single -
lesson tasks. This takes the form of a cross -subject Teaching Factory guidebook that threads production tasks
through general and vocational courses alike (Cholis et al., 2025), a project-based management structure built on
the New Teaching Factory model (Suranto et al., 2023), and problem -based tasks scaffolded through a mobile
simulator (Nanda et al., 2025). Across these accounts, digital technolog y functions as the tool through which
students design, test, refine, and present a product or solution, rather than as an add-on to an otherwise unchanged
lesson plan.

Contributions of Digital Technology Integration in Teaching Factory –Based Vocational Learning for
Strengthening the Vocational High School Center of Excellence Program

The reviewed literature reports that the integration of digital technology into Teaching Factory -based vocational
education contributes to strengthening the Center of Excellence Vocational High School Program in several ways.
Whereas RQ3 examined the pedag ogical mechanisms of integration at the classroom and workshop level, RQ4
shifts the unit of analysis to the institutional level: it asks what these mechanisms contribute to the SMK PK
program as a policy initiative, including their downstream effects on the school’s external partnerships, aggregate
human-capital outcomes, managerial capacity, revenue -generating capacity, and public identity, rather than
describing the teaching methods themselves. This contribution spans curriculum enhancement, industry
collaboration, teacher competencies, school management, entrepreneurship, and graduate readiness, in addition to
student learning. As established in the Method, each contribution below draws on a fact distinct from its RQ3
counterpart even where the same stud y is cited in both places: RQ3 reports the classroom - or workshop-level
mechanism, RQ4 reports the institutional-level outcome that mechanism is found to produce.

The first contribution operates at the institutional -policy level: the strengthening of link and match with the
industrial world as a formal, school-wide partnership arrangement, distinct from the classroom-level Work-Based
Learning practice discussed under RQ3. Many accounts position curriculum alignment, industrial training (PKL),
and Teaching Factory activity as integral, policy -mandated components of Center of Excellence implementation
itself (Elisabet et al., 2025), most often organized through the 8+i  link-and-match concept at multiple SMK PK
sites (Ahmanda et al., 2022; Sholihah, 2022; Astuti et al., 2025; Tessa & Humaedi, 2023; Fahmayani, 2021), with
partnership-management responsibility specifically examined at the school -administration level (Ningt ias et al.,
2025; Tanjung et al., 2025). From this group of articles, digital technology can be understood as a means to
strengthen this standing institutional connection between schools and industry, especially as curriculum, practical
training, and Teaching Factory (TeFa) products must keep adapting to workforce change.

The second contribution is the enhancement of students’ technical and non-technical competencies as an aggregate
outcome, rather than a single lesson's objective. This outcome is reported for a mobile QR -EFI simulator
associated with stronger 4C-skill results (Nanda et al., 2025), a production-based model associated with soft-skill
gains (Setiadi et al., 2025), a documented relationship between Teaching Factory participation, fieldwork practice,
and work readiness (Endrastiti & Sholikhah, 2024), and graduat es reported as more work -ready and
entrepreneurial after Teaching Factory participation (Sugianto & Casmudi, 2022). Read together, these accounts
treat competency development as a claimed downstream result of the classroom -level mechanisms described in
RQ3, most consistently where technology is used in simulation, production, projects, or product marketing.

reported to support the strengthening of the Center of Excellence Vocat ional High School (SMK PK) as a model
for vocational schools that are more responsive to industrial needs and technological advancements.

Altogether, RQ4 is answered by the finding that the integration of digital technology into Teaching Factory-based

may serve as an important strategy in the transformation of the Center of Excellence Vocational High School.

Challenges, Gaps, and Future Directions in Integrating Digital Technology into Teaching Factory–Based
Vocational Learning

Analysis results indicate that the integration of digital technology into Teaching Factory-based vocational learning
still faces several challenges. These challenges arise at the conceptual, pedagogical, institutional, technological,
industry collaboration, and outcome evaluation levels. Additionally, there is a research gap because few articles
have fully integrated Teaching Factory, digital technology, and Center of Excellence Vocational High Schools
into a comprehensive model. For clarity, the seven patterns discussed below are organized into two families rather
than treated as a single undifferentiated list: three concern evidence gaps, that is, aspects the reviewed literature
itself has not adequately investigated (the first, fifth, and seventh patterns , concerning uneven digital-integration
coverage across studies, limited outcome evaluation, and terminological fragmentation, respectively); the
remaining four concern challenges reported within the field itself, that is, implementation barriers described  by
the primary studies (the second, third, fourth, and sixth patterns, concerning teacher readiness, infrastructure,
industry collaboration, and product quality control). A separate closing paragraph then turns to directions for
future research.

The first challenge is the uneven integration of digital technology in Teaching Factory studies. From the corpus
of articles, many robust national studies discuss Teaching Factory, TeFa, Centers of Excellence for Vocational
High Schools (SMK), link and mat ch, and industry collaboration, but not all explicitly address the integration of
digital technology. For example, Muharam et al. (2024), Baharsyah and Irfan (2024), Prihantoro et al. (2025), and
Husna et al. (2025) discuss the evaluation and effectiveness of Teaching Factory, but their primary focus remains
on program implementation, graduate quality, CIPP, or goal -oriented evaluation. Artha et al. (2024) likewise
document the implementation of the Merdeka curriculum, Teaching Factory, and industrial inter nships at SMK
Negeri 3 Solok without a specific digital -technology focus. Meanwhile, articles such as Septianingsih (2023),
Muhardono and Meliza (2023), Nanda et al. (2025), Riemann et al. (2020), and Yondri et al. (2020) more explicitly
discuss digital marketing, simulators, VR, LMS, and Industry 4.0. This gap points to the fact that digital integration
in Teaching Factories has not yet become mainstream across all studies.

The second challenge is teacher readiness and digital competence. Several articles indicate that teachers are key
actors in the implementation of Teaching Factory and digital technology. Septianingsih (2023) highlights the need

to enhance teachers’ competence in digital marketing. Setiawan et al. (2025) discuss training to develop teachers’
competence in Teaching Factory-based learning. Vilppola et al. (2022) emphasizes the importance of vocational
teachers’ ICT competencies in work -based training. Asngad et al. (2023) also note that teachers’ adaptation to
technology is one of the challenges in developing the curriculum for Vocational High Schools of Excellence. This
means that digital technology integration may be constrained when teachers lack the pedagogical, technical, and
managerial readiness to use it in authentic learning.

The third challenge is facilities, infrastructure, and practical equipment. Elisabet et al. (2025) indicate that the
implementation of the Teaching Factory still requires updates to practical facilities to optimize learning outcomes.
Prihantoro et al. (2025) found that regarding inputs, facilities and infrastructure remain inadequate due to budget
constraints and a lack of equipment calibration. Widiastuti et al. ( 2023) similarly  found that infrastructure
facilities, alongside teacher quality and industry -partner involvement, significantly influence the achievement of
Center of Excellence status among vocational high schools in Yogyakarta. This issue is critical because a digital-
based Teaching Factory requires equipment that is both available and aligned with industry standards. If practical
facilities are not up-to-date, the Teaching Factory risks becoming merely a standard school simulation rather than
a true representation of the real-world workplace.

The fourth challenge is that industry collaboration is not always substantive. Asiah (2025) highlights that synergy
between vocational high schools (SMK), the government, and industry is not yet optimal due to conflicting
interests, weak coordination, limited capacity, and a lack of evaluation. Some collaborations also risk remaining
mere formalities of a Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) without tangible follow -through. These findings
align with articles on the 8+i link-and-match framework, such as those by Ahmanda et al. (2022), Sholihah (2022),
Astuti et al. (2025), Putra (2023), and Tessa and Humaedi (2023), which position industry collaboration as the
key to strengthening vocational high schools (SMK PK). In the context of digital integration, industry
collaboration needs to go further, specifically toward curriculum co-design, real-world projects, industry mentors,
product standards, and joint competency evaluations.

The fifth challenge is the limited evaluation of learning impacts. Many articles in the corpus employ qualitative,
descriptive, evaluative, mentoring, community service, or case study approaches. While these approaches are
essential for understanding the c ontext, they are insufficient for robustly measuring the impact of digital
technology integration on student learning outcomes. Some articles have moved toward impact testing, for
example, Nanda et al. (2025) with R&D and effectiveness testing of the mobil e QR-EFI simulator, Roll and
Ifenthaler (2021) with a staged competency test, and Endrastiti and Sholikhah (2024) who tested the influence of
Teaching Factory and PKL on work readiness. However, overall, longitudinal studies, mixed methods, and
stronger outcome measurements are still needed.

The sixth challenge is product quality control in the Teaching Factory. Prihantoro et al. (2025) indicate that
product aspects remain a weak point because product quality does not yet fully meet industry standards, the
school’s quality control system is no t yet robust, and project -based assessment is not yet optimal. Cholis et al.
(2025) also note that evaluation documentation needs to be strengthened in the development of the Teaching
Factory guidebook. In the context of digital TeFa, quality control shoul d be strengthened through digital
documentation systems, industry-standard-based rubrics, production process tracking, and product validation by
industry partners.

The seventh challenge is the fragmentation of terminology and conceptual models. In the corpus of articles, the
terms used are highly diverse: Teaching Factory, TeFa, Learning Factory, Work -Based Learning, Production -
Based Learning, Project -Based Learning, Industry-Based Learning, and Learning Factory 4.0. This diversity of
terms reflects the richness of literature but also poses challenges in establishing a consistent conceptual
framework. Riemann et al. (2020) and Roll and Ifenthaler (2021) predominantly use the term “learning factory”
in an international context, whereas national articles frequently use “Teaching  Factory” or “TeFa.” Mazlan et al.
(2025a, 2025b, 2025c) extensively discuss Work-Based Learning and Industry 4.0 talent within TVET. Therefore,
future research needs to develop a framework capable of bridging these terms within the context of the Vocational
High School Center of Excellence.

Research direction. Based on these challenges and evidence gaps, the direction for future development is to build
a Digital Teaching Factory model for Vocational High School Centers of Excellence. This model must integrate
several elements: industry-based curriculum, authentic learning, digital technology, simulation, digital marketing,
production management systems, DUDIKA collaboration, teacher competencies, quality assurance, and student
achievement evaluation. With such a model, the Teaching Factory moves beyond a production activity within the

Publication trends and thematic focuses of studies on Teaching Factory –based vocational learning, digital
technology integration, and Vocational High School Center of Excellence from 2017 to 2026

The sharp rise in publications after 2022 is better explained by policy timing than by a sudden rise in scholarly
interest in Teaching Factory or digital technology as topics, since both had already appeared earlier in the review
period (Swaramarinda & Nurmalasari, 2017; Yoto et al., 2019). A more likely explanation is policy diffusion. The
SMK Pusat Keunggulan program was launched by Indonesia's Ministry of Education, Culture, Research, and
Technology in 2021, and research on a new national policy typically appears one to three years after schools begin
implementing it, once there is something concrete for researchers to document and evaluate. This review did not
test publication lag or policy timing directly, so this explanation should be read as a plausible interpretation rather
than a proven cause.

This pattern also shows up in the composition of the corpus. Indonesian -authored articles dominate, while the
smaller international group (Riemann et al., 2020; Vilppola et al., 2022; Mazlan et al., 2025a, 2025b, 2025c) tends
to focus on transferable compe tency frameworks such as Industry 4.0 talent models and Learning Factory 4.0
pedagogy. Few articles draw on both the Indonesian policy literature and the international competency-framework
literature at the same time. This points to two research communitie s working close to the same topic without yet
reading each other closely, a gap this review tries to help close.

A similar pattern appears internationally. A review of technology adaptability in vocational education and training
reports that research in this area often focuses narrowly on technical skills, with less attention to how well schools
and workplaces connect, and that funding differences between public and private VET providers shape what gets
studied (Imama et al., 2025). Together with the present findings, this suggests that publication activity in this field
tracks funding and policy cycles at least as closely as it tracks technological change itself. In practice, Indonesian
SMK PK research would benefit from engaging more directly with this international competency -framework
literature instead of developing separately from it.

This shift also shows up as a change in what the research is about, not only in how much of it there is. The earliest
articles frame technology and vocational learning in fairly general terms, for instance an ICT -based creative
economy learning model for vocational high schools published in 2017 (Swaramarinda & Nurmalasari, 2017). By
2023 and 2024, articles narrow onto the SMK Pusat Keunggulan policy itself: curriculum development within the
program, the 8+i link -and-match initiative, DUDIKA partnership str engthening, and principal leadership in
building Teaching Factories (Asngad et al., 2023; Akyuwen et al., 2023; Muharam et al., 2024; Samengasbumi et
al., 2024; Sulistyawati & Mulyono, 2024). The growth in publications, in other words, tracks a narrowing of focus
toward one specific policy instrument, not simply a general rise in interest in vocational technology.

This has a direct implication for when useful evaluation evidence can be expected. If research on a new policy
typically appears one to three years after implementation begins, and the SMK Pusat Keunggulan program
expanded in cohorts from 2021 onward, then  evidence on how later cohorts of schools use digital technology is
only beginning to appear now and will keep accumulating over the next few years. Researchers and funders
planning evaluation studies should build this lag into their timelines rather than expect mature, generalizable
evidence from schools that have only recently joined the program.

Digital Technology and Digitalization Domains İntegrated into Teaching Factory–Based Vocational Learning

The six technology domains identified under RQ2 do not point to one dominant tool, and this is worth explaining
rather than treating as a gap. Activity theory (Engestrom, 2001) describes a learning activity as a system made up
of several connected parts: t he people involved, the tools they use, the goal they are working toward, the wider
community, the rules that guide practice, and how tasks are divided among participants. The Teaching Factory
literature fits this structure well. ICT and simulators support the relationship between students, teachers, and their
tools inside the classroom. Digital marketing platforms support the relationship between students and their
customers. Learning management systems and ISO-based digital systems (Al Hasan & Hermawan, 2025) support

the rules and division of labor within the school as an organization. Each technology serves a different part of the
same system rather than competing for the same role.

This also explains a pattern that could otherwise look inconsistent. National studies concentrate on classroom -
and management-level tools such as ICT, digital marketing, and LMS platforms, while the smaller international
group of studies (Riemann et al., 2020; Roll & Ifenthaler, 2021) focuses more on simulation and competency -
framework technologies. Rather than describing two unrelated phenomena, this reflects two groups of researchers
looking at different parts of the same activity system.

This pattern is broadly consistent with a recent review of digital -skills integration in TVET under Industry 4.0,
which also finds that effective integration works through several parallel strategies, including curriculum redesign,
new learning technologie s, and new pedagogical models, rather than one dominant type of intervention (Kurnia
et al., 2026). What stands out in the present corpus is the prominence of digital marketing, digital business, and
product commercialization as a technology domain in its own right (Septianingsih, 2023; Muhardono & Meliza,
2023; Sholihatinnisa et al., 2021), a domain that receives far less attention in the broader Industry 4.0 training
literature, where virtual reality, simulation, and augmented reality dominate (Anholon et al., 2022). This difference
likely reflects the Teaching Factory model itself: because TeFa learning is built around producing and selling real
goods or services, not only practicing industrial tasks, digital technology naturally extends into commercialization
in a way that general Industry 4.0 training does not need to.

The fifth domain, learning management systems and school digital platforms, also shows a shift over time rather
than forming one static category. Earlier accounts describe a learning management system built into a Teaching
Factory model without reference to any external standard (Yondri et al., 2020). A more recent account documents
a rental management system built specifically to the ISO 25010 software -quality standard (Al Hasan &
Hermawan, 2025), and the most recent accounts describe digital platforms use d to guide and track a school's
progress toward Center of Excellence status itself (Sumantri & Akmal, 2023; Sumantri et al., 2024). Read in
sequence, these accounts suggest that administrative and institutional technology in the Teaching Factory is
maturing from ad hoc digital tools toward systems referenced against external quality standards and tied directly
to accreditation and policy status, a maturation consistent with the publication -timing pattern discussed under
RQ1.

In practice, this points to a specific planning step for schools and program managers. Before adding new
technology, a school should map which node of the activity system its current tools already serve, classroom
instruction, customer-facing marketing, or  institutional administration, and treat gaps in coverage as deliberate
investment priorities rather than leave them to chance. The sixth domain, technology supporting innovative work
and product commercialization, is the clearest such gap in the present c orpus: it is documented in only one study
(Sunyoto et al., 2024), against five to six sources for every other domain, which makes it a reasonable next target
for schools and researchers alike rather than a category to treat as already adequately covered.

Pedagogical İntegration of Digital Technology into Teaching Factory, Learning Factory, Production-Based
Learning, Work-Based Learning, or İndustry-Based Vocational Learning

The strong presence of authentic, production-based approaches among RQ3's six pedagogical patterns fits situated
learning theory (Lave & Wenger, 1991), which holds that learning works best through real participation in a
community of practice rather than through instruction detached from practice. Seen this way, the Teaching Factory
functions as a community of practice in which students move from simple, peripheral tasks toward fuller
involvement in production, marketing, and quality control. Digital techno logy supports this process where real
industrial conditions cannot be reproduced at school, for example through mobile simulators that stand in for
equipment students would otherwise not be able to access (Nanda et al., 2025).

This also helps explain why teacher training, the fourth pedagogical pattern, matters as much as it does. The
TPACK framework (Mishra & Koehler, 2006) holds that effective technology integration needs teachers to
combine content knowledge, pedagogical know ledge, and technological knowledge together, not develop them
separately. The corpus suggests that many schools have so far strengthened only one or two of these, usually
technological infrastructure or subject expertise, without the pedagogical knowledge needed to connect them. This
gap also shows up in the type of evidence available: all five sources supporting this pattern (Septianingsih, 2023;
Muhardono & Meliza, 2023; Setiawan et al., 2025; Wahyudi et al., 2025; Ulfa & Yudianto, 2025) are community-
service or mentoring reports rather than empirical evaluations. Teacher training activity is well documented, but
its effect on classroom -level technology use has not yet been measured by any study in this corpus. A similar

pattern appears in the wider vocational teacher -education literature, where digital competence alone is treated as
necessary but not sufficient for effective work -based teaching unless it is paired with pedagogical redesign
(Vilppola et al., 2022).

The same structural gap appears outside the Indonesian context. A recent review of technology adaptability across
VET settings identifies instructor digital competence as a limiting factor, since vocational teachers need more than
general digital literacy: they need competencies specific to connecting school and workplace, subject matter, and
adaptive pedagogy (Imama et al., 2025). This closely matches the pedagogical-knowledge gap identified through
TPACK above, even though the two reviews draw on largely different sets of studies. That two independent
reviews reach a similar conclusion supports treating this as a real feature of vocational -technology integration
rather than a result specific to this review's sample.

The six pedagogical patterns identified under RQ3 also differ in how far they place students inside a real, rather
than school-simulated, work setting, a distinction situated learning theory treats as meaningful. The first pattern
keeps production tasks inside the school workshop, sequencing fieldwork practice alongside coursework (Elisabet
et al., 2025) or structuring workshop stages around a simulated industrial production cycle (Setiadi et al., 2025).
The third pattern moves further, placing students or teachers inside actual Work -Based Learning arrangements
with industry, for example a Building Information Modeling design program built around industry -embedded
tasks (Ramanda et al., 2025) or ICT tasks embedded directly into work -based teacher -training pl acements
themselves (Vilppola et al., 2022). Situated learning theory would predict that the industry -embedded form
supports fuller participation than the workshop-based form, but no study in this corpus directly compares the two,
which leaves this as a plausible extension of the theory rather than a tested finding.

In practice, this points to a specific and fairly low -cost step for teacher development: pairing teachers who are
technically strong with teachers who are pedagogically strong, so that classroom -level knowledge of how to
combine content, pedagogy, and technology develops through mentoring, rather than through further stand-alone
digital-marketing or ICT workshops (Septianingsih, 2023; Muhardono & Meliza, 2023) of the kind this corpus
already shows accumulating as documented training activity without a corre sponding measure of classroom
impact.

Contributions of Digital Technology İntegration in Teaching Factory –Based Vocational Learning for
Strengthening the Vocational High School Center of Excellence Program

The six contributions identified under RQ4 can be read together through the idea of boundary crossing (Akkerman
& Bakker, 2011), which describes the work required when people or institutions move between different practice
communities, in this case the sch ool and the industry. Seen this way, digital technology in the Teaching Factory
works less as a general productivity tool and more as an object that carries meaning on both sides of the school -
industry relationship, even when each side uses and interprets it somewhat differently. Digital products made for
real customers (Sholihatinnisa et al., 2021; Muhardono & Meliza, 2023), industry -referenced quality standards
built into digital documentation (Prihantoro et al., 2025), and shared curriculum materials lin ked to DUDIKA
partnerships (Elisabet et al., 2025; Ahmanda et al., 2022) all work this way.

This also explains a pattern in the evidence that would otherwise look uneven. As shown in the Results, the link -
and-match and student -competency contributions rely mostly on empirical implementation studies and
evaluations, while the entrepreneurship and SMK PK-identity contributions rely more on community-service and
mentoring reports. Sharing a digital tool between school and industry does not by itself guarantee real
collaboration; it depends on ongoing negotiation about how that tool is used and valued , a process most articles
in this corpus describe only briefly rather than in operational detail. The contributions with the strongest evidence,
link-and-match and competency gains, are also the ones where school -industry collaboration is most formally
established, through MoUs and curriculum -alignment committees. The contributions with weaker evidence,
entrepreneurship and identity-building, are the ones where this collaboration is still informal, which also makes it
harder for descriptive studies to capture in detail.

This pattern is not unique to this corpus. A systematic review of Industry 4.0 training reports similarly limited
attention to business-model impacts and managerial outcomes compared with technical -skill outcomes (Anholon
et al., 2022). This suggests that vocational-technology research in general finds it easier to measure a change in a
student's skill set than a change in an institution's standing or a partnership's durability.

Challenges, Gaps, and Future Directions in İntegrating Digital Technology into Teaching Factory –Based
Vocational Learning

The seven challenges identified under RQ5 are easier to understand as signs of two underlying problems than as
seven separate issues needing seven separate fixes. Uneven digital -integration coverage (the first challenge) is
visible in studies that discuss Teaching Factory implementation and evaluation in detail without a specific digital-
technology focus (Muharam et al., 2024; Baharsyah & Irfan, 2024; Artha et al., 2024), alongside inconsistent
terminology (the seventh challenge, Riemann et al., 2020; Roll & Ifenthaler, 2021; Mazlan et al., 2025a, 2025b,
2025c); together these point to the field still lacking a shared framework for describing digital Teaching Factory
integration across studies. Limited teacher readiness (the second challenge, Asngad et al., 2023) and shallow
industry collaboration (the fourth challenge, Asiah, 2025) both point to collaboration that stops at exchanging
documents or signing agreements without moving into real joint work, which matches Akkerman and Bakker's
(2011) own observation that boundary crossing is often attempted but rarely sustained without someone actively
brokering it on both sides. Inadequate facilities (the third challenge, Prihantoro et al., 2025; Widiastuti et al., 2023)
and weak product-quality control (the sixth challenge, Prihantoro et al., 2025; Cholis et al., 2025) suggest that, in
many schools, the technology and content sides of TPACK are still developed separately from the pedagogical
side needed to connect them to student learning.

Grouped this way, the seven challenges reduce to two underlying gaps rather than seven unrelated barriers. The
first is a framework gap: challenges one and seven both reflect the absence of a shared way of describing what
counts as digital Teaching Factory integration. The second is a depth-of-practice gap: challenges two, three, four,
and six show that technology and partnerships exist on paper but are not yet built into daily practice. The fifth
challenge, limited outcome evaluation, follows from both gap s rather than forming a third category on its own.
Without a shared framework, it is unclear what should be measured, and without depth of practice, there is often
not yet enough sustained activity to measure.

The same barrier pattern appears outside the Indonesian SMK PK context. A recent review of VET technology
adaptability identifies almost the same group of barriers, institutional readiness, instructor competence, curriculum
integration, and industry-education partnership, as the factors that together determine whether digital use actually
becomes effective (Imama et al., 2025). A recent review of digital skills in TVET identifies four comparable
success factors: teacher digital competence, learner readiness , institutional infrastructure, and policy support
(Kurnia et al., 2026). These reviews draw on largely different sets of studies yet arrive at similar barrier categories,
which supports treating these barriers as a real, general feature of vocational -technology integration rather than
something specific to the SMK PK program or to this corpus. Read together with the pattern above, this suggests
that the future direction identified in the Results should not be treated as a list of components to implement si de
by side, but as an attempt to close the framework gap and the depth -of-practice gap at the same time. A model is
only useful once schools share a common vocabulary for it and put it into sustained practice, and defining the
model's components alone does not guarantee either.

stating directly. First, technology availability does not match pedagogical integration: RQ2 shows wide
technology diversity, but RQ3's evidence shows integration is strongest for authentic task design and weakest for

present review is not a new list of technologies, since the domains identified here overlap substantially with what
these broader reviews already report. The contribution is bringing together three areas that are each studied on
their own but rarely combined: Teaching Factory pedagogy, digital technology integration, and a specific national
policy, SMK PK, read through one consistent set of interpretive lenses, activity theory, situated learning, TPACK,
and boundary crossing, instead of rep orted as a separate technology list. This combination shows a pattern that a
single-topic review would not: the barriers to digital Teaching Factory integration come down to a shared -
framework gap and a depth -of-practice gap rather than a checklist of sepa rate problems, and the institutional
contributions most often claimed for Teaching Factory digitalization are, on closer inspection, also the ones with
the least rigorous evidence behind them.

Beyond the RQ -specific priorities noted above, the corpus points to one shared next step for research design.
Across all five research questions, the studies most often cited as strong evidence use implementation, evaluation,
or mixed-method designs, for example Prihantoro et al. (2025), Nanda et al. (2025), and Endrastiti and Sholikhah
(2024), while the studies supporting the weaker contributions rely on single-site description or mentoring reports.
The next priority for the field is therefore not more description of which technologies schools use, but evaluation
designs, whether mixed-methods, quasi-experimental, or Design-Based Research, applied consistently across all
six technology domains and pedagogical patterns rather than only the ones already well studied, so that closing
the framework gap or the depth-of-practice gap can actually be measured rather than assumed.

This review has several limitations to consider when interpreting the discussion above. First, the corpus is
dominated by Indonesian, mostly descriptive and qualitative studies, which limits how far the interpretive patterns
discussed here, particularly th e theoretical readings drawn from activity theory, situated learning, TPACK, and
boundary crossing, can be generalized beyond the Indonesian SMK PK context without further testing. Second,
the search was limited to two databases, Scopus and Google Scholar through Publish or Perish, rather than
databases more specific to educational technology or engineering education such as ERIC or IEEE Xplore.
Articles indexed only in other databases, published only in institutional repositories, or written in languages other
than English and Indonesian may not have been captured, which could understate the true international scope of
this literature. Third, as a narrative and thematic synthesis rather than a meta-analysis, this review reports patterns
in the findings but does not statistically test the strength of relationships, for example between specific technology
types and specific competency outcomes; such quantitative synthesis was not possible given how much the
designs, outcome measures, and reporting practices va ry across the 67 included articles. Fourth, the corpus leans
toward nationally accredited (SINTA) and community -service sources rather than internationally peer -reviewed
journals: only 13 of the 67 included articles (19 percent) are Scopus -indexed, and five are conference or seminar
proceedings rather than peer -reviewed journal articles. This should be kept in mind when weighing how far the
synthesized findings generalize internationally. Fifth, article screening, eligibility assessment, and quality
appraisal were carried out by a single reviewer without independent second -reviewer verification or a registered
review protocol, as noted in the Method, which may have increased the risk of selection and coding bias; this risk
applies to the interpretive readings offered in this Discussion as much as to the original coding. These limitations
do not undermine the review's main contribution, which is to map and interpret an emerging and still-fragmented
body of literature, but they do mean the theoretical interpretations offered here should be treated as a starting point
for future empirical testing rather than as an established explanation.

Conclusion

Based on the results of a systematic literature review of 67 final articles, it can be concluded that research on the
integration of digital technology into Teaching Factory -based vocational education to strengthen the Vocational
High School Center of Exce llence Program shows a growing trend, particularly during the 2023 –2025 period.
This topic is frequently discussed in the Indonesian context due to its close association with the Vocational High
School Center of Excellence Program, although several interna tional articles also contribute to the perspective
through studies on Learning Factory 4.0, Work -Based Learning, ICT competence, virtual reality, TVET, and
Industry 4.0. The digital technology and digitalization domains integrated into Teaching Factory-based vocational
education are quite diverse, encompassing ICT, digital marketing, digital products, digital simulations, virtual
reality, augmented reality, mobile simulato rs, learning management systems, digital platforms, and Industry 4.0
orientation. These technologies serve as learning tools while also supporting production processes, marketing,
documentation, evaluation, management, entrepreneurship, and industry collab oration. Pedagogically, digital
technology is integrated through authentic production-based learning, project- and problem-based learning, work-
based learning, teacher training , Teaching Factory evaluation, and digital entrepreneurship. This integration is
reported to be associated with stronger link and match, student competency development, teachers’ pedagogical
readiness, Teaching Factory management, the commercialization of school products, and the identity of the Center
of Excellence Vocational High School as a reference school that is adaptive to industry needs.

However, this study also identified several challenges, namely the uneven integration of digital technology,
infrastructure limitations, teacher readiness, industry collaboration that is not always substantive, weak quality
control, and limited evaluation of learning outcomes. It is consequently necessary to develop a Digital Teaching
Factory model for Vocational High School Centers of Excellence that can systematically integrate industry-based
curriculum, digital technology, authentic learning, production management, DUDIKA collaboration, quality
assurance, and evaluation of student achievements.

Toward an integrated framework. Synthesizing the findings above, the review’s results can be organized into a
preliminary conceptual framework for a Digital Teaching Factory comprising seven interlocking components,
rather than treating digital technology as a single add -on: (1) curriculum: industry -based content and standards
aligned through link-and-match partnerships (RQ4); (2) pedagogy: authentic production -based learning, project-
and problem -based learning, and Work -Based Learning as the classroom - and workshop -level mechanisms
through which technology is used (RQ3); (3) technology: the digital technology and digitalization domains
identified in RQ2 (ICT, digital marketing and business, simulation/VR/AR, Industry 4.0/Learning Factory 4.0
orientation, and learning-management/digital-platform systems), mapped onto the pedagogical and institutional
functions they serve; (4) teacher capacity: sustained, TPACK -aligned training and mentoring rather than one -off
workshops (RQ3, RQ5); (5) industry: school–industry boundary-crossing arrangements that extend beyond MoUs
to joint curriculum design, mentoring, and product validation (RQ4, RQ5); (6) production and management:
systematic Teaching Factory management, documentation, and quality -control systems (RQ3, RQ4 ); and (7)
quality assurance and evaluation: outcome evaluation extending beyond descriptive or case-study designs toward
measures capable of testing the framework’s own components (RQ5). This framework is offered as a preliminary
synthesis derived from th e reviewed literature rather than as an empirically validated model; testing its
components and their interrelationships is identified above as a priority for future research.

Recommendations

Vocational High School Centers of Excellence need to develop Teaching Factories as integrated digital learning
ecosystems aligned with the curriculum, industry standards, marketing, evaluation, and school management,
rather than as practice or production u nits alone. Schools must also develop a technology needs map tailored to
vocational competencies, such as simulators, learning platforms, production documentation systems, digital
marketing tools, and project-based evaluation systems.

Productive teachers and Teaching Factory managers need to receive ongoing training in digital pedagogy,
production-based learning, project-based learning, the use of simulators, digital marketing, authentic assessment,
and production management. This training is essential so that teachers can use technology and connect it to real -
world learning experiences relevant to the industry.

Policy makers and DUDIKA partners need to strengthen support for the digitalization of Teaching Factories
through the provision of facilities, implementation guidance, teacher training, infrastructure upgrades, and more
substantive industry collaboration. Collaboration with industry should not stop at a Memorandum of
