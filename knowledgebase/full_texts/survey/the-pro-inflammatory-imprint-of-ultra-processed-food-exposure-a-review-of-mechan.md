The prevalence of ultra-processed food (UPF) consumption in pediatric
diets has raised signiﬁcant concerns regarding long-term health. This review
examines evidence for a proposed ‘pro-inﬂammatory imprint’ involving the
gut microbiome–inﬂammation axis. Using a structured narrative synthesis
of pediatric observational evidence, adult intervention studies, preclinical
research, multi-omics data, and public health evaluations, we examine
associations between UPF exposure and metabolic, inﬂammatory, cognitive,
and neurodevelopmental outcomes while distinguishing direct from indirect
evidence. We propose the Pediatric-Speciﬁc Integrated Dietary Inﬂammatory
Index (cP-IDII) as an unvalidated, hypothesis-generating research framework
for integrating reproducible nutrient- and processing-related dietary exposures;
downstream microbial, intestinal-barrier, inﬂammatory, metabolic, and immune
measuresarereservedforexternalvalidation.Furthermore,wediscusstheroleof
commercialdeterminantsandenvironmentalfactorsinshapingchildhealth.The
synthesis identiﬁes measurement gaps and complementary policy entry points
across product composition, food environments, affordability, and marketing.
Empirical development and external validation are required before the cP-IDII
can be used for clinical, epidemiological, or public health decision-making.

Over the past half-century, the prevalence of overweight and obesity among children
and adolescents (aged 5–19 years) has exhibited a substantial rise, currently aﬀecting
hundreds of millions of individuals globally (1). Pediatric overweight and obesity are
multifactorial outcomes shaped by interactions among energy intake, dietary quality,

consumed, high in free sugars, sodium, or unhealthy fats, and
low in dietary ﬁber ( 2). This is further compounded by the
complex interplay between overall environmental factors (e.g.,
air pollution, endocrine disruptors, and sedentary behaviors) and

development,infancy,andadolescence,serveaspivotalperiodsfor
metabolicprogramming,gutmicrobiomecolonization,andcentral
nervous system maturation. During these periods, UPF exposure
may alter gene expression and immune function, shaping long-
term health trajectories, particularly concerning metabolic and
neurodevelopmentalprocesses( 5,6).
Potential age-speciﬁc susceptibility should not be treated as
a uniform property of all children. Infancy includes rapid gut
microbiome assembly and maturation of intestinal and immune
functions; early childhood includes continued adipose-tissue
development and metabolic programming; and childhood and
adolescence involve ongoing synaptic remodeling, myelination,
and epigenomic regulation. These processes provide biological
plausibility for age-dependent eﬀects, but direct pediatric evidence
linking speciﬁc UPF components to these pathways remains
limited. Emerging epigenome-wide association studies (EWAS)
report associations between childhood UPF consumption and
DNA methylation diﬀerences (e.g., at loci associated with thyroid
hormone and liver function), suggesting that UPF exposure
could leave a lasting “imprint” at the transcriptomic level (7).
Chronic, low-grade systemic inﬂammation has been proposed
as a potential mechanistic link between high UPF intake and
adverse developmental outcomes. This hypothesis is supported
by observed associations with early cardiometabolic risk markers
(such as adiposity and elevated blood pressure) ( 8–10) and
may be relevant to subsequent health trajectories across the
lifespan. The Dietary Inﬂammatory Index (DII) and its pediatric-
speciﬁc adaptation (C-DII) (11), alongside the Empirical Dietary
Inﬂammatory Pattern (EDIP), are widely utilized to quantify
the overall pro-inﬂammatory potential of diets ( 12). Recent
longitudinal analyses of US adolescents report that moderate-
to-high pro-inﬂammatory dietary scores are associated with a
higher likelihood of prediabetes ( 13). Although recent meta-
analyses report associations between higher DII scores and
depression, anxiety, and other neuropsychiatric outcomes ( 14,
15), research on pediatric populations—especially integrating gut
microbiome succession and neurodevelopmental trajectories—
remainsfragmented,withmostexistingUPFliteraturefocusingon
adultcardiovasculardisease.
The DII estimates the inﬂammatory potential of dietary
components, the C-DII adapts this approach for pediatric dietary
assessment, and the EDIP is an empirically derived dietary-
pattern score. NOVA characterizes the extent and purpose of
food processing, whereas the DI-GM emphasizes dietary features
associated with microbiome support. These approaches cover
complementary dimensions but do not provide a validated
pediatric measure that integrates reproducible nutrient- and
processing-related exposures while keeping downstream biological
responses separate from the exposure score. The cP-IDII is
therefore introduced as an unvalidated, hypothesis-generating
framework and research agenda rather than as a tool with
demonstratedpredictivecapacity.
Thisstructurednarrativereviewuseslife-courseandexposome
perspectives to organize evidence by exposure, outcome, candidate
pathway,andmeasurementgap.Ameet-in-the-middleperspective
is used to distinguish external dietary exposures from candidate
intermediate molecular changes, including altered branched-
chain amino acid metabolism ( 16). Furthermore, this review
evaluates whether these pathways are biologically plausible
without presenting microbiome changes, barrier dysfunction,
inﬂammation, or BCAA metabolism as an established continuous
causal chain in children. In parallel, by contextualizing the global
public health landscape, this review discusses how commercial
marketing and underlying demographic determinants (such as
education level and socioeconomic status) (17, 18) may shape
exposure within a socio-ecological system, thereby identifying
potentialandcomplementarypolicyentrypoints.
2 Review approach
We conducted a structured narrative search of PubMed and
Embase from database inception to May 2026. Medical Subject
Headings and free-text terms related to ultra-processed foods,
NOVA classiﬁcation, dietary inﬂammation, pediatric populations,
gut microbiome and intestinal barrier function, metabolic
and neurodevelopmental outcomes, commercial determinants
of health, food environments, and policy interventions were
combined using Boolean operators. A representative search
strategy was: (“ultra-processed food” OR “ultraprocessed food”
OR UPF OR NOVA OR “dietary inﬂammatory index”) AND
(infant OR child OR adolescent OR pediatric OR pregnancy)
AND (inﬂammation OR microbiome OR “intestinal permeability”
OR obesity OR metabolic OR neurodevelopment OR cognition
OR mental health OR marketing OR “food environment” OR
policy). Reference lists of key reviews and eligible studies were
also screened, and targeted searches were conducted for speciﬁc
additives,biomarkers,dietaryindices,andpolicyinterventions.
Peer-reviewed human studies involving participants aged
0–19 years were prioritized when they assessed UPF exposure,
dietary inﬂammatory potential, relevant health outcomes, or
candidate intermediate pathways. Prenatal exposures were
considered separately from children’s own dietary intake.
Studies without relevant dietary exposures or outcomes and
non-peer-reviewed reports were excluded from the evidence
synthesis, although market audits were retained when used only
to describe product composition or marketing. Adult intervention
studies and preclinical studies were included solely to assess
mechanisticplausibility.
Evidence was prioritized by directness to the pediatric
population and by study design. Priority was given to pediatric
systematic reviews and meta-analyses, longitudinal cohorts,
randomized or quasi-experimental studies, and well-characterized
pediatricobservationalcohorts.Adultandpreclinicalevidencewas
considered indirect and was used primarily when direct pediatric

3 Ultra-processed foods: deﬁnition,
characteristics of pediatric exposure,
and environmental drivers
3.1 Pediatric-speciﬁc challenges based on
the NOVA classiﬁcation
The NOVA classiﬁcation is an internationally recognized
framework that categorizes foods according to the extent and
purpose of industrial processing. It deﬁnes UPFs as formulations
of ingredients created through a series of industrial techniques,
typically containing cosmetic food additives (e.g., colorants,
emulsiﬁers, sweeteners) and industrial ingredients rarely used
in domestic kitchens (e.g., modiﬁed starches, hydrogenated
oils) (19). Although NOVA is widely used in epidemiology, it
describes processing-related characteristics and does not itself
demonstrate pediatric-speciﬁc biological vulnerability or causal
eﬀects. Market audits of commercial complementary foods in
France, Portugal, and Australia indicate that between 61.8 and
87.9% of packaged products targeting infants and toddlers
under 36 months are classiﬁed as UPFs. Many contain free
sugars or concentrated fruit juices while also carrying claims
such as “no added sugar” (20–22). These market data describe
the composition and promotion of products available during
a period when food preferences develop, but they do not
establish developmental harm. Prospective studies are needed to
determine whether early exposure inﬂuences later preferences or
metabolicoutcomes.
3.2 Nutritional transition and the
globalization of the double burden of
malnutrition
UPF consumption is accelerating globally. In the US and the
UK, UPFs account for over 67 and 60% of total daily energy
intake among children and adolescents, respectively ( 21, 23).
EliminatingUPFsfromUKpediatricdietscouldpotentiallyreduce
the prevalence of excess free sugar consumption by 47% (21).
UPF sales and intake are also increasing in many low- and
middle-incomecountriesundergoingnutritiontransition.InAfrica
and Latin America, urbanization and large-scale investments by
transnational food corporations have coincided with increased
UPF and sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) sales ( 24, 25). An
ecologicaltime-seriesanalysisacrossnineAfricannationsreported
an ecological association between increasing SSB sales and adult
type 2 diabetes burden (25). The multicountry European I. Family
study similarly reported that UPFs contributed 50%−70% of daily
total fat, saturated fat, and sugar intake among youth and that
higher UPF intake was associated with lower overall diet quality
(measuredbyHDASscores)( 26).
This nutritional shift is equally critical in Asia. In China, over
60% of pre-packaged foods are UPFs, routinely exceeding free
sugar and sodium limits across various nutrient proﬁling models
(NPMs) (27). In India, pediatric associations have introduced the
“JUNCS” framework (comprising junk foods, UPFs, nutritionally
inappropriate foods, caﬀeinated drinks, and SSBs) to address
the childhood obesity epidemic ( 28). In Mexico and Brazil,
escalating UPF intake has been examined alongside obesity
trends and the “Double Burden of Malnutrition” (DBM)—the
coexistence of undernutrition and overnutrition within the same
population or household (29, 30). Particularly among families
with low socioeconomic status (SES), structural barriers and
health inequities position UPFs as an accessible, cheap caloric
source, and may contribute to persistent nutritional inequities
(29,31).
3.3 Commercial, household, and
socio-ecological determinants of pediatric
UPF exposure
Pediatric UPF consumption is shaped by a socio-ecological
systeminwhichcaregivers’purchasingandfeedingdecisionsreﬂect
household resources, time, food prices and availability, cultural
practices, marketing, institutional settings, and public policy (32).
Commercialdeterminants,includingproductformulation,pricing,
placement, and child-directed marketing, constitute one set of
inﬂuences within this multilevel system rather than its sole
cause(32).
Packaging and Cartoon Marketing: Market surveys in Latin
America and Europe reveal that a substantial proportion of UPF
packaging utilizes cartoons, brand mascots, and toy bundling
to captivate pediatric demographics (21, 33, 34). An 11-country
review in Latin America found that over 60% of food marketing
aimed at youth promoted unhealthy UPFs, heavily relying on
licensed characters (35). Furthermore, the industry frequently
utilizes health-oriented claims and imagery—such as “free from”
claims or realistic imagery of whole fruits and grains—which can
inﬂuence parents’ perceptions and purchase intentions for these

environments

4 Assessment tools for dietary
inﬂammatory potential and the
neurodevelopmental perspective
4.1 DII and C-DII: quantifying overall
inﬂammatory burden
The DII estimates the pro-inﬂammatory potential of dietary
intake from literature-derived weights for nutritional components
associated with pro-inﬂammatory (e.g., CRP, IL-6, TNF-α) and
anti-inﬂammatory cytokines (12). The C-DII adapts this dietary
scoring approach for children; neither the DII nor C-DII directly
measures systemic inﬂammation. Adolescent observational studies
report associations between higher dietary inﬂammatory scores
and biomarkers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), supporting
evaluation of these biomarkers as external validation outcomes
rather than as components of the dietary score (58). Studies report
that dietary patterns high in UPFs or reﬁned carbohydrates and
lowindietaryﬁberareoftenassociatedwithhigherDIIscores(i.e.,
highlypro-inﬂammatorydiets)( 59).
4.2 Impairment of neuropsychiatric and
cognitive development
Maternal and childhood inﬂammatory exposures have
been examined in relation to neurodevelopment, but evidence
diﬀers by developmental window and study design. Recent
large-scale epidemiological investigations report an association
between DII and pediatric neuropsychiatric disorders (NPDs)
(14). Observational evidence associates higher UPF intake with
metabolic outcomes, poorer psychological wellbeing, and mood
symptomsinadolescents,includinganalysesadjustedforsedentary
behavior (60). Furthermore, a higher dietary inﬂammatory score
has been associated with lower academic performance in children;
this ﬁnding does not establish that diet-induced inﬂammation
causedtheobservedcognitivediﬀerences( 61).
Interpretation of neurodevelopmental associations requires
attention to reverse causality. Sensory sensitivities, restricted food
repertoires, selective eating, and preferences for predictable or
highly palatable foods may increase reliance on UPFs among
some children with ADHD or autism spectrum disorder. Residual
socioeconomic and behavioral confounding may also inﬂuence
both diet and measured outcomes; therefore, causal direction
remainsunresolved.
4.2.1 Transgenerational effects of maternal
exposure
A meta-analysis comprising four European birth cohorts
(the ALPHABET project) reported that higher maternal dietary
inﬂammatory scores during pregnancy were associated with
oﬀspring internalizing symptoms (depression, anxiety), aggressive
behaviors, and ADHD at ages 7–10 (62). Similarly, the INMA
and RHEA cohorts in Spain reported associations between
higher maternal DII and ADHD symptoms in preschool boys
(63). Neuroimaging follow-ups further revealed that maternal
dietary inﬂammatory scores during gestation were associated
with oﬀspring cortical gyriﬁcation patterns and later verbal
IQ(64).
4.2.2 Associations involving children’s own diet
In children and adolescents, higher C-DII scores have been
associated with ADHD (potentially in a non-linear U-shaped
association)(65,66),alongsidemoreseveredepressiveandanxious
symptoms (67). Some observational analyses have evaluated
systemic inﬂammatory markers (e.g., CRP) and cardiometabolic
factors (e.g., BMI z-score and waist circumference) as statistical
mediators of associations between pro-inﬂammatory diets and
mooddisorders( 67,68).
4.2.3 Association with autism spectrum disorder
(ASD)
Recent studies reveal that children with ASD not only
suﬀer from low dietary diversity and severe selective eating

5 Early-life UPF exposure and
life-course metabolic trajectories
Longitudinal observational studies have examined whether
pediatric UPF exposure is associated with subsequent metabolic
andadipositytrajectories.
5.1 Accelerated adiposity rebound and
altered life trajectories
The Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children
(ALSPAC)provideslongitudinalobservationalevidence.Following
over 9,000 children for more than a decade, the study indicated
thatchildreninthehighestquintileofUPFconsumptionexhibited
signiﬁcantly steeper growth trajectories in Body Mass Index
(BMI), fat mass index, body weight, and waist circumference
from childhood into early adulthood compared to their low-
consumption peers (70). These trajectories may be relevant to later
cardiometabolic risk, although the observational design does not
establish causality. A large Brazilian birth cohort also reported
that higher UPF intake scores during the toddler years (2–4 years)
were associated with changes in BMI z-scores and linear growth,
supportingfurtherevaluationofearly-lifedietaryexposureandlife-
course metabolic trajectories (71). Longitudinal ﬁndings further
indicate that anthropometric indicators in early life are predictive
of low-grade inﬂammation and insulin resistance in adolescence,
suggesting that pro-inﬂammatory patterns may emerge before
adulthood(72).
5.2 Induction of “normal-weight obesity”
(NWO)
Body-composition outcomes may diﬀer among children
with similar BMI values. Research involving Brazilian school-
aged children revealed that high UPF intake and high C-DII
scores were associated with higher odds of the “Normal-Weight
Obesity (NWO)” phenotype—characterized by normal BMI but
excessive body fat percentage (59). This implies that traditional
BMI screenings may underestimate the potential metabolic risks
associated with UPF intake, but longitudinal studies are needed
to determine the temporal and biological interpretation of this
association(73).
5.3 Infancy overweight and alterations in
breast milk
Within extremely low-income households, associations with
UPF exposure have been reported as early as infancy. Brazilian
cohort studies highlight that frequent maternal UPF consumption,
combined with short durations of exclusive breastfeeding (<30
days), was associated with infant overweight in the ﬁrst year of
life (74). Furthermore, maternal obesity and dietary inﬂammatory
potential have been associated with diﬀerences in breast-milk
composition, increasing the pro-inﬂammatory fatty acid ratio
(n-6/n-3) while depleting DHA and lutein, elements critical to
neurodevelopment, thereby potentially disrupting early neural and
visualmaturation( 75).
5.4 Exposomics and epigenetics: potential
mechanisms of long-term dietary effects
Studies utilizing the “exposome” framework use metabolomic
proﬁling to examine metabolic pathways associated with UPF
exposure and obesity risk. ALSPAC metabolomic data show that
high UPF intake in 7-year-olds correlates with 115 abnormal
metabolic traits, including depleted levels of BCAAs and aromatic
amino acids, alongside elevated citrate and glutamine; these
metabolic diﬀerences are candidate intermediates rather than
establishedmediatorsoffataccumulation( 76).
Beyond transient metabolomic shifts, epigenetic regulation
is a candidate mechanism for longer-term biological embedding,
but direct pediatric and transgenerational evidence remains
limited. Prospective cohorts and animal models have examined
whether early dietary environments are associated with persistent
DNA-methylation or histone-modiﬁcation patterns. Possible
cross-generational associations remain hypothesis-generating and
require replication. For instance, the Lifeways cross-generation
study showed that pre-pregnancy and prenatal maternal diet
quality (e.g., HEI-2015 scores) was associated with diﬀerences in
DNA methylation of genes linked to skeletal development and cell
signaling(e.g.,PLEKHM1,LUZP1)in9-year-oldoﬀspring( 77).

inﬂammation (e.g., IL-6 related regions) (78). Additionally, recent
meta-analyses of large EWAS involving multiple European child
cohorts (e.g., HELIX, ALSPAC, Generation R) suggest that
childhood UPF intake was associated with methylation diﬀerences
at seven speciﬁc CpG sites (e.g., PHYHIP, NHEJ1, ATF7) largely

6 UPFs, the gut microbiome, and the
systemic inﬂammatory network
Candidate pathways linking UPF-rich dietary patterns with
inﬂammatory outcomes may involve gut microbial composition,
intestinal barrier function, and systemic immune signaling
(Figure1).Speciﬁcinﬂammatorybiomarkersmayhelpcharacterize
associations between dietary exposure and clinical outcomes by
indicating chronic responses to dietary stressors (81). Figure1
presents an evidence-informed conceptual model rather than
an established pathophysiological cascade. It distinguishes
dietary exposure, candidate microbial and barrier pathways,
and observed or proposed outcomes. The links among these

hypothesis-generating rather than established explanations of
lifelongtrajectories.
6.1 Compromised microbial diversity and
the DI-GM index
Many UPF-rich dietary patterns are high in free sugars or
fats and low in dietary ﬁber, features that may be associated
with lower availability of substrates for butyrate-producing taxa.
Pediatric studies report heterogeneous taxon-level diﬀerences
rather than a consistent dysbiosis signature. Adult cross-sectional
studies using the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-
GM) report associations between higher scores and selected
inﬂammatory or clinical outcomes (82, 83). These adult ﬁndings
provide indirect context and should not be generalized as causal
pediatriceﬀects.
6.2 Western and mediterranean dietary
patterns
Ultra-processedfoodexposuregenerallyoccurswithinbroader
dietary patterns. Western diets, characterized by high intakes
of reﬁned grains, added sugars, saturated fats, processed meats,
and UPFs but low intakes of ﬁber and plant foods, may reduce
microbiota-accessible substrates and favor less beneﬁcial microbial
proﬁles. In a cross-sectional study of 335 children and adolescents,
aWesterndietarypatternwasassociatedwithbehavioralproblems,
lower levels of butyrate-producing species, and higher levels of
selected potential pathogens; however, causality could not be
established(84).
In contrast, the Mediterranean diet emphasizes plant foods,
whole grains, ﬁsh, nuts, and olive oil while limiting reﬁned
and highly processed foods. Its higher ﬁber, unsaturated fat,
and polyphenol content may support microbial diversity and
short-chain fatty acid production. Greater adherence has been
associatedwithhighermicrobialdiversityandincreasedabundance
of taxa such as Faecalibacterium and Prevotella, although the
evidence is derived mainly from adult or mixed populations
and remains heterogeneous (85). Replacing UPFs with minimally
processed Mediterranean-style foods may therefore support
microbial and metabolic health in children, but direct evidence
that this pattern reverses UPF-related microbiome, immune, or
neurodevelopmental alterations remains limited. It should be
regarded as a health-promoting alternative rather than a proven
antidotetoUPF-relatedeﬀects( 84).
6.3 Food additives, artiﬁcial dyes, “leaky

6.4 Pathological remodeling of the

Alterations in gut microbial composition and intestinal

gut–brain pathways, but the pediatric evidence is incomplete.
Potential routes include circulating inﬂammatory signals,

8 Public health policies and clinical
interventions to address the
pro-inﬂammatory environment
Within the exposure–mediator framework, front-of-pack
labeling and reformulation primarily target product information
and composition; school food standards reduce institutional
UPF exposure; taxation and subsidies alter relative prices and
aﬀordability; marketing restrictions reduce child- and caregiver-
directed promotional exposure; and additive-speciﬁc regulation
may reduce exposure to compounds with plausible microbiome
or barrier eﬀects. These measures are complementary rather than
interchangeable and should be evaluated for behavioral, biological,
distributional,andequityoutcomes.
Individual dietary education alone is unlikely to address
the multilevel determinants of pediatric UPF exposure. A
complementary policy portfolio can act on product information
and composition, institutional exposure, relative aﬀordability,
marketing, and selected additive exposures (116). The complex
interaction between commercial drivers and strategic policy
solutionsissummarizedin Figure3.
8.1 Advancing front-of-pack warning labels
(FOPL) and product reformulation
Drawing on successes in Chile, Peru, and Mexico, mandatory
“Front-of-pack warning labels” (FOPL) eﬀectively modify
consumer purchasing behaviors. The World Heart Federation
(WHF) recommends that governments implement evidence-based
FOPL ( 117). Qualitative research among Peruvian mothers
reportedstrongrecognitionandcomprehensionofFOPLwarnings
andperceivedeﬀectsonUPFpurchasingfrequency( 118).
In real-world settings, FOPL policies may encourage product
reformulation. Longitudinal data from Peru and Colombia report
reductions in sugar or sodium content in top-selling products
following warning label implementations ( 119, 120). Mexican
regulations successfully prohibited misleading health claims on
nearly 40% of junk food packaging ( 121). Compared to the
Australian Health Star Rating (HSR) or the European Nutri-
Score (which occasionally bestow “health halos” upon UPFs),
the Chilean warning model may diﬀer in sensitivity and eﬀects
on purchasing intentions (122–124). Furthermore, as NNS are
increasinglyusedtoevadesugartaxes,expertsinBrazilandbeyond
are advocating for dedicated “NNS warning labels” to prevent
secondary microecological and metabolic risks (125). Nonetheless,
vigilance against policy dilution by food industry lobbying and
public-privatepartnerships(PPPs)remainscritical( 126–128).
8.2Reshapingtheschoolfoodenvironment
and universal free school meals
Schools are primary venues for pediatric caloric intake;
therefore, the school food environment is an important and
modiﬁable inﬂuence on dietary exposure. Natural experiments
provide evidence relevant to universal free school meals (UFSM).
Evaluations in the UK show that UFSM policies not only boost
lunch participation but reduce in-school pediatric UPF intake by
nearly7%,withthemostprofoundbeneﬁtsobservedinlow-income
demographics (129). Similarly, evaluations of the US Community
Eligibility Provision (CEP) reported lower elementary-school
obesityprevalenceorBMIz-scores( 130,131),whilesimultaneously
reducing household grocery expenditure on unhealthy foods by
5%−19%(132).
Longitudinal data indicate that food-swamp exposure and
persistent availability of unhealthy competitive foods around
schools are associated with increases in childhood obesity
prevalence (133). Globally, a review of 193 nations found only
15% enforce national school meal nutritional restrictions (134).
Detailed audits of school and university canteens reveal signiﬁcant
gaps between what is nutritionally intended and what is actually
served and consumed, often characterized by high food waste
and suboptimal nutritional proﬁles, indicating scope for structural
improvementsininstitutionalfoodenvironments( 135,136).Cities
including New York and Copenhagen have extended school meal
policies into planetary health strategies integrating sustainability
and nutritional equity (137). Enacting strict regulations limiting
UPF sales and advertising within and around schools may reduce
institutionalandmarketing-relatedUPFexposure.
8.3 Strengthening marketing restrictions,
taxation, and health equity
Confronting the escalating dual burden of malnutrition,
taxation policies targeting SSBs and UPFs have entered core public
health agendas globally (138). Simulation models in Chile indicate
that taxes of 20%−30% on products exceeding nutrient thresholds
could reduce purchasing and generate revenue to subsidize public
healthinterventions( 138,139).
An intersectional perspective highlights that low-income and
minority households may face time constraints and limited access
to aﬀordable fresh foods that shape UPF purchasing ( 140–
142). Agent-based modeling suggests that policy eﬀects may
diﬀer by social-transition stage; where UPF consumption is
common among low-income populations, warning labels without
concurrent price interventions could exacerbate health inequities
(143). Consequently, concurrent with UPF taxation and pediatric
marketing bans (34,144), subsidy mechanisms may be paired with
thesepoliciestoimprovetheaccessibilityandaﬀordabilityofwhole
grainsandfreshproduceinmarginalizedcommunities( 145,146).

9.2 Causal inference challenges and
“reverse causality” risks
Current human evidence linking UPF exposure to pediatric
neuropsychiatric disorders (e.g., ADHD, ASD) predominantly
stemsfromcross-sectionalortraditionallongitudinalobservational
studies. Although animal gut–brain–inﬂammation models provide
biological plausibility, reverse causality remains diﬃcult to exclude
in human populations. For instance, some children with ASD
or ADHD exhibit sensory processing diﬀerences, restricted
eating patterns, or diﬀerences in impulse control that may
contribute to preferences for predictable or highly palatable
foods. Distinguishing whether diet precedes neurodevelopmental
outcomes,followsselectiveeating,orparticipatesinabidirectional
relationshiprequiresstrongerlongitudinalevidence.Futurestudies
could combine genetically informed designs with long-term birth
cohorts using repeated dietary and outcome measurements to
clarify temporal sequence and causal direction. Comparative
analyses across neurodevelopmental conditions should assess
environmental,genetic,socioeconomic,andbehavioralfactors.

taxes or basic FOPL) may precipitate unintended “substitution
eﬀects”. In response to sugar-reduction policies, manufacturers
may reformulate products with non-caloric sweeteners such
as aspartame or with alternative additives to maintain sensory
characteristics. However, as elucidated in our mechanistic

10 Conclusion
UPFscontributesubstantiallytomanypediatricandadolescent
diets and warrant evaluation within the broader determinants
of diet quality and energy intake. Pediatric observational studies
most consistently report associations with poorer diet quality
and less favorable adiposity or cardiometabolic trajectories.
Processing-related characteristics, food-matrix disruption, and
selected additives provide candidate additional pathways, but their
eﬀectsindependentoftotalenergyintakeandnutrientcomposition
remainuncertain,particularlyinchildren.
The proposed “pro-inﬂammatory imprint” is an evidence-
informed, hypothesis-generating framework. Microbial
composition, intestinal-barrier function, inﬂammatory signaling,
and immune-to-neural communication are biologically plausible
candidate pathways supported by varying combinations
of pediatric, adult, and preclinical evidence. Persistent
neurodevelopmental, epigenetic, or transgenerational eﬀects
remain particularly uncertain, and associations involving ADHD
orASDmayalsoreﬂectreversecausalityandresidualconfounding.
Caregiver decisions occur within household, commercial,
institutional, cultural, and policy contexts. Front-of-pack labeling
and reformulation, school food standards, taxation and subsidies,
marketing restrictions, and additive-speciﬁc regulation act at
diﬀerent and complementary points in this system and should be
assessed for substitution and equity eﬀects. The cP-IDII remains
an unvalidated research framework; empirical score development,
regional calibration, and external validation are required before it
caninformclinical,epidemiological,orpublichealthdecisions.
