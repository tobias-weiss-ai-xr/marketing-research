Abstract
Global disruptions from the COVID -19 pandemic, climate change, and geopolitical conflicts
have triggered the breakdown of global food systems and highlighted the vulnerabilities of
food security. This review identifies digitalization in food marketing as o ne strategic way to
enhance the food system's flexibility. With the support of literature and case studies of
GrabFood, Uber Eats, and Kitopi , this study investigates the implications of digital
infrastructures such as the platform economy, cloud kitchens, and social media in relation to
the four pillars of food security. This includes availability, accessibility, affordability, and utility
of food. This study finds that during the COVID-19 pandemic, digital food marketing evolved
beyond crisis management to a mechanism of structural resilience of food systems that
continues to address and stabilize demand, sustain consumer confidence, and creat e new
market opportunities. However, a “market” variant of “captive” digital food marketing is far
from a panacea, as important issues of sustainability, workers' rights, and digital inclusion
remain. This study provides insight into the digital marketing of food and security in a fragile
and increasingly unpredictable global economy.
Keywords: Digital Food Marketing, Food Security, Platform Economy, Cloud Kitchens, Global
Disruptions

Strategic Food Marketing Approaches
Food marketing involves all activities that bring agricultural and culinary products from the
producer to the consumer. The foundation of food marketing came from the foundation
theories such as Kotler’s Marketing Mix (4Ps/7Ps)  (Kotler & Keller, 2016)., which focuses on
product, place, price, and promotion, which are the key determinants for consumer
behaviour. As time progresses, these models then evolved to include relationship marketing
and strategic marketing approaches to gain more consumer trust, resilience, and adaptability.
According to Viquez Ramirez et al. ( 2024), the team conducted a systematic bibliometric
review of 414 articles to understand and identify the cluster around consumption, nutrition,
branding, and health economics. According to the review, it was identified that marketing
strategies have increasing ly intersected with sustainability and resilience based on the
consumer shift in mindset and influence. Based on Hoffmann  (2020), he believes that
extrinsic product attributes such as price, brand, and labelling have strongly influenced
consumer food choices, which underscores the role of marketing in accessibility. However,

Food Security Framework
Today, there are 673 million people in the world, and about 8.2% of the population is facing
hunger, where nearly 2.3 billion remain moderately or severely food insecure  (FAO, 2025).
With the increasingly limited food accessibility, which leads to hunger, the Food and
Agriculture Organization (FAO) has constantly emphasized the need for countries to
strengthen their food security by transforming the food system to become more resilient and
sustainable.

In order to  help countries to understand and define food security, FAO determines food
security through 4 dimensions: Availability, Accessibility, Utilization, and Stability. With this
theoretical backbone, it enabled FAO and countries to analyse the vulnerability of  the food
system. According to the FAO’s State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World, FAO
highlighted that price inflation  and geopolitical shocks had undermined both access and
stability of food systems, and such instability has constrained the vulnerable populations and
their capacity to secure access to food (FAO et al., 2025).

As Emily and team suggest, the localization of food systems combined with the application of
agroecological principles is one of the possible approaches to achieve a resilient and
sustainable food system that promotes planetary health  (Emily et al., 2024) . Localization of
food production and procurement has not been new in the food supply chain. However, the
utilization and maximizing its capacity to leverage its benefit has not been truly discovered.
Limitations such as fragmented policy, lack of infrastructure, and weak market incentives are
the main constraints that affect the capacity to transform the food systems. To resolve such
constraints, circular economy strategies such as composting, waste -to-energy conversion,
biomass valorization , and localization should be applied to contribute to FAO’s four
dimensions of food security to improve its resilience and sustainability (FAO, 2023).

Global Disruptions and Food Systems
In 2019, when COVID -19 hit the world with massive lockdowns and border controls, it
revealed the fragility of the global food systems  (WFP, 2023) . During the pandemic,
governments worldwide have enforced different mandates to control the outbreak of COVID-
19. That said, a lot of the action and decisions taken have caused the major disruption in the
food supply system, from the crop left unharvested in the farm, to the stop of production of
food manufacturing and delivery to retail for purchase, leaving millions worldwide in panic
and over-purchasing of food to stock up. Such actions have once again proven the fragility of
the food system, as consumers faced shortages while producers struggle d with unsold
surpluses.

Due to the lockdown restriction on physical dining, platform delivery became an essential and
immediate intermediary to enable restaurants to survive and continue to service its
consumer. At the same time, the cloud kitchen model has bloomed and expanded rapidly,
which offers efficiency and resilience to ensure food availability during systemic disruptions
(Suali et.al,2024). Equally significant was the role of social media marketing. Platforms like
Instagram and Tiktok was heavily involved in millions of lives during the pandemic period,
and with that, they had reshaped consumer behaviour through viral homemade trends such
as Dalgona Coffee, Sourdough starter, Do-it-yourself (DIY) kits, etc (Rojas-Reyes et al., 2024).
Concurrent with the blooming and change of scene on social media marketing, food delivery
platforms and cloud kitchens have also taken the waste to leverage these trends by

Remarkably, the pandemic has also acted as a catalyst to accelerate the addressing of climate

example, the European Union’s Farm to Fork Strategy was escalated as part of the lessons
from the pandemics which emphasizes food waste reduction, local sourcing, and sustainable
production (European Commission, 2020). Similarly, Singapore which 90% of its food is based
on importation, recognized its vulnerability to global disruption. In response, the Singapore
government accelerate it’s “30 by 30” strategy, which was launched and reinforced during
COVID-19, to reduce its reliance on imported food (Singapore Food Agency, 2020). Owing to
the limited land in Singapore, such an initiative was heavily emphasizing how urban farming,
vertical agriculture, aquaculture, and high-tech solutions are able to help accelerate the goal.
With this strategy being reinforced during COVID -19, Singapore had also leveraged digital
platforms and social media to promote and educate consumers to raise awareness on
supporting local products.

Notably, geopolitical conflicts have also contributed to the instability of global food systems
yet reshaped the dynamic of food marketing. From the Russian-Ukrainian war, the major
disruption happened in the grain and fertilizer import, which triggered the shortage and price
volatility across the world (World Food Programme, 2023). In reaction to this, food companies
and governments were forced to transform its marketing strategies to emphasize resilience,
affordability, and trust. From promoting life -style oriented campaign to navigational value
and reliability, this strategy shift had reassured consumers amid the uncertain supply and
inflation. Meanwhile, delivery apps  and cloud kitchens have once again leveraged digital
platforms and social media to help stabilize the demands by integrating marketing campaigns
that focus on convenience and continuity.

Another example that demonstrated the evolution and adaptation was the geopolitical
tensions between the United States and Iran. As the Strait of Hormuz is the main port that
affects global oil and fertilizer trade, the war has affected the stability of this port during the
conflict period  (FAO, 2025) . That instability has resulted in rising fuel and fertilizer costs,
which are then translated into high production and distribution expenses.

Critical Synthesis of Literature
In aggregate, the COVID -19 pandemic, climate change, and geopolitical conflicts have
exposed the vulnerability in food systems while simultaneously accelerating innovation in
food strategic marketing. Strategies such as Singapore’s “30 by 30” initiative, t he EU’s Farm
to Fork Strategy (Singapore Food Agency, 2020; European Commission, 2020), and the rapid
expansion of food delivery platforms and cloud kitchen has demonstrated how governments

In the broader context, digitalization in food marketing served as a critical focus with the

pandemic. For availability, the expansion of cloud kitchens at a lower cost had ensure
consistence food supply  (Hairini et al., 2025) . For accessibility, the presence of delivery
platforms has helped reduce the barrier by connecting consumers to food directly during the
lockdown and geopolitical disruption.  The emphasis on digital marketing, it has also
offloaded the inflation pressure by promoting discounts, bundle deals, and smaller pack sizes.
Thus, digitalization had a direct impact and integrated FAO’s four dimensions of food security,
positioning digital marketing as one of the key structural components that push and influence
the market.

Nevertheless, there are several gaps and contradiction remain. For instance , studies and
review was often carried out to understand how food delivery platforms operate as a
business rather than analysing how such platforms market their functions as a resilience tool
((Arghiroiu, Bobeică, Beciu, & Mann, 2025) ). Moreover, such a marketing campaign has
advertised cheaper food options, which could clash with sustainability goals. Therefore, there
will be tension and indirect conflicts between what the consumer needs in the short term and
the prioritization of the environment for the long term. Co nversely, reliance on digital
platforms has increased the knowledge and information gap for those who do not have
internet access, which raises the question of inclusivity in such a resilience strategy
(Rojas‑Reyes et al. , 2024 ). Despite these gaps and contradictions, such evolvement of
marketing strategy has always been surrounded by a resilience message which emphasizes
trust, affordability, and continuity, which strongly align with FAO's four pillars to fight for food
sustainability.

Discussion & Implications
Impact of Food Security
The rapid growth of digitalization of food marketing during and after the COVID-19 pandemic
has disclosed both opportunities and threats in how resilience is embedded into the food
systems. Case insights such as Delivery Platform in Southeast Asia and Europe, Kitopi growth
in the Middle East have clearly demonstrated that the presence of digital platforms is not a
short-term crisis tool, but it can evolve into a structural component to strengthen the food
security frameworks (Suali et al., 2024). Each of the case studies has demonstrated how firms
are able to leverage the digital infrastructure to build and stabilize the demand, help
restaurants to maintain consumer trust, and expand the market reach, while raising concerns
about inclusivity and long-term resilience in environmental sustainability.

Business Strategies and Consumer Trust
For instance, the COVID-19 pandemic has validated that digitalization in food marketing is a
long-term and structural necessity for businesses. Firms such as GrabFood, UberEats and
Kitopi have clearly demonstrated that resilience requires diversification across multiple
channels and services. From Cloud Kitchens and Food Delivery service to Grocery Delivery,
cross-platform ecosystems stabilized the demand and e mbedded resilience into their
business models (Arghiroiu et al., 2025). Meanwhile, data-driven engagement from consumer
purchase history data has also become the center of marketing strategies that firms referred.
Platforms are now depending on consumer data analysis, consumer algorithm and loyalty
programs to personalize user experiences and strengthen trust with consumers.  This
implication is clear that businesses sho uld take digitalization as a core marketing strategy ,
emphasizing stability, trust , and continuity in the branding to build user and consumer
confidence. While discount and bundle deals might help consumers to cope with inflation
pressure, the reliance of fast-food chains and unsustainable supply chains might damage the
image, as it conflicts with long-term sustainability (FAO et al., 2025).

Platform Regulation and Labour Rights
Policymakers have played a pivotal role in shaping how digitalisation in food marketing helps
enable food security. The government should set the rules to determine whether platform
economies operate in a fair, inclusive, and sustainable manner. For instance, Uber Eats and
GrabFood leverage the use of algorithms to help privilege certain restaurants in their visibility
on the platform, which raises a concern about equity and competition  (Oh et.al, 2026) .
Without the regulatory oversight, it might put small business to be at risk of exclusion from
digital markets, which might lead to loss of customer base.

Apart from this, policymakers should ensure that platform economies impose fair payment
and equality, particularly in relation to labor rights. The rise of these platform economies has
raised concern about fair payments towards their delivery drivers, as s een in both Uber Eats

The rapid expansion of Kitopi  across the Middle East, policymakers play a critical role in
identifying how cloud kitchens contribute to food security, labour rights, and sustainability.
First, necessary regulations should be imposed to ensure labour rights and fair employment
practices within the kitchen operations , as the workforce could be substantial and often
diverse. Secondly, policymakers should incentivize eco -friendly practices to influence and
impose in the model. Such practice will not only help to deconflict Kitopis ; affordability
campaigns, which often concerns on the long-term sustainability, also help gain its consumer
trust towards sustainability, which aligns strongly with FAO’s four pillars. Third, the
government should also impose a competition policy to prevent monopolistic dominance by
Kitopi. With the rapid expansion of Kitopi, small and local food businesses could be in danger
due to the multi-brand strategy. This will not just affect the business but also potentially
reduce the diversity in the food ecosystems.

Consumers and Digital Engagements
Consumers, who are both beneficiaries and participants in the context of digitalisation of food

Conclusion
This study has shown that digitalization in food marketing could and has become a structural
pillar of food security, which has moved beyond its initial roles during the pandemic response.
Case studies from GrabFood, Uber Eats, and Kitopi have explained how platform economies
and cloud kitchens could influence and reinforce the FAO’s four pillars , a vailability,
accessibility, affordability, and utilization while acknowledging the rising concern about
sustainability, inclusivity, and labour rights. The discussion has also pointed out that
businesses must embed resilience into their strategy, and polic ymakers must regulate for
fairness and sustainability. Consumers should also critically navigate the trade -off between
convenience and long -term resilience in environmental sustainability (FAO, 2025; Haidar,
2024; Saccone & Vallino, 2025).
