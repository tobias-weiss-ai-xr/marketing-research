Abstract 5
Virtual avatar deployment has opened a new window  for many industries  in service 6
personalization and customization , offering  customers ‘anytime-anywhere’ services. It 7
becomes increasingly essential for marketing practitioners and scholars  to clearly understand 8
how avatar attributes and traits are reshaping customer value perceptions in the digital world. 9
Despite the exponential growth of research interest within this area in the last decade, the 10
literature on avatar attributes and consumer-perceived values remains fragmented and isolated. 11
Given this circumstance, this review draws on the theory -context-characteristics-methods 12
framework and explores the extant literature to provide a collective and comprehensive insight 13
into dominant theories, contexts, characteristics , and method ologies in the context of avatar -14
based marketing (ABM). A systematic literature review was applied to rigorously review 57 15
published articles on avatars and  ABM from 2005 to 202 3. Using in-depth content analysis, 16
the current review sheds light on the opportunity to extend the theoretical foundations for the 17
ABM literature. It also identifies gaps in understanding avatar attributes and the associated 18
consumer values, with potential implications for consumer behavior . Finally, it sums up a 19
comprehensive future research agenda. 20

1. Introduction 1
Digital transformation has profoundly influenced all business sectors over the last decade, 2
leading to widespread change across businesses, society, and consumer behaviors (Paul et al., 3
2024). This shift is particularly evident as m odern technologies are reshaping users’ online 4
experiences with various virtual interactive animated characters or avatars . Holzwarth, 5
Janiszewski, and Neumann (2006, p. 19)  conceptualized avatars as “general graphic 6
representations that are personified by means of computer technology”.  From the marketing 7
perspective, Keeling, McGoldrick, and Beatty (2010, p. 793)  defined them as “interactive 8
representations of sales assistants on retail websites, including all types, whether realistic or 9
cartoon humans, animals, or objects.”  Although the literature has used a variety of terms 10
(elaborated in section 4.2) to define avatars, at single point researchers have contended that 11
globally the adoption of avatars within the marketing realm represents an emerging business 12
phenomenon (Garnier & Poncin, 2013; Gonzales -Chávez & Vila -Lopez, 2021; Miao, 13
Kozlenkova, Wang, Xie, & Palmatier, 2022; Nicolescu & Tudorache, 2022; Nowak, Fox, & 14
Ranjit, 2015). Consequently, avatar-based marketing (ABM) has gained heightened attention 15
from various stakeholders, including consumers, marketers, policymakers, service providers, 16
and scholars (Hemp, 2006; Miao et al., 2022). Companies like Nike have introduced Nikeland 17
to establish a robust presence in the Metaverse through platforms like Roblox. Similarly, IKEA 18
has introduced a customer -focused avatar named Anna, who can address inquiries regarding 19
IKEA's products, services, and operating hours. 20
Behind such  heightened popularity of avatars, there are mainly two factors: first, the 21
advancement of digital technologies, e.g., artificial intelligence (AI) (Garnier & Poncin, 2013; 22
Hemp, 2006; Miao et al., 2022; Paul, Ueno, & Dennis, 2023); second, the growing importance 23
of avatars in online services (e.g., banking, gaming, education, shopping, m-health, etc.) (Hitti, 24
2020; Huang, Hew, & Fryer, 2022; Jenneboer, Herrando, & Constantinides, 2022; S. Kim, 25

3

Chen, & Zhang, 2016; SeoYoung Lee & Choi, 2017) . In the recent past , the COVID-19 1
pandemic has also compelled the altering of many customers’ traditional purchase patterns to 2
online shopping (Noble et al., 2022) . This shift highlights consumers’ inclination to obtain 3
information from websites conveniently and effortlessly, thus justifying the deployment of 4
virtual agents or avatars.  5
Research indicates that avatars can enhance customer value through service 6
customization, personalization, responsiveness, and convenience (Miao et al., 2022; Verhagen, 7
Van Nes, Feldberg, & Van Dolen, 2014; Zhang et al., 2023) . Indeed, avatars are becoming 8
integral in marketing strategies, offering immersive engagement opportunities and influencing 9
consumer perceptions and behaviors in the digital era (Kovacova, Machova, & Bennett, 2022; 10
Latoschik et al., 2017). This creates implicit and explicit competition among companies over 11
gaining a more extensive consumer base through better customer service, driving firms toward 12
investing heavily in avatar-based service offerings. The major goals are to engage consumers 13
actively and serve them better than others. Companies may realize that service employees 14
cannot provide a relentless service (Schuetzler, Giboney, Grimes, & Nunamaker Jr, 2018), and 15
this gap can be bridged by utilizing avatars. Consequently, the usage of avatars is not limited 16
to a particular industry, like banking or hotel; it is seen as a huge prospect in the virtual gaming, 17
smartphone, and fashion industries. For instance,  Samsung has launched an AI -supported 18
virtual avatar called Neon to provide customers with real -time information (Hitti, 2020) . 19
Similarly, the famous fashion brand Calvin Klein has deployed Lil Miquela, a virtual influencer 20
(another form of avatar) ; interestingly, Lil Miquela has more than 2.4 million followers on 21
Instagram (Mosley, 2021) . According to recent statistics from the American Marketing 22
Association (AMA), the avatar market is projected to grow by 47.01% annually  (Bhavsar, 23
2023). 24

Scores of studies have been undertaken in the context of exploring ABM (see Brendel, 1
Hildebrandt, Dennis, & Riquel, 2023; Butt, Ahmad, Goraya, Akram, & Shafique, 2021; 2
Chocarro, Cortiñas, & Marcos -Matás, 2023; de Brito Silva & de Oliveira Campos, 2024; 3
Hemp, 2006) . Corroborating with Hemp (2006)  and Miao et al. (2022) , we define avatar 4
marketing as using avatars to create a more engaging and personalized experience for 5
consumers, which can enhance their interaction trust and ultimately influence their purchase 6
decisions. However, comprehensive review studies depicting the emergence of avatar 7
marketing are still lacking. Gaps also exist in defining avatars and elucidating their 8
characteristics (Miao et al., 2022). With evolving ABM applications, there is also a noticeable 9
lack of synthesis of theories, avatar design, characteristics, deployment, current research trends, 10
and contextual considerations (de Brito Silva & de Oliveira Campos, 2024; Emergen Research, 11
2023; Laszkiewicz & Kalinska‐Kula, 2023). In addition, previous reviews have not delved into 12
the avatars’ value-based aspects (e.g., social, functional, emotional, epistemic, and conditional 13
value), strongly triggering consumers’ purchase decisions. Essentially, customer values cannot 14
be ignored in marketing (Mason, Oduro, Umar, & Zamparo, 2023; Yang & Lin, 2017), whether 15
in an online or offline context . As ABM is relatively new and emerging, presenting a review 16
drawing on value-based aspects may add fresh insights to the extant literature. Thus, to explore 17
customer value aspects, this review relies on the Theory of Consumption Values (TCV), which 18
has been utilized from diverse behavioral elements, such as intention (Joibi & Annuar, 2021; 19
Yang & Lin, 2017), adoption (Turel, Serenko, & Bontis, 2010), and use continuance (Yang & 20
Lin, 2017). Moreover, Sheth, Newman, and Gross (1991) urged investigating customer values 21
through the lens of the TCV to better understand and influence consumer behavior. The 22
importance of TCV is also highlighted  in predicting  digital consumer behavior  (Tanrikulu, 23
2021). 24

Microsoft’s virtual assistant, Cortana, struggled with consumer adoption, primarily due 1
to customer value perceptions, compared to competitors like Amazon’s Alexa and Google 2
Assistant. Many users found Cortana less intuitive and useful, leading to its diminished 3
presence in the market (Warren, 2023) . This example again underlines the criticality of 4
understanding customer -perceived values in ABM. Although several former studies (e.g., 5
Bialkova, 2023; van Pinxteren, Pluymaekers, Lemmink, & Krispin, 2023)  have underscored 6
avatar attributes and customer-perceived values within a customer behavioral context, the 7
majority of studies have not thoroughly addressed these aspects. For instance, Van Pinxteren, 8
Pluymaekers, and Lemmink (2020)  highlighted conversational agents’ communicative 9
behaviors from two dimensions: modality (verbal, nonverbal, appearance) and footing 10
(similarity, responsiveness).  Barrera and Shah (2023)  illustrated how firms utilize avatars’ 11
immersiveness, sociability, and environmental realism in the metaverse. In a similar context,  12
de Brito Silva and de Oliveira Campos (2024)  have traced the evolution of the avatars  and 13
uncovered several key agents involved in the  ABM strategy (please see Figure 1 for more 14
details). However, these reviews have overlooked how avatar attributes can evoke customer 15
values and how these values can drive customers toward desired behavioral outcomes from a 16
marketing perspective. Hence, by highlighting the functional, social, emotional, epistemic, and 17
conditional values based on TCV, the current review helps devis e more effective customer-18
oriented ABM strategies . From a methodological perspective, there is a dearth of studies 19
addressing popular research methods within the ABM context  (de Brito Silva & de Oliveira 20
Campos, 2024; Miao et al., 2022) . Thus, an extensive review is needed to deeply understand 21
ABM and synthesize the theories, contexts, characteristics, and methods applied and explored 22
thus far  to outline a potential research agenda for further advancement of this promising 23
research domain. To supplement our arguments, Table 1 presents the major findings of recent 24
review studies in this domain and highlights how this study is unique to those. 25

This review thus adds value to the existing literature in the following ways . First, this 8
study summarizes two key elements: form realism and behavioral realism of ABM. Although 9
the form and behavioral realism have been illustrated in some studies (see S. Kim et al., 2016; 10
Y. Kim & Sundar, 2012; Miao et al., 2022; Moon & Nass, 1996) , comparative discussions 11
between these elements in review studies are scant. A comparative discussion may help online 12
marketers optimally blend and effectively design the form and behavior realism  of avatars. 13
Second, this study comprehensively summarizes the major theories and models employed to 14
investigate ABM in various studies. This snapshot view would enable scholars and 15
practitioners to swiftly grasp and pursue further research based on the prevalent theories, 16
frameworks, and models of avatar marketing. Furthermore, this study represents the first 17
exploration of customers’ value -based aspects within the ABM context.  Our study explicitly 18
underlines the independent, moderating, mediating, and outcome variables grounded in 19
customer value dimensions, including functional, social, emotional, epistemic, and conditional 20
values. This discussion  would enable marketers to understand how effectively deploying 21
avatars can enhance customer engagement by delivering meaningful interactions that meet 22
consumer needs while maintaining trust and privacy. 23
Third, this review clearly illustrates the diverse contexts (e.g., industry, avatar label, and 24
country) of ABM. Although many studies (e.g., Apoki, Hussein, Al -Chalabi, Badica, & 25

2. Review structure and methodology 1
We adopted a framework-based review approach (Paul & Criado, 2020; Paul, Khatri, & Kaur 2
Duggal, 2023; Paul, Lim, O’Cass, Hao, & Bresciani, 2021) with the ‘scientific procedures and 3
rationales for systematic literature reviews’ (SPAR -4-SLR) protocol (Khan, Anas, & Uddin, 4
2024; Paul, Lim, et al., 2021) consisting of three stages and six sub-stages (see Figure 1). 5
2.1 Assembling 6
Assembling is the first stage, comprising two sub-stages: identification and acquisition (Paul, 7
Lim, et al., 2021).  8
The identification sub -stage involves conceptualizing the research domain, research 9
questions, source type, and source quality.  Our research domain is centered on ‘avatars and 10
avatar-based marketing.’ The research questions of this review were formulated to examine the 11
domain’s knowledge structure, emergence, and performance. Hence, we considered peer -12
reviewed research articles and conference proceedings (S. Kumar, Pandey, Lim, Chatterjee, 13
and Pandey (2021) . In doing so, we selected three major databases : Web of Science (WoS), 14
Scopus, and Google Scholar , providing high accuracy in retrieving relevant journal articles  15
(Falagas, Pitsouni, Malietzis, & Pappas, 2008; Gusenbauer & Haddaway, 2020).  16
In the acquisition sub-stage, additional refinement was applied, such as the search period, 17
subject area, document type, and publication stage, to sort out relevant documents in the subject 18
context. We set a time frame from 2005 to September 10th, 2023. Previous review studies in a 19
similar context (i.e., chatbots) have considered ‘2005’ as the starting point (Pérez, Daradoumis, 20
& Puig, 2020) due to the widespread popularity of computer-mediated communication devices 21
since the early 2000s  (de Brito Silva & de Oliveira Campos, 2024) . The initial search string 22
was: (“Avatar Marketing” OR “Avatar -Based Marketing” OR “Human -Computer 23
Interaction”) AND (“Chatbot” OR “Embodied Conversational Agent” OR “Virtual Influencer” 24
OR “Virtual Service Assistant”  OR “Virtual Assistant” OR “Digital Assistant” ). Before 25

10

performing the inclusive search syntax, the opinions of two academic experts in a similar 1
research field were consulted. The search resulted in  1237 documents from Scopus, 1428 2
documents from WoS, and 12541 documents from Google Scholar. 3
 4
2.2 Arranging 5
The arranging stage consisted of two sub-stages: organization and purification (Paul, Lim, et 6
al., 2021).  7
In the organization sub -stage, we applied the author, article title, journal title, theories, 8
contexts, determinants, and methods as organizing codes. Next, we selected the TCCM 9
organizing framework following prior studies (Basu, Paul, & Singh, 2022; A. Kumar, Dhingra, 10
& Falwadiya, 2023; Paul, Merchant, Dwivedi, & Rose, 2021; Paul & Rosado -Serrano, 2019; 11
K. Sharma, Aswal, & Paul, 2023). The TCCM framework enables the review to encompass a 12
wide range of elements within the literature of a specific field (Paul, Lim, et al., 2021). 13
The purification sub -phase involves establishing and applying inclusion and exclusion 14
criteria for publication selection . A two-step screening and evaluating benchmarks w ere 15
considered in this regard (Mishra, Singh, & Koles, 2021; Paul, Lim, et al., 2021). First, drawing 16
on earlier studies (e.g., Khan et al., 2024; Lim, Kumar, Verma, & Chaturvedi, 2022), we refined 17
the publications based on the following subject areas : ‘social sciences’, ‘business, 18
management, and accounting’, ‘economics, econometrics, and finance’, and ‘decision 19
sciences’. These subjects are clearly linked to “marketing and behavior science,” thus 20
excluding ABM research that is purely computational and offers little to no inferences for its 21
application in marketing settings. Only the papers published in the English language were 22
included. This process generated 478 finally published (including conference papers and 23
journal articles) and 67 in-press articles for the next stage. Second, duplicate articles and 24
articles not focusing on ABM were removed based on a full -text review performed by two of 25

11

this study’s authors. This resulted in  57 publications relevant to avatars in the marketing 1
context.   2

2.3 Assessing 4
The assessment also co mprises two sub-stages: evaluation and reporting  (Paul, Lim, et al., 5
2021).  6
The evaluation sub -stage details the methods for analysis and further research agenda.  7
Two of this study’s authors were involved in performing SLR through rigorous content analysis 8
for 57 articles following the TCCM framework and considering this study’s research objectives 9
(ROs 1-4). In doing so, we relied on the methodology employed by Paul, Lim, et al. (2021) and 10
Ghorbani, Karampela, and Tonner (2022)  to filter the articles and establish a sub -dataset, 11
facilitating a manageable and high-quality content analysis. Microsoft Excel was deployed to 12
conduct the content and descriptive analyses. We followed the TCCM framework to guide 13
future research direction.  14
The final sub -stage of the review protocol included reporting on the conventions and 15
limitations of this review. Consistent with previous studies (Ghorbani et al., 2022), this review 16
presents its findings using a combination of figures, tables, and words (narratives). In 17
limitations, this review acknowledged that only peer-reviewed journal articles were reviewed, 18
and non-English articles were excluded.   19

3.1 Who/What is an Avatar? 7
Scholars have used different terminologies to define avatars. Al-Natour, Benbasat, and 8
Cenfetelli (2011)  implied shopping assistants as avatars.  Ho, Hancock, and Miner (2018)  9
affirmed that chatbots with human embodiment can be referred to as avatars. Other studies 10
have noted avatars as virtual customer agents (Verhagen et al., 2014) , digital or virtual 11
assistants (Freeman & Beaver, 2018) , or embodied conversational agents (SeoYoung Lee & 12
Choi, 2017; Schuetzler et al., 2018; Sriwisathiyakun & Dhamanitayakul, 2022). Regardless of 13
all these arguments, the majority of the scholars are in line in defining avatars with inclusive 14
traits, such as anthropomorphic appearance, interactivity, and controlling entity (SeoYoung 15
Lee & Choi, 2017; Miao et al., 2022; Schuetzler et al., 2018; Sriwisathiyakun & 16

13

Dhamanitayakul, 2022). Indeed, many scholars have noted the effectiveness of an avatar’s 1
anthropomorphic appearance (a human -like appearance), playing a crucial role in attracting 2
users’ responses (S.-L. Han & Kim, 2020; Yen & Chiang, 2021) . In addition, research 3
highlights the necessity of avatars’ interactive capability  to foster customer-perceived social 4
presence and active engagement  (Chattaraman, Kwon, Gilbert, & Ross, 2019) .  Miao et al. 5
(2022) surmised that  an avatar is a virtual entity with an anthropomorphic appearance and 6
interactive competence controlled by computer programs or human operators.  7
Sometimes, personas (self -avatar) and avatars seem to be interchanging. Ang, 8
Bobrowicz, Siriaraya, Trickey, and Winspear (2013) argued that the term ‘avatar’ denotes the 9
representation of other individuals as human s in digital interactions. de Brito Silva and de 10
Oliveira Campos (2024) referred to avatars of other people as personas, synonymously known 11
as self -avatars. Notably, the distinction between a persona and an avatar depends on the 12
observer’s point of view. For example, sometimes users may perceive an avatar as their own 13
representation (self-avatar), whereas the same avatar may be perceived as an external entity 14
(avatar) by another user. 15

3.2 Avatar-Based Marketing  17
While studies on virtual avatars, such as chatbots  (Chocarro et al., 2023; Go & Sundar, 2019; 18
Huang et al., 2022; Jenneboer et al., 2022; Jin & Eastin, 2023; Seyoung Lee, Park, & Park, 19
2024; Nicolescu & Tudorache, 2022; Ogilvie et al., 2022; Pérez et al., 2020; H. Shin, Bunosso, 20
& Levine, 2023; Yen & Chiang, 2021; Zhang et al., 2023), virtual shopping agents (Y.-T. Lin, 21
Doong, & Eisingerich, 2021) , embodied conversational agents  (Derrick & Ligon, 2014; 22
Nelekar, Abdulrahman, Gupta, & Richards, 2022; van Pinxteren et al., 2023) , and online 23
assistants (Chattaraman et al., 2019) are proliferating, only a few studies (see de Brito Silva & 24
de Oliveira Campos, 2024; Miao et al., 2022) have defined ‘avatar marketing’ or ‘avatar-based 25
marketing’. The term avatar -based marketing was popularized by Paul Hemp  through his 26

14

article in the Harvard Business Review  (Hemp, 2006). According to de Brito Silva and de 1
Oliveira Campos (2024), avatar marketing started emerging in 2005. Nevertheless, the research 2
domain is fragmented, making it challenging to build a solid foundation for ABM (de Brito 3
Silva & de Oliveira Campos, 2024) . In this regard, Miao et al. (2022) focused on how virtual 4
avatars are emerging as a marketing tool. According to their study, the deployment of avatars 5
in business organizations to create, communicate, and exchange customer value is known as 6
ABM. de Brito Silva and de Oliveira Campos (2024)  opined that avatar s act in multiple 7
marketing roles, e.g., generating customer interest and engagement, increasing consumer 8
responses, reducing uncertainty, and fostering customer confidence.  9
Given that avatars play a vital role as recommenders of products and brands (Y.-T. Lin 10
et al., 2021) , they are becoming increasingly popular in contemporary marketing strategies. 11
Garnier and Poncin (2013) and Gonzales-Chávez and Vila-Lopez (2021) predicted that ABM 12
is likely to be the next -level marketing weapon for organizations . As a result, companies that 13
provide customer services online are investing in virtual avatars to ensure customer 14
engagement and serve customers better (Liew, Tan, & Ismail, 2017). Emergen Research (2023) 15
shows that globally, the human avatar market size was $29.51 billion in 2021 and is expected 16
to register revenue with a CAGR of 34.2% from 2023 to 2032. Based on these estimations, we 17
believe that a large proportion of digital marketing ahead will be centered on avatars; indeed, 18
this, in return , will provide a competitive edge for many firms assisting in sustaining the 19
marketplace in the long run.  20

5.1.2 Social Response Theory 15
Humans cannot be on the internet in person and cannot socially interact as they do in person 16
(Chattaraman et al., 2019; Fernandes & Oliveira, 2021; Sayago, Neves, & Cowan, 2019) . 17
Avatars may help consumers feel social through human -like conversations. Avatars may 18
persuade customers like a human salesperson can do in physical outlets (Miao et al., 2022) . 19
Hereby, Social Response Theory (SRT) denotes that human -like traits of avatars, including 20
voice, language, interaction, and social role, may trigger customers’ social responses even 21
though they understand that machines cannot possess human characteristics (Holzwarth et al., 22
2006; Nass & Moon, 2000) . Hence, they behave socially with avatars. For instance, when an 23
avatar salesperson interacts with customers, it can enhance their perceptions of socialness 24
toward a website and ultimately influence their purchase decisions (Wang & Russo, 2007) . 25
Schuetzler et al. (2018) and Verhagen et al. (2014) stated that human-like avatars elicit more 26

are sensitive and personal (e.g., sexual and personal health). Within such a complex, sensitive, 3
and relational transaction context, intelligent, unrealistic avatars (e.g., MIT's REA) could be 4
practical (Miao et al., 2022). 5

5.1.3 Social Presence Theory 7
Social presence implies the ability of any medium to provide users with a sense of social 8
environment (Hassanein & Head, 2007; Konya-Baumbach, Biller, & von Janda, 2023) . More 9
precisely, the Social Presence Theory ensures the vividness and interactivity of any social 10
stimuli in a communication process  (Hassanein & Head, 2007). D'Mello, Graesser, and King 11
(2010) noted that the anthropomorphic appearance of avatars creates a sense of social warmth 12
among consumers when interacting with these virtual agents . On the contrary, Liew et al. 13
(2017) found that social presence in online stores is lower than in offline stores , where 14
customers have the opportunity to touch, feel , and browse products. Besides, offline stores 15
offer a chance to build a social relationship between sales personnel and customer s based on 16
lively communication (Clark et al., 2019) . This is hardly seen online due to automated and 17
anonymous platforms w ith limited active social contact  (Schuetzler et al., 2018) . Contrarily, 18
many researchers argue that if avatars have human -like traits and behave accordingly, active 19
social contact with customers is possible (Calvaresi et al., 2021; Verhagen et al., 2014). 20

5.1.4 Anthropomorphism Theory 22
Anthropomorphism denotes employing human-like attributes, characteristics, and properties to 23
non-human virtual objects (Epley, Waytz, & Cacioppo, 2007; Khan et al., 2024; Waytz, 24
Cacioppo, & Epley, 2010) . Due to modern technologies like AI and machine learning, many 25
anthropomorphic devices ranging from self-driving cars to voice-activated virtual agents (e.g., 26
Amazon’s Alexa) have emerged (Hoy, 2018; Waytz, Heafner, & Epley, 2014) . Scholars (see 27

21

Aggarwal & McGill, 2007; Wan & Aggarwal, 2015) affirm that human-like entities can easily 1
foster brand rapport with customers and help sustain the marketplace for a long time. 2
Anthropomorphism is believed to create trust among consumers whenever they adopt new 3
technology (H. Han, Kim, & Jeon, 2021; Yen & Chiang, 2021) . H. Han et al. (2021)  also 4
identified that avatars with human-like characters can make online purchasing more interesting. 5
Touré-Tillery and McGill (2015)  contended that anthropomorphic virtual messengers can be 6
more effective than salespeople in certain contexts, such as selling medical goods, where a high 7
level of cognitive intelligence and technical knowledge is required. 8

5.1.5 Social Agency Theory 10
In the digital arena, Social Agency Theory (SAT) postulates that human -like interaction with 11
the users impels a social conversation in return (Liew et al., 2017) . More specifically, 12
computer-assisted avatars can foster building relationships with customers who might take 13
avatars as a social entity (Mayer, Sobko, & Mautone, 2003) . This happens when verbal and 14
social cues are employed in a computer -based environment. Besides, because of the persona 15
and human-like qualities, avatars possessing similar characteristics to humans, such as voice, 16
appearance, and cognitive intelligence, tend to be more widely accepted (Miao et al., 2022) . 17
Atkinson, Mayer, and Merrill (2005)  found that while the relationship between avatars and 18
customers is built as a partnership, customers realize that they interact with any digital entity 19
like a human -to-human interaction. For instance, when an organization utilizes an animated 20
computerized agent with human-like expressions (e.g., gesture, gaze, and facial expression), it 21
quickly boosts confidence and helps build customer rapport (Moon & Nass, 1996). 22

5.1.6 Technology Acceptance Model 24
The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) has been utilized to determine new technology 25
adoption behavior in many contexts. TAM describes consumers’ emotional and cognitive 26
reactions toward adopting new technology (C. H. Lin, Shih, & Sher, 2007). Several empirical 27

22

studies (e.g., T. Bickmore, Vardoulakis, Jack, & Paasche -Orlow, 2013; Butt et al., 2021; 1
Chocarro et al., 2023)  have used TAM to investigate consumers’ interaction with avatars. In 2
the gaming industry, Butt et al. (2021)  claimed that AI-based avatars are employed to assist 3
consumers in feeling more comfortable when obtaining products and services, owing to their 4
ease of use and usefulness . Former studies have concluded that consumers are more inclined 5
to use products and services favorably when they can easily discern their benefits in terms of 6
ease and usefulness (Butt et al., 2021; Mimoun & Poncin, 2015). Hence, academicians suggest 7
that avatars should be user-friendly and offer clear benefits (T. Bickmore et al., 2013; Chocarro 8
et al., 2023). 9
A gamer may face difficulties passing and going to the next level in gaming . An avatar 10
can assist the gamer in progressing through levels by providing interactive support (Butt et al., 11
2021). In addition, Chattaraman et al. (2019)  have found various contexts where avatars can 12
support customers. For example, an online purchase agent can aid older customers in finding 13
athletic shoes, an intelligent conversational agent can provide necessary information to make 14
purchase decisions on a cellular phone (K.-M. Kim, Hong, & Cho, 2007), or an automated tutor 15
can provide students with a learning experience in natural language (text or voice) like a 16
traditional human tutor (D'Mello et al., 2010). These are all instances where different forms of 17
avatars aid customers’ ease and usefulness, which are desired when they face difficulties. 18
However, despite ABM being a technology -driven domain, the TAM model has been 19
relatively less explored. A possible reason for this is that the interaction between consumers 20
and avatars involves nuanced psychological and anthropomorphic factors, such as emotional 21
engagement, trust, and social influence, which TAM might not fully address. 22

5.1.7 Threshold Model of Social Influence 24
According to the Threshold Model of Social Influence (TMSI) (Blascovich et al., 2002) , 25
individuals initially react socially only to other people or, in the context of virtual reality, to 26

23

avatars - fully controlled virtual versions of humans with high behavioral realism. However, 1
they would not react socially to virtual agents (entities not entirely under human control) unless 2
the agent's behavior is so authentic (high behavioral realism) that the user cannot tell the 3
difference between the agent and the avatar. In this regard, Von der Pütten, Krämer, Gratch, 4
and Kang (2010) found that when a situation incorporates social cues (e.g., interactivity and 5
natural speech), people's responses to agents and avatars do not differ significantly, regardless 6
of high or low behavioral realism, suggesting that the TMSI's underlying assumptions are not 7
entirely supported. Drawing on the TMSI, Yokotani et al. (2018) discovered that respondents 8
were more comfortable sharing delicate, sex -related symptoms (e.g., menstruum and body 9
weight) with virtual agents exhibiting lower behavioral realism compared to actual experts.  10

5.1.8 Self-Determination Theory 12
Self-Determination Theory (SDT) differentiates intrinsic and extrinsic motivation  (T. W. 13
Bickmore, Utami, Matsuyama, & Paasche -Orlow, 2016). Intrinsic motivation implies doing 14
something inherently to satisfy oneself, whereas extrinsic motivation is driven by several 15
external stimuli (e.g., money, goods, and services). In modern marketing, SDT is a well -16
accepted theory (Gim, Bae, & Kang, 2022) . Interestingly, gamers like to pay money even if 17
they have a chance of being rejected. This is an example of an intrinsically satisfying behavior. 18
Wong, Yuen, and Li (2015)  identified that intrinsic motivations (e.g., curiosity, a sense of 19
belonging, exploration, autonomy, and competence) act more profoundly than extrinsic 20
motivations (e.g., fame, comments from peers, and power).  21
Indeed, the SDT theory has been widely used in different contexts, such as gaming (Neys, 22
Jansz, & Tan, 2014), robotics (Chen et al., 2017), chatbots (Q. N. Nguyen & Sidorova, 2018), 23
virtual agents (Linares-Garcia, Roofigari-Esfahan, Pratt, & Jeon, 2022) , and avatars  (Fong, 24
Quinlan, & Mar, 2023). In the ABM context, Q. N. Nguyen and Sidorova (2018) aligned users’ 25
acceptance of chatbots with the paradigm of SDT and found that chatbots’ social entities (e.g., 26

24

warm interaction and presence) are designed to meet users’ intrinsic needs. Similarly, Fong et 1
al. (2023) showed how avatars might help students fulfill psychological needs (i.e., perceived 2
warmth) and functional benefits. 3

5.3.2 Consumption Values Generated by Avatars 3
This section classified customer-perceived values derived from avatar interaction drawing on 4
the TCV (Sheth et al., 1991). Value implies a set of concepts or beliefs that drive an individual’s 5
behavior and attitude  (Chakraborty, Siddiqui, & Siddiqui, 2022; Schwartz & Bilsky, 1987) . 6
Customer-perceived value is the difference between the benefits and sacrifices customers make 7
while considering a product (Zeithaml, 1988). Putting it differently, customer values refer to 8
customers’ subjective assessment of the benefits they receive - such as quality and satisfaction 9
- compared to costs and sacrifices  they incur, such as price and time (Tanrikulu, 2021; 10
Zeithaml, 1988). In the TCV, Sheth et al. (1991)  identified five types of values: functional, 11
social, emotional, epistemic, and conditional. The present review draws on TCV (refer to Table 12
4) to categorize the examined drivers, primarily for two reasons. First, delving into 13
consumption values stands out as one of the most effective approaches to understanding why 14
consumers are inclined toward specific goods or services (Sheth et al., 1991). Consequently, it 15
is crucial to grasp the types of customer values that can be collectively generated, as these 16
values largely influence customer choices or behavior s. Secondly, the existing review studies 17
have yet to categorize customer-perceived values through the lens of TCV within the context 18
of ABM. This review is the first of its kind to thoroughly investigate customer value drivers 19
and organize them for better understanding and further investigation (please refer to Table 5 20
and Figure 8 for summary).   21

6. Future research directions (RO 4) 8
Drawing on similar TCCM-based studies (Ghorbani et al., 2022; Harju, 2022; Jebarajakirthy 9
et al., 2021; Roy Bhattacharjee, Pradhan, & Swani, 2022) , future research trajectories are 10
delineated in accordance with the basic premises of ABM literature as follows. 11
6.1 Theories 12
The findings of this review indicate that the existing literature is insufficient to establish a 13
robust theoretical foundation for ABM research. Notably, the absence of conceptual papers 14

45

suggests the gap and opportunity to conceptualize constructs of human -computer interaction-1
based research from the marketing perspective. Moreover, the articles reviewed by this study 2
merely employed theories, models, and frameworks from technology adoption and IS 3
perspectives, neglecting their marketing implications. Therefore, future research is suggested 4
to introduce a broader set of theories,  going beyond the technology adoption and IS settings , 5
to examine how avatars reshape consumers’ perceptions and attitudes to elicit favorable 6
business outcomes (e.g., customer retention and profit maximization). 7
In particular, this review’s findings indicate that most  empirical studies have adopted 8
classic human-computer interaction -based theories (e.g., SRT, SAT , CASA paradigm, and 9
Anthropomorphism Theory ). This entails a narrow and inward perspective of ABM and 10
consumer perceptions. However, more recent and contemporary theoretical perspectives from 11
marketing paradigms signify that ABM literature could benefit from theories like the TCV, 12
Customer Engagement Theory  (CET), SDT, and Uncertainty Reduction Theory (URT) to 13
rethink consumers’ attitudes, lifestyles, and habits in the  spatial dimension. For instance, De 14
Vreede, Raghavan, and De Vreede (2021)  suggest re -examining the SDT to find how 15
consumers respond differently in an AI -supported environment. Another recent review has 16
urged incorporating consumer consumption values in digital marketing research to predict what 17
digital technology attributes reshape consumption values (Tanrikulu, 2021). To address the 18
literature gap in applying TCV within the ABM context, future studies should focus on 19
categorizing customer -perceived values  (e.g., functional, emotional)  derived from different 20
attributes (e.g., appearance, behavior) of ABM through the lens of TCV.  Research further 21
shows that the URT holds significant relevance within the context of ABM (SeoYoung Lee & 22
Choi, 2017; Park et al., 2023) . This theory suggests that the emotional exchange relationship 23
between humans and avatars reduces uncertainty and facilitates  more engaging emotional 24
exchanges, such as fun and entertainment (Park et al., 2023). This emotional engagement aligns 25

46

with the CET, which posits that as customer engagement increases, individuals are more likely 1
to invest additional time and money, recognizing that the adopted technology is designed to 2
fulfill both their functional and hedonic needs (Liu, Lim, Li, Tan, & Cyr, 2020).  3
Importantly, our findings reveal that several theories within the realm of ABM are closely 4
connected to customer-perceived value. For instance, the CASA framework and social 5
response theory highlight how customers may intuitively treat avatars as social entities, 6
triggering perceived social (e.g., perceived social presence) and emotional (e.g., building trust 7
and credibility) value components. Similarly, social presence theory emphasizes how avatars 8
foster emotional values in digital environments, including perceived warmth, social presence, 9
and interaction. Anthropomorphism theory, on the other hand, explains how avatars’ perceived 10
humanness boosts customers’ emotional connection. Moreover, TAM underlines that the 11
implementation of avatars should prioritize perceived ease of use and usefulness to enhance 12
users’ perceptions of functional value. 13
In contrast, the Uncanny Valley Theory advocates that when avatars appear almost 14
human but not quite, they can evoke negative values, such as discomfort, reducing users’ 15
emotional connection and trust. Thus, we reckon it is crucial to thoroughly understand how 16
theories related to ABM influence the perception of customer values, both positively and 17
negatively. We recommend that future research delve deeper into this domain to uncover more 18
nuanced insights into the theories applied and the value generated. 19
Our review also reveals a noteworthy theoretical gap in the interplay between avatar 20
attributes, customer values, and customer responses. This is crucial for unveiling the attribute-21
value-outcome process , as customer-perceived values operate like a ‘black box ’ within the 22
buyer’s mind (Lanzini & Tencati, 2023) . Understanding and diagnosing customer minds and 23
subsequent behavioral outcomes within the context of ABM necessitates further exploration of 24
predictors and attributes to shed in-depth light on this process . Hence, we suggest further 25

47

assessment of avatars’ attributes to see how they impact consumer values. This examination 1
would aid in the development of a potentially new attribute-value-outcome-linked theory. 2
6.2 Context 3
This section outlines future research directions based on multiple contexts.  First, the findings 4
of this review reveal that avatar’s efficiency varies across industries based on their forms (i.e., 5
embodiment and non -embodiment). In this regard, Miao et al. (2022)  argued that embodied 6
avatars (e.g., SK-II’s YUMI) have realistic appearance (i.e., 3D), high cognitive intelligence , 7
and can communicate with a human voice. Hence, the scholars concluded that these avatars are 8
suitable for complex transactions and highly personalized services (i.e., skin  care). On the 9
contrary, avatars, such as Amazon’s Alexa, with no anthropomorphic appearance, can be good 10
at meeting 24/7 customer queries and exploring online content. From the sy nthesis of avatar-11
related studies, this review shows that various forms of avatars are utilized in different 12
industries (e.g., apparel, education, electrical equipment, furniture, gaming, public health, 13
restaurant, retail, shoes, soft goods, travel, and tourism). The findings of this review reveal that 14
the extant literature is inadequate or scattered in providing a holistic view of industry -wise 15
usage of avatars. Hence, f uture research studies on specif ic industries and products need to 16
clearly explain the industrial context of avatar deployment and which forms of avatars are more 17
suitable for what products.  18
Second, ABM needs to be explored from diverse industries and cultural settings, 19
especially in emerg ing economies such as India, Malaysia, and Bangladesh. Importantly, 20
scholars should emphasize Middle Eastern countries (e.g., Saudi Arabia, United Arab Emirates, 21
Kuwait, etc.) considering their high per capita income and substantial opportunities for AI -22
based businesses in these regions . Besides, i t would also be interesting to explore customer 23
perceptions and behavioral intentions toward avatars in LDCs. Although AI exposure in LDCs 24
is lower and growing steadily than in high -income and developed countries (Dlugatch, 25

48

Georgieva, & Kerasidou, 2024), AI-enabled smartphones and easy Internet access might be the 1
potential reason  the ABM could progressively embark on these countries. Besides, cross -2
cultural investigation is needed as e vidence suggests that consumer  demand and attitude s 3
toward avatars are expected to vary across cultures and countries (Jin & Eastin, 2023) . 4
Accordingly, future studies may explore how consumers’ purchasing or service -taking 5
behavior with avatars differs across developed, less developed , and emerging economi es. 6
Besides, the concept s of avatars and ABM are still emerging in developed and developing 7
countries (Miao et al., 2022). To this end, we urge future research to examine how consumers 8
perceive avatars, especially consumers who fear augmented technologies (e.g., AI) due to 9
perceived risk and uncertainty in developing countries. 10
Third, existing research highlights that customer value perception is subject to variation 11
among individuals and across different products (Sheth et al., 1991) . Customers who are 12
satisfied with a product or service today may become dissatisfied in the future. This 13
phenomenon is also relevant in the realm of human -computer interaction, where the role of 14
avatars is crucial (Garnier & Poncin, 2013; S. Kim et al., 2016). However, the current literature 15
on ABM lacks a thorough discussion on how avatars can effectively persuade dissatisfied and 16
frustrated consumers and what service recovery strategies could be implemented if avatars fail 17
in their intended purpose.  18
6.3 Characteristics 19
This section outlines several key avenues for future research in terms of attributes, dimensions, 20
determinants, and consequences to extend our knowledge and understanding of the 21
characteristics of avatars and ABM.  22
First, the review synthesizes avatars’ attributes from two perspectives (Table 3), namely 23
appearance and behavior, to show  how these anthropomorphic and humanoid drivers  affect 24
customers’ diverse value sets and move toward behavioral outputs  (e.g., use intention, 25

49

satisfaction, attitudes, purchase intention, patronage intention ). With humanoid embodiment 1
(gender, voice, and hair) and cognitive intelligence, avatars are replacing the role of frontline 2
employees (Epley et al., 2007; Waytz et al., 2010) . Considering the importance of these key 3
avatar dimensions, this rev iew unveils that there is a pa ucity of extant literature about what 4
dimensions of avatars’ anthropomorphic appearances  (e.g., facial expression ) can evoke 5
customers’ anthropomorphic expectations (Miao et al., 2022). For instance, users may expect 6
avatars they are interacting with to be realistic, cute, and intelligent. Hence, we recommend 7
future research to investigate different dimensions of avatars’ anthropomorphic appearances  8
and how those dimensions effectively satisfy consumers’ expectations.  9
Second, we have identified that many avatars  have no human appearance  (e.g., Alexa, 10
Cortana, SILVIA) but possess anthropomorphic traits , such as name, gender, and cognitive 11
intelligence like humans. Drawing on the findings of this review, we have seen scattered and 12
isolated literature of ABM that compares th ese digital assistant s, herein avatars, with 13
anthropomorphic avatars. To address this gap,  further study  is suggested to determine  if a 14
virtual agent without an anthropomorphic appearance (e.g., Alexa and Cortana) can perform 15
better or worse than an avatar with anthropomorphic entities (e.g., SK-II’s YUMI).  16
Third, according to this review, very few studies have shed light on avatar attributes and 17
the corresponding value aspects. It is important to note that avatar attributes significantly 18
impact customers’ perceived values (Butt et al., 2021; S. Kim et al., 2016) . Many previous 19
studies (Butt et al., 2021; J. Lin, Guo, Turel, & Liu, 2020; Tanrikulu, 2021)  have emphasized 20
exploring consumers’ value perceptions in a digital environment. This review’s findings show 21
that most studies (e.g., Bialkova, 2023; Butt et al., 2021; Y. -T. Lin et al., 2021; Nowak et al., 22
2015; Sriwisathiyakun & Dhamanitayakul, 2022; Van Pinxteren et al., 2019; Yim, 2023) have 23
investigated functional, social, and emotional value dimensions, while little is known about the 24
epistemic and conditional values  of avatars (Table 5). Among the reviewed studies, no study 25

50

has elaborately and comprehensively illustrated the epistemic values of using avatars by 1
consumers. These findings are novel and indicate  research gaps that could be filled by  2
conducting future research on  epistemic values (e.g., novelty, curiosity, and knowledge)  3
derived from avatar attributes. On the other hand, a few studies (Noble et al., 2022; Zhang et 4
al., 2023) have emphasized incorporating conditional values (e.g., time, place, and occasion) 5
in the digital context. For instance, Noble et al. (2022) have shown how the interaction between 6
customers and different virtual agents (i.e., chatbots) was intensified during the C OVID-19 7
pandemic due to social distancing and other health protocols. However, it is crucial to uncover 8
how epistemic and conditional values strengthen avatar ado ption and post-adoption behavior 9
of consumers from marketing aspects , as  these values are the initial antecedents affect ing 10
consumers' decision-making in product trials  (Sweeney et al., 1996) . Moreover, our review 11
revealed 21 different labels (Figure 3) used in ABM. This opens avenues for future research to 12
look into customer value aspects from the perspective of different labels used in ABM. 13
Finally, this review shows that many studies have focused on user adoption -related 14
factors like usage and adoption intention (Go & Sundar, 2019; SeoYoung Lee & Choi, 2017; 15
Qiu & Benbasat, 2009) . However, scant attention has been paid to consumer and marketing-16
related factors, such as customer engagement, enjoyment, satisfaction, word of mouth, brand 17
recommendation, and purchase behavior (Crolic et al., 2022; Gonzales-Chávez & Vila-Lopez, 18
2021; Yim, 2023) . Notably, it is not yet evident in the avatar literature to what extent AI -19
augmented avatars foster positive (or negative) attitudes and trigger relevant outcomes (e.g., 20
product or service purchasing or rejection). These factors may provide more comprehensive 21
insights to predict customer behavior and formulate customer -centered strategies from 22
marketers’ points of view. 23
6.4 Methods 24
This review reveals that the leading methods utilized for research on ABM are quantita tive, 25

51

including experimental and cross -sectional designs. The findings of this review have shown 1
that there is no longitudinal research, a type of correlational research that typically entails 2
observing subjects over an extended period, in contrast to a cross-sectional study, which 3
gathers data at a single point in time (Rindfleisch, Malter, Ganesan, & Moorman, 2008). In the 4
context of ABM, it is crucial to assess avatar users’ behavioral changes in usage, satisfaction, 5
awareness, loyalty, and trust to adjust marketing strategies over time. Hence, there is enough 6
scope for future researchers to use longitudinal research in the ABM domain. 7
The findings also show that most researchers employed a primary survey, preferably on 8
young customers. Future studies are recommended to be conducted on other age groups (e.g., 9
mid or older) to identify the differences in response and perception that vary with age. Besides, 10
we have found that many researchers considered different forms of avatars (static vs. animated) 11
to collect data, primarily for experimental research. In further research, avatars and customers 12
can be simultaneously used as samples, providing a more holistic understanding of how avatar 13
reflects on customer response. 14
Although many investigations have used qualitative and experimental approaches, 15
mixed-method approaches have rarely been used. Research demonstrates that mixed methods 16
provide a clear understanding of the contexts (the what), conceptualizations, norms, and values 17
(the why or how) in a single research question, combining two different approaches (qualitative 18
and quantitative) (Harrison III, 2013) . Thus, we suggest using qualitative and quantitative 19
research tools to better understand the context of ABM across countries. Additionally, there is 20
a scope for conducting empirical research through a hybrid analytical approach (e.g., 21
integration of PLS -SEM-ANN-fsQCA). Integrating multiple analytical approaches enriches  22
the rigor and depth of examining a particular research domain. In the context of ABM, this may 23
allow researchers to detail and nuance the interpretation of their findings and draw conclusions 24
for further study.  25

7. Implications and Conclusions 2
7.1 Key Implications 3
This review offers valuable practical insights for marketers and industry practitioners.  4

54

First, avatar deployment in the ABM context has proven to be crucial for marketers due to 1
its ability to yield various functional values  for customers. Expertise, a dominant functional 2
value yield from avatars, is deemed quite indispensable for marketers as it enhances customer 3
perception of other functional benefits (e.g., customization, convenience, and personalization). 4
In case of sensitive and complex information, for instance, expert avatars can answer detailed 5
inquiries and provide personalized recommendations (Miao et al., 2022), reducing customers’ 6
time and effort costs/sacrifices. Similarly, marketers and industry practitioners should prioritize 7
enhancing customers' perceived ease of use and usefulness of avatars. When customers find 8
avatars intuitive to interact with and view as beneficial, they are more inclined to engage with 9
a particular brand or firm’s website (Chattaraman et al., 2019; Van Pinxteren et al., 2020). This 10
increased engagement can lead to higher customer traffic and improved conversion rates. 11
Second, the social values derived from avatars are also pivotal for marketers and industry 12
practitioners to effectively engage customers in active interactions with avatars. By attributing 13
human-like characteristics (e.g., humanly interaction skills), they can create more relatable and 14
personable social interactions between customers and avatars (Schuetzler et al., 2018; van 15
Pinxteren et al., 2023). This can enhance customers’ sense of social presence, which fosters a 16
deeper social bonding in human-to-avatar interaction (Liew et al., 2017; Verhagen et al., 2014). 17
In such cases, marketers and avatar designers should  embody aspirational social traits or  18
attributes with avatars to  encourage customers to align their behaviors and preferences with 19
those represented by the avatars.  20
Third, our review shows that the emotional values related to the ABM context are well-21
explored in academia. It suggests marketers should actively highlight the emotional values 22
avatars yield while communicating with customers . By leveraging anthropomorphic design 23
elements, such as facial expressions, gestures, and voice, into avatars, marketers and designers 24
can encourage customers to perceive avatars as more  credible and friendly (Keeling et al., 25

55

2010; Liew et al., 2017).  For instance, Brave et al. (2005) found that embodied conversational 1
agents’ likeability and trustworthiness evoke greater perceived caring and felt support. This is 2
reflected in customers’ behavioral outcomes. For example, Yim (2023) noted that interactivity 3
in chatbots garners users’ perception of empathy, lightening customer anger and lowering 4
negative word-of-mouth intentions. 5
Fourth, although the current review presents epistemic values still latent in the extant 6
ABM literature, avatars can be crucial in enhancing the learning experience and satisfying 7
customers' curiosity and desire for knowledge. Especially for new customers, avatars’  8
personalized, real-time information and feedback can make complex product or service content 9
more accessible and easier to understand.  This could help marketers convert new customers 10
into loyal ones.  11
Fifth, the findings of this review show that trust and privacy are the two fundamental 12
conditional value aspects that can guide a successful avatar deployment. Research indicates 13
that conditional values directly influence customer adoption and post-adoption behaviors (e.g., 14
enjoyment and purchase intention) (Masri, Ruangkanjanases, & Chen, 2020; Song & Shin, 15
2024; Van Pinxteren et al., 2019) . Building trust, in particular, is  crucial because customers 16
need to believe that the avatar s will provide accurate and reliable information, maintain 17
confidentiality, and act in their best interest. It may involve designing avatars with transparent 18
communication, consistent behavior, and a clear demonstration of expertise. Privacy is equally 19
important, as customers are more likely to engage with avatars if they feel their personal 20
information is secure and handled respectfully (Youn, Hwang, Zhao, & Kim, 2023; Zhang et 21
al., 2023) . Ensuring robust data protection measures and giving users control over their 22
information can increase their comfort, ease anxieties related to data privacy (sources of 23
potential costs or sacrifices), and enhance their willingness to engage with avatars. 24

56

The above discussions clearly signify the practical marketing implications of this 1
review’s findings, suggesting that effective deployment of avatars can lead to enhanced 2
customer engagement by providing meaningful interactions that meet multiple functional, 3
social, emotional, and epistemic needs of the consumers while ensuring trust and privacy. This 4
enhanced customer engagement and trust can foster long -term relationships with the brand, 5
resulting in strong brand loyalty.  6
7.2 Concluding Remarks 7
This review investigates and synthesizes avatar and ABM corpus from customer value aspects 8
using the TCCM framework. It addressed four primary research objectives: (1) elucidating the 9
concepts of avatars and ABM, (2) examining the progression of ABM research in terms of (a) 10
theoretical frameworks, (b) research contexts, (c) characteristics—mainly focusing on avatars’ 11
features and value -based aspects —and (d) methodologies, (3) proposing a conceptual 12
framework drawing on the findings of 2c, and (4) identifying future research directions to 13
enhance the understanding and application of ABM. To do so, we reviewed a total of 57 14
academic publications published since 2005. The output of the publication trends revealed a 15
rising trajectory since the 2020s. We attribute this to the increased use of technology in 16
consumer interactions necessitated by social distancing during the COVID -19 pandemic. In 17
fact, the trend is upward in the post -pandemic era due to firms’ widespread deployment of 18
avatars (chatbots, conversational agents, and service agents) by businesses, advancing the field 19
of research under review.  Besides, this review conceptualizes avatars and ABM through the 20
lens of marketing and value perspective, which is still lacking in the extant literature.  In this 21
respect, we propose an inclusive ABM definition drawing on this study’s findings and 22
discussions: ABM is the strategic approach of creating and deploying virtual characters, or 23
avatars, with varying levels of form and behavior in an online environment to enhance 24
consumer engagement and drive marketing outcomes by leveraging various customer values 25

57

- such as functional, social, emotional, epistemic, and conditional values. 1
Furthermore, using the TCCM framework, we conducted an in-depth content analysis to 2
evaluate dominant theories, emerging contexts, key characteristics (values derived from 3
avatars), and popular research methods from the most cited, recent, and highly ranked journal 4
articles on avatars and ABM. Our study explicitly underlined the independent, moderating, 5
mediating, and outcome variables grounded in customer value dimensions, including 6
functional, social, emotional, epistemic, and conditional values. Lastly, we draw future  7
research trajectories, followed by critical insights and implications for marketing practitioners. 8
Nonetheless, this study has some limitations. First, though we have thoroughly elucidated 9
the consumption values generated by avatars, future researchers could extend this inquiry by 10
investigating which types of avatars (e.g., virtual/digital assistants, chatbots, autonomous 11
agents) are most effective in generating specific customer values (e.g., social, emotional). 12
Second, we used three major databases (i.e., WoS, Scopus, and Google Scholar); however, to 13
add more rigor, future research endeavors may use other databases, including PsycINFO and 14
EBSCO. Third, this review only considered the articles written in English and published 15
between 2005 and 202 3, meaning relevant articles written in other languages may have been 16
excluded. Fifth, excluding manuscripts under review or still in progress overlooks a specific 17
portion of the research landscape regarding ABM. As the domain continues to evolve, there 18
remains ample opportunity for additional seminal works to advance our understanding further.  19
