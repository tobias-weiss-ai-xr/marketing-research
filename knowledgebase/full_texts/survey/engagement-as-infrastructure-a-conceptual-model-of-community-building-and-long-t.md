Abstract
Digital content channels, including video platforms, podcasts, newsletters, and creator led
communities, present a persistent puzzle. Many achieve rapid early reach yet fail to endure, while
others sustain participation and production across years despite comparatively modest scale.
Prevailing accounts treat engagement as an outcome variable, a metric of attenti on to be
maximized, or a marketing construct subordinate to reach and conversion. This paper advances
an alternative conceptualization: engagement as infrastructure. Drawing on infrastructure
studies, social capital theory, and commons governance scholarship, it argues that engagement is
best understood not as a momentary signal but as the relational, technical, normative, and
economic substrate that allows a channel to persist. The paper develops a layered conceptual
model in which engagement infrastructure comprises four interdependent layers and is sustained
through ongoing maintenance and repair rather than through episodic growth. It theorizes long
term sustainability as a composite of continuity, resilience, regeneration, and economic viability,
and it  derives seven propositions linking infrastructural depth, distributed relational ties,
maintenance labor, platform dependency, reciprocity, commons governance, and onboarding to
sustainability outcomes. The model reframes virality as a stress on infrastru cture rather than a
measure of health, repositions invisible maintenance labor as a central activity, and clarifies why
platform native engagement can simultaneously accelerate growth and erode durability.
Implications for creators, platform designers, and  researchers are discussed, and an agenda for
empirical validation is outlined.

Keywords: audience engagement, digital content channels, creator economy, online community,
infrastructure, sustainability, social capital, commons governance

1. Introduction
The economics and culture of media production have shifted decisively toward channels owned
and operated by individuals, small teams, and loosely bounded communities. A single creator with
a camera, a newsletter writer with a mailing list, or a moderator st ewarding a discussion forum
now occupies a structural position once reserved for institutions with substantial capital. This
redistribution of productive capacity has been widely celebrated and widely studied, yet it has also
surfaced a problem that the celebratory literature tends to understate. Reach is comparatively easy
to acquire and notoriously difficult to keep. A piece of content can travel to millions through
algorithmic amplification and then leave behind almost nothing durable, while a quieter ch annel
with a fraction of that reach can sustain consistent production and participation for a decade. The

question this paper takes up is why some digital content channels endure and others do not, and
what conceptual vocabulary best captures the difference.
The dominant answer in both practitioner discourse and a large body of research is engagement.
Channels are advised to maximize engagement, platforms optimize for it, and scholars
operationalize it as likes, comments, shares, watch time, and related behavioral traces. Engagement
in this usage is an outcome and a metric. It is something a channel produces and something an
analyst counts. This paper does not dispute that engagement can be measured, nor that the
measures carry information. It arg ues instead that the metric framing obscures what engagement
does in a sustainable channel. When engagement is treated only as an output, the analyst is
positioned to ask how to generate more of it. When engagement is treated as infrastructure, a
different and arguably more important question becomes available: what does engagement
support, and what is required to keep it functioning over time.
The central claim of this paper is that engagement, in channels that endure, operates as
infrastructure. It is the largely invisible substrate of relationships, technical arrangements, shared
norms, and value flows on which continued production and participation depend. Like other
infrastructures, it tends to disappear into the background when it works, becomes visible primarily
when it breaks, is built incrementally on an installed base, and requires continual maintenance that
is easy to neglect because its benefits are diffuse and its labor is unglamorous (Star & Ruhleder,
1996; Star, 1999). Reframing engagement this  way reorganizes the field of explanation. Virality
is no longer a measure of health but an episode of stress that infrastructure may or may not be able
to absorb. The work of moderation, ritual facilitation, and newcomer onboarding is no longer
peripheral housekeeping but the core maintenance activity that keeps the system running.
Dependence on platform supplied engagement is no longer an unambiguous asset but a form of
infrastructural reliance with its own risks.
This paper makes three contributions. Fir st, it reconceptualizes engagement for digital content
channels by importing the analytic apparatus of infrastructure studies, social capital theory, and
commons governance, moving the construct from the language of outputs to the language of
substrate and  maintenance. Second, it develops a layered conceptual model that specifies the
components of engagement infrastructure, the processes that sustain it, and the dimensions of
sustainability it produces. Third, it derives a set of testable propositions that translate the model
into claims amenable to empirical scrutiny, providing a bridge from conceptual reframing to a
research program. The paper is conceptual in method. It does not present new empirical data, and
its propositions are offered as hypotheses for future testing rather than as established findings.
The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 frames the sustainability problem and
distinguishes durability from reach. Section 3 reviews the theoretical foundations on which the
model draws. Section 4 develops the core conceptualization of engagement as infrastructure and
maps the infrastructural properties of engagement. Section 5 presents the layered model, the
maintenance and repair processes that drive it, the composite construct of sus tainability, and the
propositions. Section 6 discusses theoretical and practical implications, including the platform
dependency paradox. Section 7 addresses limitations and outlines future research, and Section 8
concludes.

2. The Sustainability Problem in Digital Content Channels
A digital content channel is here defined as a persistent, identity bearing locus of recurring content
production through which a producer or producing collective addresses an audience and through
which that audience can respond and interact. The definition is deliberately broad. It encompasses

a creator's video presence, a serialized podcast, an email newsletter, a streaming channel, and a

rather than the affordances of any single platform. What unite s them is that each depends, for its
continued existence, on a recurring relationship between a producer and an audience that is
mediated by technology and sustained, or not, over time.
The sustainability problem becomes visible once durability is distinguished from reach. Reach is
the size of the audience a channel can address in a given period. Durability is the channel's capacity
to keep producing and keep an audience participating acr oss many periods. These are frequently
conflated because growth in reach is the most legible signal available to creators and platforms
alike, surfaced through the dashboards on which performance is routinely monitored (Sanni &
Atima, 2021b), and beca use platform recommendation systems reward content that travels (van
Dijck, 2013; Bucher, 2018). Yet the two can diverge sharply. A channel may acquire enormous
reach through a single amplified item and then find that almost none of the arriving audience
returns, because nothing in the encounter established the relational, normative, or technical
conditions for a continued relationship. Techniques for audience segmentation and forecasting
offer one means of distinguishing the arriving audiences that are lik ely to return from those that
are not (Basnet et al., 2021). Conversely, a channel may grow slowly while accumulating exactly
those conditions, so that its audience compounds rather than churns.
Three recurring threats to durability motivate the analysis. The first is audience churn. Attention
acquired through amplification is weakly attached, and weakly attached audiences leave at the first
reduction in novelty or the first competing claim on their time. The second is producer exhaustion.
Recurring production is labor, and when that labor is sustained only by the volatile reward of
fluctuating reach, it is prone to the burnout that ends many channels regardless of their nominal
audience size (Cunningham & Craig, 2019). The third is platform volatility. Channels that depend
on a platform's distribution are exposed to changes in that platform's recommendation logic,
monetization terms, and policy, none of which they control (Nieborg & Poell, 2018; Poell et al.,
2019). Each threat names a way that a channel with adequate reach can nonetheless fail to endure,
which is precisely the divergence that a metric centered account of engagement struggles to
explain. The limitation of the metric framing can now be stated precisely. If engagement is the
count of interactions a channel produces, then a viral item with millions of fleeting interactions
registers as a triumph, even when it leaves the channel no more durabl e than before. The framing
has no natural place for the difference between an interaction that builds the conditions for future
interaction and one that does not. It treats all engagement as fungible, when the central fact about
durable channels is that their engagement is not fungible at all. It is patterned, accumulated, and
load bearing. Capturing that qual ity requires a vocabulary in which engagement is not the score
but the structure. The following section assembles that vocabulary.

3. Theoretical Foundations
The model developed in this paper draws on four streams of scholarship: the reconceptualization
of engagement as relation rather than metric, infrastructure studies, social capital and community
theory, and commons governance with its associated literature  on maintenance and repair. Each
contributes a component of the eventual model, and together they supply the warrant for treating
engagement as infrastructure.

3.1 From Engagement as Metric to Engagement as Relation
Engagement has been theorized across marketing, communication, and information systems, and
the more careful treatments already resist the reduction to a behavioral count. In the marketing
literature, customer engagement has been defined as a psychological state arising from interactive
experiences and characterized by cognitive, emotional, and behavioral dimensions rather than by
any single observable action (Brodie et al., 2011; Hollebeek, 2011; Vivek et al., 2012).
Engagement in this richer sense is a relationship-oriented construct that exceeds the moment of
transaction and extends to the disposition to participate, contribute, and advocate (Calder et al.,
2009; Hennig -Thurau et al., 2004). These accounts establish that engagement has depth and
duration, that it is more th an the visible trace, and that its value lies in what it indicates about an
ongoing relationship. Communication research adds the insight that the relationship between a
media producer and an audience member is itself a meaningful social bond. The concept of
parasocial interaction describes the one-sided intimacy that audiences develop toward media
figures, an intimacy that can be cultivated, sustained, and felt as genuine connection (Horton &
Wohl, 1956). In the contemporary creator context, this bond is no longer strictly one sided, because
the channels through which creators reach audiences also allow audiences to reach back, and the
resulting connection is a central asset of the creator's practice (Baym, 2018; Marwick, 2013).
Engagement, on this view, is partly the accumulation and maintenance of such bonds. What the
present paper adds is the observation that these relat ional treatments, while they correctly locate
engagement in relationship rather than in metric, stop short of theorizing engagement as a structure
that supports a channel's persistence. They describe the bond without describing the load it bears.

3.2 Infrastructure and the Infrastructural Inversion
Infrastructure studies provide the analytic move at the center of this paper. Infrastructure, in this
tradition, is not simply the visible apparatus of pipes and wires but a relational property of systems
in use. Something becomes infrastructure when it sinks into the background of practice, is taken
for granted by those who rely on it, and is noticed chiefly when it fails (Star & Ruhleder, 1996;
Star, 1999). Star identifies a set of properties that characterize i nfrastructure: it is embedded in
other structures, transparent in the sense that it does not have to be reinvented for each use, has
reach beyond a single event or site, is learned as part of becoming a member of a community of
practice, is linked with the conventions of that practice, becomes visible upon breakdown, is built
on an installed base rather than from scratch, and is fixed in modular increments rather than all at
once (Star, 1999; Star & Ruhleder, 1996).
Two further ideas from this tradition matter here. The first is the infrastructural inversion, the
analytic gesture of bringing the taken for granted background into the foreground of attention,
treating the substrate as the object of study rather than the  medium through which other objects
are s tudied (Bowker & Star, 1999). Applied to digital content channels, the inversion directs
attention away from individual pieces of content and their performance and toward the substrate
that lets content production continue. The second is the recognition th at infrastructure is not self-
sustaining. It persists only through work, and much of that work is the unglamorous, often invisible
labor of maintenance and repair (Graham & Thrift, 2007; Jackson, 2014). These ideas, developed
for information systems and la rge technical systems, transfer with surprising directness to the
problem of why content channels endure, because the thing that endures in a durable channel has
exactly the character of infrastructure: it is in the background, it is relied upon, it is bui lt
incrementally, and it is noticed when it breaks.

3.3 Social Capital and Community
If engagement infrastructure is partly relational, social capital theory specifies what that relational
substrate consists of and how it generates value. Social capital refers to the resources embedded in
and accessible through networks of relationships (Bourdieu, 1986; Coleman, 1988; Lin, 2001). A
widely used formulation distinguishes structural, relational, and cognitive dimensions: the
configuration of ties, the quality of those ties including trust and reciprocity, and the shared
representations and norms that allow parties to make sense of one another (Nahapiet & Ghoshal,
1998). The structure of ties matters in a specific way that bears on channel sustainability. Networks
rich in strong, redundant ties produce cohesion and support, while networks that also contain
weaker bridging ties produce reach and renewal (Granovetter, 1973; Putnam, 2000). A channel
whose relational substrate is concentrated entirely in the bond bet ween the producer and each
isolated audience member has a different and more fragile structure than one in which audience
members are also tied to one another.
Research on online communities translates these ideas into the specific conditions of mediated
participation. A sense of community arises from membership, influence, integration and
fulfillment of needs, and shared emotional connection (McMillan & Chavis, 1986), and these can
be experienced in virtual settings under the right conditions (Blanchard & Markus, 2004; Preece,
2001). Communities cohere through two distinct mechanisms, identity-based attachment to the
group as a whole and bond-based attachment to specific other members, each of which is fostered
by different design choices and each of which has different implications for resilience (Ren et al.,
2007). Brand and consumption communities demonstrate that such attachment can sustain
collective participation and even collective production over long periods (Algesheimer et al., 2005;
Kozinets, 1999; Muniz & O'Guinn, 2001), and brand positioning frameworks treat the resulting
community as a form of brand equity (Sanni et al., 2020b). The communities of practice tradition
supply the final piece: belonging is something newcomers learn through legitimate participation
that moves them from the periphery toward the core, which makes onboarding a structural rather
than incidental feature of any durable community (Wenger, 1998).

3.4 Commons, Governance, Maintenance, and Repair
The fourth stream addresses the dynamics of sustaining a shared resource over time. Engagement
in a content channel has the character of a common pool resource. It is produced collectively, it
can be drawn upon by the producer to fund attention, revenue, or advocacy, and it can be depleted.
A community's willingness to participate, comment, defend, and contribute is finite and
exhaustible, and it can be degraded by extraction that outpaces replenishment, the dynamic
classically described as the tragedy of the commons (Hardin, 1968). Th e crucial corrective from
commons scholarship is that such tragedies are not inevitable. Communities routinely govern
shared resources sustainably through institutions that define boundaries, match appropriation to
local conditions, enable collective choice, monitor use, and graduate sanctions for over extraction
(Ostrom, 1990). Governance, in other words, is the mechanism that converts a depletable shared
resource into a renewable one.
Maintenance and repair scholarship complete the picture by characterizing the work that sustaining
infrastructure actually requires. This literature insists that the default condition of any complex
sociotechnical arrangement is decay, and that continuity is an achievement of ongoing, distributed,
and frequently invisible la bor rather than a property of robust initial design (Graham & Thrift,
2007; Jackson, 2014). Repair is not a deviation from normal functioning but a constitutive part of
how functioning is produced. For content channels, this reframes a wide range of activi ties,

4. Engagement as Infrastructure: Conceptual Development
4.1 Definition
Engagement infrastructure is defined here as the accumulated and interdependent substrate of
relational ties, technical arrangements, shared norms, and value flows thro ugh which a digital
content channel sustains recurring production and participation over time. The definition has
several deliberate features. It is relational and structural rather than behavioral, locating
engagement in an arrangement rather than in any observable interaction. It is accumulated,
meaning that it is built incrementally on an installed base and cannot be conjured at the moment it
is needed. It is interdependent, meaning that its components support one another and that failure
can propagate across them. And it is oriented to persistence, defining engagement by reference to
what it allows a channel to keep doing rather than by reference to any momentary signal. On this
definition, observable interactions such as comments and shares are not enga gement itself but
traces that an underlying infrastructure has been used, in the same way that traffic on a road is
evidence of, but not identical to, the road.

4.2 The Infrastructural Properties of Engagement
The claim that engagement operates as infrastructure can be assessed against the properties that
characterize infrastructure in Star's account (Star, 1999; Star & Ruhleder, 1996). Engagement in a
durable channel is embedded, in that it is woven into the te chnical platform, the producer's
routines, and the audience's habits rather than standing apart from them. It is transparent in use, in
that established participants do not reinvent the terms of their participation for each encounter but
draw on settled conventions. It has reach beyond any single piec e of content, persisting across
items and across time. It is learned as part of membership, since newcomers acquire the channel's
norms and practices through participation rather than receiving them ready made. It is linked with
conventions of practice, encoding the shared expectations that govern how participation proceeds.
It is built on an installed base, since each new interaction occurs against the accumulated history
of prior interaction and is shaped by it. And it is repaired in modular increments th rough the
continual maintenance work described above rather than rebuilt wholesale.
The property with the greatest analytic leverage is that infrastructure becomes visible upon
breakdown. Engagement infrastructure is typically invisible to participants and even to producers
precisely because it works, which is why it is so readily mistaken for the metrics that are its visible
traces. Its existence and importance become apparent at moments of failure: when a trusted
moderator departs and conflict that was pr eviously contained begins to spread, when a format
change disorients a community whose participation depended on settled conventions, when a
platform alteration severs the technical layer on which distribution relied, or when over extraction
exhausts a com munity's goodwill and participation collapses. These breakdowns reveal that
something was being relied upon all along, and that the something was not the count of interactions
but the structure that produced them. This is the diagnostic signature of infras tructure, and
engagement in durable channels exhibits it.

5. A Layered Conceptual Model
The model comprises three elements: a layered architecture that specifies the components of
engagement infrastructure, a set of maintenance and repair processes that sustain that architecture,
and a composite construct of sustainability that the architecture, when maintained, produces. The
three elements are linked by propositions stated at the end of the section. The logic of the model
is that the layered infrastructure is the load bearing structure, maintenance is the work that keeps
it standing, and sustainability is the outcome that results when the structure is kept standing under
the recurring stresses to which content channels are subject.

5.1 The Four Layers of Engagement Infrastructure
Engagement infrastructure is theorized as comprising four interdependent layers. The technical
layer consists of the platform affordances, distribution mechanisms, notification systems, and data
flows through which content reaches audiences and audiences respond. Real-time analytics of live
engagement surfaces such as streaming illustrate how densely instrumented this layer has become
(Basnet et al., 2023). It is the most visible layer and the one over which the channel typically has
least control, since it  is largely supplied by platforms (Gibson, 1979; Hutchby, 2001; van Dijck,
2013). The relational layer consists of the ties that bind participants: the bond between producer
and audience and, critically, the ties among audience members themselves. Its stru cture, dense or
sparse, centralized or distributed, determines much about the channel's resilience and renewal
(Granovetter, 1973; Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998; Ren et al., 2007). The normative layer consists of
the shared norms, rituals, identity, and conventions that make participation legible and meaningful
and that newcomers learn as they move from periphery to core (McMillan & Chavis, 1986;
Wenger, 1998). The economic layer consists of the flows of value, monetary and non-monetary,
that fund continued production and reward continued participation, including the reciprocity that
sustains contribution in the absence of direct payment (Hennig-Thurau et al., 2004; Ostrom, 1990).
The layers are interdependent in specific ways. The technical layer conditions but does not
determine the relational layer, since the same affordances can support concentrated or distributed
ties depending on how they are used. The relational layer carries the normative layer, since norms
are transmitted and enforced through relationship s. The normative layer regulates the economic
layer, since shared expectations govern what forms of value extraction a community will tolerate.
And the economic layer feeds back to the technical and relational layers by funding the production
and maintenance that keep them functioning. Failure can propagate across the layers in either
direction. A technical disruption can sever relationships, an erosion of norms can poison the
economic layer by making extraction feel like betrayal, and an economic collapse can end the
production around which the other layers are organized. Table 1 summarizes the layers, their
components, and their characteristic failure modes.

5.2 Maintenance and Repair as the Engine of Sustainability
A layered infrastructure, however well designed, decays without work. The model therefore places
maintenance and repair at its center as the processes that keep the four layers functioning under
recurring stress (Graham & Thrift, 2007; Jacks on, 2014). Four maintenance processes are
distinguished. Moderation maintains the normative and relational layers by containing conflict,
enforcing norms, and protecting the conditions under which participation feels safe (Gillespie,
2018). Ritual facilita tion maintains the normative layer by reproducing the recurring practices
through which shared identity and emotional connection are renewed. Onboarding maintains the
relational and normative layers by moving newcomers from peripheral to fuller participati on,
replenishing membership as inevitable attrition removes existing participants (Wenger, 1998);
frameworks for funnel optimization and the diagnosis of revenue leakage examine the same drop
off dynamics from the standpoint of conversion (Sanni & Wedraogo , 2024). Reciprocity
cultivation maintains the economic layer by sustaining the give and take that converts episodic
attention into durable commitment and by signaling that the community's contribution is
recognized and returned rather than merely extracted (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998; Ostrom, 1990).
Two features of this maintenance work deserve emphasis. First, it is systematically invisible. Its
successful performance produces nonevents, the conflicts that did not escalate and the newcomers
who did not leave, and nonevents are difficult to perceive and easy to undervalue (Star, 1999).
This invisibility creates a structural tendency toward underinvestment, since the labor whose
payoff is the absence of breakdown competes for attention and resources with the  production
whose payoff is visible reach. Process oriented analysis of the marketing automation lifecycle
offers one route to rendering this otherwise invisible maintenance work legible (Sanni et al., 2025).
Second, maintenance is governance. The processe s that sustain the engagement commons are
precisely the boundary setting, monitoring, and sanctioning institutions that commons scholarship
identifies as the means by which shared resources are kept renewable (Ostrom, 1990). A channel
that performs this ma intenance well governs its engagement as a renewable resource; a channel
that neglects it allows the resource to be depleted.

5.3 Sustainability as a Composite Outcome
Long term sustainability is theorized not as a single quantity but as a composite of four dimensions,
each corresponding to a way that a channel can endure or fail. Continuity is the ongoing capacity
to produce content and to attract participation across s uccessive periods, the baseline sense in
which a channel persists. Resilience is the capacity to absorb exogenous shocks, such as a change
in platform recommendation or monetization, a controversy, or a temporary lapse in production,
without collapse. Regeneration is the capacity to renew membership and leadership, onboarding
newcomers to replace departing participants and, in the limit, surviving transitions in the producing
role itself. Economic viability is the capacity to capture sufficient value, monetary or otherwise, to
fund continued production and to compensate the maintenance labor t he channel requires.
Approaches to attribution, revenue, and yield optimization speak directly to this dimension (Basnet
et al., 2024). A channel is sustainable to the degree that it possesses all four, and the dimensions
can diverge, so that a channel may  be economically viable but fragile, or resilient but unable to
regenerate. Table 2 summarizes the four dimensions and the infrastructure each most depends
upon.

5.4 The Integrated Model and Its Propositions
Assembled, the model holds that the four layered infrastructures, sustained by maintenance and
repair processes that double as commons governance, produces the four dimensions of
sustainability under conditions of recurring stress. The visible metrics of engagement are traces of
the infrastructure in use, informative but not constitutive. Multi-touch attribution provides one way
of relating these traces to downstream outcomes without mistaking them for the structure that
produces them (Sanni et al., 2022). Virality enters the model not as an outcome but as  a stressor,
a sudden surge in load that tests whether the infrastructure can convert arriving attention into
durable participation or merely passes it through. Platform dependence enters as a feature of the
technical layer that trades short term reach aga inst long term control. From this structure, seven
propositions follow. They are stated as directional relationships intended for empirical testing and
are summarized in Table 3.

Proposition 1.  The infrastructural depth of engagement, defined as the degree  to which
engagement is embedded across the technical, relational, normative, and economic layers rather
than concentrated in one, is positively associated with channel resilience to exogenous shocks.
Proposition 2. Channels whose relational layer distributes ties among audience members, rather
than concentrating them in the producer to audience bond alone, exhibit greater regenerative
capacity.
Proposition 3. Investment in visible and invisible maintenance work, including moderation, ritual
facilitation, a nd onboarding, is negatively associated with participant attrition; however, the
invisibility of this work produces a structural tendency toward its underinvestment.
Proposition 4. Reliance on platform native engagement infrastructure increases short term reach
but, beyond a threshold, decreases long term sustainability through infrastructural dependency that
exposes the channel to uncontrollable technical layer shocks.
Proposition 5. Reciprocity norms mediate the relationship between content quality and participant

Proposition 6. Engagement functions as a common pool resource; in the absence of governance

6. Discussion
6.1 Theoretical Implications
The reframing carries three theoretical consequences. First, it relocates engagement from the
dependent variable to the explanatory structure. In the metric framing, engagement is what a
channel produces and the analyst's task is to explain its quantity. I n the infrastructural framing,
engagement is what allows a channel to keep producing, and the analyst's task is to explain its
durability and load bearing capacity. This relocation makes available questions that the metric
framing forecloses, particularly the question of what distinguishes interaction that builds future
capacity from interaction that merely registers present attention. Second, the reframing integrates
literatures that have largely developed in parallel. Marketing's relational construct of engagement,
communication's parasocial bond, the social capital tradition, online community research, and
commons governance are shown to describe different layers and processes of a single
infrastructure, which suggests that their insights are complementary rather than competing. Third,
the model repositions maintenance from the margins to the center of the theory of sustainable
channels, aligning the study of digital content with the broader turn in science and technology
studies that treats repair as constitutive of functioning rather than as its failure (Graham & Thrift,
2007; Jackson, 2014).

6.2 The Platform Dependency Paradox
Proposition 4 names a tension that the infrastructural framing makes legible and that the metric
framing tends to obscure. Platforms supply a powerful technical layer at low cost, including
distribution, recommendation, and the data that make audiences reachable, and a channel that leans
on this layer can acquire reach far faster than one that builds its own (van Dijck, 2013; Nieborg &
Poell, 2018). The same reliance, however, is a form of infrastructural dependency. The platform
owns the layer, sets its terms, and can alter it unilaterally, so that the channel's continuity rests on
infrastructure it neither controls nor can repair (Gillespie, 2018; Poell et al., 2019). The paradox is
that the engagement infrastructure most readily available is also the least durable to depend upon,
because its breakdown is outside the channel's capacity to prevent or fix. The model implies that
durable channels treat platform supplied engagement as a complement to, rather than a substitute
for, the relational, normative, and economic layers they can themselves maintain, and that they
invest deliberately in ties and norms that would survive a severing of the technical layer. The
recurring practice of migrating an audience from a rented platform to an owned channel, such as a
mailing list, is in these terms an act of infrastructural risk reduction. Predictive analytics applied
across owned and operated platforms can inform the retention work that such migration is intended
to protect (Oghenemaiga et al., 2024).

6.3 Practical Implications
For creators and community stewards, the model reorients practice away from the maximization
of visible metrics and tow ard the construction and maintenance of infrastructure. It implies that
early investment in audience to audience ties, even at some cost to short term reach, raises later
resilience and regenerative capacity, and that the labor of moderation, ritual, and onboarding is not
overhead to be minimized but the core work of sustainability, deserving of resources
commensurate with its importance. It implies that monetization should be governed as the
extraction of a renewable resource, paced to replenishment and co nstrained by the community's
norms, rather than maximized in the short run at the risk of depleting the goodwill on which the
channel depends (Ostrom, 1990). And it implies that virality should be prepared for as a stress test

7. Limitations and Future Research
The paper is conceptual, and its central limitation follows from that status. The model and its
propositions are theoretical claims that have not been tested, and their value rests on whether they
can be operationalized and on whether the predicted relationships are observed. Several constructs
require careful operationalization. Infrastructural depth might be measured through the distribution
of engagement across the four layers; the structure of the relational layer through network analysis
of audience to audience interaction; maintenance investment through the labor and attention
allocated to moderation, ritual, and onboarding; and the sustainability dimensions through
longitudinal indicators of production continuity, shock recovery,  membership renewal, and
revenue adequacy. A second limitation is scope. The model is framed to apply across video, audio,
written, and community-based channels, but the relative weight of the four layers and the salience
of particular maintenance processes are likely to vary by form and by platform, and the model does
not yet specify these contingencies. A third limitation is that the model treats the producer and the
community as the primary actors and gives the platform a conditioning role, whereas platf orms
are active parties whose own incentives shape the infrastructure in ways the model could theorize
more fully. These limitations define an agenda. Quantitative work could test the propositions using
longitudinal data on channels that survive and channels that fail, with particular attention to
Proposition 4, since the predicted nonmonotonic relationship between pla tform dependency and
sustainability is both central and counterintuitive. Qualitative and ethnographic work could
perform the infrastru ctural inversion directly, studying maintenance labor and breakdown
moments to characterize the work that sustains durable channels and the ways it becomes visible
when it fails (Bowker & Star, 1999; Jackson, 2014). Comparative work across platforms and forms
could establish the contingencies the present model leaves open. And design-oriented research
could test whether affordances that support tie distribution, norm transmission, and onboarding
measurably improve the durability of hosted channels (Kraut & Resnick, 2012; Ren et al., 2007).
Across these directions, the unifying empirical question is whether durability is better predicted
by the depth and maintenance of engagement infrastructure than by the volume of engagement
metrics, which is the core wager of the model.

8. Conclusion
Digital content channels live or die not by the attention they momentarily attract but by the
substrate they accumulate and maintain. This paper has argued that the substrate is best understood
as infrastructure: the interdependent layers of technical arra ngement, relational tie, shared norm,
and value flow through which a channel sustains production and participation, kept functioning by
the continual and largely invisible work of maintenance and repair. The infrastructural fra ming
reorganizes the field of explanation. It treats visible engagement metrics as traces rather than as
the thing itself, recasts virality as a stress on infrastructure rather than a measure of its health,
