Orientation: In a context marked by growing environmental awareness and
increasing health-related concerns, sustainable consumption has become a
key issue for both firms and policymakers, particularly in emerging markets.
Understanding the psychological mechanisms underlying green purchase inten-
tion is therefore essential.
Research purpose: This study investigates the impact of the green marketing mix
on green purchase intention, examining the mediating role of perceived value
and the moderating effects of environmental concern and health consciousness
in the Moroccan context.
Motivation for the study: Despite extensive research on green marketing, lim -
ited studies have integrated perceived value as a mediating mechanism while
simultaneously considering both environmental concern and health conscious-
ness as boundary conditions. Moreover, evidence from emerging markets such
as Morocco remains scarce, particularly in relation to green product categories
including food, cosmetics, and hygiene products.
Research approach and method: A quantitative research design was adopted.
Based on a comprehensive literature review, a conceptual model and corre -
sponding hypotheses were developed. Data were collected through a structured
questionnaire administered to Moroccan consumers in the Casablanca–Settat
region, yielding 200 valid responses. The data were analyzed using partial least
squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM).
Main findings: The results reveal a selective pattern of effects within the green
marketing mix, where only green product and green promotion significantly
influence perceived value, while green price and green place are not significant.
Perceived value positively affects green purchase intention and transmits the
effects of green product and green promotion on intention. Furthermore, envi-
ronmental concern does not significantly moderate the relationship between
perceived value and green purchase intention, whereas health consciousness
exerts a significant positive moderating effect.
Practical/managerial implications: The findings suggest that firms should pri -
oritize tangible product attributes and effective communication strategies to

1 Introduction
Recent environmental degradation, climate risks, and increasing
health awareness have significantly transformed consumer behavior.
Consumers are progressively integrating environmental responsibility
and personal well-being into their purchasing decisions, alongside
functional and economic considerations (Skackauskiene and Vilkaite-
Vaitone, 2023; Zawadzki et al., 2020; Nekmahmud et al., 2022). This
shift is particularly visible in product categories such as food, cosmet-
ics, personal care, and household goods, where sustainability attri-
butes increasingly influence consumer preferences (Khan and Mohsin,
2017; Luo et al., 2022).
In response, firms have adopted green marketing strategies inte-
grating environmental considerations into product, price, place, and
promotion decisions (Kotler, 2011; Leonidou et al., 2013). The green
marketing mix represents a structured set of market signals intended
to shape consumer evaluations and encourage sustainable consump-
tion (Mukonza and Swarts, 2020; Ahmed et al., 2023).
However, empirical findings remain inconsistent regarding the
effectiveness of green marketing in generating behavioral outcomes.
While positive associations are often reported, the magnitude and
significance of effects vary across contexts and product categories (Luo
et al., 2022 ). This suggests that green marketing does not directly
translate into behavioral intention but operates through internal cog-
nitive evaluation processes.
A key limitation in the literature is the insufficient integration of
perceived value as an explanatory mechanism. Consumers evaluate
marketing stimuli based on perceived trade-offs between benefits and
sacrifices (Zeithaml, 1988). In sustainability contexts, this evaluation
becomes multidimensional, encompassing functional, emotional,
social, and economic value (Sweeney and Soutar, 2001). The Theory
of Consumption Values (Sheth et al., 1991) provides a robust frame-
work for understanding this evaluative process.
Despite growing interest, the mechanisms through which
green marketing shapes perceived value and subsequently influ -
ences behavioral intention remain underexplored, particularly in
emerging markets such as Morocco. The Moroccan context is
characterized by a transitional consumption structure, where
green products coexist with traditional consumption patterns and
face constraints related to price sensitivity, availability, and infor -
mation asymmetry ( El Frioui and Benyoussef, 2024 ; Saad and
Malainine, 2025 ).
Against this background, this study develops a value-based frame-
work examining how the green marketing mix influences green pur-
chase intention through perceived value, while considering
environmental concern and health consciousness as boundary condi-
tions. The study contributes by extending green marketing literature
through a structured value-based explanatory model in an emerging
market context.
2 Literature review and hypotheses
development
2.1 Theoretical foundation
This study is primarily grounded in the Theory of Consumption
Values (TCV) developed by Sheth et al. (1991), which provides a com-
prehensive framework for understanding consumer decision-making
processes. According to TCV , consumer choices are not solely driven
by economic rationality or functional utility but are shaped by mul-
tiple value dimensions that consumers associate with products and
services. Specifically, the theory posits that consumption decisions are
influenced by functional, social, emotional, epistemic, and conditional
values, each contributing to consumers’ overall evaluation of a
product.
The relevance of TCV to sustainable consumption lies in its ability
to explain why consumers choose environmentally friendly products
despite the potential economic sacrifices associated with them. Green
products often involve higher purchase costs, limited availability, and
uncertainty regarding their performance relative to conventional
alternatives. Consequently, consumers’ decisions depend on whether
the perceived benefits of these products outweigh the associated sac-
rifices. TCV suggests that such evaluations emerge from a multidi-
mensional assessment process that incorporates functional
performance, environmental benefits, social recognition, emotional
satisfaction, and contextual considerations (Sheth et al., 1991).
Building upon this perspective, Sweeney and Soutar (2001) devel-
oped the PERV AL framework, which operationalizes perceived value
as a multidimensional construct composed of quality value, emotional
value, social value, and price value. PERV AL has been widely applied
in consumer behavior research and provides a suitable framework for
understanding how consumers evaluate green products. In sustainable

consumption contexts, perceived value reflects consumers’ overall
assessment of the benefits derived from environmentally friendly
products relative to the sacrifices incurred in acquiring and
using them.
The TCV perspective further suggests that marketing actions
influence consumer behavior indirectly through value creation pro-
cesses. Green marketing initiatives provide consumers with informa-
tional and symbolic cues regarding product characteristics,
environmental commitment, and sustainability performance. These
cues are cognitively processed and transformed into value percep-
tions, which subsequently shape behavioral outcomes. Accordingly,
consumers do not respond directly to marketing stimuli; rather, they
form evaluative judgments that determine their willingness to engage
in sustainable purchasing behavior.
This reasoning is consistent with the Value–Attitude–Behavior
(V AB) hierarchy, which proposes that values constitute fundamental
drivers of behavioral responses through intermediate evaluative pro-
cesses. Within this framework, perceived value represents the proxi-
mal cognitive mechanism through which consumers translate external
stimuli into behavioral intentions. Consequently, perceived value is
positioned in this study as the central mediating mechanism linking
green marketing practices to green purchase intention.
Furthermore, consumer responses to value perceptions are unlikely
to be homogeneous. Individual characteristics influence the extent to
which favorable evaluations are translated into behavioral intentions.
Therefore, this study incorporates environmental concern and health
consciousness as boundary conditions affecting the strength of the rela-
tionship between perceived value and green purchase intention. These
factors reflect differences in consumers’ sensitivity to environmental
sustainability and personal well-being and are expected to condition
how perceived value influences purchasing decisions.
2.2 Hypotheses development
2.2.1 Green marketing mix and perceived value
Perceived value is a central construct in consumer behavior
research and is generally defined as the consumer’s overall assessment
of the utility of a product based on perceptions of what is received and
what is sacrificed ( Zeithaml, 1988 ). Contemporary perspectives
extend this definition by emphasizing the multidimensional nature of
value and its dependence on contextual factors, marketing stimuli, and
individual consumer characteristics (Konuk, 2018). In sustainable
consumption contexts, perceived value incorporates not only func-
tional and economic considerations but also environmental, social,
and emotional benefits associated with green products.
Green marketing plays a crucial role in shaping these value per-
ceptions. Beyond its traditional commercial function, green market-
ing communicates environmental responsibility, signals product
quality, reduces uncertainty, and enhances consumer trust in sustain-
ability claims (Ahmed et al., 2023; Sembiring, 2021). Consequently,
the green marketing mix can be viewed as a system of value-generat-
ing mechanisms that influence consumers’ evaluations of green
products.
Among its dimensions, green product represents the most
fundamental source of perceived value because environmental
attributes are directly embedded in the product itself. Features
such as reduced toxicity, natural ingredients, recyclability, energy
efficiency, and environmentally responsible production methods
enhance functional value by improving product quality and safety.
Simultaneously, they generate emotional value through the satis -
faction associated with responsible consumption and contribute
to social value by reinforcing consumers’ environmental self-
image ( Misra and Singh, 2016 ; Vijaya, 2020 ).
Green price reflects the economic dimension of sustainable con-
sumption. Although green products are often associated with pre -
mium pricing, consumers may perceive such premiums as justified
when they are accompanied by credible environmental and quality-
related benefits. In this case, consumers evaluate price not merely as a
monetary sacrifice but as an indicator of value, fairness, and long-term
benefits (Jamal et al., 2021; Nuttavuthisit and Thøgersen, 2017).
Green promotion constitutes another important source of per-
ceived value. Through advertising, environmental claims, certifica-
tions, and sustainability-oriented communication, firms provide
information that helps consumers understand product benefits and
reduce uncertainty. Effective green promotion may enhance trust,
increase awareness of environmental benefits, and strengthen emo-
tional attachment to sustainable products ( Bhatia and Jain, 2013 ;
Sembiring, 2021; Irshad, 2025).
Green place refers to environmentally responsible distribution
practices and the accessibility of green products. Sustainable logistics,
environmentally friendly retailing, and convenient product availability
can increase consumers’ confidence in the authenticity of green claims
while reducing the effort associated with product acquisition. These
factors contribute to functional and conditional value perceptions
(Sugandini et al., 2020; Walia and Kumar, 2021).
Empirical evidence generally supports the relationship between
green marketing activities and perceived value. Krishnan et al. (2024)
demonstrate that green marketing practices significantly enhance con-
sumers’ value perceptions, which subsequently influence behavioral
outcomes. Similarly, Alameri and Hassoon (2021) report that different
dimensions of the green marketing mix exert distinct effects on vari-
ous components of perceived value, supporting the multidimensional
assumptions of the PERV AL framework.
Despite these advances, previous studies have frequently exam-
ined individual green marketing dimensions in isolation, thereby pro-
viding a fragmented understanding of value formation processes. By
adopting an integrated perspective, this study argues that each dimen-
sion of the green marketing mix contributes to consumers’ overall
value assessments and therefore influences perceived value.
Accordingly, this study adopts an integrated perspective of the
green marketing mix to provide a more comprehensive explanation of
perceived value formation in sustainable consumption contexts. Based
on this reasoning, the following hypotheses are proposed:
H1: Green product positively influences perceived value.
H2: Green price positively influences perceived value.
H3: Green place positively influences perceived value.
H4: Green promotion positively influences perceived value.

intention
Perceived value occupies a central position in sustainable con-
sumption research because consumers rarely make purchasing

decisions based solely on environmental awareness or ethical consid-
erations. Rather, they evaluate the extent to which green products
provide meaningful benefits relative to the sacrifices associated with
their acquisition and use. Consistent with the Theory of Consumption
Values, perceived value reflects an overall evaluative judgment
through which consumers assess the utility and desirability of a prod-
uct based on multiple sources of benefits (Sheth et al., 1991).
Within sustainable consumption contexts, perceived value rep -
resents a key mechanism linking product evaluations to behavioral
responses. Green purchase intention refers to consumers’ willingness
and readiness to purchase environmentally friendly products and is
widely recognized as an important predictor of future purchasing
behavior. The formation of such intentions depends largely on
whether consumers perceive green products as valuable, useful, reli-
able, and beneficial.
The PERV AL framework proposed by Sweeney and Soutar
(2001) provides a useful foundation for understanding value for -
mation by suggesting that consumers derive value from several
complementary sources, including functional, emotional, social,
and price-related benefits. Functional value reflects product qual -
ity, reliability, and performance; emotional value captures feelings
of satisfaction and well-being; social value reflects social approval
and identity enhancement; whereas price value relates to consum -
ers’ assessment of benefits relative to monetary sacrifices. In green
consumption settings, these value dimensions collectively contrib -
ute to an overall perception of value that influences purchasing
decisions.
Consumers who perceive greater value are generally more willing
to pay premium prices, accept behavioral adjustments, and engage in
sustainable purchasing practices. Perceived value reduces uncertainty,
increases confidence in product performance, and legitimizes pur-
chasing decisions. Consequently, it acts as a cognitive mechanism that
facilitates the translation of favorable evaluations into purchase inten-
tions (Nabilla, 2019; Ghofrani et al., 2017).
Empirical evidence consistently supports this relationship.
Medeiros et al. (2015) demonstrate that perceived value significantly
increases consumers’ willingness to pay premium prices for green
products. Suphasomboon and Vassanadumrongdee (2022) show that
environmental, functional, and emotional value positively influence
green purchase intention. Similarly, Arora and Manchanda (2022)
report that perceived value constitutes a significant predictor of pur-
chase intention in sustainable product markets.
Previous studies also suggest that the relative importance of
value sources may vary across consumption contexts and product
categories. Functional and environmental benefits often exert
stronger influences, whereas emotional and social benefits appear
more context-dependent ( Lin and Dong, 2023 ). Furthermore,
perceived value contributes to consumer satisfaction and trust,
which in turn reinforce favorable behavioral responses ( Luo et
al., 2022 ).
Overall, existing evidence indicates that perceived value consti-
tutes a critical evaluative mechanism through which consumers assess
the desirability and legitimacy of green products. By synthesizing mul-
tiple sources of benefits into an overall judgment, perceived value
strengthens consumers’ willingness to engage in green purchasing
behavior. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed:
H5: Perceived value positively influences green purchase
intention.
2.2.3 Mediating role of perceived value
Drawing on the Theory of Consumption Values and the Value–
Attitude–Behavior hierarchy, this study conceptualizes perceived
value as the central mechanism through which green marketing activ-
ities influence green purchase intention. Rather than affecting behav-
ioral intentions directly, green marketing stimuli first shape
consumers’ value perceptions, which subsequently determine their
willingness to purchase green products.
This perspective assumes that consumers do not react automati-
cally to environmental marketing efforts. Instead, they interpret,
evaluate, and assign meaning to sustainability-related information
before making purchasing decisions. Consequently, perceived value
operates as a cognitive filter through which consumers assess the ben-
efits and sacrifices associated with green products.
The mediating role of perceived value has received increasing
empirical support. Xu et al. (2022) demonstrate that perceived value
significantly mediates the relationship between green product attri-
butes and repurchase intentions in the context of green agricultural
products. Their findings suggest that value perceptions enable con-
sumers to justify environmentally responsible purchasing decisions
(Hassan et al., 2022).
Similarly, Saleh et al. (2024)  report that green perceived value
mediates the effects of green promotion and green price on electric
vehicle purchase intentions. These results indicate that marketing
activities become effective only when consumers perceive meaningful
benefits from them.
Ahmed et al. (2023) further show that green marketing practices
influence purchase intention through the development of green cus-
tomer value. Their findings highlight the central role of perceived
value as an intermediary mechanism connecting marketing stimuli
and behavioral responses.
Collectively, these studies suggest that the effectiveness of green
marketing depends largely on its ability to generate favorable value
perceptions. Therefore, perceived value is expected to transmit the
effects of green marketing mix dimensions on green purchase inten-
tion. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:
H6: Perceived value mediates the relationship between green
product and sustainable purchase intention.
H7: Perceived value mediates the relationship between green
price and sustainable purchase intention.
H8: Perceived value mediates the relationship between green
place and sustainable purchase intention.
H9: Perceived value mediates the relationship between green pro-
motion and sustainable purchase intention.
2.2.4 Moderating effects
2.2.4.1 Environmental concern as a boundary condition
Environmental concern refers to the degree to which individuals
are aware of environmental problems and support efforts aimed at
protecting the natural environment. It reflects consumers’ beliefs,
values, and sense of responsibility regarding environmental sustain-
ability (Taufique et al., 2019).

Although environmental concern is frequently examined as a direct
predictor of sustainable consumption, previous research suggests that its
effects are often indirect and context-dependent. Consumers may
express strong environmental concern without necessarily translating
such concern into purchasing behavior. This phenomenon is commonly
associated with the well-documented attitude–behavior gap in sustain-
able consumption research (Vermeir and Verbeke, 2006; De Canio et
al., 2021).
Within the TCV framework, environmental concern can be
understood as a factor that increases the salience of environmental
benefits during product evaluation. Consumers with higher levels of
environmental concern are more likely to assign greater importance
to sustainability-related attributes, ecological benefits, and responsible
production practices when evaluating green products (Astuti et al.,
2024; Tripopsakul, 2024; Wei et al., 2018; Hudayah et al., 2023).
Consequently, environmental concern is expected to influence not
the formation of value itself, but rather the extent to which perceived
value is translated into purchase intention. When environmental con-
cern is high, favorable value evaluations should be more likely to result
in stronger behavioral intentions because environmental benefits
become more relevant in consumers’ decision-making processes.
Previous studies support this contingent role. Environmental con-
cern has been found to strengthen the influence of green satisfaction,
green trust, and perceived quality on green purchasing outcomes
(Al-Quran et al., 2020; Wasaya et al., 2021; Tandon et al., 2020; Kaur
et al., 2022 ). Furthermore, environmentally concerned consumers
tend to process sustainability-related information more deeply and
respond more positively to environmental marketing initiatives
(Andika and Nuvriasari, 2024; Cachero-Martínez, 2020; Cerri et al.,
2017). Accordingly, environmental concern is conceptualized in this
study as a boundary condition that may strengthen the relationship
between perceived value and green purchase intention.
H10: Environmental concern positively moderates the relation-
ship between perceived value and sustainable purchase inten-
tion, such that the relationship is stronger for consumers with
higher levels of environmental concern.
2.2.4.2 Health consciousness as a boundary condition
Health consciousness refers to the degree to which individuals are
concerned about their health and actively engage in behaviors aimed at
maintaining or improving their well-being (Jayanti and Burns, 1998;
Moorman and Matulich, 1993). In recent years, health consciousness has
become an increasingly important determinant of sustainable consump-
tion due to growing concerns regarding food safety, product ingredients,
environmental pollution, and long-term health risks (Iqbal et al., 2021;
Parashar et al., 2023).
Health-conscious consumers generally pay greater attention to prod-
uct quality, safety, natural ingredients, and potential health benefits. As
a result, they are more likely to evaluate green products favorably because
such products are often associated with reduced chemical exposure,
higher safety standards, and healthier lifestyles (Iqbal et al., 2021).
From a value-based perspective, health consciousness does not
necessarily determine purchasing intentions directly. Instead, it influ-
ences how consumers interpret and utilize value perceptions when
making decisions. Individuals with higher levels of health conscious-
ness tend to place greater importance on health-related benefits
embedded in green products, thereby increasing the relevance of per-
ceived value in the decision-making process.
Empirical evidence supports this perspective. Health conscious-
ness has been shown to strengthen favorable evaluations of organic
and environmentally friendly products and increase consumers’ will-
ingness to engage in sustainable purchasing behavior (Escobar-Farfán
et al., 2025; Li and Shan, 2025; Parashar et al., 2023; Nguyen et al.,
2023). Moreover, consumers with strong health motivations are gener-
ally more responsive to value propositions emphasizing safety, quality,
and personal well-being.
Nevertheless, prior findings also suggest that the influence of
health consciousness may vary across product categories and con-
sumption contexts, indicating the need to examine its role as a contin-
gent rather than universal determinant of behavior ( Ecevit, 2023).
Accordingly, health consciousness is conceptualized as a boundary
condition that amplifies the relationship between perceived value and
green purchase intention.
H11: Health consciousness positively moderates the relationship
between perceived value and sustainable purchase intention,
such that the relationship is stronger among consumers with
higher levels of health consciousness.
This study proposes an integrated conceptual framework
(Figure 1) grounded in the Theory of Consumption Values (Sheth et
al., 1991). The model suggests that Green Marketing Mix dimensions
(Green Product, Green Price, Green Place, and Green Promotion)
influence Green Purchase Intention through Perceived Value as a cen-
tral mediating mechanism. Furthermore, Environmental Concern and
Health Consciousness are introduced as boundary conditions that
may strengthen or weaken the relationship between Perceived Value
and Green Purchase Intention.
3 Methodology
3.1 Research design
This study employs a quantitative, cross-sectional, and explana-
tory research design to investigate the impact of the Green Marketing
Mix on Green Purchase Intention, with the mediating role of Perceived
Value and the moderating roles of Environmental Concern and Health
Consciousness.
A quantitative approach was considered appropriate because the
study’s objective is to test causal relationships among latent constructs
and validate a conceptual model grounded in consumer behavior
theory and sustainable marketing literature. Cross-sectional data col-
lection was employed since the study aims to capture consumers’ per-
ceptions and behavioral intentions at a specific point in time rather
than across multiple periods (Hair et al., 2022).
The proposed conceptual model assumes that green marketing
practices, represented by green product, green price, green place, and
green promotion, influence consumers’ perceived value, which in turn
affects their intention to purchase organic and natural products. In
addition, environmental concern and health consciousness are incor-
porated as moderating variables to explain variations in consumers’
behavioral responses.

This predictive and explanatory orientation justifies the use
of Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-
SEM), which is particularly suitable for complex models involv -
ing mediation and moderation effects, multidimensional
constructs, and relatively moderate sample sizes ( Chin, 1998 ;
Hair et al., 2022 ).
3.2 Population and sampling procedure
The target population of this study comprises Moroccan consum-
ers who have previously purchased green products at least once. In the
present research, the term green products refers to a broad category
of environmentally and health-oriented products, including food
products, cosmetics, personal hygiene products, and eco-friendly
household products. This definition is consistent with prior research
on sustainable consumption, which typically focuses on individuals
with prior exposure to green products in order to ensure informed
and meaningful responses regarding their perceptions, evaluations,
and purchase intentions.
The inclusion of multiple product categories under the umbrella
term “green products” is consistent with both the objectives of the
study and the characteristics of the Moroccan market. As an emerging
and still-developing green market, Morocco is characterized by a rela-
tively limited segmentation of green product categories compared
with more mature markets. Consumers are generally exposed to vari-
ous forms of green consumption that share common sustainability-
related attributes, particularly environmental friendliness and
perceived health benefits. Consequently, the present study adopts a
cross-category perspective and focuses on the common mechanisms
through which Green Marketing Mix practices influence Perceived
Value and Green Purchase Intention. Rather than examining cate-
gory-specific purchasing behavior, the objective is to capture the
broader dynamics of green consumption in an emerging market con-
text where sustainability-related motivations frequently transcend
individual product categories.
The study was conducted in the Casablanca–Settat urban area,
one of the main consumption and economic hubs in Morocco. This
region is characterized by relatively high accessibility to consumers
engaged in or exposed to green consumption practices. Accordingly,
the unit of analysis was defined at the individual level, focusing on
consumers rather than firms or households, in line with behavioral
research traditions in marketing and sustainability studies.
A non-probability convenience sampling technique was employed
due to the accessibility of respondents and the exploratory–predictive
nature of the research design. This approach is widely applied in con-
sumer behavior research, particularly when a complete sampling
frame is unavailable and when the primary objective is the testing of
theoretical relationships rather than statistical generalization to the
population (Sekaran and Bougie, 2016). Moreover, convenience sam-
pling is widely accepted in structural equation modeling studies, espe-
cially when using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling
(PLS-SEM), which is less restrictive regarding distributional assump-
tions and sampling design requirements (Hair et al., 2017; Hair et al.,
2022). Methodological literature further supports the use of non-
probability sampling in explanatory research focused on relationships
among latent constructs, rather than on population parameter estima-
tion (Anderson and Gerbing, 1988; Chin, 1998). In addition, in mar-
keting and behavioral research, convenience sampling remains a
commonly adopted approach due to practical constraints and the
emphasis on theory development and model validation (Calder et al.,
1981; Etikan et al., 2016).
Data collection involved the distribution of 250 questionnaires,
administered both physically in supermarkets, organic and specialized
retail stores, and commercial areas, as well as digitally via online con-
sumer communities and social media platforms. A total of 217
responses were obtained. Following a rigorous data screening process,
including the removal of incomplete questionnaires and responses
containing missing data or inconsistencies, 200 valid observations
were retained for final analysis, resulting in a usable response rate
deemed adequate for PLS-SEM-based analyses.

3.3 Measurement instrument and scale
development
Data were collected using a structured self-administered ques-
tionnaire composed of two sections. The first section gathered respon-
dents’ socio-demographic information, including gender, age,
education level, employment status, monthly income, and frequency
of purchasing green products. Consistent with the scope of the study,
green products refer to environmentally friendly products commonly
purchased in categories such as food, cosmetics, personal care, and
eco-friendly household products.
The second section measured the latent constructs included in the
conceptual model. All measurement items were adapted from estab-
lished scales previously validated in the green marketing, sustainable
consumption, and consumer behavior literature. Responses were
recorded using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly dis-
agree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”).
The Green Marketing Mix was operationalized through four
dimensions: Green Product, Green Price, Green Place, and Green
Promotion. Green Product items were adapted from Chen (2010,
2013) and Mostafa (2007) and capture consumers’ perceptions of the
environmental, quality, and health-related attributes of green prod-
ucts. Green Price was measured using items derived from Zeithaml
(1988), Sweeney and Soutar (2001), and Dodds et al. (1991), reflecting
consumers’ evaluation of the price–benefit trade-off associated with
green products. Green Place was operationalized using indicators
adapted from the green marketing literature, emphasizing product
availability, accessibility, and environmentally responsible distribution
practices (Leonidou et al., 2013; Hossain and Rahman, 2018; Kautish
et al., 2019). Green Promotion was measured using items adapted
from Rahbar and Wahid (2011), focusing on the effectiveness of envi-
ronmental communication and promotional activities.
Perceived Value was measured using a scale adapted from the
PERV AL framework developed by Sweeney and Soutar (2001), which
is grounded in the Theory of Consumption Values (Sheth et al., 1991).
The original PERV AL framework conceptualizes consumer value
through four complementary sources of value: functional value, price
value, emotional value, and social value. Rather than modelling these
dimensions as separate constructs, the present study operationalizes
perceived value as an overall evaluative judgment reflecting the aggre-
gate benefits associated with green products. The selected indicators
capture consumers’ assessments of product quality, value for money,
emotional satisfaction, and social recognition derived from purchas-
ing and consuming green products. This operationalization is consis-
tent with previous studies that conceptualize perceived value as a
higher-order global evaluation of benefits in sustainable consumption
contexts (Konuk, 2018; Ahmed et al., 2023).
Environmental Concern was measured using items adapted from
the revised New Ecological Paradigm (NEP) scale developed by
Dunlap et al. (2000). This construct captures the extent to which indi-
viduals are aware of environmental issues and concerned about the
consequences of environmental degradation. Health Consciousness
was measured using validated items adapted from Michaelidou and
Hassan (2008), reflecting consumers’ awareness of, and involvement
in, health-related behaviors and lifestyle choices.
Finally, Green Purchase Intention was measured using established
scales adapted from Chan (2001) , Chen and Chang (2012) , and
Mostafa (2007). The construct reflects consumers’ willingness and
intention to purchase environmentally friendly products in the future.
To ensure content validity and contextual relevance, minor wording
modifications were introduced to adapt the measurement items to the
Moroccan context while preserving their original conceptual mean-
ing. This procedure follows established recommendations for cross-
cultural scale adaptation and contributes to maintaining both
conceptual equivalence and measurement validity.
3.4 Common method bias assessment
Given that all data were collected through a single self-adminis-
tered questionnaire, common method bias was assessed through both
procedural and statistical remedies. Procedurally, respondents were
assured of anonymity and confidentiality, and questionnaire items
were carefully designed to reduce ambiguity and social desirabil -
ity bias.
Statistically, Harman’s single-factor test revealed that the first
factor accounted for <50% of total variance, suggesting that common
method bias was unlikely to pose a substantial threat. In addition, full
collinearity assessment indicated that all VIF values remained below
the threshold of 3.3, providing further evidence that common method
bias was not a serious concern.

5 Discussion
The findings provide a nuanced understanding of green pur -
chase intention by revealing a selective and context-dependent
influence of green marketing practices on consumers’ value per -
ceptions and behavioral intentions. Contrary to the assumption
that all dimensions of the green marketing mix contribute equally
to consumer evaluations, the results indicate that only green prod -
uct and green promotion significantly enhance perceived value.
The significant effect of green product highlights the importance of
tangible product attributes in shaping consumers’ evaluations. In the con-
text of green products, consumers appear to rely primarily on observable
characteristics such as quality, safety, natural ingredients, reliability, and
perceived environmental benefits when assessing product value. This
finding is consistent with previous studies suggesting that functional and
experiential product attributes constitute major sources of value in sus-
tainable consumption contexts (Chen, 2010; Vijaya, 2020; Krishnan et al.,
2024). In emerging markets such as Morocco, where green markets
remain in a developmental phase, consumers may place greater emphasis
on concrete product benefits than on abstract sustainability claims.
Similarly, green promotion exerts a significant positive effect on
perceived value. This finding underscores the importance of commu-
nication in reducing information asymmetry and strengthening the
credibility of environmental claims. Promotional activities appear to
facilitate consumers’ understanding of product benefits while enhanc-
ing trust in firms’ sustainability efforts. This result supports previous
research emphasizing the informational and signaling functions of
green communication (Konuk, 2018; Sembiring, 2021).
In contrast, green price and green place do not significantly influ-
ence perceived value. One possible explanation is that price is per-
ceived primarily as a market constraint rather than as a source of
value. Although consumers may recognize the benefits associated with
green products, higher prices may not necessarily increase their per-
ception of value. Likewise, the non-significant effect of green place
may reflect the growing accessibility of green products in urban areas,
reducing the importance of distribution-related considerations in con-
sumers’ value assessments. These findings suggest that consumers
selectively process marketing cues and prioritize those perceived as
most relevant to product performance and credibility.
The results further confirm the central role of perceived value in
shaping green purchase intention. Consistent with the Theory of
Consumption Values, consumers are more willing to purchase green
products when they perceive them as providing meaningful benefits
relative to the sacrifices involved. By integrating quality, economic,
emotional, and social benefits into an overall evaluative judgment,
perceived value reduces uncertainty and strengthens consumers’ will-
ingness to engage in green purchasing behavior. This finding is
consistent with prior research emphasizing the importance of per-
ceived value as a key determinant of sustainable consumption deci-
sions (Medeiros et al., 2015; Arora and Manchanda, 2022).
The mediation analysis provides additional insights into the mecha-
nisms through which green marketing influences behavioral intentions.
The results indicate significant indirect effects of green product and green
promotion on green purchase intention through perceived value. These
findings suggest that consumers do not respond directly to marketing
stimuli; rather, they first evaluate the benefits associated with green prod-
ucts before forming purchase intentions. In this respect, perceived value
acts as a transmission mechanism linking specific green marketing prac-
tices to behavioral outcomes.
The moderation analysis highlights the importance of indi -
vidual differences in shaping sustainable consumption decisions.
Contrary to expectations, environmental concern does not signifi -
cantly moderate the relationship between perceived value and
green purchase intention. However, this finding should not be
interpreted as evidence that environmental concern is unimport -
ant in green consumption. Rather, it suggests that environmental
concern may operate through alternative mechanisms not cap -
tured in the present model, such as direct effects on purchase
intention or indirect effects through trust, attitudes, or perceived
risk. Similar observations have been reported in previous sustain -
able consumption studies conducted in emerging market contexts
(De Canio et al., 2021 ).
By contrast, health consciousness significantly strengthens the rela-
tionship between perceived value and green purchase intention.
Consumers with higher levels of health consciousness appear more likely
to translate favorable value perceptions into purchasing intentions. This
finding suggests that health-related considerations reinforce the effective-
ness of value-based evaluations when consumers assess green products.
Rather than indicating the superiority of health motivations over environ-
mental motivations, the results suggest that health consciousness repre-
sents an important complementary factor that enhances the conversion
of perceived value into behavioral intention within the specific context
examined in this study.
This finding may also be related to the cross-category nature of the
study, which simultaneously considers organic and natural products
from different sectors, including food, cosmetics, and hygiene prod-
ucts. While health-related benefits may constitute a common evalua-
tive criterion across these categories, the relative importance of
environmental, health, price, and convenience considerations may
vary depending on the specific product category.
Consequently, future research could investigate these relation-
ships separately across different green product categories to determine
whether the moderating role of health consciousness remains equally
salient across consumption contexts.

6 Implications
6.1 Theoretical implications
This study offers several contributions to the literature on green
marketing and sustainable consumption.
First, it extends the Theory of Consumption Values by demon -
strating that the influence of the green marketing mix on con -
sumer behavior is selective rather than uniform. The findings
indicate that consumers do not attribute the same importance to
all green marketing dimensions when forming value perceptions.
Instead, green product and green promotion emerge as the pri -
mary drivers of perceived value, highlighting the importance of
product-related benefits and credible communication in value
formation processes.
Second, the study contributes to the growing literature on per-
ceived value by confirming its role as a central explanatory mechanism
linking green marketing practices to green purchase intention. By
demonstrating significant indirect effects through perceived value, the
findings support a process-based view of consumer decision-making
in which marketing stimuli influence behavioral intentions through
cognitive evaluations rather than through direct effects alone.
Third, the study contributes to the literature on boundary con -
ditions of sustainable consumption by examining the moderating
roles of environmental concern and health consciousness. The find -
ings reveal that the effectiveness of perceived value depends partly
on consumers’ health-related motivations. At the same time, the
absence of a significant moderating effect of environmental concern
suggests that the role of environmental motivations may be more
complex than commonly assumed and may operate through alter -
native pathways. This result calls for a more nuanced understanding
of how individual differences shape sustainable consumption
decisions.
Finally, by providing empirical evidence from Morocco, the study
contributes to the external validity of green marketing and consump-
tion theories. Most existing evidence originates from developed econ-
omies, whereas emerging markets are characterized by different
institutional environments, consumption habits, and market struc-
tures. The findings therefore enrich current knowledge regarding the
mechanisms underlying green purchase intention in transitional
market contexts.
6.2 Managerial implications
From a managerial perspective, the findings provide actionable
insights for firms operating in sustainable product markets.
First, companies should prioritize the development of green prod-
ucts with tangible and verifiable attributes, particularly those related
to quality, safety, naturalness, and traceability. This is especially rele-
vant in sectors such as food, cosmetics, and personal care, where con-
sumers appear to rely heavily on observable product characteristics
when forming value perceptions.

Second, green promotion should be strategically leveraged as a

where certification systems are still developing and consumer skepti-

is likely to play a critical role in shaping perceived value.
Third, the findings suggest that price may not function primarily
as a value-enhancing signal, but rather as a potential constraint influ-

adopting strategies aimed at improving affordability, such as product
format adjustments, tiered pricing, or targeted promotional efforts.
Finally, the significant role of health consciousness indicates that
marketing strategies should explicitly integrate health-related benefits
alongside environmental claims. This implies the need for more refined
segmentation approaches, targeting consumers whose motivations are
driven by personal well-being in addition to sustainability concerns.
Furthermore, firms should recognize that green product catego-
ries are not necessarily driven by identical motivations. Consumers
purchasing food, cosmetics, personal care products, or eco-friendly
household products may emphasize different benefits when evaluating
green offerings. Consequently, firms should adapt their communica-
tion and positioning strategies according to the specific characteristics
of each product category.
7 Conclusion
This study examined the influence of the green marketing mix on
green purchase intention through the mediating role of perceived
value and the moderating effects of environmental concern and health
consciousness in the Moroccan context. The findings demonstrate that
consumers’ purchase intentions are primarily shaped by value-based
evaluations generated through product-related attributes and promo-
tional efforts. Perceived value emerged as a central mechanism
through which green marketing practices influence behavioral
intentions.
The results further indicate that the relationship between per -
ceived value and green purchase intention is strengthened by health
consciousness, whereas environmental concern does not significantly
moderate this relationship. However, this result should not be inter-
preted as evidence that environmental concern is irrelevant to green
consumption. Rather, it suggests that environmental concern may
influence consumer behavior through alternative mechanisms beyond
those examined in the present study.
However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the
use of self-reported and cross-sectional data may expose the findings
to common method bias and limits the ability to establish causal rela-
tionships. Although additional diagnostic procedures were conducted,
future studies could employ longitudinal or experimental designs to
strengthen causal inference. Second, the relatively modest sample size
and the focus on consumers from the Casablanca–Settat region may
limit the generalizability of the findings to other geographical contexts.
Third, the study focuses on green purchase intention rather than
actual purchasing behavior. Consequently, it does not fully capture the
well-documented intention–behavior gap that frequently character-
izes sustainable consumption decisions.
Fourth, the study combines several categories of green prod -
ucts, including food products, cosmetics, personal care products,
and eco-friendly household products. Although this cross-category
approach is appropriate for capturing the overall dynamics of green
consumption in an emerging market context, differences may exist
across product categories regarding the relative importance of
health, environmental, price, and convenience considerations. As a
result, category-specific effects may remain partially concealed
within the aggregated analysis. Future research could therefore con -
duct category-specific investigations or multi-group analyses to
examine whether the proposed relationships vary across product
categories.
Finally, future studies could enrich the proposed framework by
incorporating additional psychological and contextual variables, such as
green trust, perceived risk, environmental knowledge, or prior green
consumption experience. Comparative studies across different emerging
economies would also contribute to a deeper understanding of the con-
textual conditions shaping sustainable consumption behavior.
