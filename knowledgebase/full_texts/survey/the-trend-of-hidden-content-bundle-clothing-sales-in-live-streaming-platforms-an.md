 Due to the growth of live streaming, e -commerce businesses have introduced innovative marketing strategies, including hidden -content
bundle sales, in which consumers purchase products without prior
knowledge of the exact contents. While such practices are widely
accepted on platforms such as TikTok and Instagram Live, its raise
important concerns regarding shariah compliance, particularly in relation
to the principle of gharar (uncertainty). Limited research has examined
public awareness of the shariah compliance, particularly within the
context of Brunei Darussalam, where commercial activities are guided
by the philosophy of Melayu Islam Beraja (MIB). This study is among
the first to investigate Bruneian consumers' awareness and acceptance of
hidden-content bundle sales from the perspective of the Islamic principle
of gharar, thereby addressing an important gap in the literature on
Shariah-compliant digital commerce.  This study examines public
awareness and acceptance of hidden-content bundle sales from a shariah
perspective. Quantitative research was employed using an online survey
questionnaire through Google Forms. Data from this study were analysed
using IBM SPSS version 29. The findings revealed that most respondents
were aware of the concept of gharar and recognized its connection to
hidden content bundles. Consequently, results showed that individuals
tend to accept hidden content bundles when qualities such as
transparency, honesty, and buyer protection are ensured. It is expected
that this study cont ributes to the literature and offer helpful insights in
improving awareness of the practice of hidden-content bundle practices.

1.  INTRODUCTION
The e -commerce industry has experienced remarkable growth in recent years, primarily due to
increasing consumer preference for online shopping and digital business transactions. The proliferation of
the internet has fundamentally transformed traditional re tail models (Wahab & Abdulmajeed, 2025)
Platforms such as Amazon and eBay grew strong on this shift by using user-friendly interfaces, established
customer bases, and integrated transaction and shipping systems (Moaiad, 2023). Social media networks,
including Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok, now occupy a similar strategic position to e-commerce brands
in the competitive marketing landscape. In addition to offering various advertising formats that facilitate
user interaction and encourage consumer participation, these platforms play a critical role in shaping online
purchasing behaviour (Uddin, 2024).
Building on this trend, live -broadcast shopping sites have a big role in online commerce, letting
companies connect instantly with customers through real -time interaction. Live -streaming strengthens
customer-brand relationships, which play a crucial role in shaping consumer purchasing decisions (Widodo
et al., 2023). Within this interactive environment, some businesses have introduced new sales tactics to
further attract and engage audiences. One emerging trend is the sale of hidden goods. The concept of hidden
content bundles refers to a strategy where buyers do not know exactly what they purchase until after the
sale. This approach is similar to blind or mystery boxes, where the contents remain unknown until they are
opened. (Zhan, H., 202 4).  Implementing this concept has prompted concerns about its compliance,
particularly within the principle of gharar (uncertainty) context.
In the context of muamalah, particularly within economic transactions, Islamic teachings emphasize
the principle of justice. This principle requires that business interactions between sellers and buyers are
conducted equitably and result in mutual benefit, thereby preventing harm t o any party (Farikhin &
Mulyasari, 2022). The principle of gharar refers to a practice that may lead to the exploitation or harm of
one party, and according to Islamic law, gharar is considered an unlawful means of generating profit, as it
involves deceptive element or uncertain transfer of rights.

2.1  Hidden bundle content sales in e-commerce
The term hidden content bundle lacks a standardized definition; it resembles the concept of mystery
boxing, which originated in Japan as Fukubukuro  (or lucky bags), a marketing scheme where consumers
purchase sealed packages without knowing their exact contents. This unique marketing model has gained
popularity across various industries by offering curated but undisclosed items at a fixed price, leve raging
consumer curiosity (Luo, 2025).  Interestingly, this modern form of mystery-based selling intersects with a
more traditional practice in the clothing industry. According to Hassan et al. (2022), the word bundle is
originally derived from English, wh ich means a tie or a package. It is used within industry to describe the
method of packaging garments in burlap sacks, typically comprising assorted items such as jeans, shirts,
shorts, or blouses of varying grades, which are offered at affordable prices. These garments are sold in bulk
by overseas suppliers and purchased by local distributors for the domestic market.

2.3   Dalil on the prohibition of gharar
This study is guided by the Islamic principle of gharar in Islamic transactions, which derives from
Islamic jurisprudence and highlights the prohibition of excessive uncertainty in commercial dealings. The
Principle of Gharar serves as an analytical lens for evaluating the Shariah compliance of hidden -content
bundle sales conducted through live -streaming platforms. From this perspective, commercial transactions
should provide sufficient transparency regarding the subject matte r, price, and conditions of sale to ensure
fairness and protect the rights of both buyers and sellers. Accordingly, the principle provides the theoretical
basis for assessing whether the characteristics of hidden -content bundle sales are compatible with Is lamic
commercial ethics.
According to Imam al-Nawawi as cited by Azuan, (2025), to protect the benefits of human well-being,
the application of gharar in all instances is not allowed. However, the engagement in practices involving
gharar and deception is prohibited in the Qur’an. In Surah al -Baqarah (2:188), Allah SWT highlights the
prohibition of transactions that contain elements of gharar; “Do not consume one another’s wealth unjustly,
nor deliberately bribe authorities in order to devour a portion of others’ property, knowing that it is a sin.
Gharar is prohibited by Quran 2:188 because hiding contract details leads to the unjust consumption of
another's wealth, which the verse directly forbids.
Furthermore, in a hadith cited from Azuan, (2025) which narrated by Abu Hurairah: The Prophet SAW
has prevented (us) from (doing) a sale (by means of a small stone throw and sale of goods in a gharar. This
prohibition seeks to safeguard buyers in sales transactions by prohibiting the sale of non -existent goods,
preventing price manipulation and ensuring mutual consent between contracting parties. Furthermore, it
preserves property and wealth among indi viduals, aligning with the objectives of Maqasid  al-shariah. In
this context, Islamic jurists emphasize when the subject matter of a transaction is unclear to the buyer, the
contract is deemed either invalid or, at minimum, legally defective. This principle is supported by the legal
maxim al-Gharar yufsid al-ʿuqūd, which holds that uncertainty can nullify a contract.

2.4 Contemporary scholars’ view
Contemporary scholars have conducted extensive analyses of the application of gharar in modern
commercial transactions. According to Zamharir  (2024), Wahbah al -Zuḥaylī, in Fiqh al -Islāmī wa
Adillatuhu, explains that online transactions are particularly prone to elements of gharar due to the absence
of face-to-face interaction and the difficulty in verifying the authenticity of products. He emphasizes that
contracts conducted through digital platforms must include clear and comprehensive information regarding
product specifications, pricing and return policies in cases where the item received does not align with the
description. Other than that, it has been stated by Yusuf al-Qaradawi as cited in Risnawati et al. (2024) that
elements of fraud, ambiguity and injustice must be avoided in online transactions, in accordance with the
general principle of Islamic jurisprudence, ‘al-gharar mufsidu li al-ʿuqūd’ (gharar invalidates the contract).
It was asserted that, to maintain public confidence and prevent actions that may potentially harm consumers,
transparency and accountability must be upheld by the seller.
2.5  Classification and types of gharar
Islamic jurists from various schools of thought including Imam Malik, Imam al -Shafie, Imam Abu
Hanifah, and Imam Aḥmad ibn Hanbal are in consensus that gharar (excessive uncertainty) in commercial
transactions is prohibited (Muthia Azzahra et al., 2024). While there are differences of opinion among the
schools regarding the degree of gharar that may be tolerated, there is unanimous agreement that substantial
or excessive gharar (gharar fāḥish)  must be avoided. Minor or insignificant gharar (gharar yasīr ),
however, may be permitted provided it does not lead to any considerable harm or injustice within the
contractual agreement.
According to Al-Saati (2003), the degree of permissibility of gharar can be classified into four types;
firstly, the prohibited gharar (excessive uncertainty). This form of gharar closely resembles gambling,
involving considerable risk, deception, and an imbalance of fairness, which often results in conflicts and
animosity. Traditional scholars such as Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn al -Qayyim compare excessive gharar to
maysir (gambling). Examples of this typical gharar including pebble, touch, and toss sales, have been
explicitly prohibited in authentic hadiths. Most scholars concur that the primary reason for banning all these
sales is due to their excessive gharar. Secondly, permissible gharar, according to scholars such as Al -
Shatibi, not every instance of gharar is prohibited. Minor or unavoidable gharar is accepted, particularly
when it does not result in conflicts or is necessary for the public good. Examples of this type of gharar
include two sales in one, the option sale, condition ( muallaq sale), gharar in the kind/type of the object,
gharar in the species of the object, gharar in the attributes of the object and gharar in the quantity of the
object. Although there is some flexibility, there is disagreement among scholars on what constitutes
permissible gharar. While certain schools of thought commonly accept specific forms, others continue to
be a topic of discussion and may even be dismissed by different groups. Thirdly, the acceptable gharar. In
Islamic finance, not all variations of gharar (risk or uncertainty) are considered forbidden. While certain
scholars view all types of risk as gharar, scholars like Ibn Taymiyyah argue that only excessive or gambling
like gharar is forbidden, not the general uncertainty found in trade. For instance, economic activities
naturally involve risk, such as changes in market demand, technology, or weather, and uncertainties about
product quality or business partners. These types of gharar are accepted since they are part of ordinary
business practices and do not involve deception or exploitative gain. Fourth, the mandatory gharar, where
gharar must be avoided for a contract to be valid. This is a well -accepted rule for contracts such as

2.6  Islamic consumerism and ethical finance
Islam offers a comprehensive guide on how individuals should conduct their lives. It includes
regulations for all aspects of human existence, covering areas such as business, economics, and legal issues.
Consequently, in Islam, its followers are expected to follow the established principles in all their endeavours
(Haque et al., 2017). The concept of Halalan Thayyiban based on the Quran and Sunnah, form the basis of
Islamic Consumerism. In addition to confirming that a product is permissible (halal), the importance of
quality, safety, and ethical factors in consumption is emphasized by thayyiban. The terms Halal and
“Thayyiban” are repeatedly found together in various verses of the Quran. For instance, in Surah al-Baqarah
verse 168, “O mankind, eat from whatever is on earth [that is] halal (lawful) and thayyiban (good) and do
not follow the footsteps of Satan. Indeed, he is to you a clear enemy”.
Thus, a product might be technically halal, but if it is sold in a way that creates uncertainty, it falls short
of fulfilling the thayyiban standard. For example, clothing may be halal in nature, but if the items in the
package are concealed and the customer is unaware of what they are purchasing, this ambiguity introduces
gharar (uncertainty), which is prohibited in Islam. According to Jie et al. (2023) the foundations of Islamic
business ethics are based on several essential principles that encourage justice, transparency, and social
responsibility in commercial transactions. Fun damental principles such as the prohibition of involvement
in dishonesty from sellers, underlining that honesty, integrity and trust are not just moral concepts but
crucial necessities in business. Islam prioritizes the principles of fairness and openness in every transaction
(Irmawati, 2021). This requires sellers to provide honest information about the products they offer. If the
seller hides defects in goods or provides descriptions that do not correspond to reality, then this action
constitutes fraud (tadlis) and is strictly prohibited in Islam.
Admonitions have also been raised by scholars such as Sheikh Abdul Aziz bin Baz regarding the rise
of fraud in unclear transactions, whether conducted physically or online. The importance of honesty,
transparency and moral accountability in all forms of transactions, including those in the digital realm, has
been strongly emphasized. According to him, as explained by (Juliana et al., 2022) sellers have a moral and
legal obligation to accurately explain the condition of goods to prospective buyers. This pers pective is
aligned with ethical deliberations as a pathway to barakah. According to research done by Ujiyanto (2025),
participants highlighted that avoiding riba, deception and exploitative behaviour are considered to seek
Allah’s pleasures and His blessings. The pursuit of barakah guided them to reject practices deemed
unethical, even when such decisions resulted in financial disadvantage. Other than that, the im portance of
mutual trust, a core value in both Islamic teachings and modern financial systems, is further highlighted
through the emphasis placed on honesty and clarity in trade. Mutual trust is based on respect for individual
and collective rights. Cultivating trust is essential not only for personal transactions but also for the
sustainability of financial markets (Vujnović-Gligorić et al., 2018). This is consistent with the principle of
halalan thayyiban, which emphasises lawful and wholesome business practices that uphold ethical values
and contribute to societal well-being.

2.7 Challenges to Upholding Traditional Islamic Business Ethics on Modern Live Sales Platform
Despite the clear foundations and ethical guidelines established in Islamic teachings, applying these
principles on digital platforms such as TikTok and Instagram Live remains challenging, reflecting the
broader struggle to uphold Islamic business ethics i n a highly globalized economy. According to (Sikki et
al., 2025), the dominance of secular economic system where profit maximations is prioritized over ethical
considerations is one of the significant issues. In global markets, to remain competitive, muslims
entrepreneurs are often pressured in yielding on certain principles especially those that operate under
different ethical frameworks.
Other than that, challenges such as regulatory diversity across countries, where some nations have
established sharia compliant business laws and regulations and others do not acknowledge Islamic
commercial principles, resulting in inconsistencies in enfor cement and compliance. Not to mention, the
perception gap between non -Muslim stakeholders and Islamic business ethics may lead to
misunderstandings and stereotypes which can cause disinclination from potential customers. In some cases,
even though Islamic ethical are adaptable and progressive, they are viewed as inflexible and incompatible
in some modern business trends. Lastly, inadequacy of knowledge in Islamic commercial jurisprudence,
lack of transparency and corruption within Muslims entrepreneurs disr upt the complete implementation of
ethical business practices. Hence, legal reforms as well as cultural transformation and ethical education are
required to address these issues.
In addition to these challenges, a researcher has been done to identify related issues in digital marketing
environments. Azwan (2025) concluded that addressing these shortcomings requires greater efforts to raise
public awareness about strengthening Shari ah compliance so these values can be passed on to future
generations particularly the concept of gharar. To minimize the risk of fraud in the second-hand (“bundle”)
clothing business, several precautionary measures should be observed. Individuals are advised to carefully
inspect the condition and grade of the clothing and to seek relevant information from s uppliers regarding
the items offered. For online transactions, it is essential to request additional photographs and verify the
credibility of the seller. The use of reputable platforms with established buyer‐protection mechanisms, as
well as a clear under standing of return and exchange policies, further enhances transaction security.
Employing secure payment methods, such as PayPal or credit cards, can also reduce the likelihood of
fraudulent activity. Moreover, awareness of potential warning signs such as unusually low prices or sellers
lacking a verifiable history is crucial.
Adhering to these measures increases the likelihood of a safe and reliable buying or selling experience

3.  METHODOLOGY
The author has employed a quantitative study by distributing questionnaire survey through various
social media platforms allowing open participation from Bruneian individuals between the ages of 18 and
45 years old and above of both male and female to gath er insights into their perceptions. The intended
sample size was 384 respondents. However, only 234 respondents from the public were obtained. Despite
this, according to the Raosoft calculator, the sample size is still adequate in terms of statistical precision, as
indicated by the approximate 95% confidence level and margin of error. However, as convenience and
voluntary online sampling were employed, these estimates reflect numeric al precision rather than
population representativeness. To ensure content validity, a pilot study was conducted with a small group
of 27 respondents to test the clarity, relevance, and structure of the questionnaire, and necessary refinements
were made. The reliability of the Likert-scale items was assessed using Cronbach’s Alpha to determine the
internal consistency of the measurement items.  To analyse quantitative data, IBM SPSS version 29 was
utilized to evaluate descriptive statistics such as frequenci es, percentages, and means to interpret the
responses. Whereas for open -ended questions, data was analysed by identifying the recurring themes and
insights related to respondents’ perceptions.

(i)  Respondents’ awareness and familiarity with the concept of Gharar
Based on the results from the close-ended questions, in the context of respondents’ familiarity with the
concept of gharar, most respondents recognize and familiar with the concept likely due to foundational
understanding and prior exposure. Not to mention, most respondents understand that gharar refers to
elements of ambiguity and aware its prohibition. Other than that, one of the questions under this section
examined whether respondents had learned about the concept of gharar through any sources. Most

Moreover, most respondents are also confident that they could identify examples of gharar in their daily
consumer transactions. These findings differ from those reported by Sikki et al., (2025), who identified
inadequate knowledge of Isltamic commercial jurisprudence as a challenge in upholding traditional Islamic
business practices. However, their study specifically focused on Muslim entrepreneurs. Therefore, this
limitation may not be applicable to general consumers.

SWT as wasteful behaviour is contrary to His commands.

In addition, the most frequently mentioned factors based on respondents’ answers were due to religious

due to features resembling gambling. According to Abdullah (2017), most scholars view gharar as being

principles including prevention of dishonesty, trust and integrity are crucial necessities in business and not
only a moral concept (Jie et al., 2023). Hence, truthfulness and transparent disclosure of information is one
of the ethical obligations placed between parties. Therefore, transparency is essential when practicing
hidden content bundles to ensure fairness and informed decision-making.

6.  CONCLUSION
In conclusion, this study aimed to examine the level of awareness among Bruneians about the shariah
compliance of hidden content bundles conducted through live streaming platforms. The results provide
insight into several areas related to respondents’ knowledge, attitudes, and general opinions abo ut the
concept of hidden content bundles. Referring to the first of the research objectives which is investigating
the level of awareness among Bruneians regarding the concept of gharar. Results show proof through
survey that most respondents possess awareness of gharar and recognize its connection to hidden content
bundles. As regards to the consumers’ perspective on the permissibility of such sales. Based on the findings,
most respondents are willing to accept the hidden content bundles practices if they adhere to shar iah
compliance, ensure fairness to buyers and maintain honesty between the sellers and buyers. Despite this
awareness, the findings further reveal that knowledge of gharar does not necessarily prevent acceptance of
such practices. This suggests a gap between theoretical understanding and actual consumer behaviour in
the context of digital commerce, where acceptance is influenced not only by religious knowledge but also
by perceived fairness and ethical conduct within the transaction. However, this gap should be interpreted
with caution, as the study employed a non -probability sampling method and relied on voluntary online
participation, which may limit the representativeness of the sample.
Furthermore, the practice of hidden content bundles is generally viewed as debatable and subject to
conditional acceptability within the Islamic context. Most respondents are familiar with the concept of
gharar and acknowledge its presence in hidden content bundles. However, the findings indicate that such
practices are generally viewed as non-compliant with Shariah due to elements of uncertainty, dispute with
business ethics, and their resemblance to gambling. Consequently, such practice is not entirely rejected, as
respondents emphasized that transparency, disclosure, reasonable pricing, and fair value can reduce these
concerns. Overall, the study concludes that hidden content bundles can only be deemed permiss ible when
conducted in accordance with Islamic business ethics, such as honesty, transparency, avoidance of waste,
and the prevention of harm to ensure fairness. Therefore, despite conditional acceptance, such practices are
more appropriately viewed as generally non-compliant in their current form.
Moreover, the findings provide practical implications for stakeholders involved in the digital
marketplace. For online sellers, the study highlights the importance of transparency, honesty and adequate
disclosure of product information to reduce uncertaint y and maintain consumer trust. Although hidden -
content bundle sales may serve as an innovative marketing strategy, sellers should ensure that such practices
do not involve excessive uncertainty or unfair advantages between buyers and sellers. For policymak ers
and relevant Islamic authorities, this study provides insight into the need for greater awareness and potential
guidance regarding emerging digital commerce practices to ensure alignment with Shariah principles and
consumer protection. Furthermore, the study encourages consumers to evaluate online purchasing practices
based on ethical considerations rather than solely on trends, promotions, or emotional motivations such as
FOMO.
This study contributes to the understanding of halal e -commerce in Brunei by highlighting the
limitations of existing consumer protection frameworks in addressing Shariah -specific concerns,
