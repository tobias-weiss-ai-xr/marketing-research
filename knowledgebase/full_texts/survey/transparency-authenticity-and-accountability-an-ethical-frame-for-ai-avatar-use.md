# Transparency, Authenticity, and Accountability: An Ethical Frame for AI Avatar Use in Digital Marketing

Transparency, Authenticity, and Accountability:
An Ethical Frame for AI Avatar Use in Digital Marketing

Andra Blaj*
Nicoleta Andreea Neacșu

Abstract

Nowadays, any brand can generate an AI avatar to interact with its customers, or a synthetic
brand representative, to communicate key messages, all in a matter of hours at minimal cost. The
increased accessibility of AI generative entities is transforming digital marketing at a pace that can’t
keep up with ethical regulatory frameworks.
We examine three recurring issues in the literature: limited transparency, simulated authenticity,
and diffused accountability, based on 35 peer-reviewed studies published between 2020 and 2026.
We maintain that these issues cannot be addressed separately because a brand can be compliant
with transparency policies and, at the same time, take advantage of parasocial attachment, and the
involved parties can establish conduct policies without accountability for the avatar-consumer
relationship.
Therefore, we propose an integrated ethical framework in which transparency, authenticity, and
accountability are interconnected obligations that define the ethical conditions for AI avatars'
responsible and sustainable marketing practices.

Key words: AI avatars, transparency, accountability, ethics, authenticity, digital marketing

1. Introduction

Online platforms such as Synthesia, Veed, and HeyGen allow brands to generate digital avatars
that can talk, express emotions, and gesticulate, acting as real human representatives of the company.
Creating complex CGI virtual influencers, such as Imma or Lil Miquela, that support brand
communication is now an accessible opportunity for any company, regardless of size or marketing
budget. Generative AI has transformed the digital avatar from a niche experiment into a brand-
standard communication tool, accelerating adoption. Platforms such as TikTok Shop and Taobao
Live attract millions of consumers daily who interact extensively with AI avatars in live commerce.
The expansion of digital avatars is happening at such a rapid pace that ethical debate cannot keep
up. Three categories of ethical problems with systemic potential are noticeable. The most prominent
and largely discussed is transparency. Consumers interacting with an AI avatar may be mistaken by
its appearance and treat it as a human  (Arsenyan & Mirowska, 2021; Martin Sierra et al., 2024).
There are several scenarios to be investigated here: the avatar's realism, the non-disclosure of the
avatar, or the limited digital literacy. For example, the Lil Miquela case illustrates this dimension
precisely. This CGI avatar with millions of Instagram followers was launched in 2016 with no clear
status for her fictional form, and no distinction between commercial and personal content was
explained in her posts.

Another ethical issue arises in the interaction process. While aware of the avatar's artificial form,
consumers can develop emotional attachment to avatars and therefore confuse strategic design with
authenticity (Arsenyan & Mirowska, 2021; Jain et al., 2025). An emerging concern is the lack of a
framework for holding the avatar accountable for its actions (Nabavi et al., 2024; Khin & Adderley,
2026). From offensive content to racial stereotypes, consequences for harmful avatar behavior
remain absent. The FN Meka case, discussed in detail in the Accountability section, illustrates this
dimension.
In the European context, the AI Act transparency obligations come into effect in August 2026
(European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024). It represents a significant step
towards the governance of AI-generated content. The framework explicitly regulates chatbots,
deepfakes, and information of public interest; it remains unclear whether the obligation applies to
marketing AI avatars. This ambiguity, combined with the fragmented obligations to disclose across
platforms, inconsistent implementation, and the absence of a collective accountability mechanism
for all actors involved in the use of avatars, calls for an ethically integrated framework from both
academic and practical standpoints.
The present article addresses this gap by exploring the conditions for an ethical framework
structured around these three foundational concepts for governing AI avatars in digital marketing:
transparency, authenticity, and accountability. In contrast to existing analyses that treat these
dimensions separately, we argue that each pillar presupposes and reinforces the others. The article’s
contribution is conceptual to the emerging literature on AI avatar governance and for the practices
of brands, platforms, and regulators that either use or research the integration of synthetic entities in
digital marketing.

2. Literature review

AI avatars are a growing category of synthetic agents increasingly used in digital marketing. They
are designed in various technical forms and with different levels of autonomy. This article considers
an operational definition of AI avatars that includes both scripted virtual characters (CGI) such as
Lil Miquela or Imma, with partial or fully generative AI behavior, and synthetic avatars generated
by platforms such as HeyGen or Synthesia.
In our analysis, we consider their technical forms less relevant to the ethical analysis than their
common functions, such as being controlled by organizations, communicating commercial messages,
and exhibiting emotional expressions and responses to consumers. This functional approach is
consistent with the emerging avatar marketing theory proposed by Miao et al. (2022, p. 69), which
defines avatars as digital entities with anthropomorphic appearance, controlled by human operators
or software, and capable of interacting with users.
Generative AI is defined as a system that can automatically and simultaneously produce
personalized visual, textual, and audio content, in contrast to classic CGI, which requires manual,
demanding construction. This technological democratization has systemic consequences: ethical
issues with AI avatars are not exclusive to large companies but pose potential risks to any
organization that uses them, regardless of industry or size (Martin Sierra et al., 2024; Khin &
Adderley, 2026).
The central mechanism through which AI avatars influence consumers is anthropomorphism,
meaning attributing human traits, emotions, and social cues to non-human agents (Wang et al., 2025,
p. 513), which shapes consumers' responses to AI-driven entities in digital marketing.
In social media and online live-streaming shopping contexts, anthropomorphic design reduces
psychological distance and increases consumers' willingness to accept virtual streamers through
cognitive trust (Chen et al., 2024), while also enhancing engagement and perceived authenticity (Shi
et al., 2025; Williams, 2025).
Importantly, these effects are maximized when the visual and behavioral anthropomorphic cues
are aligned. The coherence between aspect and behavior reduces the psychological distance and
consolidates the purchase intention (Cao et al., 2026, p. 9).

Uysal et al. (2022, p.1155) investigate a constant pattern: the anthropomorphic design determines
consumers to attribute agency, intention, and moral responsibility to the AI avatar. It seems that the
avatar is making choices. It does not. This perception has a direct impact on the three ethical
dimensions discussed in the article.
The parasocial relationship concept is another fundamental mechanism to understand the AI
avatar impact. This relation was questioned in the context of celebrities and human influencers, and
is defined by asymmetric interactions in which the consumers develop familiarity, friendship, and
loyalty feelings towards an entity with no capacity for reciprocal engagement (Arsenyan &
Mirowska, 2021; Breves & Van Berlo, 2025).
The article operates with the distinction between retrospective accountability and prospective
responsibility. This separation clarifies the obligations for the actors involved and supports proactive
governance before ethical issues arise (Diakopoulos, 2025, p. 126). It is also essential, given the
opaque, fragmented decision-making among creators, brand managers, and platforms regarding the
operationalization of AI avatars as fixing responsibility becomes critical when decisions lead to
adverse effects. Proactive ethical frameworks are necessary to clarify obligations before issues arise
(Al Haj Eid et al., 2024, p. 2300-2301).
The ethical literature capturing these dimensions is fragmented. Studies on transparency focus on
the disclosure effects on consumers' trust (Kembau, 2025; Majerova, 2025). Research about
authenticity highlights the paradox of parasocial relationships with virtual avatars (Arsenyan &
Mirowska, 2021; Jain et al., 2025). Normative research addresses the organizational accountability
in AI (Al Haj Eid et al., 2024).
There are also studies that treat ethical dimensions together. Pushparaj et al. (2025, p. 631)
identify authenticity, trust, and parasocial relationships as being central to the interaction between
consumers and virtual avatars and offer empirical support to argue that these concepts operate
together. Al Haj Eid et al. (2024, p. 2297) propose that transparency, fairness, and consumer
autonomy are not technically independent properties of AI-driven marketing systems but are
fundamental ethical dimensions of AI systems that require an integrated AI governance framework.
This perspective anticipates the proposed ethical framework of the article, which treats
transparency, authenticity, and accountability not as standalone concepts but as interdependent
pillars, a gap in the existing literature that this article addresses. The analysis is even more relevant
given that one of the few experimental studies available confirms that while anthropomorphism
enhances purchase intention through social presence and trust, transparency disclosure moderates
and weakens these effects, indicating that ethical considerations around AI disclosure create
measurable trade-offs in commercial communication effectiveness (Wang et al., 2025, p. 524).

3. Research methodology

The present article uses literature review as a research methodology, as a conceptual endeavor of
synthesis and critical analysis. We have identified a pattern in the current literature and proposed a
new integrated structure that has not been discussed previously. Literature review is a rigorous,
autonomous methodology recognized in academic research as an independent study that synthesizes
existing knowledge, identifies gaps, and proposes research directions (Kraus et al., 2022; Chigbu et
al., 2023). In emerging domains with fragmented literature, this approach is relevant for developing
integrated conceptual frameworks (Stroumpoulis & Kopanaki, 2022, p. 2).
The analyzed corpus comprises 35 peer-reviewed studies published between 2020 and 2026,
selected for thematic relevance at the intersection of AI avatars, digital marketing, and ethics. The
inclusion criteria are (1) publications in indexed journals or academic volumes; (2) coverage for at
least one of the three studied ethical dimensions: transparency, authenticity, and accountability; (3)
articles published in the period 2020–2026, to reflect the current state of the literature in the emerging
field.
Through critical analysis, we have identified three categories of ethical issues with systemic
potential in the use of AI in digital marketing, which we argue are inseparable. The ethical framework
proposed is the result of this synthesis.

4. Findings

4.1 Transparency as a foundational ethical principle

We propose that transparency is the first and most visible ethical dimension of using AI avatars
in digital marketing. It stands for the brand’s obligation to inform consumers regarding the synthetic
nature of the entities with which they interact and towards the commercial character of the
communication. Consumers are unable to properly evaluate the credibility of the source, the
communication intention, and the objectivity of the recommendation without a clear disclosure frame
(Arsenyan & Mirowska, 2021), as the Lil Miquela case showed. Consumers could not distinguish
between a paid campaign and an apparent expression of the avatar's personality.
Recent research confirms that disclosure can partially reduce these effects. Gu et al. (2026, p. 11)
found that causal evidence suggests explicit AI labeling consistently reduces brand attitudes across
multiple product categories and advertising formats, with the negative effect being particularly
pronounced when emotional appeals are used. This suggests that disclosure alone does not resolve
consumer skepticism; the content of the message matter as well. Kembau et al. (2025, p. 1230)
demonstrate that disclosure can positively influence purchase intention when it is embedded
organically in communication rather than as a formal, mandatory element. Other studies show that
the disclosure visibility strongly influences consumers' answers; prominently disclosed avatars are
perceived as less credible than those with more subtle disclosures (Van Berlo & Breves, 2025, p.8).
The transparency efficiency is not uniform. Research on algorithmic personalization shows that
the disclosed benefits depend on the user's digital literacy. Users with low digital literacy can be
confused by technical explanations rather than adjusting their behavior (Shin, 2020; Shin et al., 2022;
Moon et al., 2025). Applied to AI avatars, this finding suggests that a disclosure label has different
effects on consumers.
In practice, transparency obligations extend to the algorithmic logic that determines the avatar's
behavior, data personalization practices, and to the commercial objectives that dictate what the avatar
says and when. Benimma and Bennouna (2025, p.190) indicate that the more sophisticated AI
systems are the more they undermine consumers’ trust because their decisional logic remains opaque
and inaccessible to them. Majerova (2025) illustrates that there is still a persistent credibility deficit
in AI avatars even when full disclosure is in place. Consumers still perceive a significant difference
between the entity’s artificial nature and the projected authenticity.
Altogether, we can see that transparency is a necessary but insufficient condition; its limitations
reveal authenticity as a complementary ethical pillar. Transparency is not just a labeling obligation;
it is a responsibility of communication design that enables customers to understand whom they
interact with, whose interests they represent, and what their intentions are. To achieve this definition,
the concept of authenticity is central to the analysis.

4.2 Authenticity as a fabricated paradox

The most common concept used to describe authenticity is paradox; this description is a valid
perspective for AI avatars in digital marketing, too. More precisely, we encounter the paradox of
fabricated authenticity with AI avatars. The consumer knows that the avatar is artificial; it has no
personal experiences, no real vulnerability or independent identity beyond the commercial brand
intention that operates it, but still, consumers can develop emotional attachments to the avatar
(Arsenyan & Mirowska, 2021), which raises ethical concerns. For example, the Japanese avatar
Imma communicates a coherent personality through posts about day-to-day experiences, personal
reflections, and partnerships. Here, we can recognize the tension between a synthetic identity and the
ability to foster emotional attachment.
AI avatars fabricate authenticity as a projected effect based on their design qualities, such as visual
realism and emotional closeness, but do not express identity in contrast with human influencers who
build their credibility on personal history and real experience (Jain et al., 2025; Arsenyan &
Mirowska, 2021).

Avatars develop a strong social presence; by expressing emotions, they capture the attention and
convert through building artificial relationships with consumers. This bond is explained in literature
through the concept of parasocial relationships. Parasocial relationships are sustained through
continuous interaction on social media and by the avatar's apparent capacity to respond to messages
and comments, thereby fostering the character's narrative consistency (Arsenyan & Mirowska, 2021;
Breves & Van Berlo, 2025).
By exceeding expectations, Breves & Van Berlo (2025, p. 19) demonstrate, through empirical
research, that virtual avatars can generate parasocial relationships with an intensity comparable to
those with human influencers, without authentic reciprocity.
Studies indicate that, regardless of the cognitive evaluation of source credibility, the intensity of
a relationship with an avatar significantly influences purchase intention (Farivar & Wang, 2023, cited
in Yin et al., 2025, p. 2838). In other words, consumers may rationally know that avatars have limited
credibility, but their emotional attachment would still influence their purchasing behavior. A
pertinent question arises: Is the influence from an entity with no real identity ethical?
A complementary dimension is the relation between the degree of realism and commercial
efficiency. Studies reveal that this relationship is unstable.
Pan et al. (2024, pp. 3233–3234, 3242–3243) demonstrate that the degree of anthropomorphism
of avatars' images does not directly translate into enhance consumer responses. The more the avatar
almost seems human, but not quite, the more the avatar is problematic. Consumers find them
bothering and are less triggered to buy, the AI entity drives the strongest algorithmic aversion and
the lowest purchase intention.
Gutuleac et al. (2024, pp. 1425–1426) provide empirical evidence that highly anthropomorphized
virtual influencers elicit greater uncanniness among consumers, which negatively affects both
intention to follow and purchase behavior. Importantly, however, this effect can be attenuated when
social cues are present. Specifically, when a real person appears alongside the virtual influencer in
the post, the negative impact of anthropomorphism on uncanniness is significantly reduced.
Thus, it seems that there is no optimal level of realism; the commercial efficiency of the avatar
balances visual appearance with behavioral coherence, an equilibrium dictated by the audience,
platform, and communicational objective. Consumers evaluate virtual influencers as being less
credible than human influencers, especially when the message presupposes a personal, authentic
narrative (Khalfallah & Keller, 2025). Moreover, the credibility differences depend on cultural
perspective. The disclosure standards and the character design cannot be applied in all markets
(Khalfallah & Keller, 2025; Khin & Adderley, 2026). What can seem charming in one context can
be perceived as deceptive in another.
In comparison to the avatar transparency problem, which, in practice, has solutions, authenticity
raises a structural problem. For AI avatars, authenticity cannot be an inner expressive state; this is a
problem that design cannot solve either. There is no disclosure label or design that can make an AI
avatar authentic. Even an informed consumer interacting with an AI avatar continues to be vulnerable
to emotional manipulation through parasocial mechanisms, which cannot be neutralized by
disclosure. The fabricated authenticity is not an issue of deception; the consumer is not deceived by
the nature of the avatar, but rather a problem of disproportionate power: the brand owns the control
of the avatar and its communication strategy while the consumer is emotionally invested in a
commercial unilateral relationship. This unbalanced relationship, empirically analyzed as stronger
effects of affective over cognitive processes in parasocial relations (Yin et al., 2025, p. 2841) calls
for organizational accountability as a third interconnected ethical principle.

4.3 Accountability as a dispersed organizational responsibility

If transparency defines the terms of communication and authenticity raises questions about the
consumer-avatar relationship, accountability settles who is responsible for the consequences of that
relationship, and on what basis.
The FN Meka case illustrates the consequences of a lack of accountability in managing avatars.
FN Meka is surrounded by controversies, such as the use of racially offensive language (including
the N-word in songs produced by non-black writers), its aesthetics built on African American culture

for the reputational risk association. Capitol Records has decided to drop the contract due to the
media pressure, not because of an existing normative frame. No parties assumed accountability for
the content created, including the avatar creators, the owning company, Factory New, or the record
label. The case illustrates a governance failure: the absence of pre-launch accountability mechanisms
— specifically the clear delineation of roles and responsibilities across design, deployment, and
procurement stages, and the lack of an organizational AI risk tolerance framework that Xia et al.
(2024, p. 103) identify as foundational process metrics for responsible AI development. Moreover,
the case highlights that the accountability void is not abstract: reputational and cultural damages are
identifiable, as are the accountable actors.
The normative literature identifies three interconnected layers of accountability that need to be
operationalized in the context of AI avatars: organizational, platform, and cultural.
At the organizational level, brands using AI avatars need to be held accountable for the content
they generate and distribute, regardless of the generative AI system's autonomy level. The avatar's
technical autonomy does not transfer moral or legal accountability to the system itself — the brand
remains the responsible actor in relation to consumers (Nabavi et al., 2024, pp. 58–60; Xia et al.,
2024, p. 103; Khin & Adderley, 2026). Companies that design, train, and implement these systems
own accountability, no matter whether the users realize it or not. This responsibility covers the
accuracy of the communicated information, the fairness of behavioral targeting, and any resulting
harm caused by deceiving or manipulative practices (Nabavi et al., 2024, pp. 58–60).
Al Haj Eid et al. (2024,p. 2297) extend this perspective and propose the inclusion of proactive
audit content mechanisms, clear procedures for withdrawing and correcting abuses, and accessible
channels for consumers to signal problems.
At the platform-level, Shahid et al. (2024, p. 229) and Papagiannidis et al. (2025, p. 11) identify
structural responsibilities for developers, organizations, and intermediaries – platforms as being
essential for AI ethical adoption. Fairness, privacy protection, and oversight are not optional add-
ons; they are fundamental requirements. Xia et al. (2024, pp. 103, 107) add the platforms' governance
accountability and suggest that the intermediaries - distribution platforms - have the obligation to
impose minimum transparency and conduct standards for synthetic entities operating within their
ecosystem.
Social responsibility principles must be integrated into the decision-making process from the
design phase, not as afterthoughts in conformity measures (Morante et al., 2024, p. 4).
Returning to the FN Meka case, Khin & Adderley (2026) offer a complementary perspective on
cultural accountability. Brands that create AI avatars that mimic cultural identities have a
responsibility to cultural communities beyond legal and commercial obligations. This perspective is
relevant as technology makes it easier to create an avatar that adopts the aesthetics, language, and
cultural symbols of a community without a connection to that community. The FN Meka was not a
technical error; it can be interpreted as a cultural governance error, produced in the absence of a
cultural impact evaluation mechanism prior to launch.
In practice, this can entail internal review of AI-generated content, periodic audits of avatar
behavioral automation, if in place, and public documentation that holds organizations responsible for
deploying virtual influencers (Khin & Adderley, 2026).

5. Implications

The implications for companies include acknowledging that the use of AI avatars implies ethical
risks that require proactive management. Transparency means developing communication plans
calibrated to the audience profile; a label alone does not guarantee ethical consideration for
consumers. Authenticity can be managed responsibly by preventing the intentional exploitation of
parasocial relationships for commercial goals through personalized narratives, rather than design
alone. Moreover, technological democratization implies that such obligations are relevant to all
company profiles, regardless of size or domain.

For platforms, accountability cannot be completely delegated to content creators. They have the
obligation to impose minimum transparency and conduct standards for synthetic entities operating
in their ecosystems. Also, at the platform level, where algorithmic decisions about content
dissemination can amplify existing inequities, there is a complementary dimension of fairness that
must be applied to ensure these systems do not promote synthetic entities over human creators.
For consumers, digital literacy is a precondition of an informed interaction with AI avatars.
Fassiaux (2023, pp. 712, 714–715) argues that consumer autonomy, understood as the independence
from unwanted commercial influence, is an ethical precondition that legitimizes marketing as a social
system. In the context of AI avatars, this autonomy is reduced by algorithmic personalization.
From the public policy perspective, digital literacy is a complementary governance tool. Article
4 of the AI European Act imposes explicit obligations regarding AI digital literacy and fosters
national-level digital literacy initiatives, which serve as another governance tool alongside the
obligations imposed on companies and platforms.
For researchers, the AI avatars literature needs interdisciplinary approaches to integrate ethical,
legal, and behavioral perspectives. Existing studies primarily examine transparency, authenticity, and
responsibility in isolation, thereby limiting the overall understanding of systemic ethical risks.
Empirical validation of integrated frameworks and cross-cultural analyses remains the most pressing
research gap.

6. Conclusions

This article explores the conditions for a conceptual ethical framework between three
interdependent pillars: transparency, authenticity, and accountability to govern AI avatars in digital
marketing. The current literature is fragmented regarding these dimensions; no coherent system has
been found.
The analysis showed that each pillar addresses a distinct problem generated by the specific nature
of AI avatars. As avatars become more realistic, the boundary between real and artificial agents blurs,
and the risk of deception increases when synthetic identities and the algorithmic processes that enable
them are not disclosed to consumers (Martin Sierra et al., 2024; Khin & Adderley, 2026).
Transparency addresses this informational asymmetry, and its efficacy depends on the audience's
digital literacy and on how disclosure is included in the communication, not on a simple AI-generated
label. A related problem is consumer autonomy: AI systems can suggest behavior through subtle,
mostly invisible, personalized algorithmic nudging, raising ethical questions about fairness and
manipulation that traditional advertising was not designed to address (Waqas & Qadri, 2025).
Authenticity raises a more profound structural problem. The design qualities that make an avatar
influential, such as realism, emotional warmth, and coherent character, generate authenticity
expectations at the same time. The synthetic entity cannot uphold lived experience; authenticity for
AI avatars is therefore an expressive attribute transformed into a projected strategic result (Jain et al.,
2025; Arsenyan & Mirowska, 2021).
Accountability is the third dimension that highlights a constant negotiated tension among the
actors involved in the creation and distribution of AI avatars, which is addressed in the literature and
in regulatory practice, too. The platforms that enable AI avatar creation can argue that they do not
control the commercial use of the entity; the company that uses the avatar can claim that it cannot be
held accountable for the technical content generated by an external system; and the distribution
platform can invoke its status as a neutral intermediary. The result is an accountability void in which
no actor assumes explicit responsibility for the avatar's performance. We consider that the technical
autonomy of the avatar does not transfer the moral responsibility, regardless of whether the decisional
chain is distributed between creators, brands, and platforms.
The proposed system can function only if the three dimensions are treated as intertwined. A brand
can check the disclosure label and still create an appealing, realistic avatar capable of authenticity. It
can have clear policies and still maintain the algorithmic process that drives the avatar's behavior,
invisible to consumers.

Taken together, the three dimensions indicate that the current ethical regulation is insufficient to
govern AI avatars in digital marketing. Existing ethical frameworks were built for human actors with
real experiences and clear attribution of responsibility, conditions that synthetic identities do not
fulfill (Khin & Adderley, 2026). This structural mismatch demands an integrated framework, not just
an extension of existing rules.
The pillars define the ethical conditions for AI avatar use, not as constraints but as a foundation
for responsible and sustainable marketing practice.
This article is subject to limitations that reside in its primarily conceptual character and the lack
of empirical validation across cultural platforms or different demographic groups. The most
prominent gap is the lack of intercultural analysis of AI avatars: the research area focuses mainly on
Western and East Asian contexts, overlooking substantial differences in cultural norms and consumer
protection policies across markets (Khalfallah & Keller, 2025). Another important direction is the
longitudinal study of exposure effects to AI avatars on consumer behavior, and the empirical analysis
of these effects on authentic human communication. Future research should test this framework as
an evaluation tool to assess platforms' compliance with disclosure requirements and to explore its
extension to fairness and private data protection.
