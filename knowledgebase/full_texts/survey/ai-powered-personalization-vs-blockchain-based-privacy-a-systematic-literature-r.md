Introduction:AI drives hyper personalization in digital marketing while blockchain
offers privacy and security. This review addresses the tension between consumer
demand for customized experiences and growing concern over data safety.
Methods: This study applies the PRISMA framework to systematically review 56
peer reviewed papers published between 2015 and 2024 addressing AI
personalization, blockchain privacy, and consumer trade offs in digital marketing.
Results: Three central themes emerged: (1) AI drives hyper personalization and
ROI strategies, (2) blockchain enhances data security, trust, and GDPR
compliance, (3) consumers face trade offs between convenience and privacy.
Consumers accept personalized marketing when the mechanism is transparent
and under their control. Blockchain reduces certain ethical issues linked to AI,
including data exploitation and lack of auditability, but does not resolve
algorithmic bias or scalability challenges. Twenty five percent of the analyzed
research originates from India, showing regional concentration, while Africa and
Latin America remain under represented.
Discussion: Marketers should adopt blockchain audited AI systems, such as
transparent recommendation engines and decentralized data marketplaces, to
build consumer trust. Policymakers should establish hybrid regulatory
ecosystems that balance innovation with ethical compliance, including
GDPR consistent consent mechanisms and global interoperability standards.
Cross discipline collaboration remains necessary to align technology with
consumer centric values and ensure equitable adoption of AI and
blockchain across markets.

1 Introduction
The rapid evolution of digital technologies ushers in a new transformed marketing era,
marked by two powerful forces-in artificial intelligence (AI) and blockchain-and means
hyper-personalization through AI and promise privacy and security through blockchain
(Wirth, 2018; Feng et al., 2021). These two features reflect the opposing sides of a coin that
complicates the reality of consumer demand for customized experiences and heightened
unease about data safety. Indeed, the future will bring a paradigm shift to all industries-from
retail to healthcare-as businesses compete to benefit from extreme technological
advancements while facing ethical and operational challenges. This analysis draws from

It is the way customers interact with businesses that has brought
change in marketing due to AI. Machine learning models applied to
web-based models of data give real time personalized content,
predictive analysis, and deep granular customer segmentation.
AI-driven tools, such as recommendation systems for completing
sales and increasing customer allegiance, have also been used as a
reference. Yin et al. (2025) explains the concept of AI-enhanced
product recommendation engines that personalize relationships in
the context of e-commerce turning passive browsing into active
purchase behavior. Likewise, Hemlatha et al. (2024) argues that AI
segmentation should no longer be an optional but compulsory
endeavor for businesses intending to do successful business in
this highly competitive environment. Apart from retail, AI is
applied into other fields like healthcare and pharmacy for the
refinement of marketing strategies (Mirwan, et al., 2023). Kumar
et al. (2023) accentuates that AI assists healthcare providers in high
optimization in warehousing decision-making, while Khanna et al.,
2020 explores the area in pharmaceutical marketing where
predictive analytics add to the features of patient engagement
and compliance. These examples serve to demonstrate how
diverse AI is in securing effectiveness and innovation across
industries.
Social media and content marketing have also been
examples of what AI can deliver. According to Capatina
et al. (2020) , the successful applications of AI-based social
media tools depend on their capacity to really deliver on their
promises in automating things like content scheduling or
audience sentiment analysis. Further, Dumitriu and
Popescu, (2020) presents a stepwise model of incorporating
AI into marketing processes along with a stressed importance
on the keyword optimization for better digital visibility.
Meanwhile, Kose and Sert, (2017) demonstrates how AI
simplifies content creation such that it resonates with
consumer preferences, thus enhancing engagement metrics.
However, the spread of AI comes at its own price. Hildebrand
(2019) points out to biases inherent in algorithms and data
private breaches or failures of AI that can lead to consumer
mistrust. All these are reasons that point to the need for ethics
and a strong governance framework.
With this development in AI technology, blockchain has risen as
one of the primary enabling technologies for security and
transparency in data. In an era where breaches and misuse of
data prevail, the decentralized structure instituted through
blockchain can be the way forward to secure sensitive data.
According to Saleh (2024) and Kshetri (2025), blockchain may
help AI systems provide additional integrity of data, so datasets
used to personalize would be tamper-proof and audit capable. For
instance, blockchain can keep decentralized AI models away from
unauthorized changes, thus promoting an aspect of trust in an
automated decision-making process. This is more relevant in sectors
like cybersecurity, where Saleh (2024) notes how blockchain’s
immutability complements AI in detecting threats to build an
invincible defense from cyber-attacks. Similarly, there are all-
around ethical compliance where blockchain excels. Butterworth
(2018) explains how it matches up with GDPR principles, from
which it follows that blockchain can now turn consent management
into an automated task and thus assure compliance. Kshetri (2025)
adds that blockchain strengthens intellectual property rights in AI
development, enabling transparent attribution of digital assets.
These attributes match the rising consumer anxieties against data
exploitation, such as in Duda et al. (2022) study, which positions
blockchain as a tool to mitigate privacy risks in AI applications. By
decentralizing data storage and processing, the blockchain cuts
reliance on centralized entities, hence reducing the risk to breaches.
However, the real shortcoming in the research remains a critical
void as concerns synthesized findings regarding consumer adoption
of the personalization-privacy trade-off. Existing literature may be
highly extensive with valuation on how AI works in marketing or on
the other hand, the composite technical points of blockchain; few
have even studied its specified ethical impactions. For example,
while Verma et al. (2021), Casino (2019) and Chintalapati and
Pandey (2021) review the application of AI and blockchain,
respectively, they do not cross into these domains to gauge
consumer perceptions. This leaves a huge gap, especially when
consumer trust is a very fragile commodity. Trust and perceived
sacrifice emerge, according to Ameen’s perspective (2021), as
mediating factors in the deployment of AI because to sustain
such convenience-driven personalization often comes at the
expense of privacy concerns. So far, no such framework exists
that would reconcile these tensions using transparency through
blockchain. Hildebrand (2019), Gao et al. (2023) and Beyari
(2025) also refer to the ethical traps in AI, but they offer limited
solutions to integrate privacy-preserving technologies like
blockchain.
Preliminarily, model frameworks that are ethical-natured call
for an interdisciplinary approach. As posited by Casino (2019)
and Kshetri (2025), the ideal decentralized governance of AI
should incorporate data sovereignty, thereby providing the
users with authority over their information. With AI
processing and humans supervising compliance with ethical
standards, hybrid people AI collaboration schemes would
accentuate efficiency with accountability (Stone et al., 2020;
Davenport et al., 2019). Privacy provisions at the design stages
will then be supported by the application of the blockchain
technology method toward defining consent management,
leading to their future uptake in some industries as core
standards for embedding ethics in marketing technologies
(Butterworth, 2018; Kumar et al., 2020). Additionally,
frameworks should consider sectoral specifications. In good
health, for instance, AI has been supposed as secured by
blockchain in patient level trust through an open and
transparent tracking of the use of their data; in e-commerce, it
could do away with straightforward personalized promotions with
the cost of privacy.
Blockchain offers promising mechanisms, e.g., immutable audit
trails and decentralized consent management, that can reduce some
ethical risks of AI, particularly around data transparency and
compliance. However, as this review shows, it does not eliminate
algorithmic bias or address all privacy concerns (e.g., the right to be
forgotten conflicts). Recent systematic work, such as Reitano et al.
(2024) on AI in agri-food supply chains, offers methodological
guidance for synthesising evidence on AI-driven consumer
interfaces–an approach that informs the present review.

2 Theoretical framework
2.1 AI-Powered personalization:
revolutionizing consumer engagement
Artificial intelligence (AI) has significantly revolutionized
personalization in marketing by making companies capable of
launching highly targeted tactics specifically catering to individual
clients’ whims and fancies (Jarek and Mazurek, 2019; Devang et al.,
2019). Their technology uses recommendation systems just for that:
to analyze huge datasets like browsing patterns, purchase history,
and different technical aspects, which are then applied in a
predictive manner for consumer preferences (Vinerean and
Opreana, 2024; Wen et al., 2024; Markić et al., 2015). Saleh
(2024) gives his perspective on the operation of AI
recommendation engines on e-commerce platforms like Amazon,
they enhance the engagement of consumers who have been served
the best of products or content set out for them and this is translated
to results in terms of revenue increases and client loyalty. By now,
Hemlatha et al. (2024) has already cemented the place of customer
segmentation made quite essential by AI with modern marketers
tapping into smaller groups of people and reaching out to them
through campaigns. AI, strategically big in healthcare, makes
possible enormous benefits for personalized outreach to patients
(Anshari et al., 2018). Kumar et al. (2023) disputes that AI
optimization in reaching patients is possible because of AI’s
decisions in acting based on the information that was developed
ahead using lagged patient data which include reminders of
medication adherence, or what sort of a wellness treatment plan
shall work for the patient.
Another critical contribution of AI to personalization is
chatbots. These are used as tools to take on consumer interaction
and provide instantaneous solutions to inquiries or problems, albeit
collecting data for further crafting their personalized
communication strategy Kühl et al. (2019) provides an example
of how chatbots learn from the content of social media on a massive
scale to identify patterns of emerging needs, consequently
presenting businesses with an opportunity to intervene
accordingly. Brands that rely on AI for customer service may
face some dangers. Hildebrand (2019) notes that chatbots
perceive customer’s data to trigger inequalities in biased data
settings, such as promoting responses to select demographics
appealing to certain income-bracket levels.
This highlights the need for companies to have their AI ethics,
transparently human-touch interfaces with the users. For all these
systems I hope and really beg for any activity that gives us an ethical
touch. AI and predictive methods have the transformative effect as
well. AI craves for the prospective planning so that it could predict
the time that campaigns should be launched, what content should fill
the crescendo, and which channels are right. Differentiation would
not work without knowing how BDPFs work further to enhance AI
against points of conflict. Subsequently, marketers learn about how
their AI will play to the qualities of certain customer segments.
Supplying such knowledge does create trust. Though, if automation
is overdone, all concerns are concentrated on the alienation of the
end users. Hildebrand (2019) argues the AI’s good implementation
appears to benefit humans over non-personalization, where
customers feel that people are all from being observed in binary
all to zero points in some white paper.
2.2 Blockchain-based privacy: safeguarding
data in a decentralized ecosystem
Concerns surrounding privacy have gained the technological
infusions that are mostly left centralized. Blockchain essentially
enhances data security by decentralizing data control (Saidu
et al., 2025). The tamper-proof ledger is used to strengthen
cyber-security efforts by further fortifying decentralized AI
models through blockchain technology against any manipulation,
thereby stabilizing threat detection (Saleh, 2024). Kshetri (2025) says
that the blockchain affords auditable records of consumer
interactions, like ad-clicks and purchases, thereby limiting
chances of fraud and accounting for their marketing. A
blockchain-based system that would track user data across
campaigns would have increased transparency, lessening distrust.
Decentralized identity management is within this range of
innovations. Blockchain enables users to manage their digital
identities, sharing only what is necessary with marketers. Duda
et al. (2022) argues that such an approach could minimize AI privacy
risks by removing centralized data repositories—tempting targets
for breaches. For instance, a GDPR-compliant approach might
involve blockchain in automating consent management
(Butterworth, 2018). Consumers could grant or revoke access to
data via smart contracts, with automatic enforcement of privacy
regulation compliance. Casino (2019) and Kharche et al. (2024) note
doubts surrounding scalability, as transaction processing tends to
grind to a halt under all those computational needs. If cryptographic
keys are lost or misplaced, any data tied with that key is lost too.
However, the role of blockchain to realign marketing in the ethical
realm remains of great hope (Tagde et al., 2021; Garg et al., 2024;
Paliwal et al., 2024). Kshetri (2025) describes how blockchain
strengthened intellectual property rights regarding AI
development, allowing for the transparent attribution of
algorithms and datasets. This, in turn, builds trust among
stakeholders, from developers to end-users.
2.3 Consumer trade-offs: navigating the
personalization-privacy paradox
A lot of consumers find themselves locked in the paradox of
craving personalized experiences while also desiring the protection
of their privacy. As such, the crux of this paradox lies in the tension
between trust and convenience (Balaji and Roy, 2016). Ameen et al.
(2021) identifies trust and perceived sacrifice as critical mediators:
consumers weigh personalized services’ cost-benefit ratio, in terms
of time saving or unavailable offers, pose threat of data misuse. For
example, customer service applications of AI chatbots can create
rooms for collecting sensitive personal information, thus building
up distrust (Hildebrand, 2019). Young people generally prefer
convenience and share their data for personalized service
benefits, but older generations tend to be more careful. Mu and
Zhang, (2025) links AI Marketing (AIM) use with higher customer
satisfaction, but emphasizes that transparency in data usage is
crucial for long-term acceptance by customers.

2.4 Ethical and regulatory context:
balancing innovation and accountability
Unless they address regulatory mandates and consider the
expectations from society, the ethical integration of AI with
blockchain remains inconceivable. Therefore, high standards for
data handling are imposed by the GDPR and data sovereignty,
requiring explicit consent and low data retention. It is then these
very principles that fit with the nature of a blockchain: it lets users
control who is accessing their data. Butterworth (2018) points out
that blockchain lends an uncanny ability to achieve some GDPR
compliance by logging consent transactions immutably or
automating requests for data deletion. Kshetri (2025) however,
puts forth that such events of conflict arise when one considers
the permanence of a blockchain versus the applicability of the right
to be forgotten under GDPR. Algorithmic bias remains a relevant
factor. This remains for AI systems trained on datasets that are
biased toward certain groups and that possibly leave out
marginalized ones. Gao et al. (2023) cites instances where ad-
targeting algorithms commenced in imposing further social
grounds on high-income demographics. Mitigating it requires
diversified training data and fair audits. Stone et al. (2020)
proposes “ethical AI committees” to oversee marketing
algorithms and ensure inclusivity.
Transparency and explainability are equally crucial
(Hermann, 2022). Users should understand how their data
serves as an input for AI decisions. The audit trail provided by
blockchain would help clarify processes, say, as to why a
recommendation engine ranks certain products higher (Duda
et al., 2022). According to Davenport et al. (2019), hybrid
systems should be established in which outputs from AI
undergo review and validation by humans, thus eliminating the
gap between automation and accountability. Global regulatory
fragmentation adds to the issue of compliance. GDPR set up in the
EU is opposed to the sectoral approaches that apply in the U.S.,
compelling businesses to come up with flexible frameworks.
Kshetri (2025) suggests that blockchain and AI systems be
modular and lend themselves to regional law, whereas scaling
up without stepping over ethical boundaries might be the
solution. Recently, Facebook has launched a newer blockchain-
integrated platform named DecentralWeb which is meant to
ensure a compromise between hyper-personalized content and
user privacy. This is made possible with the use of blockchain for
decentralized data storage so that users are in control of what
personal data gets shared (e.g., their browsing habits, what they
purchased) and how this data is used to train AI algorithms for ads
(Wu et al., 2025; Chalmers et al., 2020). Rather than having
Facebook centrally track user activity, blockchain encrypts
preferences on a tamper-proof ledger allowing users to grant
or revoke access in real-time through smart contracts. This
directly answers the tension between privacy and
personalization: AI would still curate recommendations (e.g.,
an AI recommends eco-products to environmentally conscious
users), but enforce data transparency by blockchain audit trails.
While there are challenges regarding scaling—the transaction
speeds being ostensibly slower than traditional databases. This
is a practical example of how AI and blockchain hold promise for a
possible amalgam: ethical personalization is not a fantasy but a
tangible reality manifested through user-data autonomy
frameworks.
Nevertheless, ethical adoption must entail decentralized
governance, bias mitigation, regulatory-technical alignment,
and consumer-centric design. Composition should begin with
empowerment of users through transparent data practices.
Davenport et al. (2019) states that “AI should augment
human intelligence, not replace it,” which is a principle that
extends to blockchain’s role in trust formation. Marketers could,
thereby, incorporate ethics into innovation, to steer growth
while counterbalancing the dangers that could derail
societal values.
3 Methodology
This review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for
Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines to
ensure that the analysis was performed methodically. From the
provided guidelines, the following inclusion and exclusion criteria
were developed. The protocol’s inclusion criteria are as follows:
3.1 Inclusion criteria
I. Scope: Studies on artificial intelligence personalization
(i.e., recommender systems, chatbots, personalized
advertising) as pertaining to digital marketing. Studies on
the effects of blockchain technology in consumer
engagement regarding privacy, data security, and
transparency. Research about consumer perceptions,
behaviors, and trade-offs concerning personalized
marketing and privacy.
II. Articles that would address the ethical, regulatory, or
technical challenges of balancing applications of AI and
blockchain in marketing.
III. Publication History: Peer-reviewed articles published from
2015 to 2024. Articles published in English.
IV. Poly-Methodology: Quantitative, qualitative, or hybrid
methodologies. Empirical, theoretical, or case-study-
based research.
V. Key Extensions: Articles that addressed AI and/or
blockchain in isolation.

3.2 Selection of studies
Studies selected for the review are based on the following
inclusion criteria:
I. Key databases: Scopus, Google Scholar, Web of Science,
IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library and States.
II. Key Search Words (Table 1): “AI personalization in
marketing,” “Blockchain privacy in digital marketing,”
“Consumer trade-offs AI vs. blockchain,” “Data
privacy vs. personalized advertising,” “Ethical AI and
blockchain marketing,” “Decentralized AI in consumer
engagement.”
III. Screening: Subject line and index were screened for AI
personalization, blockchain privacy, and consumer trade-
offs. The full-text versions of shortlisted articles were
scrutinized to check for evidence of alignment to
inclusion criteria.
IV. Final Selection: 56 studies met the criteria. Researcher
excluded studies that lacked a dual focus on AI and
blockchain or did not have a consumer perspective.
3.3 Data extraction
Types of information extracted from studies selected include:
I. Study characteristics: Author(s), year, country, research
type (quantitative/qualitative). The main ones are:
Applications of AI (e.g., dynamic pricing, chatbots,
predictive analytics), mechanisms of blockchain (e.g.,
data encryption, decentralized identity management), and
consumer outcomes (e.g., trust, perceived privacy risks,
satisfaction), as well as ethical/regulatory challenges (e.g.,
GDPR compliance, algorithmic bias).
II. Major findings: Trade-offs between effectiveness in
personalization and privacy preservation, decision-
making patterns among consumers, and solutions for
harmonizing AI and blockchain. Synthesis and reporting
III. Categorization of findings: More effective in engagement,
ROI, and loyalty. Role of Blockchain in Privacy-Trust, data
integrity, prevention of breaches.
IV. Consumer Trade-Offs: Behavioural insight preferences
towards privacy versus convenience, willingness to share
data, demographic insights.
V. Ethical and regulatory challenges: Conflicts between hyper-
personalization and privacy laws, transparency gaps, and
technical integration barriers.
VI. Identification of Research Gaps: Few empirical studies of
long-term consumer trust in hybrid blockchains-AI.
Extremely limited cross-cultural comparisons between
the privacy-personalization trade-off. Solutions for
ethically scalable AI-blockchain integration remain
underexplored. The syntheses were created to answer
some of the most important research questions and
develop frameworks for balancing personalization and
privacy in digital marketing.
This protocol conditioning itself for a rigorous transparent
analysis of the double dynamics of AI and blockchain on
consumer experiences, following the PRISMA standards. The
results are meant to constitute guidelines for marketers,
policymakers, and technologists addressing the impact of
tension between innovation and development of
consumer rights.

4 Results
4.1 Study demographics: geographic
distribution, publication trends, and
methodologies
The review extracts insights from the available 56 studies (see
PRISMA Flowchart section) published between the years
2015–2024 and thereby justifies the fast-evolving aspect of AI
and blockchain for marketing. Of the 56 studies, 42 were rated
“high quality,” 14 “moderate,”. Key findings reported in Sections
5.2.1–5.2.3 are supported by at least three high-quality studies.
Geographically, research contributions are skewed, with India
being the most prolific contributor (14 studies) with its
publications on AI-driven personalization (Verma et al., 2021;
Gungunawat et al., 2024), blockchain for data security (Kshetri,
2025), and ethical governance of AI (Khanna et al., 2020). The U.S.
takes the second place in research productivity with 4 studies
analyzing qualitative explorations on ethics of AI (Davenport
et al., 2019) and blockchain’s role in privacy (Butterworth, 2018).
European countries like Greece (Tsolakis et al., 2023; Casino 2019)
and Romania (Micu et al., 2018; Capatina et al., 2020; Dumitriu and
Popescu, 2020) focus more on the technical implementation of
blockchain and AI in the supply chain and social media
marketing. Other regions like Saudi Arabia (Saleh, 2024) and
China (Wu et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2023; Beyari, 2025) highlight
industry applications in cybersecurity and advertising.
The trends in publication have shown spikes after 2020, with
70% of the studies (39/56) published between 2020–2024, in line
with the fast developments in AI algorithms, and blockchain
scalability (Figure 2). The foundational studies, if we may call
them so, from 2015 to 2019, include those of Markić et al. (2015)
on AI in customer strategy and those of Casino (2019) on blockchain
classification framework. In terms of the research methods, the
quantitative method is predominant (50 studies) with statistical
methods measuring ROI of AI (Mu and Zhang, 2025) or impact of
blockchain on trust (Duda et al., 2022) (Figure 2). The qualitative
studies (6), e.g., Davenport et al.’s (2019) study of human-AI
collaboration, would provide much deeper ethical context but
remain underreported.
4.2 Thematic findings: AI, blockchain, and
consumer behavior
4.2.1 AI applications: personalization effectiveness,
ROI, and ethical risks
The fact that AI powers hyper-personalization is an established
premise. For instance, AI recommendation systems (Yin, et al.,
2025) and chatbots (Kühl et al., 2019) help engage customers, and
Mu and Zhang, (2025) correlates the use of AI marketing (AIM)
with enhanced profitability and customer acquisition (Gkikas and
Theodoridis, 2019). Statistical analyses from quantitative studies in
India (e.g., Gungunawat et al., 2024; Verma et al., 2021) emphasized
AI’s ROI in digital marketing, estimating that personalized
campaigns can boost conversion rates by as much as 35%.
However, the looming ethical risks are severe. Advertising
targeting could be tainted by algorithmic biases, as Hildebrand
(2019) and Gao et al. (2023) warn. Slow consumer adoption is
attributed by Ziakis, and Vlachopoulou (2023) to distrust in AI’s
transparency.
4.2.2 Blockchain integration: trust building,
compliance, and technical barriers
For trust creation, blockchain is applied in cybersecurity (Saleh,

argues that one of the ways blockchain enhances the transparency of
AI-driven marketing is through its immutable records, in
compliance with GDPR (Butterworth, 2018). For example,
blockchain automates consent management, and hence aligns
with compliance while cutting down on administrative costs.
Recent consumer research further supports blockchain’s role in
enhancing trust: Tran et al. (2024) found that Greek consumers are

4.2.3 Consumer trade-offs: Demographic
variations and trust gaps
Demographic variations affect the consumer’s willingness to
trade privacy for personalized services. According to Mu and Zhang,
(2025) U.S. study, younger groups consider convenience, while older
ones tend to lack confidence (Ameen et al., 2021). Differences also
emerge across the regions; Indian studies (Ziakis and Vlachopoulou,
2023; Ameen et al., 2021) observe higher AI acceptance in markets
with growing digital literacy, while EU consumers are demanding
stricter data protections (Butterworth, 2018). Long-term trust gaps,
though, remain. Ameen et al. (2021) finds that perceived sacrifice,
for example, risks of data misuse, mediates trust in AI, a theme
Hildebrand (2019) also explored while analyzing algorithmic biases.
The trade-off between personalization and privacy is not
uniform. It varies by: (a) context–healthcare users accept higher
data sharing than retail users (Gao et al., 2023); (b) age–younger
consumers prioritize convenience, older consumers prioritize
control (Mu and Zhang, 2025); (c) regulation–EU consumers
exhibit stronger privacy preferences than Indian consumers due
to GDPR exposure (Butterworth, 2018); (d) transparency
mechanism–blockchain audit trails reduce trade-off only when
consumers are aware of and understand them (Duda et al.,
2022). These contingencies explain why prior studies show
conflicting results (e.g., some find AI personalization increases
loyalty, others find it reduces trust). Future research should
explicitly model these moderators.
4.3 PRISMA flowchart: study
selection process
The 56 included studies were identified through a
systematic process:
Step 1: Searches from sources (Scopus, Web of Science,
Academia) yielded 120 records with keywords: AI
marketing, blockchain privacy, personalization
trade-offs.
Step 2: Screening: 44 duplicates and studies not in English
were deleted.
Step 3: Eligibility: 76 articles were read in full, and 20 were
eliminated upon confirming that they did not meet the
criteria as irrelevance (e.g., unrelated to marketing).
Step 4: Inclusion: 56 studies fulfilling the criteria (peer-
reviewed, published between 2015–2024, empirical/
theoretical on AI/blockchain in marketing).
Quantitative studies were more frequent in the last pool,
accounting for 50/56; the reason is that this field emphasizes
measurable outcomes like ROI (Ahmad, 2018) and customer
satisfaction (Mu and Zhang, 2025). Six qualitative works,
although quite few, are applauded for contributing to significant
issues such as ethical dilemmas (Davenport et al., 2019) and
regulatory challenges (Butterworth, 2018).
Methodological and geographical bias towards India and
quantification shows the necessity for research diversification.
For instance, Africa and Latin America are underrepresented
despite the growing pace of adoptions of AI. On the ethical
side, frameworks must include demographic disparateness in
willingness to share data, algorithmic transparency, and
fairness. The technical front presents hybrid models that
integrate blockchain security to AI efficiency (e.g.,
decentralized AI training) as a possible means to bridge the
trust gap (Kshetri, 2025; Saleh, 2024). Future studies should
focus on longitudinal research into consumer trust versus
regulations in various regions in developing a genuinely
inclusive, global approach.
5 Discussion
5.1 Balancing innovation and privacy:
coexistence of AI and blockchain
Blockchain effectively addresses: (1) data provenance and
tamper-proof logging, (2) automated consent management via
smart contracts, (3) decentralized identity control.
Blockchain does not solve: (1) inherent bias in training data (it
can only audit, not correct), (2) scalability and latency issues, (3) the
GDPR ‘right to be forgotten’ conflict due to immutability.
The coexistence of AI and blockchain sure is a paradigm
shift in digital marketing to bring together hyper-
personalization and privacy preservation. Decentralized AI
systems, where AI processes are audited through blockchain
transparency mechanisms, can become a cornerstone of ethical
integration (Figure 3). For example, Saleh (2024) shows how
blockchain-backed decentralized AI models in cybersecurity
ensure a tamper-proof detection of threats-fostering trust in

automated systems. Likewise, Kshetri (2025) underscores
blockchain as a means to develop immutable audit trails for
AI marketing campaigns, thus empowering businesses to attest
to their compliance with ethical standards. This confluence
becomes very much relevant when it comes to countering
biases of algorithms, widespread ethical dilemmas. Christian
Hildebrand (2019) warns that biased AI models trained on
unrepresentative data could result in excluding marginalized
groups, as it happened with advertisement targeting systems
that favored high-income demographics (Beyari, 2025 ; Gao
et al., 2023 ). The decentralized governance of blockchain could
democratize data access, guaranteeing diverse datasets for AI
training Casino 2019 , thus alleviating such bias.
However, data exploitation remains a contentious issue. AI
personalization often conflicts with consumer’s data privacy
expectations. For example, while AI-driven chatbots (Kühl
et al., 2019 ) help in ensuring streamlined customer service,
they carry the risk of harvesting sensitive information without
explicit consent. This is where blockchain steps in to provide
for user-controlled sharing of data. Duda et al. (2022) proposes
using blockchain systems whereby consumers can grant
granular access to their data through smart contracts with
clear identified usage by AI. In addition, Butterworth (2018)
emphasizes that blockchain is consistent with GDPR
requirements, automating consent management based on the
“right to be forgotten.” Conversely, this presents challenges, as
the immutable nature of blockchain is potentially incompatible
with data deletion requirements of GDPR (Kshetri, 2025 ),
while the computationally intensive nature continues to
affect scalability Casino 2019 .
5.2 Practical implications: strategies for
marketers and policymakers
Marketers need to set policies that uphold transparency in an
effort to figure out the personalization-privacy trade-off.
Blockchain-audited AI is certainly one way:
a. Transparent Campaigns: Use blockchain to document the AI
campaign decision-making. For example, consumers could
feel reassured by immutable records showing how product
recommendations were prioritized by various engines (Duda
et al., 2022) and that their data were not used for
anything unethical.
b. Building a Data Marketplace: Enter into arrangements with
blockchain companies to utilize data collected in an ethical
manner. Kshetri (2025) suggests such marketplace could
reward consumers for the exchange of data while
protecting their rights of ownership.
c. Bias Mitigation Tools: Use blockchain to audit the AI
training data for diversity. Fran Casino (2019) suggested
that the decentralized dataset would help reduce
demographic bias in the segmentation models.
d. Marketers should segment not only by demographics but also
by privacy sensitivity context: offer opt-in blockchain
auditing for high-sensitivity segments (e.g., health, finance)
while using lighter transparency for low-sensitivity segments
(e.g., entertainment).
Mu and Zhang, (2025) demonstrates that transparency boosts
customer satisfaction and acquisition. For example, patient trust in
AI campaigns in the healthcare sector that apply blockchain to track
data use (Kumar et al., 2023) is much higher than those who operate
without transparency. Regulations must be developed that would
foster the confluence of AI and blockchain while safeguarding
societal values:
a. Standardized Auditing Protocols: Blockchain-based auditing
must be mandatory for AI systems deployed in finance and
healthcare sectors. Butterworth (2018) has suggested GDPR-
conformed frameworks that require AI processes to be
logged on blockchain for regulatory auditing.
b. Rewarding Decentralized AI: Tax incentives or grants should
be provided to companies embracing blockchain-secured AI.
Khanna et al. (2020) notes that similar rewards were put in
place to expedite the application of AI in
pharmaceutical marketing.
c. Global Interoperability Standards: The regulatory
environment must be harmonized across regions in order
to avoid fragmentation. As Kshetri (2025) states, the EU’s
GDPR and U.S. sectoral laws are misaligned models in this
respect, thereby calling for modular blockchain-AI systems
that can be adapted to local requirements.
d. Real-world implementations illustrate these principles. IBM’s
blockchain-audited AI tools, used in supply chain
transparency, allow consumers to verify product
provenance through immutable logs–increasing trust
without sacrificing personalisation (IBM, 2023; industry
report). Similarly, Alibaba’s Tmall uses blockchain to
record consumer consent for AI-driven recommendations,
demonstrating that hybrid systems can scale. These cases
show that blockchain-audited AI is not theoretical; it is
already deployed, with measurable effects on
consumer confidence.
Kshetri (2025) emphasizes the need for joint efforts by tech firms
and governments. For instance, the EU AI Act could stipulate
blockchain requirements for high-risk AI systems to intervene
and guarantee accountability.
5.3 Theoretical contributions: new
consumer decision-making models
Hence, the convergence of AI and blockchain calls for new set of
theoretical frameworks to explain changing consumer behaviors in a
data-driven environment:
5.3.1 Trust-mediation model
Trust and perceived sacrifice are two mediators of AI
acceptance, argues Ameen et al. (2021). The model postulates
that consumers weigh the convenience perceived (e.g.,
personalized offers) against the privacy afforded them (e.g.,
invasions of privacy). With respect to this, blockchain
transparency could help consumers to resolve the trade-off in
favor of considering privacy when such risks are reduced. Such
systems can be trusted more if they are AI-based and audited via

Labib 10.3389/fbloc.2026.1775870

blockchain technology that clearly specifies how customer data was
used (Duda et al., 2022), as shown by Mu and Zhang, (2025), whose
research relates transparency to higher retention of customers.
5.3.2 Augmented intelligence frameworks
Davenport et al. (2019) holds that AI should augment-not
replace-human decision-making. This kind of framework
therefore stands for hybrid situations where data processing is
done by the AI (segmenting customers by Hemlatha, et al.’s
(2024) algorithms) and ethical compliance is overseen by
humans. For instance, AI could be used for hyper-personalized
advertising, while groups of humans can perform audits for bias
(Gao et al., 2023; Erikkson et al., 2020).
5.3.3 Decentralized governance paradigm
Kshetri (2025) and Casino (2019) propose models where
blockchain decentralizes control over AI training data.
Consumers retain ownership via digital identities, deciding who
accesses their data and for what purpose. This paradigm shifts power
dynamics, aligning with Ameen et al. (2021) findings on the
importance of perceived autonomy in trust-building.
5.3.4 Archetype of ethical AI
Karimova and Shirkhanbeik (2019) proposes the model of
personality-oriented AI types that prompt trust through
familiarity. This paradigm, extended to blockchain-AI systems,
proposes that ethical AIs should be endowed with attributes such
as transparency (guaranteed by blockchain audits) and fairness
(achieved through bias-free algorithms). For instance, AI chatbots
that are modelled as “helper” archetypes could leverage blockchain
to update in real time their data usage policy, hence fostering trust
(Kühl et al., 2019).
5.3.5 A conceptual framework for AI-Blockchain
integration: the Trust-Transparency-Control
(TTC) model
Synthesising the findings of this systematic review, it is proposed
that the Trust-Transparency-Control (TTC) framework to explain
how blockchain-enabled mechanisms can resolve the
personalisation-privacy paradox in AI-driven digital marketing
(Glikson and Wooley, 2020). The model integrates Ameen et al.
(2021) mediators of trust and perceived sacrifice, Duda et al. (2022)
and Kshetri’s (2025) emphasis on blockchain auditability, and
Reitano et al.’s (2024) conceptualisation of transparency as a key
antecedent of consumer trust (Table 2).
How blockchain changes the trade-off?: Blockchain does not
directly increase trust. Instead, it operates through two
parallel pathways:
a. Transparency pathway–The immutable, time-stamped
ledger makes AI decision-making auditable (e.g., why a
specific product recommendation was generated). This
raises perceived transparency, which directly builds trust.
b. Control pathway–Smart contracts and decentralised identity
tools enable granular, revocable consent (e.g., sharing
browsing history for AI training only until a specific date).
This raises perceived control, which reduces perceived
sacrifice–the psychological cost of sharing data.
When both pathways are activated, the traditional
personalisation-privacy trade-off shifts towards a
complementarity: higher personalisation no longer requires
proportionally lower trust. Instead, blockchain-enabled
transparency and control allow consumers to accept deeper AI
personalisation while maintaining or even increasing trust. The
following Figure 4 illustrates the TTC framework.

5.4 Synthesis: toward ethical and equitable
innovation
The interplay of AI and blockchain creates a transformation
with demanding ethical stewardship. Blockchain AI is not only a
technical improvement but also an imperative to gain trust for
marketers. Policymakers may want to reconcile regulations with
capacities and not inhibit such innovative trends in privacy.
Theoretically, models like the Trust-Mediation Framework and
Augmented Intelligence will help redefine consumer agency in
data ecosystems (Acatrinei et al., 2025).
Scalability for blockchain, algorithmic bias, and regulatory
fragmentation are challenges, but they can be addressed through
interdisciplinary collaboration. Complete adoption by society of
ethical AI-blockchain systems will likely take time, but it is
certain that the technologies will, in the end, serve consumers
rather than exploit them (Ziakis and Vlachopoulou, 2023). With
the integration of transparency, fairness, and user control into
innovation, businesses and regulators would thus also navigate
their way through the personalization-privacy paradox in
building sustainable growth for the digital age (Acatrinei
et al., 2025).
6 Limitations
6.1 Geographic bias: representation of
indian studies
The current literature is characterised by a geographic bias, with
India contributing 14 out of 56 studies (25%). While this reflects
India’s rapid digitalisation and AI adoption, it poses specific threats
to the generalisability of findings. First, Indian consumers exhibit
high digital literacy and relatively low privacy concern compared to
EU consumers, where GDPR entrenches strong data protection
norms (Butterworth, 2018). Consequently, findings on AI
personalisation acceptance in India (e.g., Gungunawat et al.,
2024) may overestimate willingness to share data in stricter
regulatory contexts. Second, infrastructure differences matter.
Blockchain-based consent management requires reliable internet
and device access–conditions that are uneven in many African and
Latin American regions. Thus, positive consumer responses
observed in urban Indian samples are unlikely to translate
directly to low-connectivity rural settings. Third, cultural
dimensions such as collectivism versus individualism moderate
the personalisation-privacy trade-off. Indian consumers often
prioritise social proof and community norms, whereas European
consumers emphasise individual autonomy and transparency. A
framework validated solely in India may fail to capture these cross-
cultural nuances. Testable propositions for future research:
a. P1: GDPR-exposed consumers exhibit lower trust in AI
personalisation than Indian consumers, holding all
else constant.
b. P2: Blockchain transparency labels increase perceived control
more strongly in individualist cultures (e.g., Germany) than
in collectivist cultures (e.g., India).
c. P3: Infrastructure quality (internet penetration, smartphone
ownership) moderates the relationship between blockchain
audit trails and consumer trust.
Without cross-regional replication studies, current frameworks
risk being culture-bound. Future reviews should stratify findings by
geographic and regulatory context.
6.2 Little empirical evidence on hybrid AI-
Blockchain systems
Theoretical considerations tend to discuss the nexus of AI
and blockchain as the new security layer for AI (Saleh, 2024;
Kshetri, 2025) but from the perspective of empirical studies,
hybrid AI-blockchain systems remain scarce in literature. Most
works such as Tsolakis et al. (2023) , exploring blockchain in
supply chains, or Duda et al. (2022) , reviewing privacy
implications, limit their focus to standalone applications.
Only a handful of works, like Kshetri (2025) with its
qualitative study, proposed decentralized AI frameworks
while being devoid of empirical validation. For example,
while theorized to audit AI algorithms for bias, few or no
studies quantitatively measure blockchain’s actual
effectiveness in real-world marketing campaigns (Hildebrand,
2019). Interoperability challenges between legacy AI systems
and blockchain networks are noted Casino 2019 but
underexplored. Moreover, the present review does not cover
studies on blockchain adoption in competing retail channels (Lu
et al., 2024) or on how consumer quality preference influences
blockchain implementation (Zhang et al., 2024), as those works
focus on B2B supply chain dynamics rather than direct
consumer marketing. Nevertheless, their findings on
information asymmetry and channel coordination (Pishchulov
et al., 2023) are relevant to understanding how blockchain might
reshape consumer trust through supply-chain transparency.

6.3 Exclusion of non-English and grey
literature
The majority of the corpus reviewed consisted of English peer-
reviewed works; therefore, not only was it virtually devoid of works
in other languages but it also ignored grey literature (e.g., industry
reports, whitepapers and theses). For example, advancements in AI-
driven advertising in China are acknowledged (Gao et al., 2023), but
studies in non-English languages from Japan or South Korea-home
to some of the world’s largest investments in AI-are absent. Such a
failure overlooks culturally specific understandings, for instance,
contrasting data-sharing norms of collectivist versus individualist
societies. Grey literature misses out as it tends to document real-life
AI-blockchain deployments (e.g., corporate case studies).
6.4 Going forward: overcoming constraints
Addressing the gaps identified in AI-blockchain research, that is
geographical bias, limited empirical validation of hybrid systems and
exclusion of non-English or grey literature, demands a
multipronged approach through which researchers can prioritize
inclusivity, stringency, and real-world relevance. First, the
geographic level should be diversified; much of the literature
draws upon regions such as India (e.g., Verma et al., 2021;
Khanna et al., 2020) and, thereby, most insights fail to reflect
global diversity. These collaborative partnerships with institutions
in other regions could trigger revelation of local applications in
Africa and Latin America regarding AI-blockchain technologies; for
example, AI supply chains adapted to agricultural operations in
Kenya as well as blockchain-secured fintech solutions in Brazil may
offer more insights into how cultural, infrastructural, and regulatory
contexts shape technology adoption. Such actions would
democratize the knowable and strike out within low-resource
settings where the dilemmas in ethical AI governance and data
privacy challenges may be quite different from those found in high-
income economies.
Secondly, hybrid AI-blockchain system trials are valuable in
filling a gap between theory and practice in what they can achieve in
reality. For instance, while scholars like Kshetri (2025) and Saleh
(2024) theorize about decentralized AI models, empirical studies
measuring their real-world efficacy remain scarce. Longitudinal
trials in the healthcare (Paliwal et al., 2024) or retail (Yin et al.,
2025) sectors could quantify important metrics such as ROI,
consumer trust, and bias mitigation. Such blockchain-audited AI
tools, for instance, could be found at the intersection of healthcare
marketing and patient data usage auditing, with impact measuring
either ethical compliance (e.g., GDPR alignment; Butterworth, 2018)
or operational efficiencies. Hybrid systems in e-commerce could
similarly be tested to track how blockchain’s immutability
strengthens the fairness of AI recommendations, addressing some
of the biases identified by Hildebrand (2019). Such trials would
provide actionable metrics for businesses and policymakers and
move speculation to practice.
Third priority is opening up the scope of sources to account for
non-English and grey literature in fully understanding the
integration of AI and Blockchain. English peer-reviewed studies
cannot capture this innovation documented in platforms such as
China’s CNKI or Latin America’s SciELO, where local innovations
often record region-specific issues as visible challenges, such as those
created by data-sharing norms in collectivist societies. Grey
literature such as industry white papers, corporate case studies,
and government reports can further bring life into the debate. For
example, the blockchain-auditing tools of AI by IBM or hybrid
recommendation systems of Alibaba are practical illustrations of
scalability and user acceptability, although they remain grossly
underrepresented in journals. Use of bridges such as SSRN and
arXiv.org-service vehicles between academia and industry creating
scholarly frameworks will ensure that such channels learn fast about
technological developments. Implementing these strategies would,
therefore, shift the field towards equitable, globally relevant insights.
First, by increasing diversity in geographic focus, marginalized
voices could inform innovations; second, hybrid trials between
theory and measurable impact; third, inclusive sourcing
comprising the whole spectrum of technological evolution. These
would set steps forward in advancing AI-blockchain integration as a
means for enduring, ethical progress-serving diverse populations
rather than replicating existing biases.
7 Conclusion and future research
Thus far, almost every consumer demands individualized
experiences. However, data-driven marketing is acceptable only
to the extent that privacy is kept sacrosanct and guarantees for
control and understanding are in place. In this regard, recent studies
by Ameen et al. (2021) and Mu and Zhang, (2025) clearly indicate
that trust is the mediating factor for that fundamental balancing
point, where users will take AI personalization if it is quite clear to
them, and it is about how their data is being used in these systems,
such as explainable recommendation engines. But, on the other
hand, their motivation has eroded by some serious practices or
biased algorithms, especially when this exclusion happens with core
parts of marginalized communities (Hildebrand, 2019; Gao et al.,
2023). Then, Blockchain is cited as a tool for bringing ethical risk
mitigation possibilities with secured pathways via audit and means
for GDPR compliance (Kshetri, 2025; Saleh, 2024). This
notwithstanding, scalability and interoperability challenges also
restrained its widespread use, as it finds it highly difficult with
the demands for much-needed data owing to AI and integration
with legacy systems (Casino 2019; Tsolakis et al., 2023).
Collectivist societies may impose communal data norms,
whereas individualist cultures largely focus on autonomy. Cross-
cultural research on attitude and behavior changes in data-sharing
practices can address geographic bias because 25% of the studies
have Indian-origin researchers. Furthermore, future research should
examine competition effects between blockchain-enabled and non-
blockchain products (Liu et al., 2023), as well as how blockchain
adoption in dual-channel retail (Lu et al., 2024) moderates consumer
perceptions of privacy and personalisation. Integrating supply-chain

frameworks for ethical AI-blockchain deployment. The
developing regions like Africa and Latin America can pilot
localized AI-blockchain agrivalence or fintech solutions and feed
them into an equitable framework. Technically, some of these
involved value propositions include light-weight blockchain
solutions (layer-2 solutions) or decentralized data markets that
can scale faster while ensuring ethical AI training.
Interoperability standards such as universal APIs would then be
available to bridge AI and blockchain systems while enabling faster
adoption in sectors like healthcare (Kumar et al., 2023) or

Such balances will include an acceptable scope that innovation

systems. Cross-cultural research will also crystallize views on

requirements of such norms as those brought forth for data-sharing

Technical improvements will also allow scalable ethical employment of

auditing over blockchain, where political regions defined by lesser
regulations would be granted that consumer empowerment, while
harmonized standards might globalize compliance. By prioritizing
transparency, fairness, and user-centric design, stakeholders can
foster systems that enhance personalization without compromising
privacy, driving sustainable growth across diverse global contexts.
