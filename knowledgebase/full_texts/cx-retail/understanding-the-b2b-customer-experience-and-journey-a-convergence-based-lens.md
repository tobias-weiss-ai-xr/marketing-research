ABSTRACT
This article advances our understanding of Business-to-Business (B2B) Customer Experience (CX) and the B2B
Customer Journey (CJ) by introducing a convergence-based theoretical lens. This perspective highlights how
psychological and operational convergence shape B2B CX and CJ by (1) aligning CX across multiple levels
(individual, team, organization) within the buyer organization and (2) facilitating interactions between the
buyer and seller organizations across the CJ. The authors offer six core insights that enrich CX and CJ theory,
form the basis for actionable managerial recommendations, and inform a future research agenda to address
ongoing complexities and challenges in B2B settings.
1. Introduction
Practitioners and academics have devoted significant attention to
understanding customer experience (CX) and the customer journey
(CJ)—which we respectively define as “customer’s responses during
direct and indirect interactions with an organization” and “the series of
touchpoints associated with the purchase and usage of goods and ser-
vices” (De Keyser, Verleye, Lemon, Keiningham, & Klaus, 2020; Lemon
& Verhoef, 2016). This focus is well justified, as both concepts are
central to understanding how customers interact with organizations,
perceive these interactions, and form long-term relationships that ulti-
mately drive financial success (Lemon & Verhoef, 2016; Wetzels &
Wetzels, 2023).
To date, CX and CJ research have predominantly focused on
Business-to-Consumer (B2C) contexts (Lemon & Verhoef, 2016; Voo-
rhees et al., 2017). However, with the scale of Business-to-Business
(B2B) markets—estimated at $25 trillion in North America alone
(Mastercard, 2022)—and the rising expectations of B2B buyers (Harvard
Business Review Analytic Services, 2022), there is a growing recognition
of the need to extend these concepts to B2B environments (Purmonen,
Jaakkola, & Terho, 2023; Witell et al., 2020).
Despite this shift, B2B CX and CJ research still heavily draws from
B2C insights and remains limited in several key areas. For instance,
while the multi-level nature of B2B CX is acknowledged (e.g., Zolkiewski
et al., 2017; Witell et al., 2020), there is no comprehensive theoretical
lens to explain how CX emerges and aligns across different organiza-
tional levels, from individual experiences to the team level and beyond
to the broader organization (i.e., spanning 2 or more teams). Similarly,
although the B2B CJ literature identifies the various stages and phases of
the CJ (Steward et al., 2019; Grewal & Sridhar, 2021), it lacks a detailed
discussion of how buyer and seller organizations align throughout the CJ
and the crucial role of sellers in this process.
In response to these gaps, we introduce a convergence-based theo-
retical lens that integrates psychological convergence and operational
convergence as key components for understanding and managing B2B
CX and the CJ. Psychological convergence refers to the perceived align-
ment of individual experiences within teams and/or the organization,
whereas operational convergence concerns the alignment of actions,
processes, and touchpoints between different entities (i.e., the buyer and
seller organizations) along the CJ. This convergence lens offers a novel
framework for understanding how CX develops at multiple organiza-
tional levels and how buyer–seller interactions can be optimized to

2. Literature background on B2B CX and CJ
2.1. B2B customer experience
We reviewed the newly developed B2B CX literature. In Table 1, we
summarize core articles that have been particularly important in
shaping this field. Notably, most of these articles discuss both CX and CJ,
as they are inherently connected concepts.
Much of the B2B literature has adopted a common B2C CX definition
(e.g., Becker & Jaakkola, 2020), describing B2B CX as encompassing a
buyer’s nondeliberate, spontaneous responses and reactions to offering-
related stimuli along the customer journey. This perspective frames B2B
CX at the individual level (e.g., McColl-Kennedy, Zaki, Lemon, Urmetzer,
& Neely, 2019), referring to the responses of individual employees within
the buyer organization. These experiences are multidimensional in na-
ture, including cognitive, emotional, sensorial, social, and behavioral
dimensions—see the work of De Keyser et al. (2020; 2025) for an
elaborate discussion. For instance, cognitive experiences arise when
employees process substantial amounts of information during a bidding
process. Similarly, various emotional experiences (e.g., joy, anger, fear,
disgust, surprise) may occur when interacting with the seller and their
service offering (e.g., McColl-Kennedy et al., 2019).
While individual-level CX is crucial, recent work has emphasized the
multilevel nature of B2B CX. Specifically, individuals typically operate
within teams or units—such as a buying center, a user center, a depart-
ment, or a multi-functional team—which together make up the organi-
zation (e.g., Witell et al., 2020; Zolkiewski et al., 2017). This aligns with
Gummesson’s (1987) characterization of B2B customers as “multi-
headed” entities. Understanding how individual CXs are shared within a
team and how team CXs are shared within an organization necessarily
requires a multilevel conceptualization. However, despite growing
recognition of B2B CX’s multilevel nature (e.g., Witell et al., 2020), the
current literature has largely treated this aspect as a background factor.
To date, we are unaware of any systematic conceptualization of how B2B
CX develops and converges across individuals, teams, and the
organization.
We pay special attention to the article of Witell et al. (2020), which
makes insightful remarks about the multilevel nature of B2B CX. These
authors develop a comprehensive framework for CX management,
anchored in two key dimensions: “relationship control” and “customer
entity”. They provide useful examples describing the roles of employees,
teams, and organizations and highlight the need to synchronize these
different entities. Although this foundational work underscores the
multilevel nature of B2B CX, it does not treat it as its central focus.
Rather, Witell et al. (2020) primarily concentrate on managing B2B CX,
rather than providing a conceptualization of B2B CX and a theoretical
lens explaining its emergence.
2.2. B2B customer journey
The B2B CJ (see Table 1) is typically defined as the path taken by an
organizational buyer in procuring and utilizing offerings, which includes a
series of touchpoints associated with the purchase and usage of solutions
(Purmonen, Jaakkola, & Terho, 2023). Notably, while CX captures
customers’ responses that arise from these touchpoints, the CJ repre-
sents the structured sequence in which they unfold. In this sense, the CJ
acts as the backbone of CX, shaping how experiences are formed and
accumulated and ultimately influencing decision-making processes
within and across multiple individuals in the buying organization. At the
core of the CJ are touchpoints, which entail any point of contact between
a seller organization and a buyer organization (McColl-Kennedy et al.,
2019). These touchpoints vary in nature, being human (e.g., a sales-
person), digital (e.g., chatbot, website), physical (e.g., an exhibit booth),
or any combination thereof (Steward, Narus, Roehm, & Ritz, 2019).
Furthermore, touchpoints differ in terms of their level of organiza-
tional control (Witell et al., 2020). Some touchpoints are fully controlled
by the selling organization (e.g., service offering, ad campaigns), which
makes them highly adaptable to the requirements of industrial buyers.
Other touchpoints, however, fall outside the seller organizations’ con-
trol and include “final customer”-controlled (e.g., an individual
employee within the buyer organization using their own electronic de-
vice to interact with the seller’s software), social (e.g., online reviews
and blogs), and other external (e.g., independent dealerships) touch-
points (Purmonen et al., 2023).
Importantly, this literature has recognized different stages of the
customer journey—all building on the original steps of the buying pro-
cess (see Steward et al. [2019] for a review)—with the purpose of
simplifying and organizing holistic journeys. The simplest division is
inspired by the B2C literature; it boils down to recognizing pre-purchase,
purchase or service, and post-purchase stages (e.g., Homburg and
Tischer, 2023). Other articles have recognized the need for a finer-
grained conceptualization fitting the complexities of the B2B CJ. For
instance, Grewal and Sridhar (2021) propose a journey with eight
stages, starting with “need activation,” going through “maintenance or
repair,” and finishing with “disposal or repurchase.” In turn, Witell et al.
(2020) make a distinction between the “pre-bid engagement,” “negoti-
ation,” “implementation,” and “operations” stages.
We place particular emphasis on Purmonen et al’s. (2023) work,
which proposes a detailed and well-justified conceptualization of the
B2B CJ. These authors make a distinction between the purchase jour-
ney—which is composed of need recognition, information search,
comparison of alternatives, and selection—and the usage journey—-
which encompasses the stages of offering deployment, usage, mainte-
nance, and reassessment. They explain the connection between these
two stages for different purchasing contexts (e.g., rebuy, new buying
situation), the differences between direct and indirect touchpoints, and
the influence of a series of relational factors (e.g., relationship atmo-
sphere, interaction history, and interconnected relationships). They also
provide a useful lexicon of concepts related to the B2B CJ.
Within Purmonen et al.’s (2023) insightful conceptualizations, we
identify a critical area that requires further development. Although
Purmonen et al. (2023) acknowledge that the seller organization plays a
vital role along the CJ, they do not formally address the need for
convergence between the seller and buyer organizations across the CJ.
While research on the B2B CJ recognizes—implicitly or explicitly—that
sellers play a role in managing the B2B CX (e.g., Homburg & Tischer,
2023; Witell et al., 2020), the precise nature and extent of their
involvement is somewhat overlooked in the B2B CX/CJ literature.
3. Convergence
3.1. A broad definition of convergence
We propose that the gaps previously identified can be addressed
through a convergence lens. Drawing from marketing (e.g., Ahearne,
Atefi, Lam, & Pourmasoudi, 2022; Becker et al., 2023; Gupta, Kumar,
Grewal, & Lilien, 2019; Lawrence, Crecelius, Scheer, & Lam, 2019) and
organizational psychology/behavior (e.g., Echterhoff, Higgins, & Lev-
ine, 2009; Fulmer & Ostroff, 2016; Kozlowski & Klein, 2000), we define
convergence as the process of increasing alignment in psychological re-
sponses and/or operational activities between different individuals and/or
teams. This alignment can occur within an organization (e.g., between
employees, departments, or cross-functional teams) or across organiza-
tional boundaries (e.g., between an organization and its suppliers or
external partners).
The suggested broad definition has two key implications. First, B2B
CX may remain an exclusively individual affair if there is no conver-
gence among individuals (i.e., a divergence of individual views). Alterna-
tively, it may be confined to one or more specific teams that are more
cohesive in their views without emerging as an organization-wide
phenomenon (Becker et al., 2023; Kozlowski & Klein, 2000). Second,
B2B CX requires time to emerge as it results from the “experiential

3.2. Psychological and operational convergence
We propose that two distinct but related types of con-
vergence—psychological and operational—are relevant for B2B CX and
B2B CJ, respectively. For B2B CX, the process of psychological conver-
gence is essential for the alignment of individuals’ perceptions of CX
within and across team(s). Relatedly, a seamless B2B CJ involves a high
degree of operational convergence, ensuring alignment in the activities
and processes of the buyer and seller organizations. We expand on these
two types of convergence below.
Psychological convergence pertains to the perceived alignment of ex-
periences among individuals within the buying (or selling) organization;
for instance, this type of convergence is captured in expressions such as
“we feel that” or “we believe that” (Becker et al., 2023; Echterhoff et al.,
2009). Building on research on collective mind and consciousness, we
recognize that psychological convergence is not merely an aggregation
of individual experiences but rather a shared perspective, being cogni-
tively constructed, that emerges through social synchronization and
joint attention (Shteynberg 2024; Shteynberg et al., 2023). Represen-
tations of the collective mind suggest that when individuals encode their
experiences as part of a shared mental framework, these experiences
gain psychological salience and are prioritized in cognitions and com-
munications (Shteynberg et al., 2023).
Psychological convergence allows individuals to communicate more
effectively internally, ensuring that different people are on the same page
regarding their interactions with the seller (Kozlowski & Klein, 2000;
Rafferty, Jimmieson, & Armenakis, 2013). Furthermore, collective con-
sciousness theory suggests that shared representations of CX within teams
and organizations can enhance trust, coordination, and cooperation
(Shteynberg, 2024), which stimulate the further emergence of B2B CX. In
sum, the notion of psychological convergence is beneficial to grasp the
multilevel development of B2B CX within the buyer organization.
Next, we define operational convergence as the alignment of actions,
processes, and touchpoints between two or more entities (e.g., buyer and
seller organizations) as they coordinate and integrate their activities
throughout the CJ. For example, in the implementation phase of a
complex B2B service solution, operational convergence may occur when
the seller’s deployment team works closely with the buyer’s IT and
procurement teams to ensure seamless solution integration (e.g., align-
ing resources, synchronizing timelines, developing troubleshooting
processes to prevent disruptions, etc.). This convergence emerges from
the dynamic and collaborative relationships formed between in-
dividuals, teams and/or organizations, where continuous interactions
and adaptations facilitate efficient operations over time (Provan, Fish, &
Sydow, 2007; Shamsollahi, Chmielewski-Raimondo, Bell, & Kachouie,
2021). In this research, we posit that operational convergence plays a
central role in understanding the role of sellers in shaping the B2B CJ.
In what follows, and building on the convergence lens, we identify
six insights (see Table 2 for an overview) that are central to under-
standing B2B CX and CJ. Ultimately, these insights can help guide the
development of the B2B CX and CJ domains. In the following section, we
first focus on the multilevel aspect of B2B CX (insights 1 to 3). The
subsequent section then focuses on B2B CJ and the role of seller-buyer
interactions in shaping journeys (insights 4 to 6).
4. From individual-level CX to multilevel CX
This first section, which addresses the multilevel nature of CX, is
organized as follows: First, we differentiate different CX levels (insights
1–2) and then discuss the process of psychological convergence (insight
3).
4.1. Differentiating between CX levels
When considering B2B CX, it is imperative to account for the fact that
business services/goods are typically used by collectives of individuals
belonging to functional units or teams with determined roles and ob-
jectives within an organization (Becker et al., 2023; Burns, 2016;
Pacherie, 2018). As a result, B2B CX requires a multilevel perspective
composed of minimally three layers: an individual-level, a team-level
CX, and an organization-level CX (i.e., spanning across two or more
teams of the organization)—see Fig. 1 for a visual representation.
Consequently, our first core insight recognizes that B2B CX requires a
multilevel perspective composed of three layers: an individual-level, a team-
level, and an organization-level CX. As recently highlighted by Becker
et al. (2023), the study of collective experiences (i.e., team-level and
organization-level) requires more than a process of straightforward
“aggregation” of individual assessments to a higher level of analysis.
Collective entities, like a buying unit, cannot consciously experience
stimuli (Burns, 2016; Pacherie, 2018). This suggests that individual
perceptions of a shared experience with a given seller organization
represent the foundation of B2B CX at a higher and collective level of
analysis (Becker et al., 2023).
More precisely, we propose that, through a process of psychological
convergence, individuals within a team or an organization develop a
collective sense of their experience with a seller organization. Team- and
organization-level CXs are fundamentally grounded in experiences
communicated and perceived by individuals in the group as having
common cognitive, emotional, social, behavioral and/or sensorial fea-
tures.1 Obviously, such sharing and interactions evolve and may change
over time; accordingly, creating that sense of individual and collective
experiences is an iterative and longitudinal process (Sandberg & Tsou-
kas, 2020). Therefore, our second core insight proposes that higher-level (i.
e., team and organization) CX emerges from the sharing of individual-level
CXs that psychologically converge over time in a collective experience.

We note that while individual members of a team or organization
may believe that they share a common experience with a seller, it is
important to recognize that this perceived alignment might not be ac-
curate. Individual perceptions of convergence—where team members
feel they are on the same page—can mask actual differences in how each
individual really experiences and interprets interactions with the seller
organization. Specifically, while some individuals believe that their
perspectives are well aligned with those of others, divergence can still
exist beneath the surface, particularly when a certain level of uncer-
tainty is present (Shteynberg et al., 2023). That is, people may confi-
dently believe that they know the perspective of others in their
organization; however, it still conceivable that these “others” hold
subtly different or even contradictory views. This false sense of com-
monality can create “blind spots” in CX management, where hidden
discrepancies in the collective experience go unnoticed until they cause
frictions in decision-making. Thus, it is crucial for B2B CX management
not only to encourage convergence but also to be aware of potential
(hidden) divergence that can impact the collective experience.
4.2. Drivers of psychological convergence
The organizational psychology literature has identified several main
factors driving the emergence and subsequent convergence of higher-
level psychological constructs: (1) social interactions and comparison,
(2) leadership, (3) organizational structures, (4) homogeneity of in-
dividuals, and (5) buyer–seller alignment (i.e., operational convergence)
(e.g., Fulmer & Ostroff, 2016; Ostroff, Kinicki, & Muhammad, 2012).
Accordingly, our third core insight is that social interactions, leadership,
organizational structures, homogeneity of individuals, and buyer–seller
alignment facilitate psychological convergence and, therefore, lead to the
emergence of higher-level B2B CX. To the best of our knowledge, we are
the first to consider the relevance of these factors in the emergence of
B2B CX. We acknowledge that the relative influence of each factor may
vary depending on the organization, as well as the level of investigation
(i.e., team versus organization).
Social interactions, as the essential factor for a high-level CX to
emerge, include exchanges between individuals who share relevant in-
formation concerning their experiences with a seller organization.
Research on social networks has demonstrated (Adler & Kwon, 2002;
Rost, 2011) that such exchanges can be social or hierarchical. The di-
versity of the ties impacts the level of organizational control and in-
fluences the information exchange process among concerned
individuals. This network of interactions leads to the development of a
higher-level CX through three interrelated mechanisms of social
comparison (Feldman, 1984; Schachter, 1959), contagion (Hatfield,
Cacioppo, & Rapson, 1992), and amplification (Boothby, Smith, Clark,
& Bargh, 2016).
Social comparison is grounded in individuals’ observations of others’
behaviors and responses. Individuals—especially when uncertain about
their own evaluation of the seller organization—will observe others to
learn how they should feel and respond toward the seller (Gump &
Kulik, 1997). At earlier stages in the relationship, team members might
compare their perceptions with the experiences of others to understand
how they should evaluate the seller. Rooted in psychological theories of
behavioral mimicry (Hatfield et al., 1992), contagion offers a similar
mechanism. Observing others’ positive responses to an offering will
immediately trigger a positive response through mechanisms of imita-
tion, which are independent of the cognitive learning implicit in social
comparisons (Howard & Gengler, 2001). This is especially so when in-
dividuals desire to get along with others, so that attuning one’s beliefs
and perceptions is more likely to happen. Interestingly, research also
shows that sharing an experience strengthens its intensity (Boothby,
Clark, & Bargh, 2014; Boothby et al., 2016). Here, when colleagues in a
team or organization share their views about the seller, an amplification
process takes place, which increases the negativity or positivity of their
individual experiences.
These mechanisms align with research on collective mind and con-
sciousness, which shows that simultaneously experiencing information
strengthens shared perceptions and psychological convergence
(Shteynberg, 2024; Shteynberg et al., 2023). Experiencing an event at
the same time—whether through co-attending meetings, discussing
seller performance in real-time, or jointly reacting to service inter-
actions—makes these experiences become more psychologically salient
and socially reinforced (Shteynberg et al., 2023).
Leadership is a second factor that impacts the psychological conver-
gence of individual CX to a higher-level property at the team and/or
organization levels. Here, top management provides critical information
that shapes how individual team members make sense of a particular
offering and interact with the seller organization (Maitlis & Lawrence,
2007; Zaccaro, Rittman, & Marks, 2001). Beyond the influence due to
their status, leaders can impact the convergence of team-level and
organization-level CX disproportionately because they are likely to have
more relationships (or ties) within the network of interactions. Social
network research suggests the importance of considering the number,
strength, and density of these relationships to assess each individual’s
relative importance in a given network (Payne, Moore, Griffis, & Autry,
2011). Leaders are expected to have many important relationships, both
professionally and socially, with other individuals across the

Organizational structures encompass characteristics like the size and
configuration of the organization, as well as its internal practices and
institutions. These help establish an ordinary reality for employees
(Mintberg, 1979), which may provide a basis to create a shared

internally consistent whole. Visibility makes these practices and in-
stitutions more salient. Consensus means that practices and institutions
are communicated widely and clearly, and that they are administered
consistently throughout the organization.
Homogeneity of individual employees within a team and organization
also contributes to B2B CX convergence at higher levels. Specifically,
building on the principle of homophily, individuals who are like each
other in terms of personal attributes, goals, and values are more likely to
be attracted to each other and typically communicate more frequently
(Fulmer & Ostroff, 2016). Furthermore, research on social tuning sup-
ports this idea, suggesting that individuals are more likely to mentally
prioritize and encode stimuli when they believe those stimuli are
experienced by similar others (Shteynberg, 2010). That is, when in-
dividuals assume that their group members are exposed to the same
seller interactions, their cognitive accessibility of these experiences in-
creases, reinforcing shared perceptions of CX. This, we posit, subse-
quently leads to a higher likelihood of psychological convergence.
Finally, the level of alignment between the seller and buyer organi-
zations across the B2B CJ (i.e., operational convergence) impacts the
occurrence and level of psychological convergence within the buyer
organization. Through such alignment, buyer and seller organizations
develop shared understandings, common goals, and capabilities that
enhance their ability to operate cohesively across different phases of the
CJ. Consistent and aligned interactions with the seller will lead to a
higher likelihood of a shared and common perception across individuals
working within the buyer organization. We discuss this alignment in
more detail in section 5.2 as well as in the concluding section (i.e.,
section 6.1).
5. The Multi-Phase, Multi-Stage customer journey
This section elaborates on three additional insights (see Table 2)
regarding the B2B CJ. Briefly stated, these insights emphasize the
different phases and stages of the B2B CJ (insight 4), the longitudinal
and parallel nature of different sub-journeys (insight 5), and the ne-
cessity of operational convergence between the buyers and sellers
(insight 6).
5.1. CJ phases and stages
Our fourth insight asserts that the B2B CJ is a multi-phase process,
consisting of a buying phase and an implementation-and-usage phase, each of
which is further divided into multiple stages (i.e., pre-, core-, and post-). The
first phase is the buying phase, where buyers and sellers negotiate the
agreement, guiding the subsequent implementation and usage phase
(Purmonen et al., 2023). The buying phase comprises three stages: pre-
buying, core-buying, and post-buying (Lemon & Verhoef, 2016), while
the implementation phase again consists of three stages: pre-service,
core-service, and post-service (Voorhees et al., 2017).2
Fig. 2 illustrates the complexity of the B2B CJ and the B2B CX
therein. As mentioned above, the B2B CX is a multilevel concept, spanning
across the individual employee, team, and organizational levels (i.e.,
across two or more teams). The figure delineates the multi-phase, multi-
stage nature of B2B CJ. In the purchase of complex B2B solutions, the
B2B CJ starts with a lengthy and complex buying phase through which an
organization selects a seller and negotiates the procurement conditions
(Purmonen et al., 2023). This initial phase is followed by another long-
term phase involving the implementation and usage of the procured solu-
tions (Purmonen et al., 2023). The seller organization is often (co–)
responsible for implementing, testing, maintaining, repairing, and/or
upgrading its offered solutions.
We elaborate on each of the phases (buying and implementation/
usage) and their underlying stages (pre-, core-, post-) below. Within all,
a strong interaction between the buyer and seller organizations is
(ideally) taking place.
5.1.1. The buying Phase: Pre-Buying, Core-Buying, Post-Buying
The pre-buying stage is arguably a critical period of the buying phase
in a B2B context, especially for complex solutions. The pre-buying stage
encapsulates the consideration and evaluation of potential seller orga-
nizations that can provide the necessary solutions to a particular need or
problem the buyer organization is facing. This starts with the buyer’s
consideration, which is marked by the recognition of a need (Edelman &
Singer, 2015; Steward et al., 2019). For example, the buyer’s procure-
ment team can identify a need for a new software, subsequently artic-
ulating and quantifying the required features, quantity, location, and
other relevant details. This stage involves input from the solution users
and experts, who provide valuable information; and it requires the seller
organization’s involvement, as it could help understanding the buyer’s
unique needs and preferences.
Next comes the core-buying stage, which is characterized by moments
of choice-making, order finalization, payment transactions, and
contractual agreements. Here, the choice to work together with a
particular seller organization marks the initiation of the core-buying
stage. However, this stage continues with multiple rounds of negotia-
tions between the buyer and seller. Even after reaching an initial
agreement, buyer organizations frequently raise objections regarding
various aspects (e.g., price, quantities, expected delivery time, return
policies, warranties, and other negotiated terms). Achieving an agree-
ment requires coordinating actions and individuals within and between
both sides. For example, a buyer’s purchasing agents, in conjunction
with their finance agents, determine the optimal price and delivery
details. Simultaneously, the seller’s representatives, working with their
logistics and finance units, finalize the details that will (ideally) be
customer-centric yet still profitable (Hochstein, Chaker, Rangarajan,
Nagel, & Hartmann, 2021). The conclusive agreement on these terms
finalizes the procurement contract. Agreements can take diverse for-
mats—either as one-time purchases or recurring orders (Feng, Zhang, &
Zhang, 2023).
Finally, the post-buying stage considers elements like the finalization
of contracts and the initial development of an implementation plan. This
further includes handling objections and the constant modifications of
the initial agreements to account for the challenges experienced in the
implementation phase (our next point). Negotiations and objection
handling may persist after the initial formal agreement—particularly for
complex solutions, where the sales cycle is long and payment unfolds
over multiple years. For high-value acquisitions, for instance, a buyer’s
objection may arise from the perception that a significant portion of the
total cost is due too early in the sales cycle. Here, the buyer organization
may ask for more balanced payments, which align with the completion
core steps in the implementation and usage phase.

5.1.3. Parallel journeys over time
As illustrated in Fig. 2, different individuals—be it in a role of
decision-makers, experts, or users—across various teams (e.g., legal,
financial, operational) may participate in different phases of the B2B CJ.
In most cases, especially for complex solutions, only a limited set of
individuals or teams—if any at all—remains involved throughout the
entire CJ. We posit this as our fifth core insight: The B2B CJ typically in-
volves different individuals/teams participating across different CJ phases
and stages, resulting in the occurrence of multiple (often parallel) sub-
journeys that together shape the overall B2B CJ. During this longitudinal
journey, multiple individuals/teams from the buyer and seller side-
s—playing different roles—go through their parallel journeys and
evolve at different paces (Purmonen et al., 2023). Although these in-
dividuals/teams have their own journeys, they also communicate with
one another, and their actions must be carefully aligned, as discussed
next.
As illustrated in Fig. 2, different individuals/teams commence their
journeys at different times because they engage in the CJ at earlier or
later stages. For example, the financial team may get involved once the
agreement is finalized to ensure payment during the buying phase. An
internal operations team may only be engaged during the implementa-
tion phase to facilitate the implementation of a bought solution. This
means that their specific journey begins at a later moment in the overall
CJ.

5.2. Operational Convergence: Alignment of the buyer and seller
organizations
The many phases (i.e., purchase and implementation/usage) and
stages (pre-, core-, post-) of the B2B CJ enhance the complexity of its
management. Our sixth insight emphasizes that a successful B2B customer
journey requires strong operational convergence between the buyer and seller
organizations, which develops over time. Importantly, we further posit that
the psychological convergence of B2B CX within the buyer organization
may help facilitate operational convergence by ensuring that internal
alignment exists when engaging with the seller. When teams within the
buyer organization share a cohesive perception of CX, their interactions
with the seller will likely be more consistent, likely simplifying the
process of operational convergence.
Considering operational convergence, significant efforts are needed
from the seller’s side (Homburg & Tischer, 2023). Although prior work
acknowledges this role, it has been somewhat underrepresented in the
B2B CJ/CX literature. A key aspect of operational convergence is
ensuring alignment between the buyer’s CJ and the seller’s sales fun-
nel—the structured process sellers use to guide prospects from initial
engagement to purchase and beyond (Moncrief & Marshall, 2005).
While the B2B CJ represents the buyer’s experience, the sales funnel
organizes the seller’s efforts into sequential steps, such as prospecting,
approach, presentation, overcoming objections, closing, and follow-up
(Moncrief & Marshall, 2005). For a smooth exchange, the seller’s ac-
tions must be adapted so that they match with the different steps of the
buyer’s CJ, ensuring a value-enabling B2B CX (Burchett et al, 2024).
This collaboration should lead to more informed purchasing decisions
and mutually beneficial outcomes—a concept known as holistic selling
(Kalwey et al., 2025).
Specifically, the seller organization is responsible for facilitating
operational coherence by strategically managing touchpoints across the
CJ. As highlighted in the work of Homburg and Tischer (2023), effective
CJ management hinges on four key capabilities of the seller organiza-
tion: (1) ensuring that each touchpoint aligns with the value proposition
of the buyer organization; (2) maintaining consistency across touch-
points; (3) integrating touchpoints to facilitate seamless transitions in
the CJ; and (4) closely monitoring and adapting the touchpoints so they
meet the diverse needs of the buyer organization. These capabilities are
essential for achieving operational alignment in B2B exchanges
(Schmitt, Klein, & Lussier, 2025).
Here, customer success management—characterized by a proactive
approach and a strong focus on customer value realization (Hochstein
et al., 2023)—has emerged as a novel critical function within seller or-
ganizations (Hochstein et al., 2020; Eggert et al., 2020; Goad et al.,
2024). This function plays an increasingly important role in ensuring
operational convergence between buyer and seller organizations, by
building a strong relationship between the buyer and seller
organizations.
Traditionally focused on the implementation/usage phase—where
customer success managers support buyers in realizing value (Prohl-
Schwenke & Kleinaltenkamp, 2022)—recent research also highlights the
potential role of customer success management in the buying phase. For
instance, the customer success management function can collaborate
with the dedicated sales team in the buying phase by aligning expecta-
tions, advising prospects, and ensuring a smooth transition from sales to
implementation (Boghe et al., 2024). Additionally, customer success
teams now facilitate onboarding by streamlining the transition from
purchase to implementation, reducing uncertainty, and ensuring a
smooth handover (Hilton et al., 2020). This makes sellers shift from
transaction-focused sales to an integrated, long-term relationship
approach that enhances mutual value in the B2B ecosystem (Goad et al.,
2024; Kleinaltenkamp et al., 2022).
To support these objectives, many customer success teams leverage
real-time technologies to monitor customer interactions across the CJ,
allowing them to track buyer engagement and identify friction points.
These technologies help sellers ensure that buyers experience value at
every stage of the CJ by proactively detecting and addressing challenges
before they escalate (Eggert et al., 2020). Key components of this pro-
active approach include customer health scoring and other advanced
metrics (Rouziou et al., 2025), which allow sellers to assess buyer
engagement, usage patterns, and satisfaction levels—often in real time.
By systematically analyzing these data points, managers can anticipate
potential churn risks and implement corrective measures before dissat-
isfaction escalates (Hochstein et al., 2023).
6. Conclusion
6.1. Conceptual Contributions
This conceptual research introduces a convergence-based theoretical
lens to advance our understanding of B2B CX and the B2B CJ. Specif-
ically, we focus on the role of psychological convergence for B2B CX and
that of operational convergence for the B2B CJ. Overall, we address
several gaps in the B2B CX/CJ literature.
First, we noted the lack of a comprehensive multilevel conceptuali-
zation of B2B CX—one that accounts for the interplay between indi-
vidual, team, and organization CX. While prior work has acknowledged
the multilevel nature of B2B CX, it has mostly remained a peripheral
consideration rather than a systematically theorized construct
(Homburg & Tischer, 2023; Purmonen et al., 2023; Witell et al., 2020;
Zolkiewski et al., 2017). Our research applies and extends the work of
Becker et al. (2023) on multi-level CX by using psychological conver-
gence as a theoretical lens (Kozlowski & Klein, 2000; Rafferty et al.,
2013). Psychological convergence explains how individual CX percep-
tions within a buyer organization may align over time, forming collec-
tive CXs at the team and/or organizational (i.e., across two or more
teams) levels. Various factors, including social interactions, leadership,
organizational structures, individual homogeneity, and the alignment
between the buyer and seller organizations may stimulate this form of
alignment. By establishing psychological convergence as the foundation
of B2B CX, our research provides a novel lens to clarify how B2B CX
emerges, evolves, and diffuses across the organization—a perspective
underexplored in the literature.
Second, we address the necessity of alignment between the buyer
and seller organizations and discuss the critical role played by sellers in
shaping CJs. Specifically, our research introduces and formalizes the
concept of operational convergence, which refers to the degree to which
buyer and seller organizations align their actions, processes, and
touchpoints throughout the B2B CJ. As articulated in our work, opera-
tional convergence underscores the importance for sellers to align their
operations closely with the buyer’s journey, thereby ensuring a cohesive
and frictionless experience that spans all phases of the CJ. This approach
is supported by recent studies that emphasize the need for sellers to
develop strong CJ management capabilities (Homburg & Tischer, 2023)
and literature on customer success management (Hochstein et al.,
2023). Here, our work bridges the B2B CX/CJ literature with the
emerging domain of customer success management. While B2B CX and
CJ research have traditionally focused on the buyer’s experience,
customer success management offers a structured approach for man-
aging long-term buyer–seller interactions, particularly in complex B2B
exchanges (Eggert et al., 2020; Hochstein et al., 2020).
Next, the introduction of a convergence-based lens in the B2B CX and
CJ space not only strengthens the conceptual foundations of these

interrelated concepts; it also offers a foundation for understanding the
reciprocal dynamics between the B2B CX and CJ. Rather than treating
CX and CJ as separate domains, our approach highlights their interde-
pendence through a convergence lens, positioning psychological and
operational convergence as mutually reinforcing mechanisms that shape
buyer–seller relationships over time. On the one hand, operational
convergence leads to a cohesive B2B CX at the team and organization
levels. With operational convergence, individuals within both organi-
zations can more easily develop shared understandings, common goals,
and complementary capabilities that allow them to operate cohesively.
This alignment enhances the likelihood that individuals’ CX within the
buyer organization will converge psychologically. On the other hand,
psychological convergence within the buyer organization may reinforce
and enhance operational convergence. When individuals within and
across teams share a mutual perception of their CX, it simplifies the
coordination of decision-making processes. This internal alignment, in
turn, facilitates more structured collaboration with the seller, allowing
the seller organization to optimize its actions, processes, and touch-
points accordingly. As a result, psychological convergence feeds back
into the operational convergence between the two organizations.
This dynamic interaction creates a virtuous circle of convergence.
Operational convergence fosters psychological convergence within the
buyer organization by ensuring that the seller’s actions are perceived
consistently and positively across different individuals and teams. In
turn, psychological convergence strengthens operational convergence.
Here, we argue that a unified internal perspective within the buyer or-
ganization more easily enables effective collaboration and alignment
with the seller. This virtuous circle may enhance the overall B2B CX and
CJ, creating deeper and more sustained value for both buyer and seller
organizations.
6.2. Managerial implications
Our research provides actionable insights for B2B managers to
navigate the complexity of the B2B CX and CJ. These implications focus
on two key areas: managing the multilevel nature of B2B CX and
ensuring operational convergence throughout the B2B CJ. For each, we
offer concrete recommendations for both seller and buyer organizations,
emphasizing practical steps rather than restating conceptual
foundations (see Table 3 for a summary).
A first key implication is the need for a multilevel approach to
managing CX within the buyer organization. Seller organizations must
recognize that CX is not restricted to a singular, individual-level phe-
nomenon; rather, it spans across teams and the organization. Sellers
must assess CX convergence: Do individual experiences converge at the
team and organizational levels? If not, what barriers exist, and how can
they be overcome? Therefore, CX strategies should not be confined to
interactions with a set of individuals but must account for how CXs are
shared and interpreted within and across relevant teams of the buyer
organization. This requires sellers to map internal influence structures
and proactively engage with key individuals who shape how their teams
perceive and evaluate the seller. Engaging these “CX amplifiers” can
accelerate convergence within and across teams, ensuring the seller’s
value is communicated effectively inside the buyer organization. Rather
than relying solely on procurement contacts, sellers should actively
foster relationships with those responsible for implementation and daily
usage to strengthen CX beyond the purchase decision.
From the buyer’s side, organizations must ensure internal coordi-
nation to prevent divergence in their CX with the seller. For instance,
procurement, IT, finance, and operational teams often engage with the
seller at different points, leading to fragmented CXs if these interactions
are not appropriately managed. Buyers should, therefore, establish in-
ternal CX convergence mechanisms, such as structured seller evaluation
processes that integrate feedback from multiple teams. By formalizing
internal discussions about seller performance, buyers can identify in-
consistencies early and ensure seller-related actions are based on a
comprehensive organizational perspective.
A second implication is that sellers must recognize that CX does not
necessarily and naturally converge in all cases—it is shaped by, among
other things, internal discussions, social influence, and leadership
within the buyer organization. To support this process, sellers could
design engagement initiatives encouraging shared positive CXs, such as
cross-team training sessions, co-creation workshops, and multi-
stakeholder onboarding programs. Ensuring consistent messaging and
touchpoints across all interactions with the buyer limits the chances for
CX divergence. Additionally, sellers must proactively identify potential
sources of misalignment, such as discrepancies between how decision-
makers and end-users perceive the seller. One practical step is to

Next, along with B2B CX convergence, operational convergence is
critical to ensuring a seamless CX throughout the CJ. The transition from
“buying” to “implementation-usage” is a frequent pain point in B2B
relationships, as different buyer teams engage with the seller at different
stages/phases. From the seller’s side, ensuring a smooth transition re-
quires a tight integration between the different teams that interact with
the buyer organization. Rather than treating the CJ as separate stages
owned by different teams, sellers should create a continuous engage-
ment model where responsibility shifts smoothly across internal func-
tions. A key enabler of operational convergence is a dedicated customer
success function, which plays a central role in coordinating interactions
between the buyer and seller organizations (Hochstein et al., 2023).
Sellers should ensure that these managers are well-integrated into their
organization, coordinating cross-functional efforts and proactively
addressing potential frictions. Establishing practices (such as structured
onboarding frameworks, continuous performance monitoring, and
multi-stakeholder reviews) may allow to keep both buyer and seller
teams aligned throughout the CJ.
From the buyer’s perspective, operational convergence also requires
a certain level of internal coordination. Buyers may be advised to
establish cross-functional project teams that engage with the seller
across all CJ phases, ensuring that knowledge and expectations transfer
smoothly. A structured approach to seller onboarding—where imple-
mentation teams are involved in pre-buying discussions—can prevent
misalignment once the contract is signed.
Finally, both seller and buyer organizations may implement mea-
surement frameworks to track B2B CX across the CJ. Sellers should
integrate CX analytics across different buyer touchpoints, moving
beyond general satisfaction scores to assess alignment between indi-
vidual-, team-, and organization-level experiences. By segmenting
feedback across different buyer teams, sellers can detect early warning
signs of misalignment and intervene proactively. Buyers, in turn, should
implement structured seller evaluation models that consolidate insights
from multiple functions, ensuring that seller performance assessments
reflect the entire CJ rather than isolated interactions.
6.3. Future research Directions
Our research highlights the importance of managing the multilevel
nature of B2B CX and ensuring operational convergence throughout the
B2B CJ. Yet, several research questions remain (see Table 4 for an
overview and detailed questions).
First, future research should further explore the mechanisms driving
psychological convergence in B2B CX. While we have identified factors
such as social interactions, leadership, and organizational structures as
key drivers, it remains unclear how these factors interact over time and
whether specific organizational settings facilitate faster and/or stronger
convergence than others. For example, at the team level, the history of
how a group forms, its background, and its stage of development could
shape how CX perceptions align or diverge over time (Kozlowski & Bell,
2003). Similarly, the entry of newcomers or turnover within teams may
disrupt psychological convergence, requiring further research on how
CX evolves within dynamic team structures (De Keyser et al., 2020;
Rubin & Martin, 1994). Beyond internal organizational factors, external
pressures such as market conditions or competitive dynamics may also
influence convergence; yet their role remains largely unexplored.
Second, CX at the team and organization level is not always fully
converging, and divergence may be an overlooked aspect of B2B CX.
While we have argued that individual CX can align into a collective team
or organization CX, this process is neither automatic nor guaranteed. In
practice, different individuals may be influenced by distinct factors (De
Keyser et al., 2020; 2025), leading to misalignment in their perceptions
of the seller and its offerings. Future studies should investigate the an-
tecedents and consequences of CX divergence and how sellers can best
intervene to manage misalignment. Additionally, while sellers typically
strive for CX convergence, not all divergence forms are necessarily
negative. At the beginning of the CX process, a certain degree of
divergence is natural and may even be beneficial (Reinartz, Krafft, &
Hoyer, 2004). For example, if a seller introduces a new technological
innovation, some members of the buyer organization may embrace it
enthusiastically while others resist, leading to temporary divergence.
Future research should examine under what conditions CX divergence
fosters creativity and adaptability versus when it leads to friction,
dissatisfaction, or loss of trust.
Also, the longitudinal nature of B2B CX and its evolution over time
remains underexplored. Psychological convergence is rarely stat-
ic—buyer teams may initially perceive their interactions with a seller
differently, but their evaluations may align (or further fragment) over
time. Future research should examine how B2B CX unfolds dynamically,
identifying key turning points or touchpoints that significantly impact
convergence or divergence. Longitudinal research designs—tracking CX
perceptions at multiple time points—would provide valuable insights
into how convergence strengthens or weakens across different journey
phases, enabling organizations to develop proactive strategies rather
than reactive corrections.
Beyond psychological convergence within the buyer organization,
future research should also focus on how to better align buyer–seller
interactions throughout the CJ. While our conceptualization of opera-
tional convergence highlights the need for sellers to align their actions,
processes, and touchpoints with buyer needs, we still lack a precise
understanding of how this alignment unfolds, what obstacles arise, and
what interventions are most effective at different CJ stages.
Moreover, with the increasing role of digital tools and Artificial In-
telligence (AI) in B2B exchanges (Mikalef, Islam, Parida, Singh, & Alt-
waijry, 2023), it is crucial to explore how emerging technologies
facilitate—or disrupt—operational convergence in B2B CJ. The strategic
use of social media, for instance, has been proposed as a way to enhance
B2B interactions (Cartwright, Liu, & Raddats, 2021), but we still lack
clarity on how social media fosters CX alignment in complex, multi-
stakeholder settings. Research should investigate which platforms are
most effective at different CJ stages, how digital touchpoints integrate
with human interactions, and what types of content best facilitate
convergence.
Similarly, AI-driven tools, such as large language models (LLMs),
predictive analytics, automated recommendation systems, and conver-
sational agents, are expected to enhance convergence by providing real-
time, data-driven insights and interaction capabilities (Li, Peng, Xing,
Zhang, & Zhang, 2021). However, their implementation remains poorly
understood in B2B contexts. While AI has the potential to accelerate
convergence by standardizing exchanges and ensuring consistency, it
may also introduce risks, such as depersonalized interactions or algo-
rithmic biases, which could create new CX divergence challenges
(Gligor, Pillai, & Golgeci, 2021; Grewal, Guha, Satornino, & Schweiger,
2021). Future research should critically examine both the benefits and
unintended consequences of AI in shaping psychological and operational
convergence in B2B CX and CJ.

Lastly, a promising but underexplored area of research concerns the
role of internal touchpoints within the buyer’s organization. While prior
studies have focused on buyer–seller interactions, the internal coordi-
nation of different teams within the buyer organization also shapes CX
and CJ outcomes. Future studies could investigate how teams like IT,
procurement, or operations—acting as internal intermediaries—affect
the overall CX/CJ trajectory. For example, after purchasing a software
solution, the buyer’s IT team may become (partly) responsible for
implementing it and training other departments. In this role, the IT team
acts as an internal touchpoint, representing the seller by interpreting
instructions, troubleshooting issues, and ensuring adoption across the
organization. Key research questions include: How can sellers effectively
engage with and influence these internal touchpoints to ensure a
smoother CJ? What strategies can sellers use to identify key internal
stakeholders and align their interactions to optimize CX outcomes across
the buyer’s organization?
