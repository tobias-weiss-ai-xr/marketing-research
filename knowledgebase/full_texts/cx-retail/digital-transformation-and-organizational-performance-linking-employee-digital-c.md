Abstract
Digital transformation creates organizational value only when technological resources are translated into
employeecapabilities,superiorcustomerinteractions,andultimatelystrongereconomicoutcomes.Thisstudy
develops and tests a three-construct model linking Employee Digital Competence (EDC), Customer
Experience (CE), and Financial Outcomes (FO). PLS-SEM was applied to 200 complete survey cases using
IIISR SEM v2.0.7. The reflective measurement model demonstrated satisfactory reliability, convergent
validity, and discriminant validity. EDC positively influenced CE (beta = 0.545, p < .001), while CE
positively influenced FO (beta = 0.545, p < .001). The indirect effect of EDC on FO through CE was
significant (0.297, p < .001), supporting the proposed mediating mechanism. The model explained
approximately 30% of the variance in both CE and FO and demonstrated positive predictive relevance. The
findingspositioncustomerexperienceasanimportantmarket-facingpathwaythroughwhichemployeedigital

1. Introduction
Digital transformation has moved from a technology-
adoption issue to a broader organizational challenge
concerned with how firms create and capture value
through digital technologies. Contemporary research
describes digital transformation as a process in
which digital technologies trigger strategic responses,
changes in organizational structures and routines,
and new pathways for value creation (Vial, 2019).
Related work emphasizes that digitization,
digitalization, and full digital transformation reshape
business models, customer behavior, organizational
capabilities, and performance metrics rather than
merely automate existing activities (Verhoef et al.,
2021). This shift is important because investments in
platforms, analytics, artificial intelligence, cloud
systems, and digital communication do not
automatically produce organizational performance.
Their value depends on whether people within the
organization can use them productively and translate
them into improved market outcomes.
Employee Digital Competence (EDC) is therefore a
central but sometimes under-specified micro-level
component of digital transformation. Digital
competence encompasses the ability to locate and
evaluate digital information, communicate and
collaborate through digital technologies, solve
problems, and use digital tools creatively in work
settings (Van Laar et al., 2018). These capabilities
matter because employees are the operational
interface between digital infrastructure and day-to-
day value creation. Even when organizations possess
advanced technological resources, weak employee
competence can limit adoption quality, slow
response time, reduce service consistency, and
prevent digital tools from being integrated into
customer-facing processes. Conversely, digitally
capable employees can use information more
effectively, coordinate across channels, respond to
customers faster, and adapt their work as
technologies and customer expectations evolve.
A second issue concerns the mechanism through
which employee-level digital capability becomes
organizational value. Much of the digital
transformation literature examines strategy,
technology, business-model innovation, or firm-level
digitalization, while customer experience research
commonly focuses on the customer's journey,
touchpoints, emotions, service encounters, and post-
purchase evaluations (Lemon & Verhoef, 2016;
Homburg et al., 2017). These streams are related but
are not always integrated. This separation leaves an
important explanatory gap: an organization may

The financial relevance of customer experience is
supported by the service-profit logic, which links

Empirical work has shown that customer satisfaction

with financial performance and shareholder value
(Anderson et al., 2004; Chi & Gursoy, 2009). A meta-

digital settings, the implication is that employee
competence may become financially valuable not
simply because employees can operate digital tools,
but because those abilities help produce smoother,
more responsive, and more valuable customer
experiences.
Existing digitalization research also suggests that
organizational resources do not always have a
straightforward direct association with financial
performance. For example, Eller et al. (2020) found
that digitalization linked organizational resources to
financial outcomes and cautioned against assuming
that employee skills alone automatically create
financial returns. Similarly, dynamic-capabilities
research portrays digital transformation as an
ongoing process of sensing, learning, adapting, and
reconfiguring activities around new technologies and
customer value (Warner & Wager, 2019; Matarazzo
et al., 2021). This evidence strengthens the case for
testing an explicit intermediate pathway rather than
treating employee digital competence as a direct
proxy for organizational performance.
Accordingly, this study examines a parsimonious
three-variable model in which EDC influences CE,
which in turn influences FO. The study addresses
three questions: whether employee digital
competence improves customer experience, whether
customer experience improves financial outcomes,
and whether customer experience carries an indirect
effect from employee digital competence to financial
outcomes. The study contributes by connecting a
workforce-level digital capability with a customer-
level value mechanism and an organizational
economic outcome in one structural model.
Practically, the model helps managers distinguish
between technology training as an input and
customer experience as the mechanism through
which that input can be converted into financial value.
The empirical analysis uses PLS-SEM on 200
complete cases and evaluates measurement quality,
structural relationships, mediation, explanatory
power, and predictive relevance.
2. Literature Review and Hypothesis
Development
2.1 Theoretical foundation: digital capabilities
and the service-profit mechanism
The conceptual model combines a digital-capabilities
perspective with the logic of the service-profit chain.
Digital transformation research increasingly treats
technological change as an organizational capability
problem rather than a hardware or software problem.
Bharadwaj et al. (2013) argue that digital
technologies blur the boundary between business
strategy and information-technology strategy and
create new possibilities for value creation and
capture. Vial (2019) similarly conceptualizes digital
transformation as a process in which digital
technologies generate disruptions, strategic
responses, structural changes, and altered value-
creation paths. From a dynamic-capabilities
perspective, organizations must continuously sense
opportunities, learn, coordinate, and reconfigure
resources so that digital technologies become
embedded in operational routines (Warner & Wager,
2019). Matarazzo et al. (2021) further show how
digital transformation can support customer value
creation when sensing and learning capabilities
translate technologies into new channels,
interactions, and value propositions. Within this logic,
employee digital competence can be understood as a
micro-level capability that makes organizational
digital resources usable in practice.
The service-profit perspective provides the
complementary market-to-financial logic. Its core
proposition is that internal organizational conditions
influence employee behaviors and service delivery,
which shape customer responses and subsequently
financial results. Evidence has repeatedly linked
customer satisfaction and customer-facing outcomes
with economic performance (Chi & Gursoy, 2009;
Anderson et al., 2004), and the meta-analysis by
Hogreve et al. (2017) supports the broader chain
while also showing contextual variation. The present
study adapts this logic to digital transformation:
employee competence is the internal capability,
customer experience is the market-facing mechanism,
and financial outcomes represent organizational
value capture. This combination is useful because it
avoids assuming that digital capability has value in
isolation; instead, it proposes that capability must be
translated into experiences that customers recognize
and reward.

customer processes. The relationship is also

transformation can create new channels and new

hypothesis is proposed:
H1: Employee Digital Competence has a positive and
significant effect on Customer Experience.
2.3 Customer Experience and Financial Outcomes
Customer Experience represents the customer's
holistic evaluation of interactions with an
organization, including the extent to which needs are
understood, desired outcomes are achieved,
interactions require minimal effort, problems are
handled effectively, and the provider is dependable.
Klaus and Maklan (2012) developed the EXQ scale to
capture experience beyond conventional service-
quality or satisfaction measures, and later work
showed that experience quality can predict
important customer outcomes such as loyalty and
recommendation (Klaus & Maklan, 2013). Customer
experience therefore has economic relevance
because it can influence retention, repurchase,
advocacy, price tolerance, and the stability of future
customer relationships.
The link between customer evaluations and financial
value has a strong empirical foundation. Anderson et
al. (2004) reported a positive association between
customer satisfaction and shareholder value, arguing
that customer satisfaction influences the level, timing,
and risk of future cash flows. Chi and Gursoy (2009)
found that customer satisfaction had a positive effect
on financial performance and could transmit
employee-related effects to financial outcomes. At a
broader level, Hogreve et al. (2017) found meta-
analytic support for the service-profit chain and
highlighted the role of customer responses in
connecting internal service conditions to firm
performance. Although customer experience is
conceptually broader than satisfaction, the same
economic logic applies: when customers perceive
interactions as easy, responsive, trustworthy, and
outcome-oriented, the organization is better
positioned to retain demand and realize financial
benefits. Hence:
H2: Customer Experience has a positive and
significant effect on Financial Outcomes.
2.4 Mediating role of Customer Experience
The mediation argument follows from the distinction
between organizational capability and realized
market value. Employee digital competence is an
internal resource: it can improve information
processing, coordination, technology use, and
problem solving, but these benefits become
economically meaningful only when they alter
outcomes valued by customers. This reasoning is
consistent with studies showing that digitalization
can mediate resource-performance relationships
(Eller et al., 2020) and with service-profit research in
which customer outcomes carry internal
organizational effects toward financial performance
(Chi & Gursoy, 2009). A mediation model is therefore
theoretically preferable to an assumption that
employee digital competence must directly produce
profit or revenue. In the present model, customer
experience is the mechanism through which digitally
capable employees contribute to financial outcomes.
The indirect pathway also aligns with dynamic-
capabilities thinking because resources create value
when they are deployed and reconfigured around
market needs rather than merely possessed (Warner
& Wager, 2019). Accordingly:
H3: Customer Experience positively mediates the
relationship between Employee Digital Competence
and Financial Outcomes.

3. Methodology
3.1 Research design and analytical sample
The study used a quantitative, cross-sectional
explanatory design to test the proposed relationships
among Employee Digital Competence, Customer
Experience, and Financial Outcomes. The analytical
dataset contained 200 complete cases. The SEM
export reported that no case was excluded for
missing or non-numeric values on model indicators
or control variables. The model contained 17
observed indicators: six for EDC, eight for CE, and
three for FO. All indicators were coded on a five-point
response metric, with the observed values spanning
1 to 5. The analytical export available for manuscript
preparation does not retain sector, geographic
location, respondent demographics, sampling-frame
information, or recruitment metadata. Accordingly,
the study does not make population-specific claims
that are not supported by the supplied analysis
record; this limitation is revisited in the discussion.
A sample of 200 is adequate for estimating this
parsimonious PLS-SEM model, which has only one
predictor for each endogenous construct.
Contemporary PLS-SEM guidance discourages
exclusive reliance on the traditional '10-times rule'
and recommends considering statistical power and
model characteristics instead (Kock & Hadaya, 2018;
Hair et al., 2019). The present sample provides a
substantial ratio of observations to indicators and
structural paths and is well above levels typically
encountered in minimum-sample discussions for a
model of this simplicity. No control variables,
moderators, higher-order constructs, or multi-group
comparisons were specified in the estimated model.
3.2 Measures
All three constructs were specified as reflective Mode
A constructs. Employee Digital Competence was
measured with six adapted indicators based on the
working-professional digital-skills framework of Van
Laar et al. (2018). The indicators capture information
search and evaluation, digital communication and
collaboration, creative use of digital technologies, and
digital problem solving. Customer Experience was
represented by eight adapted indicators informed by
the EXQ framework of Klaus and Maklan (2012,
2013), focusing on need fulfilment, ease, desired
outcomes, flexibility, problem resolution, confidence,
and dependable interaction. Financial Outcomes was
measured with three perceptual business-economic
performance indicators - sales/revenue growth, net-
income/profit growth, and return on investment -
based on Venkatraman and Ramanujam (1987). The
use of perceptual financial measures is consistent
with strategy research when comparable objective
accounting data are not available across respondents
or organizations.

and evaluates both the explanatory and predictive
performance of a compact mediation model. The

Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach's

AVE above .50 indicating that a construct explains

Discriminant validity was evaluated through the
Fornell-Larcker criterion and the heterotrait-
monotrait ratio (HTMT). Because the Fornell-Larcker
criterion can fail to detect discriminant-validity
problems in some settings, HTMT was treated as the
primary complementary diagnostic (Henseler et al.,

bootstrap confidence intervals based on 500 HTMT
resamples; values below .85/.90 and upper
confidence limits below 1.00 were interpreted as
supportive. Structural paths were evaluated using
standardized coefficients, standard errors, t statistics,
p values, and 95% bootstrap confidence intervals.
Inner VIF values were inspected for collinearity,
while R-squared and f-squared were used to describe
explanatory power and effect magnitude.
The mediating hypothesis was tested from the
specific indirect effect EDC -> CE -> FO. Modern
mediation analysis emphasizes direct inference on
the bootstrapped indirect effect rather than relying
on a sequence of separate significance tests (Nitzl et
al., 2016). Importantly, the structural model did not
include a direct EDC -> FO path. Consequently, the
analysis can establish whether the specified indirect
mechanism is significant, but it cannot distinguish
partial from full mediation or compare the indirect
pathway with an estimated direct effect. Predictive
assessment included Q-squared and a construct-level
PLSpredict procedure, comparing PLS prediction
errors with a benchmark. Shmueli et al. (2019)
recommend such out-of-sample-oriented checks to
complement explanatory PLS-SEM assessment.
Composite-model indices including SRMR, d_ULS, NFI,
and RMS_theta were also reported as descriptive
screening diagnostics; because definitions can vary
across PLS-SEM implementations, these indices were
not treated as stand-alone proof of model validity.

4.2 Structural model and hypothesis testing
The structural model produced two positive and
statistically significant direct relationships. EDC had a
positive effect on CE (beta = .545, SE = .047, t =
11.544, p < .001, 95% CI [.452, .634]), supporting H1.
The associated f-squared value was .422, indicating a
large effect within the estimated model. CE, in turn,
positively affected FO (beta = .545, SE = .048, t =
11.306, p < .001, 95% CI [.449, .639]), supporting H2;
its f-squared value was .421, also classified as large.
Inner VIF was 1.000 for both structural paths, so
collinearity was not a concern in this one-predictor-
per-equation model.
The model explained 29.7% of the variance in CE (R-
squared = .297; adjusted R-squared = .293) and
29.6% of the variance in FO (R-squared = .296;
adjusted R-squared = .293). The specific indirect
effect EDC -> CE -> FO was .297 (SE = .042, t = 7.119,
p < .001, 95% CI [.220, .385]). Because the confidence
interval excluded zero, H3 was supported. The result
demonstrates a statistically reliable indirect pathway
from employee digital competence to financial
outcomes through customer experience. However,
the direct EDC -> FO path was not estimated in the
specified model, so the result should not be labeled
partial or full mediation.

effect indicate that employees' ability to use digital

digital-skills literature beyond employee task
performance. Van Laar et al. (2018) established
digital skills as a multidimensional set of capabilities
for working professionals; the present results
suggest that these capabilities also matter for how
customers experience organizational processes. The
result is consistent with digital-transformation
research emphasizing that technologies create value
only when they are embedded in organizational
routines and capabilities (Vial, 2019; Warner &
Wager, 2019). It also aligns with current evidence

showing a different downstream consequence:

Second, Customer Experience was positively
associated with Financial Outcomes. The effect was
again .545 and was statistically precise, with the 95%
confidence interval remaining well above zero. This
finding supports the central economic logic of
customer-experience management. Customers who
perceive that an organization understands needs,
delivers desired outcomes, reduces interaction effort,
responds flexibly, and resolves problems effectively
are more likely to produce behaviors that support
revenue stability and profitability. The result is
consistent with work demonstrating links between
customer satisfaction and shareholder value
(Anderson et al., 2004), with evidence that customer
satisfaction predicts financial performance (Chi &
Gursoy, 2009), and with the broader service-profit-
chain meta-analysis (Hogreve et al., 2017). The
present study contributes by using a broader
customer-experience construct rather than treating
satisfaction alone as the market-facing outcome.
Third, the indirect effect from EDC to FO through CE
was significant. This is theoretically important
because it clarifies how an internal digital capability
can be connected with an organizational financial
outcome. Digital competence does not generate
revenue merely because employees possess technical
skills. Its business value emerges when employees
use those skills to improve customer-relevant
processes and interactions. This mechanism is
consistent with the dynamic-capabilities perspective,
in which resources must be deployed and
reconfigured around changing market requirements
(Matarazzo et al., 2021), and with findings that
digitalization can transmit the effects of
organizational resources to financial performance
(Eller et al., 2020). It also parallels the service-profit-
chain logic in which customer outcomes carry
internally generated improvements toward financial
results. The model therefore offers a concise micro-
to-market-to-financial explanation of digital
transformation value creation.
The study makes three theoretical contributions.
First, it places employee digital competence at the
microfoundation of digital transformation. Digital
transformation studies often operate at the level of
strategy, technologies, business models, or
organizational architecture (Bharadwaj et al., 2013;
Hanelt et al., 2021; Verhoef et al., 2021). By explicitly
modeling employee competence, the study shows
that the human ability to use digital resources is a
necessary analytical layer between organizational
digital investment and market performance. Second,
it connects the digital-capabilities literature with
customer-experience theory. Customer experience is
frequently treated as the result of customer journeys
and touchpoints (Lemon & Verhoef, 2016), but the
results emphasize that employee competence is one
organizational antecedent capable of shaping those
touchpoints. Third, the mediation result connects
these literatures with financial performance and
supports a mechanism-based interpretation rather
than a simple technology-performance association.
The explanatory and predictive evidence strengthens
this interpretation. EDC explained 29.7% of the
variance in CE, and CE explained 29.6% of the
variance in FO. These values indicate meaningful but
incomplete explanatory power, which is realistic
because customer experience and financial outcomes
are affected by many factors beyond the two modeled
constructs. The positive Q-squared and Q-
squared_predict values, together with lower PLS
prediction errors than the benchmark, also indicate
that the model is not solely descriptive within the
observed sample. This is important because PLS-SEM
is increasingly evaluated as a causal-predictive
approach in which explanation should be
accompanied by predictive assessment (Shmueli et
al., 2019). At the same time, the findings should not
be interpreted as proving causality because the data
are cross-sectional.
For managers, the findings imply that digital-
transformation programs should not evaluate
employee training only through completion rates,
technology-adoption counts, or system usage.
Training and capability development should be tied
to customer-facing tasks: retrieving the right
information at the point of service, coordinating
across digital channels, solving customer problems
without unnecessary transfers, using digital data to
personalize responses, and maintaining consistent

whether digital competence is being converted into
value. If competence rises but customer experience
does not, the problem may lie in process design,
system usability, incentives, or cross-functional
coordination rather than in skill alone.

Several limitations qualify the conclusions. First, the
analysis is cross-sectional and cannot establish
temporal causality. Second, the analytical export used
for manuscript preparation does not contain sector,
geography, respondent demographics, or sampling-
frame information, restricting claims about
generalizability. Third, the model contains no
controls, moderators, or group comparisons;
industry conditions, firm size, digital maturity,
employee role, and customer-contact intensity could
alter the observed relationships. Fourth, financial
outcomes are perceptual rather than directly audited
accounting values. Fifth, the supplied SEM output
does not report a dedicated common-method-bias
test, so method variance cannot be ruled out. Future
studies should use multi-source designs in which
employees report digital competence, customers
provide experience evaluations, and financial
outcomes are drawn from organizational records.
Temporal separation and marker-variable or
method-factor approaches could further strengthen
inference.
A final modeling limitation is especially important for
interpreting H3. The specified structural model does
not contain a direct EDC -> FO path. Therefore, the
significant indirect effect establishes the proposed
mediated pathway but does not permit a statistical
conclusion that mediation is 'full' rather than
'partial.' Future research should estimate the direct
path alongside the indirect path and compare
competing models. Additional work could test
whether digital maturity, service climate, leadership
support, organizational agility, or task
interdependence strengthens the EDC-CE
relationship. Multi-group analysis could examine
whether the mechanism differs across service versus
manufacturing contexts or across high- and low-
digital-intensity sectors. These extensions would
preserve the parsimonious logic of the present model
while improving boundary-condition knowledge.
6. Conclusion
This study examined how employee-level digital
capability can be connected with organizational
performance through a customer-facing mechanism.
The proposed model positioned Employee Digital
Competence as the internal capability, Customer
Experience as the mediating pathway, and Financial
Outcomes as the organizational result. Using 200
complete cases and PLS-SEM, the analysis found
strong measurement quality and support for all three
hypotheses. Employee Digital Competence positively
influenced Customer Experience, Customer
Experience positively influenced Financial Outcomes,
and the indirect EDC -> CE -> FO effect was
statistically significant. The model explained about
30% of the variance in each endogenous construct
and also demonstrated positive predictive relevance.
The central implication is that digital transformation
should be understood as a value-conversion process
rather than a technology-acquisition process.
Employees may possess digital skills, but those skills
become strategically meaningful when they improve
the way customers interact with the organization.
Customer experience is therefore not merely a
marketing outcome; it is a mechanism through which
workforce digital competence can be translated into
financial value. This interpretation integrates insights
from digital transformation, dynamic capabilities,
customer-experience management, and the service-
profit chain.
For practice, the findings support investment in
digital capability development that is explicitly
connected to customer journeys and service
processes. Organizations should identify which
employee digital behaviors reduce customer effort,
improve responsiveness, strengthen problem
resolution, and increase confidence, and then
monitor whether improvements in those experience
indicators precede stronger financial results. For
research, longitudinal, multi-source, and multi-
industry studies are needed to establish temporal
ordering, test the omitted direct EDC-to-FO
relationship, incorporate objective financial
measures, and identify boundary conditions. Within
these limits, the study provides a parsimonious
empirical explanation of how the human side of
digital transformation can contribute to
organizational performance through customer
experience.
