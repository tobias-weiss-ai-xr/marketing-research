Abstract
Due to the rapid evolution of digital media, digital platforms pose a regulatory and self-regulatory challenge.
This study aims to examine patterns of exposure to traditional and digital channels of alcohol marketing and
their association with current alcohol use among adults in Thailand using a multi-province cross-sectional sur -
vey. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among adults in Thailand across five designated provinces repre -
senting Thailand’s major geographical regions: Bangkok (central), Chonburi (eastern), Chiang Mai (northern),
Nakhon Ratchasima (northeastern), and Nakhon Si Thammarat (southern). Data were collected and analysed
using Stata version 17. Descriptive statistics were computed. Design-based bivariable analysis (F(df), Rao-
Scott chi-square test) and complex-survey logistic regression analysis, incorporating sampling weights, 15 Pri -
mary Sampling Units (PSUs) and 5 Strata (df = 10), were employed to evaluate the association between current
drinking (drank/did not drink in the last 12 months) and exposure to alcohol marketing over the past 30 days.
Complex survey logistic regression analysis, incorporating sampling weights, with 15 Primary Sampling Units
(PSUs) and 5 Strata (df = 10), was employed to evaluate the association between digital alcohol marketing
exposure and sociodemographic characteristics. Association was declared at P-value < 0.05 and adjusted odds
ratio (AOR) with 95% confidence interval. Archer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was employed to evaluate
goodness-of-fit for complex survey design. The highest traditional exposure was advertising through signs at
retail/wholesale shops (47.3%) and television (46.9%). The highest digital alcohol marketing exposure was
advertising through ads appearing while watching video (16.8%) and parties/concerts/live music streams online
(6.5%). Digital alcohol marketing, traditional alcohol marketing exposure, and number of multichannel expo -
sures were not significantly associated with the last 12 months’ alcohol consumption. In Multivariable analysis,
government officer/State enterprise (AOR = 0.41, 95% CI 0.23–0.74), business/trading/freelance (those with no
regular salary) (AOR = 0.46, 95% CI 0.27–0.78), 10,000–20,000 Baht monthly income (AOR = 1.96, 95% CI
1.21–3.15) and being Buddhist (AOR = 0.22, 95% CI 0.08–0.61) had significantly associated with digital alco -
hol marketing. The traditional alcohol marketing exposure (retail/wholesale shops and television) and digital
marketing exposure (video streaming ads and online parties/concerts/live streams) were the most prominent
forms of marketing. However, digital marketing exposure and traditional marketing exposure, and the number
of multichannel marketing exposures, were not significantly associated with the last 12 months’ alcohol con -
sumption. However, digital marketing alcohol exposure was associated with average monthly income, primary
occupation (Government officer/State enterprise and no regular salary), and being a Buddhist. These findings

Background
Globally, alcohol consumption is one of the leading public health problems, contributing to 2.6 million deaths,
representing 4.7% of all deaths1 and causes a serious economic burden2–4. In Thailand, alcohol consumption is a
major public health problem. According to WHO regional reports, Thailand ranks second-highest in alcohol con-
sumption and heavy episodic alcohol drinking in Southeast Asia5. Thailand implemented strict legislative policy
under the Alcohol Beverage Control Act; recent evidence shows gaps in the enforcement of alcohol regulation
have led to substantial increases in alcohol consumption among the productive population 6. A population-based
survey in 2021 revealed that the prevalence of alcohol consumption among the adult Thai population was 28%7,
47% in males and 11% in females8,9.
Recent evidence indicates the prevalence of binge drinking among the productive-age Thai population was
11.7%, which was unlikely to be distributed uniformly across all geographic areas 10, indicating a unique spatial
pattern of chronic health outcomes and lifestyle risk factors observed across Thai provinces 11, representing the
second leading risk factor for disability adjusted life years (DALYs) among Thai men7. Recent evidence indicates
that regular alcohol consumption is linked with simultaneous use of tobacco among Thai adults, showing that
commercial digital marketing promotion works within interconnected different behavioural risks12 and recent evi-
dence indicating that environmental factors such as alcohol marketing play a vital role in alcohol consumption13.
Alcohol marketing has a positive impact on increasing alcohol consumption and binge drinking 13. Exposure
to digital alcohol marketing affects adults with the initiation and ongoing alcohol consumption 14,15, and recent
evidence indicated the remarkable increase in adoption of digital media use among older adults in Thailand,
though previous research focused on young age in Thailand16. Especially, adults are susceptible to traditional and
digital alcohol marketing containing alcohol messages that influence young adults positively to drink more and
experience drinking-related problems14. In many countries, including Thailand having strict alcohol policies on
advertising17, there is continued use of alcohol branding and sponsorship, and an increasing adoption of technical
ways to avoid restrictions18.
Consequently, there has been a marked shift toward digital and social media channels, which offer more
covert, interactive, and highly targeted marketing approaches. Digital platforms, including social media influ -
encers, branded content, and online campaigns, pose a regulatory and self-regulatory approach that is challeng -
ing related to the rapid evolution of digital media 19,20. In Thailand, control of digital media is challenging, with
adults exposed to alcohol related user generated content predominantly carrying a celebratory tone, reinforcing
a pro-alcohol narrative that influences young users’ attitudes towards drinking21. To combat the rapidly growing
influence of the digital environment, primary health systems need readiness. However, recent national studies
revealed significant gaps in e-health competencies, digital intelligence, and workforce readiness among primary
health care personnel across Thai provinces 22,23. Despite the emerging concern, there is a paucity of evidence
detailing a specific pattern of exposure to both traditional and digital media among Thai adults. Therefore, this
study aims to examine patterns of exposure to traditional and digital channels of alcohol marketing and their
association with alcohol consumption among adults in Thailand using a multi-province cross-sectional survey.
This study provides critical evidence to inform stronger regulatory policies and mitigate the public health burden
of alcohol consumption in Thailand.

Methods and materials
Study design and setting
A cross-sectional survey was conducted among adults in Thailand across five designated provinces representing
Thailand’s major geographical regions: Bangkok (central), Chonburi (eastern), Chiang Mai (northern), Nakhon
Ratchasima (northeastern), and Nakhon Si Thammarat (southern). This study was approved by the Human
Research Ethics Committee (EC) of Walailak University (WUEC-21-315-02) and supported by the Center for
Alcohol Studies and implemented by the Center for Social and Business Development, Thailand.
Study participants and sampling
A stratified multistage sampling technique was employed. One province was randomly selected, and five desig -
nated provinces served as the primary strata. Within each stratum, sampling was conducted across administrative
stages using a cumulative systematic sampling approach based on administrative population data. In the second
and third stages, districts and sub-districts were randomly selected with a probability proportional to their popu-
lation size and sorted in descending order to establish the sampling frame. Sampling intervals(k = N/n) were cal-
culated, and units were selected starting from a simple random sampling (r) followed by a systematic sampling
interval of r+(n-1) k, ensuring selection proportional to population weight. Fourth-stage sampling was sampling
households using systematic sampling based on civil registration records. Fifth stage: Selection of one adult aged
18 years or above per household selected using simple random sampling when more than one eligible individual
is present. The initial target sample was 1303, and the final achieved sample was 1330 participants (102.1% of
the target). Data were collected through field surveys, using a questionnaire administered face-to-face by trained
interviewers during the data collection period. Data were collected in May, 2022.
Measurement and variables
Data were collected using a structured questionnaire (Supplementary file S1) and face-to-face interviews. The
data collection tool included demographic characteristics (age, sex, education level, occupation, average monthly
income, and religion), access to alcohol, exposure to alcohol advertising, and digital alcohol marketing. Par -
ticipants demographic variable included in the study including sex variable had two level: male and female,
age categorized into six categories (< 20, 20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60 + years), primary occupation had six
categories (farmer/labourer, government officer/state enterprise, private company (regular salary), business/trad-
ing/freelance (no regular salary), retired/homemaker/unemployed/unable to work and student), average monthly
income classified into four level (≤ 10,000 Baht, 10,001–20,000 Baht, 20,001–30,000 Baht and > 30,000Baht),
level of education into three level (grade 6 or lower, grade 7–12 or vocational certificate and bachelor’s degree/
high vocational diploma/associate degree and above) and religion into two level (Buddhist and others). Alcohol
consumption in the last 12 months was categorized as a binary outcome coded “1” (Drank), while those who
reported “drank before, but not in the past 12 months” or “never drank in lifetime” were grouped and coded as
“0” (Did not drink). Traditional alcohol marketing exposure over the past 30 days includes (television, radio,
newspapers or magazines, brochures/pamphlets, advertising signs at retail/wholesale shops, advertising signs
at restaurants, pubs, bars, and karaoke, billboards, transport vehicles/trucks, sports events/competitions, other
festive events, movies, and symbols on clothing. Traditional alcohol marketing exposure was defined as when
participants reported exposure (yes) if participants answered at least one digital marketing exposure (yes) for all
listed items and not exposure(no) if participants answered no across all listed items. Participants who reported
“can’t remember” were excluded from complete-case analysis (n = 941). Digital alcohol marketing exposure
over the past 30 days includes posts selling alcohol online/social media with home delivery, posts advertising/
discounts/promotions for alcohol online/social media, posts inviting responsible drinking at home from alcohol

Data quality management
Before actual data collection, the data collectors and supervisor were selected, and two days of training were
given. Data was collected by 5 data collectors who had a Bachelor’s degree. At the time of data collection, a daily
field checkup and supervision by a trained supervisor were done to check whether the data collector was col -
lecting data based on the plan or not, and report every evening a field report to the lead researcher to check any
incomplete data and any ambiguity during field data collection to develop a census on the next day of the data
collection process. Double data entry by two independent persons was employed before actual data entry into the
software to prevent inaccurate data entry errors. Before proceeding to data entry and processing stage, accuracy
and consistency of interconnected data were verified at the data entry and processing stage.
Data analysis
Data were analysed using Stata version 17. Descriptive statistics were computed. Design-based bivariable analy-
sis (F(df), Rao-Scott test) was employed to evaluate the association between alcohol consumption over the last
12 months (outcome) and exposure to alcohol marketing over the last 30 days (exposure to traditional alcohol
marketing exposure, digital alcohol marketing exposure, and multichannel exposure). Data were analysed using
complex survey procedures, incorporating sampling weights, 15 Primary Sampling Units (PSUs) and 5 Strata
(df = 10) to evaluate the association with digital alcohol advertising exposure, and explanatory variables were
socio-demographic characteristics, including sex, age, education level, primary occupation, average monthly
income, and religion. Bivariable and multivariable complex-survey logistic regression were conducted. In bivari-
able analysis, p-values < 0.25 were selected for the final model. In multivariable analysis, variable with p-values
< 0.05 was declared statistically significant. The Archer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was evaluated for model
diagnosis for complex survey design [F(9,2) = 1.17, P = 0.4112], confirming the model is acceptable. Finally, the
result was reported with the adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with 95% confidence interval. A multicollinearity test was
conducted by using variance inflation factors (VIF), and the mean VIF across all variables was 1.03, and individ-
ual variable VIF remained below 10.0, showing there was no multicollinearity among variables (Supplementary
Table S1). Sensitivity analysis was conducted by complex-survey logistic regression analysis ( n = 1,330 using
complete case analysis of inclusion of the sample of participants with full recollection (Supplementary Table S2).

Results
Socio-demographic characteristics
A total of 1330 adults participated in the current study. More than half of the participants, 731(55.0%), were
female. The mean age of study participants was 44.4 years (SD = 14.6), with the largest age group, 321(24.1%),
being aged 50–59 years. Over one-third of study participants (497, 37.4%) were engaged in business, trading, or
freelance work without a regular monthly salary; 329 (24.7%) were engaged in farming or labour. Nearly half,
625 (47.0%), reported monthly salaries between 10,001 and 20,000 baht, followed by more than one-third, 552
(41.5%), who reported a monthly salary of not exceeding 10,000 baht. Nearly half, 609(45.8%), were Grade
7–12 or V ocational Certificate educational level, followed by nearly one-third, 413(31.1%), who were Grade 6 or
lower level of education. The majority, 1232(92.6%), were followers of the Buddhist religion. Regarding alcohol
behaviour, 44.2% of participants reported drinking alcohol within the last 12 months, while the remaining 56.8%
had not drunk in the last 12 months (had a history of alcohol consumption but not in the last year and had never
consumed alcohol in their lifetime) (Table 1).

Association between alcohol marketing exposure and past 12-month alcohol drinking
In complete-case analysis after the exclusion of unremembered exposure, among the participants ( n = 959),
720(75.08%) of participants had traditional alcohol marketing exposure and 239(24.92%) of study participants
had no traditional alcohol marketing exposure.
In complete-case analysis after the exclusion of unremembered exposure, among the participants ( n = 941),
637(67.69%) of participants had exposure to digital alcohol marketing and 304(32.31%) of study participants had
no digital alcohol marketing exposure. Furthermore, among the participants ( n = 920), 280(30.43%) of partici -
pants had exposure to ≥ 4 more channels, 426 (46.30%) had 1–3 channels, and 214 (23.26%) had no (0 channels)
exposure to alcohol marketing (digital and traditional marketing).
Design-based bivariable analysis (F(df), Rao-Scott chi-square test) and complex-survey logistic regression
analysis. Design-based bivariable analysis revealed a varying pattern of association between alcohol marketing
exposure and past 12-month alcohol consumption behaviour (Table 4). Exposure to traditional advertising was
reported by 48.3% of past 12-month drinkers, slightly lower than 51.7% among those who did not drink; this dif-
ference was not statistically significant (X2 = 2.42, F(1, 10), p = 0.152, and AOR = 0.76, 95% CI:0.45–1.28, p =
0.269). Exposure to digital advertising was reported by 41.6% of past 12-month drinkers, compared to 58.8% of
those who did not drink; this difference was not statistically significant (X2 = 2.41, F(1,10), p = 0.151, and AOR
= 1.27, 95% CI:0.77–2.10, p = 0.297).
Furthermore, the number of marketing channels exposed was not significantly associated with drinking behav-
iour. Among those who drank alcohol in the last 12 months, 51.4% had exposure ≥ 4 channels compared to 46.9%
who had exposure 1–3 channels and 42.6% reported no exposure, which were non-significant associations across
categories (X2 = 1.85, F (1.6,16.3), p = 0.192) (Table 4).

Discussion
In the current study, traditional alcohol marketing exposure remains highly prevalent, particularly at the point
of sale (POS) (retail/wholesale shops: 47.3%) and on television (46.9%). These were supported by studies con -
ducted on alcohol marketing exposure in different parts of the world24–26, including a study conducted on alcohol
advertising in stores and adolescent alcohol use 27, 28, as well as national studies conducted in Australia 29–31 and
a study conducted in China 32. The persistence of POS was mainly correlated with manufacturers adding value
to their relationship with retailers, as well as tools increasing sales for both parties 33. Furthermore, despite the
self-regulation measures by the alcohol industry in different countries, including Thailand 34, 35, POS market -
ing remains a major loophole in Thailand 36. Similarly, the high exposure to television might be associated with
alcohol advertisements during a televised live match, and superimposed on the original game, such as during
the English Premier League37. In many settings, digital marketing was over-dominating, reflects it rapid growth;
however, this finding does not substantially underscore the importance of traditional marketing exposure25.

According to the current study, the highest prevalence of digital alcohol marketing was video streaming ads
(16.8%) and online parties/concerts/live streams (6.5%). Whereas traditional alcohol marketing, such as radio
(0.5%) and newspapers (1.1%), was shown to be nearly unused or forgotten. Current study evidence is supported
by a study that demonstrated that nearly two-thirds (59%) of content contained video ads 38. Furthermore, this
evidence is supported by studies that revealed the majority of alcohol marketing brands use the video format on
social media platforms39, 40. This might be due to the video format being more understandable and participatory
in the digital era of alcohol marketing, to connect both supplier and customer 19. Even in the digital era, digital
marketing may overlook traditional marketing; traditional marketing plays a pivotal role in the contemporary
landscape41.
The findings showed digital and traditional alcohol marketing exposure was not significantly associated with
the last 12 months’ alcohol consumption was not statistically significant. The observed study evidence was in
contrast to studies that demonstrated an association between digital alcohol marketing exposure and alcohol-
related behaviour13, 15, 42–45. This discrepancy might be explained by the fact that the study measured behaviour
of past-12 month may suffer from recall worsening compared to recent 30-day recall46, and simple exposure may
not determine alcohol consumption behaviour; rather, careful analysis of selection attention is needed47.
In the current study, being Buddhist protective factor against exposure to digital alcohol marketing. Being
Buddhist had 88% lower odds of digital alcohol marketing exposure compared to other religion followers (AOR
= 0.22, 95% CI 0.08–0.61, P = 0.008). This explained Buddhist predominantly led the community campaign
chanting during the dry season Buddhist Lent, which was associated with the suffering of media exposure 48, 49
and digital marketing-driven, and relied on peer-to-peer norms, and when local culture rejects drinking culture,
which is associated with less promotion, user-linked with marketing algorithms 50, 51. Furthermore, it might be
explained by the fact that the algorithm of the ad network mainly uses behavioural data, which targets users52.
Based on the observed study findings, participants with 10,000–20,000 Baht monthly had 1.96 times higher
odds of digital alcohol marketing exposure compared to higher monthly income (AOR = 1.96, 95% CI 1.21–
3.15). This evidence is supported by recent study evidence from a study conducted in Hong Kong shown low-
to-middle income population in rapid urbanization spend majority of their time on screens and is easily exposed
to ad network easily 53, 54. This is explained by industries frequently targeting lower- to higher-income groups
through tailored digital marketing, sponsorship, and digital engagement due to rapid urbanization, increased
population density, increased disposable incomes, and uneven policy enforcement, in which commercial actors
can expand market reach and influence consumption patterns55, 56.
In the current study finding participants with Government officer/State enterprise had 59% lower odds of
digital alcohol marketing exposure compared to farmer/labourer (AOR = 0.41, 95% CI 0.23–0.74), and business/
trading/freelance (no regular salary) had 54% lower odds of digital alcohol marketing exposure compared to
farmer/labourer (AOR = 0.46, 95% CI 0.27–0.78). This is explained by evidence from conducted which shown
that digital media algorithms focus on manual workers and agricultural workers 57, and manual labourers are
highly exposed to local/digital ads for local social gathering practices and festivity with local policy sanctions
during these festivity and local gathering events56, 58. This observation might be due to digital alcohol promotion
primarily targeted at the local community that is easily accessed in local community social gatherings and social
festivity in Thailand.
Limitations of the study
Though this study had several strengths, there are some limitations. First, the study employed a weighted sample
size for data analysis, limit national wide generalizability. Second, the study employed a cross-sectional study
design, which may limit the establishment of a causal relationship. Third, information for the current study was
collected based on self-reported past 30-day exposure, which may introduce recall bias and misclassification.
However, the current study used a large sample size, which helped to mitigate recall bias in practice. Fourth,
marketing exposure was measured for recall of the last 30 days, while alcohol consumption was assessed for the

Finally, the study included five provinces’ strata across Thailand, which may limit true national generalizability.
Public implication of study
Current study findings suggest critical evidence that digital platforms and POS marketing remain major loop -
holes in current alcohol control policies. Regulatory policy revision and enforcement of restrictions on algorithm
behaviour targeting, user-generated promotion accountability and online brand and marketing influencers should
be restricted beyond general restrictions in Thailand.
Conclusion
In the current study, traditional alcohol marketing exposure remains highly prevalent, particularly at the POS
(retail/wholesale shops) and on television, whereas radio and newspapers were nearly unused/forgotten. The
highest prevalence of digital alcohol marketing was video streaming ads and online parties/concerts/live streams.
Digital alcohol marketing, traditional alcohol marketing, and the number of multichannel exposures for the past
30 days and past 12-month alcohol consumption behaviour were not significant. However, average monthly
income (10,001–20,000 Baht), primary occupation (Government officer/State enterprise and Business/Trading/
Freelance (no regular salary) and being a Buddhist were significantly associated with exposure to digital alcohol
marketing.
