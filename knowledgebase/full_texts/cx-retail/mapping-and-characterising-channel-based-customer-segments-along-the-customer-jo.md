Abstract
The proliferation of retail channels is driving a shift towards omnichannel and, in turn, reshaping customer behaviour,
thereby presenting unique opportunities and challenges for data-driven marketing strategies. Given these developments,
this systematic literature review provides a novel overview of channel-based customer segmentation research by cat -
egorising 62 articles into a three-dimensional classification framework and conducting a multi-layered analytical approach
across the primary findings. The empirical findings confirm ten recurring, analytically established segments with distinct
channel patterns, which merge into four main groups called “Traditionalists”, “Research Shoppers”, “Multi-/Omnichannel
Shoppers”, and “Indifferent Shoppers”. This approach provides a comprehensive perspective, helping practitioners and
researchers to better understand customers’ complex channel behaviours within today’s dynamic omnichannel landscape.
The article further emphasises the significance of product categories on the prevalence of specific segments, and explic -
itly examines key demographic, psychographic, and behavioural variables which are essential when analysing customer
behaviour and channel choice-based segmentation in particular. These insights highlight the need for data-driven, analyti -
cal decision-making when designing segment-specific marketing strategies and communication tactics. Lastly, this review
identifies the limits of the current research body and offers clear directions for future research avenues.

Meanwhile, the developments within the retail sector are

and comprehend which channels and touchpoints are used

impact of marketing strategies (Cham et al. 2022; Ramos

In order to gain insights into customers’ shopping
behaviour, authors increasingly conduct studies segment -
ing shoppers based on their channel and touchpoint usage
or preference along the customer journey (e.g., Ieva and
Ziliani 2018; Park and Kim 2018). However, it becomes
apparent that the approaches to studying this research topic
vary greatly between articles. Various sales channels or
touchpoints and different stages of the buying process are
observed, while the inclusion of product categories, as well
as the consideration of psychographics, demographics, or
behavioural variables characterising customers, diverges.
Research finds differences in the influence of these vari -
ables on segment affiliation, leading to divergent num -
bers of segments and characteristics of shoppers in multi-,
cross-, or omnichannel environments, possibly tracing back
to the product category investigated (e.g., Sands et al. 2016;
Tueanrat et al. 2021).
This systematic literature review (SLR) addresses the
growing and diverse body of literature by providing a
novel overview within the field of channel-based customer
segmentation and segment-defining features, filling a gap
that has so far remained unexplored. While an initial step
within this SLR involves the compilation and categorisa -
tion of existing primary studies implementing a framework
that spans the literature’s key dimensions of customer jour-
ney stages, channel ecosystems, and product categories, its
primary contribution lies in deeper, analytically integrative
analyses. Rather than solely listing existing studies or enu -
merating isolated results, this SLR provides analytically
overarching insights in response to calls for empirical
generalisations that synthesises idiosyncratic findings into
cross-sector knowledge (Anderl et al. 2016). Simultane -
ously, the SLR reveals critical research gaps and identi -
fies key avenues for future research. In doing so, this SLR
delivers clear advancements to omnichannel segmentation
literature and customer journey research, while highlight -
ing operational directives for marketing analytics. Specifi -
cally, these contributions are addressed through three core
research objectives.
In order to resolve the fragmentation across prior chan -
nel-based customer segmentation results, the first objective
comprised the confirmation of overarching customer seg -
ments that differ in their channel and touchpoint usage along
the customer journey. This synthesis advances customer
journey and omnichannel segmentation literature by reveal-
ing how diverse shopper groups utilise, combine, switch,
and thus navigate from pre-purchase to purchase and post-
purchase stages. As customer segmentation represents a
core pillar of marketing analytics (France and Ghose 2019),
these findings directly bridge theoretical findings with mar-
keting analytics. Firstly, they provide initial insights into ten
substantial existing clusters, and secondly equip analytical
teams with a pre-validated, viable customer segmentation
base.
The second research objective strives to evaluate if and
how product categories can moderate channel choices
across the customer journey and, consequently, alter the
emergence and persistence of specific customer groups. On
the one hand, customer journey literature can be advanced
by identifying which touchpoints are particularly critical
within specific product contexts. On the other hand, while
segmentation literature is enriched by pinpointing which
segments persist across product categories versus those
that are context-specific, thereby addressing the demand for
cross-industry generalisation highlighted by Anderl et al.
(2016). Simultaneously, these findings offer crucial input
for marketing analytics, as accounting for channel patterns
on a product-specific-level improves the accuracy of chan -
nel attribution in omnichannel environments and enables
practitioners to design and implement more effective, con -
text-specific targeting strategies for key shopper groups.
Beyond the product-level analyses, the impact of channel
ecosystems and the inclusion of specific customer journey
stages is further incorporated to illustrate how the ten iden -
tified segments evolve and emerge across these underlying
research dimensions.
Finally, the third research objective aims to identify addi-
tional variables that drive significant differences between
segments beyond channel choice along the customer journey.

Specifically advancing omnichannel segmentation litera -
ture, this SLR adopts a two-fold analytical approach. First,
it systematically evaluates the overall statistical strength of
commonly investigated demographic, psychographic, and
behavioural indicators by synthesising how consistently
they yield significant results across product categories. Sec-
ond, it investigates where cross-study tendencies emerge to
profile the ten identified shopper groups. The findings reveal
that statistically significant variables oftentimes display
varying characteristics for the same cluster across primary
studies, proving that segment profiling cannot rely on static
traits alone. These insights deliver actionable advancements
for marketing analytics as they pinpoint which indicators
may serve as universal differentiators across the research
field and which as intra-group differentiators that further
separate clusters with identical channel behaviours. While
the findings provide initial insights and targeting strategies,
they simultaneously highlight the need for context-specific
validation, as empirical differences often emerge through
the interplay of multiple factors. Analytical teams can lever-
age these results to select appropriate covariates, refine
database segmentation models, and design data-driven seg-
ment-specific targeting strategies.
Conceptual background
Customer journey
The customer journey describes a shopper’s purchase
decision process and encompasses all interactions with a
retailer through touchpoints. With the increasing number
and complexity of touchpoints within omnichannel envi -
ronments, customer journeys become more complex and
the need to understand customers’ channel and touchpoint
choices intensifies (Hallikainen et al. 2019; Zimmermann
et al. 2022). Prior research identified three to five main
stages of decision processes (Neslin et al. 2006; Puccinelli
et al. 2009; Lemon and Verhoef 2016). Lemon and Verhoef
(2016) comprise the previous frameworks into a three-stage
model, whereas the pre-purchase phase includes all interac-
tions of the customer before a purchase, e.g., need/problem
recognition and information search. The purchase phase
contains interactions with the retailer during the actual pur-
chase, covering, among others, choice and payment behav -
iours. Every customer’s interaction with a retailer after a
purchase is incorporated within the post-purchase stage,
which might include consumption or service request. The
literature within this review draws on this concept to inves-
tigate customers’ channel choices along the journey stages
to discover segments with heterogeneous usage patterns
(e.g., Frasquet et al. 2019).
Segmentation variables
Market segmentation targets to group heterogeneous cus -
tomers into homogenous clusters to serve them according to
their differing needs and usage behaviours (Yankelovich and
Meer 2006; Dolnicar et al. 2018). According to Dolnicar et
al. (2018) geographic, socio-demographic, psychographic,
and behavioural variables are the prevalent coefficients to
distinguish customer groups. The (socio-) demographic
base includes variables like age, gender, or income, while
psychographics combine psychological and demographic
criteria and comprise variables such as interests, values,
and opinions (Kotler et al. 2017). Lastly, behavioural vari-
ables investigate actual or reported behaviour and might
include prior experiences or spending, also including chan -
nel behaviour as the most important variable in this review
(Yankelovich and Meer 2006; Dolnicar et al. 2018).
Product categories
Customer (omnichannel) behaviour might deviate between
product categories (e.g., Valentini et al. 2020). The dis -
tinction between hedonic and utilitarian goods represents
a common approach (Viejo-Fernández et al. 2020). While
products comprise hedonic and utilitarian traits to vary -
ing degrees, many might be characterised as primarily
hedonic or primarily utilitarian (Dhar and Wertenbroch
2000). Hedonic goods are characterised by their emotional
perception, whereas utilitarian goods are defined by their
functional and practical properties and focus on fulfilling
basic requirements (Hellén and Sääksjärvi 2011). Other
methods for differentiating goods encompass the level
of involvement and risk (Kushwaha and Shankar 2013;
Nakano and Kondo 2018) or differentiating between search
and experience goods (Kim et al. 2019). As the included
studies repeatedly draw on hedonic and utilitarian motives
to explain diverse multi- and omnichannel behaviours (e.g.,
Cervellon et al. 2015; van Nguyen et al. 2024) and suggest
that multi- or omnichannel tendencies are more prevalent in
the context of hedonic goods (e.g., Kushwaha and Shankar
2013; Frasquet et al. 2019), this distinction is also applied
to the present SLR.
Methodology
Research objective
The main objective of SLRs is to categorise and analyse
the literature within a given field of research, this study
specifically investigates research on customer segmenta -
tion based on preferred channel usage along the customer

journey. Although some articles provide brief overviews of
channel-based customer segmentation literature, encom -
passing four to 11 studies (e.g., Nakano and Kondo 2018;
Ngoh and Groening 2022; Blömker and Albrecht 2024),
no comprehensive review covering the entire body of lit -
erature currently exists. To address this synthesis, discover
future research avenues, and derive actionable insights for
both researchers and practitioners, a SLR shall be conducted
(Kitchenham 2004). The current SLR attempts to answer
the following research questions (RQ).
RQ1:  Can recurring customer segments be identified based
on their channel usage across different stages of the
customer journey?
RQ2:  Does product category influence the presence and
characteristics of common customer segments?
RQ3:  Which psychographic, socio-demographic, and
behavioural variables significantly distinguish and
characterise customer segments?
Search protocol
Following the PRISMA scheme by Page et al. ( 2020), the
available literature on customer segmentation based on
channel usage is accumulated. The first step comprised a
selection of keywords and terms that were based on the
respective research topic (Siddaway et al. 2019). Different
combinations of terms were created, which resulted in a
total of 16 search strings. The entire search protocol can be
found in Fig. 1. The literature search was carried out within
the databases Web of Science, EBSCOhost, and Science
Direct, and was limited to titles, abstracts, and keywords
to enhance the eligibility of the identified research. The
next step comprised the definition of suitable inclusion and
exclusion criteria (Page et al. 2020).
To ensure the author’s and readers’ correct understand -
ing, only English articles were taken into consideration,
while only peer-reviewed studies were included to ensure
research quality. The temporal scope of the search com -
prised articles from 1992 onwards, as the groundwork for

Focusing on the research design and content of articles,
studies needed to conduct a segmentation of shoppers based
on their channel usage in at least one customer journey
stage. Articles only examining determinants of (omni-)
channel usage or choice without a segmentation have been
excluded. Additionally, the studies needed to include at least
two distribution channels in their research design to meet the
multiple-channel perspective of this work. Studies purely
focusing on the service sector, like banking, insurance, or
government services, were further removed. Due to unique
decision-making and buying processes, as well as diverg -
ing channels, a direct comparison of services to physical
goods might prove difficult and inexpedient (Dalla Pozza
et al. 2018; Li et al. 2021). Existing research predominantly
focused on business-to-consumer (B2C) contexts, while
the business-to-business (B2B) perspective remains scarce,
comprising only three articles up until 2025 (Schwarz et al.
2025). Given the substantial differences in research matu -
rity and the contextual differences between B2C and B2B
buying behaviour (Grewal et al. 2015), a direct comparison
would be inconclusive. Thus, only studies examining a B2C
environment with a distinct product focus were included
within this SLR.
The literature search was conducted in late February 2025
and identified a total of 1,135 articles. After the removal of
duplicates, an additional 550 articles had to be excluded
due to ineligible document types (n=541, e.g., book chap -
ters, newspaper articles, or non-academic online sources)
or language (n=9). The next step included the screening of
titles, abstracts, and keywords, and an initial assessment by
means of the eligibility criteria, leading to 132 remaining
articles for full-text evaluation. Due to institutional restric -
tions, three studies could not be retrieved. While assessing
the remaining articles, a citation search took place, of which
19 records were eligible for full-text assessment. Finally, 62
articles, 51 from the database search and 11 from the cita -
tion search, were included in the review. Figure 2 displays
the PRISMA flow diagram.

To ensure transparency regarding channel usage and seg-
ment affiliation, coding guidelines were developed. Given
the scope of the coding guide, representative proxies are
outlined below to illustrate both channel usage rules and
segment classification.1 First, channel or touchpoint usage
was categorised into three levels: non-usage, occasional/
light usage and actual usage. To accommodate heterogenous
measurement approaches across the research body, baseline
thresholds had to be applied. For instance, when 1–7 Likert-
scales were used, usage levels were operationalised as non-
usage (1–2), occasional/light usage (3–4), and actual usage
(5–7). For percentage data, baselines were set to: non-usage
(< 10.0%), occasional/light usage (10.0–39.9%), and actual
usage (≥ 40.0%). The second step comprised the identifi -
cation and classification of customer groups according to
their channel usage behaviours across the customer journey.
For instance, a group was coded as mainly store-focused if
they primarily used the store across all examined customer
journey phases, with light usage of other channels occurring
only as isolated incidents. Illustratively, the “Conventional
consumers” in Wang et al. ( 2014) exhibited 68.0% store
and 32.0% web usage during the search stage, while they
almost exclusively used the store (95.3% store vs. 4.7%
web) for purchasing. Conversely, a segment was coded as
“Multiple channels to search – buy offline” if two condi -
tions were met: First, the primary study included at least the
pre-purchase and purchase stages, and second, the custom -
ers displayed actual channel usage behaviour in at least two
channels, or occasional/light usage across multiple alterna -
tive channels in addition to a primary channel during search,
yet they completed the purchase in a traditional offline chan-
nel, typically the store. Channels or touchpoints within the
pre-purchase phase could encompass combinations of mul -
tiple offline or online channels, or a combination of both. 2
For example, the “Online to offline shoppers” (2013 data
set) in Herhausen et al. ( 2019) displayed 60.0% web and
40.0% store usage within the search phase, but purchased
100.0% in-store (with mobile usage at only 1.2% along the
customer journey).

descriptions, as well as quantitative measures. Original seg-
ment names were of secondary importance, as this review
aimed to reflect observed channel patterns rather than rely -
ing on initial labels. In some cases, this resulted in certain

To minimize potential researcher bias, both intracoder and
intercoder reliability tests were conducted using Brennan
and Prediger’s (1981) Kappa (k n). Since the coding guide -
lines and coding scheme were developed through a deduc -
tive and iterative process, the intracoding test encompassed
all segment affiliations in a second coding stage, yielding a
value of k n = 0.86, representing an “almost perfect” result
(Landis and Koch 1977). Following literature suggestions,
10.0–25.0% of a data set should be recoded to assess a reli-
able estimate for intercoder reliability (O’Connor and Joffe
2020). Accordingly, the next step involved an independent
researcher recoding a randomly drawn subsample (26.2%)
to assess intercoder reliability, resulting in a similarly high
value of kn = 0.85. Any coding disagreements were resolved
using a consensus approach through which deviations were
discussed until a joint decision was reached (O’Connor and
Joffe 2020). Overall, the reliability checks validated the
high quality of the coding process and the suitability of the
coding guidelines.
Following key literature on SLRs, data syntheses serve to
structure, summarise, and collate extracted results into core
contributions that provide practical, real word relevance,
while also evaluating the extent of shared consensus across
primary studies (Tranfield et al. 2003; Kitchenham 2004). In
market segmentation, a fundamental criterion is substantial-
ity, meaning that customer segments must exhibit sufficient
empirical evidence to be economically feasible and justify
targeted marketing strategies (Tynan and Drayton 1987;
Dolnicar et al. 2018). Leveraging methodological guidelines
from Hair et al. (2019), managerially relevant segments are
often operationalised using a threshold of approximately
10.0%, ensuring they are substantial enough for segment-
specific marketing efforts. Applying this rationale to the cur-
rent SLR, shopper segments reported in at least six articles
(≈9.7%, approximating the 10.0% threshold) were included
in the main analysis to ensure meaningful cross-study com-
parability as well as theoretical and managerial relevance. 3

cific products, channel ecosystems, and customer journey
phases, contrasting within-segment distributions with con -

Traditionalists
The category “Traditionalists” holds customer segments
who mainly use a single channel throughout their whole
customer journey, with little to no cross-channel interac -
tions. It comprises the subgroups: “Mainly store-focused
shopper”, “Call centre or catalogue prone shopper”, and
“Mainly web-focused shopper”.
The “Mainly store-focused shoppers”  predominantly
use the physical store throughout the whole buying process
and represents the second largest cluster within this review
(56.5%) (e.g., Papatla and Bathnagar, 2002; Thomas and
Sullivan 2005; Soysal and Krishnamurthi, 2015). “Call
centre or catalogue prone shopper” segments were detected
by 17.7% of studies, which were predominantly published
before 2015. These consumer groups primarily use either
the catalogue (e.g., Kaufman-Scarborough and Lindquist
2002; Dholakia et al. 2005), the call centre (e.g., Gensler
et al. 2007), or in two cases both channels (Li et al. 2015;
Mark et al. 2019), whereas the predominant segment is the
catalogue-focused one. Furthermore, 50.0% of the primary
studies revealed “Mainly web-focused shopper” segments,
which predominantly use the web channel across all cus -
tomer journey stages (e.g., Cao 2012; Huang et al. 2016;
Hernant and Rosengren 2017).
Research shoppers
The original term of research shopping is defined as a pro -
cess in which shoppers search in one channel but make
their purchases in another channel (Verhoef et al. 2007).
The most prominent behaviours are webrooming (searching
online and buying offline) and showrooming (searching
offline and buying online) (van Nguyen et al. 2024). How-
ever, as customer journeys become more complex (Heitz-
Spahn et al. 2024), customer segments appear with a more
intense search phase using multiple channels while buying
in one channel only (e.g., De Keyser et al. 2015; Blömker
and Albrecht 2024), broadening the original definition of
research shopping behaviours (van Nguyen et al. 2024).
While showrooming is widely discussed within the litera -
ture, segments of pure showroomers as well as those search-
ing in multiple channels but purchasing online fell below
the 10.0% threshold. Hence, this shopper category focuses
on the two prevailing subgroups of “Webroomers” and
“Multiple channels to search - buy offline shopper”.
“Webroomers” were discovered by 16.1% of primary
studies. Beyond the basic definition, webroomers can be
further divided into subgroups using diverse segmentation
criteria like free-riding behaviours and loyalty intentions
(Kleinlercher et al. 2020), or digital channel formats in the
pre-purchase stage (e.g., Nakano and Kondo 2018; Kim et
al. 2019). 21.0% of the literature body detected “Multiple
channels to search – buy offline shopper”  segments. They
may use a variety of diverse offline and/or online channels
in the pre-purchase phase, but purchase exclusively in an
offline channel, mostly the store (e.g., Pauwels et al. 2011;
Park and Kim 2018). However, in one case it comprises the
store and catalogue channel (De Keyser et al. 2015).
Multi-/omnichannel shoppers
The category “MC/OC Shoppers” includes four different
subgroups of customers that show diverse multichannel or
omnichannel behaviours throughout the customer journey.
This confirms the statement of Mosquera et al. ( 2019) that
omnishoppers are not one large homogenous group, but
rather diverse segments of shoppers with varying channel
usage behaviours.
Although the first segment uses multiple channels
throughout their buying process, they do so in a moderate
extent. “Occasional MC/OC shoppers”  (43.5%) differ in
their primary channel choice, and fall into two subgroups:
the predominant cluster with an offline focus and light
online usage (e.g., Bèzes 2021; Danaher et al. 2020) and
the minority cluster exhibiting the reverse behaviour (e.g.,
Wang et al. 2014; Tueanrat et al. 2021). Subordinate chan-
nel usage typically ranges from 10.0 to 15.0% (e.g., Brand
et al. 2020; Mulders et al. 2024), though some cases report
rates up to 30.0% or even slightly under 40.0% (Harris et al.
2017; Kondo and Okubo 2022).

Appearing in 58.1% of primary studies, “MC/OC shop-
pers (only web)” represent the most prominent group in this
analysis. While regularly using multiple channels across
the buying process, their online usage is limited to the web
channel, omitting mobile and social media channels. These
segments differ in channel usage levels of diverse offline
formats, like discounters vs. supermarkets, or the web chan-
nel at different stages of the customer journey (e.g., Cervel-
lon et al. 2015; Homburg et al. 2019). It is worth noting that
the high group share may partly derive from primary stud -
ies failing to differentiate between specific digital channels,
even in recent years (e.g., Brand et al. 2020; van Nguyen et
al. 2024).
“Digital MC/OC shoppers (web, mobile [, social
media])” embody the smallest segment within this analysis
(11.3%). They use a variation of digital channels throughout
their buying process, while omitting any offline channels.
Shoppers divide into a primary cluster that uses only web
and mobile options (e.g., Wang et al. 2015; Park and Kim
2018) and a minority cluster that adds social media to one
or both channels (e.g., Valentini et al. 2020; van der Veen
and van Ossenbruggen 2015). Since two primary studies
revealed both subgroups (Herhausen et al. 2019; Blömker
and Albrecht 2024), variation in digital channel preferences
is unlikely to be a mere artifact of excluding social media
from the research design.
The “True OC/MC shopper (web, mobile, [, social
media])” segment (16.1%) uses the entirety of available
offline and online channels to varying degrees within their
buying process. These shoppers divide into a minor sub -
group comprising shoppers only using web and mobile
alongside offline channels (Ieva and Zilliani, 2018; Mag -
gioni et al. 2020), and a dominant cluster of costumers who
additionally incorporate social media (e.g., Sands et al.
2016; Frasquet et al. 2019; Valentini et al. 2020). Findings
have largely emerged in more recent years, which is likely
the result of social media becoming increasingly important
in the context of shopping (Frasquet et al. 2021a), and there-
fore becoming a subject of interest in research as well. Nota-
bly, articles take on highly different approaches in channel
inclusion. Some studies only include social media within the
search and/or aftersales stages (e.g., Frasquet et al. 2021a;
Blömker and Albrecht 2024), while others include it within
all phases of the buying process (e.g., Sands et al. 2016), or
only refer to it within a bundle of digital touchpoints (e.g.,
van der Veen and van Ossenbruggen 2015; Ieva and Ziliani
2018). Hence, segments should be interpreted with consid -
eration to their specific research context.
Indifferent shoppers
The “Indifferent/Uninvolved shopper” segment displays an
exception within the omnichannel context and is therefore
highlighted individually. Their key differentiating feature
is not a unique channel usage pattern, but rather their pre -
sumptive low involvement, low shopping enjoyment, and
indifferent attitude towards the shopping process in general
(e.g., Konuş et al. 2008; Frasquet et al. 2015; Ieva and Zil-
iani 2017). They generally do not differ from the average
consumer regarding channel usage and might use various
channels throughout their journey without any clear prefer-
ence (e.g., Nakano and Kondo 2018; Park and Kim 2018;
Nagy et al. 2024). Despite or precisely because they show
no clear channel preferences, they already exhibit multi -
channel or omnichannel behaviour at least to some degree
and are therefore relevant for this review.
Characterisation of customer segments

ments not only differ in their channel and touchpoint usage
behaviour, but also in variables such as demographics, psy-
chographics, and other behavioural factors (e.g., Schnei -

are highly relevant for marketing analytics, especially for
predictive segment allocation and segment-specific target -
ing strategies. Therefore, the majority of studies take vari -
ous variables into account to test for statistical differences
between segments (e.g., Valentini et al. 2020; Tueanrat et
al. 2021; Kondo and Okubo 2022). With regard to RQ3,

et al. 2004; Valentini et al. 2020), books (n=1; Shim et al.
2004), and consumer electronics (n=1 significant vs. n=1
partially significant; Steinmann et al. 2016; Valentini et al.
2020). It should be noted that the operationalisation of chan-
nel experience diverges between studies. While a majority
measured offline and online experience as latent constructs
(e.g., Kleinlercher et al. 2020; Maggioni et al. 2020), others
captured digital experiences via indicators such as “aver -
age internet use”, “years using computers”, or “time spent
on mobile devices” (e.g., Mcgoldrick and Collins 2007;
Steinmann et al. 2016; Valentini et al. 2020). Despite these
methodological differences, the overall findings clearly
emphasise the factor’s relevance for channel-based segmen-
tation, although product-specific insights should be inter -
preted as initial tendencies given the limited data points.
At last, findings regarding purchase frequency, which was
also examined by 14.5% of articles, appears to be a modest
factor when investing consumer characteristics. Turning to
the utilitarian category, specifically the grocery setting, a
slight tendency towards significant relevance can be identi-
fied (n=2 vs. 1; e.g., Frasquet et al. 2021b; Valentini et al.
2020). The hedonic category, on the other hand, was repre -
sented by a single study, which resulted in exclusively sig -
nificant group-wise comparisons for consumer electronics,
and partially significant findings for apparel (Valentini et al.
2020). Research articles taking on a mixed-product perspec-
tive or omitting a specific product indication, predominantly
reported (partially) significant outcomes (n=4 vs. 1; n=1 vs.
0; Hernant and Rosengren 2017; Herhausen et al., 2019;
Kleinlercher et al. 2020), reinforcing the inconsistent effect
of purchase frequency within channel-based segmentation,
yet indicating a slight degree of importance.
Discussion and managerial implications
The purpose of this study was threefold: First, to examine
whether common shopper segments with diverse chan -
nel behaviour exist throughout the literature included in
this review. Second, to identify if product categories play
an important role in the existence and characterisation of
specific segments. And third, to inspect if other characteris-
tics besides channel behaviour can significantly distinguish
clusters in an omnichannel context.
This review was able to identify ten shopper subgroups
exhibiting heterogeneous channel usage behaviour, which
were merged into four main consumer categories of “Tra -
ditionalists”, “Research Shoppers”, “Multi-/Omnichannel
Shoppers”, and “Indifferent Shoppers”. While the “Tra -
ditionalists” include segments that mainly exhibit single-
channel behaviour, the other categories comprise segments
exhibiting varying degrees of multi- or omnichannel behav-
iour. The research findings can thus provide a clear answer
towards RQ1 by confirming that shoppers, in fact, use differ-
ent channels along their customer journey and that common
customer segments with similar channel behaviours exist. A
detailed overview of the main clusters and their subgroups,
as well as their primary channel usage patterns across the
customer journey is synthesised in Table 6.
As certain consumer groups appear more frequently
within the hedonic or utilitarian product categories, the
analysis findings reinforce that overall product category
alters segment occurrence across primary studies, thus
answering RQ2 in the affirmative. “Mainly store-focused
shoppers”, for instance, were found in 70.0% (n=14 out of
20) of studies investigating hedonic products, compared to
50.0% (n=10 out of 20) of studies in the utilitarian setting.
In contrast, “Occasional MC/OC shoppers” were identified
in 55.0% (n=11 out of 20) of articles within a utilitarian con-
text as opposed to 45.0% (n=9 out of 20) of the hedonic
category. Turning to the specific product-level, this SLR
indicates certain tendencies regarding segment prevalence
across individual goods, reflecting their context-dependent
relevance. For example, 100.0% of studies investigating

With regard to RQ3, different demographic, psycho -

which might help to further characterise customer segments
in an omnichannel context. While demographic findings
are mostly volatile, psychographic results display greater
impact in segment characterisation. Lastly, if included,

by researchers and practitioners.
While customer segmentation represents a core pillar
of marketing analytics, identifying which indicators might
yield distinct, substantial, and actionable segments within
a field of ample customer data remains a challenge (France
and Ghose 2019). Subsequently, this SLR’s findings pres -
ent clear evidence of channel usage being a key differ -
entiator in today’s omnichannel environment, therefore
offering marketing analytic teams a shortcut by providing
a pre-validated, viable customer segmentation base. Addi -
tionally, the results highlight supplementary demographic,
psychographic, and behavioural indicators, which enrich
channel-based segmentation and enable marketers to con -
struct multidimensional customer profiles.
The paper’s findings supply valuable initial insights into
the occurrence, behaviours, and characteristics of ten cus -
tomer segments. Marketers can leverage this knowledge to
guide their search for and analyses of specific customer pat-
terns within their own data systems and appropriately feed
advanced analytical tools. However, Yankelovich and Meer
(2006) emphasise the need to move beyond static, singular
segment assignment and employ dynamic models, which
account for changing consumer behaviours and needs, as
well as evolving market conditions. Hence, predictive ana -
lytics and dynamic segmentation based on a firm’s own
accumulated data are necessary on a continuous basis
(France and Ghose 2019). To operationalise these findings,
managers need to recognise which channels are particu -
larly relevant to their specific customer base (Tueanrat et
al. 2021) and validate which of the ten recurring shopper
segments from this review represent substantial clusters.
Importantly, they must also evaluate whether customer
segments of high strategic value exist that were of minor
prominence within the reviewed literature or did not appear
at all. The results suggest that segment-specific differences
often emerge through the interplay of multiple factors and
may be highly contingent on a specific organisational or
market context. Leveraging multiple demographic, psy -
chographic, and behavioural indictors may further enhance
a firm’s understanding of their customer base and enable
more tailored targeting strategies. To gain deeper under -
standing of each segment, marketing analysts should further
track advanced analytical metrics, such as customer lifetime
value, churn risk, click-through rates, channel migration,
promotion response, or (in-store/digital) navigation paths
(e.g., Anderl et al. 2016; France and Ghose 2019), with
specific metrics recommended for each group displayed in

on data tracking, which serves as a key enabler of market -

tracking allows for the systematic collection, monitoring,
analysis, and optimisation of customer data across channels

Based on these synthesised findings, marketers should
deploy segment-specific communication tactics tailored
along three analytical dimensions: first, channel and touch -

behavioural), and third, key analytical metrics. Initial direc-

can further leverage analytical tools such as Google Ana -

them into customised marketing communication campaigns
while enhancing operational efficiency (Cham et al. 2022;
Ramos et al. 2025). These analytical synergies ultimately
guide marketers in making highly informed and data-driven
marketing decisions, which are crucial for firms to achieve
a sustainable competitive advantage and succeed in the
long run within today’s dynamic omnichannel environment
(Cham et al. 2022). The increasing complexity of market -
ing communications, which stems from employing such
targeted strategies in omnichannel environments, further
deepens the need for stronger marketing performance ana -
lytics (Buhalis and V olcheck, 2021).
Building on this imperative, assessing the individual con-
tribution of each digital and offline channel towards effec -
tive marketing, their spill-over effects, and their overall
interplay must be made a top priority in marketing analyt -
ics (Kannan et al. 2016), particularly within today’s evolv -
ing omnichannel environments. Accordingly, this paper’s
findings provide initial insights into channel relations and
their specific attribution for specific customer segments. As
Anderl et al. (2016) state, channel attribution must account
for the whole customer journey, moving beyond simple
heuristic models, like first- or last-click attribution in order
to allocate channel investments effectively. This necessity
is clearly reinforced by the paper’s findings, as customers
in all except the three “Traditionalist” clusters utilise more
than one channel across their journey stages on a regular
basis.
Subsequently, integrating the various channels and
touchpoints into a holistic omnichannel setting should be
prioritised to provide smooth shopping experiences and
leverage channel synergies and spill-over effects (Mosquera
et al. 2017; Kaskas and Kitchen 2024). Especially within
omnichannel environments, where shoppers seek unique
experiences, the physical store must reassert itself as far
more than just a transactional place (Errajaa and Bilgihan
2025). Components for achieving this goal may comprise
synergies between sensory, aesthetic, and emotional aspects
of store atmosphere, including the installation of lounge
areas, implementation of smart technologies, and the pro -
motion of in-store mobile usage to deliver functional ben -
efits such as accessing store inventory or product reviews
(Banerjee et al. 2023; Errajaa and Bilgihan 2025; Lee et
al. 2026). In digital environments, the creation of authen -
tic customer experiences catering to shopper enjoyment,
proximity, and physicality is crucial to bridge the gap
between offline and online channels (Kaskas and Kitchen
2024). Offering (free) deliveries to physical stores, incor -
porating location-based availability checks (e.g., Mosquera
et al. 2017; Herhausen et al. 2019), or the integration of
AI-powered chatbots and algorithmic cross-selling might
enhance the digital environment. Companies should further
embrace a phygital environment that merges the digital and
physical worlds, which might be accomplished by imple -
menting augmented and virtual reality applications (Errajaa
and Bilgihan 2025). Examples comprise virtual mirrors, the
enrichment of physical objects with digital data in stores or
catalogues, the integration of virtual try-ons or the place -
ment of three-dimensional images of a product in the real
world in digital channels (e.g., Hilken et al. 2018; Romano
et al. 2021).
Future research agenda
As displayed in Table 2 (see " Categorisation of research
within the classification framework" ), it becomes apparent
that research taking on an explicit omnichannel perspective
is still limited but has become more relevant in recent years.
Therefore, more research that specifically addresses an
omnichannel retailing perspective should be initiated. Turn-
ing to the customer journey perspective, research towards
the post-purchase phase, namely purchase and post-pur -
chase or all stages, has so far received little attention (e.g.,
Dalla Pozza et al. 2018; Kleinlercher et al. 2020).
Especially, studies considering utilitarian goods beyond
groceries, as well as research comparing different product
categories, are scarce, particularly in studies including the
post-purchase stage. The latter also applies across the frame-
work, as studies focus on one product category alone, take
on a mixed perspective, or might compare products with

services, such as mortgages or holiday travels (e.g., Konuş
et al. 2008; van der Veen and van Ossenbruggen 2015). The
majority of studies investigate similar product categories,
in particular clothing, consumer electronics, and groceries,
while neglecting individual products, as well as the B2B
sector in general (Schwarz et al. 2025). While a handful of
studies include individual products, they compare them to
broader product categories (e.g., Shim et al. 2004; Konuş
et al 2008). To date, only four articles explicitly focus on
the investigation of specific products (e.g., Homburg et al.
2019; Kondo and Okubo 2022). Consequently, the field of
study needs more research that explores alternative product
categories and explicit products (Dalla Pozza et al. 2018;
Kleinlercher et al. 2020). Research could, for instance,
focus on specific sectors such as healthcare (e.g., over-the-
counter products or corrective glasses), toys (Viejo-Fernán-
dez et al. 2020), luxury goods (Mosquera et al. 2019), or
vehicles (Wolf and Steul-Fischer 2023). Santos and Gon -
çalves ( 2024) identified customer segments with varying
journey complexity by investigating specific high- vs. low-
involvement products. However, their qualitative analysis
was limited to two products across different categories and
33 respondents. Consequently, to validate and extend these
findings, future research should examine varying involve -
ment levels within a single product category using a larger
sample. Lastly, building on Koizumi and Kato’s (2026) find-
ing that planned and unplanned purchases differ in search
and buying behaviour, future research should explore chan-
nel-based segmentation across these distinct purchasing
situations.
While 12 studies in this review acknowledge social
media as a touchpoint, most examine it only in specific cus-
tomer journey stages or omit the distinction between phases
entirely (e.g., Ieva and Ziliani 2018; Frasquet et al. 2021a).
Only three articles investigate social media across all stages
of the customer journey (Sands et al. 2016; Mosquera et
al. 2019; Tueanrat et al. 2021). Consequently, research
that includes social media in all phases, and particularly
the purchase stage, is needed. Additionally, research lacks
channel-specific insights, highlighting the need to evaluate
platforms, such as Facebook, Instagram, or TikTok indi -
vidually (Hallikainen et al. 2019; Frasquet et al. 2021a).
Other studies point out the necessity for research including
firm- vs. customer-initiated digital channels, touchpoints
from multiple brands, and deeper insights into the mobile
channel, and inclusion of in-store technologies (e.g., Her -
hausen et al. 2019; Mosquera et al. 2019; Danaher et al.
2020). Furthermore, Romano et al. (2021) and Hilken et al.
(2018) specifically call for research on customer behaviour
and segmentation in the context of augmented and virtual
reality usage. Finally, Schultz and Kaiser ( 2025) note the
growing role of chatbots and voice-based digital assistants,
like Amazon Alexa, in customer journeys. Including these
novel channels, along with broader AI usage, offers a prom-
ising direction in channel-based segmentation research.
Moreover, the findings of this SLR reveal that evidence
regarding characterising features seems inconsistent and
sometimes even contradictory. The results particularly
highlight the need for further research on the influence of
psychographic and behavioural variables. In accordance
with previous studies as well as the findings of this SLR,
a more detailed characterisation of shopper segments in
an omnichannel context is required to consolidate context-
specific findings (e.g., Frasquet et al. 2021b; Li et al. 2021;
Tueanrat et al. 2021).
Lastly, as already highlighted by Mishra et al. (2021) and
Wolf and Steul-Fischer 2023, omnichannel research still
lacks theory- and model-driven approaches. This limita -
tion is also evident within the body of literature reviewed
in this SLR, as only approximately one-third of analysed
studies explicitly draw on established theoretical or model-
based approaches when establishing their research frame -
work (e.g., Cervellon et al. 2015; Brand et al. 2020). Future
research could expand insights on previously used theories
or integrate additional models into channel-choice-based
customer segmentation research. For instance, extending the
approach-avoidance theory to broader omnichannel envi -
ronments offers a promising avenue. Although Wang et al.
(2014) and Harris et al. ( 2017) confirm its general applica -
bility by contrasting online vs. offline (dis)advantages, their
aggregated analyses lack channel-specific insights. Further-
more, the cognitive-affective-conative model introduced by
Lavidge and Steiner ( 1961) could help explain omnichan -
nel purchasing behaviour across the customer journey. Its
general relevance is supported by Mishra et al. (2021), who
successfully applied the dimensions to structure consumer
decision-making literature in omnichannel environments.
Finally, Shi et al.’s ( 2020) omnichannel experience frame -
work, including consistency, connectivity, integration, per -
sonalisation, and flexibility, provides a valuable foundation
for future channel-based segmentation research. Particu -
larly, the latent construct of flexibility, reflecting shoppers’
channel-switching behaviour throughout the shopping pro -
cess, could be better operationalised via observed channel
choice patterns. Consequently, customer segments with
distinct channel usage behaviours may also differ in the
remaining omnichannel experience dimensions.

Limitations
Although the present study provides a novel overview of
the literature on and nature of common customer segments
based on their channel usage along the customer journey
and their characterising features, the study is subject to some
limitations. First, due to the substantial heterogeneous com-
positions of the studies, this SLR could not comprehensively
account for all study-specific qualities and methodological
differences. These comprise, among others, variations in
the consideration of channels and touchpoints, examined
product categories, as well as the inclusion of the entirety
of demographic, psychographic, and behavioural vari -
ables. Subsequently, both the comparability of the studies
and the interpretation of the findings inherently reach cer -
tain boundaries. The observed patterns should therefore be
interpreted with caution as they may reflect the composition
of the underlying literature base rather than solely under -
lying customer behaviour and characteristics. This further
emphasises the need for future research, as outlined in the
"Discussion and managerial implications " section, to vali -
date and extend the SLR’s findings. Additionally, all studies
report significant results regarding the existence of distinct
customer segments, which might indicate a potential pub -
lication bias, meaning that non-significant results are less
likely to be published (Duval and Tweedie 2000). Lastly, a
potential limitation lies in the inherently qualitative nature
of SLRs compared to quantitative meta-analyses (Siddaway
et al. 2019). Although substantial methodological heteroge-
neity precluded a quantitative meta-analysis, this SLR con -
siderably exceeds a mere compilation of existing literature
by deploying a multi-layered analytical approach extending
from a novel categorisation framework to segment-specific
targeting strategies for each of the ten identified shopper
segments.
Conclusion
By addressing the fragmented and growing literature on
channel-based segmentation within omnichannel environ -
ments, this SLR successfully targets a distinct research gap.
Moving beyond mere enumeration, it employs a multi-lay -
ered analytical approach to present the literature and derive
concrete findings that advance customer journey literature in
omnichannel environments, specifically channel-based seg-
mentation, and provide marketing analysts with actionable
insights. Starting with an initial compilation and categorisa-
tion of the literature body according to the key dimensions of
customer journey stages, channel ecosystems, and product
categories, a structured overview of the primary studies was
provided. By carefully analysing channel behaviours of
each of the identified segments included in the primary stud-
ies, ten recurring shopper clusters were successfully identi -
fied across the literature, which merge into four main groups
called “Traditionalists”, “Research Shoppers”, “Multi-/
Omnichannel Shoppers”, and “Indifferent Shoppers”.
These findings directly answer RQ1, while also providing
marketing analytics teams with a pre-validated, viable seg -
mentation base in addition to the proof of the existence of
potentially important segments. By confirming that product
categories influence the presence and characteristics of the
ten identified segments, this SLR provides a clear answer
to RQ2. Furthermore, it outlines valuable future research
avenues for researchers, as to which product categories and
specific products should be investigated next, and showing
practitioners the importance of specific segments across dif-
ferent contexts. Lastly, further demographic, psychographic,
and behavioural indicators were evaluated regarding their
statistical consistency across different product categories
and for segment characterisation. Although findings with
regard to RQ3 provide initial insights and serve as a base
for segment-specific targeting strategies, the analyses also
revealed their context-specific nature, thereby proving the
importance of validation in research and practice. Mar -
keting analytics managers can still leverage these results
to select appropriate covariates, thereby refining database
segmentation models, and thus designing segment-specific
targeting strategies. In addition, this SLR derives actionable
metrics alongside conceptual targeting directives for each of
the ten segments, which marketing managers and especially
analytical teams can leverage and expand after confirming
the existence of segments within their own customer base.
Consequently, this SLR’s findings help in the development
of more effective, customer-centric omnichannel strategies.
Finally, research gaps were identified, which serve as impor-
tant future research avenues. Researchers should therefore
focus on the implementation of theory- and model-driven
methodologies, address additional product categories, such
as healthcare products, or specific comparisons of low-
and high-involvement products within the same category,
advance B2B research, and finally provide channel-specific
insights into diverse social media channels across the cus -
tomer journey stages.
