Abstract
As service robots become increasingly embedded in hospitality settings, especially restaurants, understanding how customers
experience and respond to these technologies is essential for advancing theory and informing design. This study examines
how functional, emotional and relational dimensions jointly shape customer satisfaction and revisit intentions in the con-
text of non-humanoid, task-oriented robots. Addressing gaps in existing adoption-focused models, the research integrates
the stimulus–organism–response (S–O-R) framework with the USUS model to investigate how constructs such as service
efficiency, enjoyment, friendliness, trust and rapport influence post-adoption engagement. Using data from 308 real-world
restaurant customers and analysed through partial least squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM), the study explores
both mediation and moderation pathways, including the roles of rapport and individual novelty-seeking tendencies. This work
contributes to the human–robot interaction (HRI) and service marketing literature by shifting focus from humanoid social
mimicry to functional robotic design’s affective and relational potential. The findings explain how emotional connection,
operational performance and psychological traits shape sustained customer engagement with robotic services. Implications
are offered for designing customer-centric, socially acceptable and operationally effective robot experiences in high-contact
service environments.

Introduction
The integration of service robots in the hospitality industry
is reshaping customer experiences, particularly in restaurant
environments. These robots provide efficiency, consistency
and scalability, making them essential for operational opti-
misation (Ivanov et al., 2020; Jörling et al., 2019). How -
ever, their increasing presence introduces critical trade-offs
between functional efficiency and the human-centric service
qualities that define hospitality (Kim et al., 2021; Mende
et al., 2019). While robotic service adoption enhances oper-
ational performance, long-term customer satisfaction and
loyalty require a broader understanding of how functional,
emotional and relational factors collectively influence cus-
tomer experiences (Ali et al., 2024; Balaji et al., 2025). The
challenge lies in determining how these dimensions inter -
act, and shape sustained engagement with robotic services
(Tuomi et al., 2021).
Gaps in existing research
Much of the literature on technology adoption has been
shaped by utilitarian frameworks such as the Technology
Acceptance Model (TAM) (Davis, 1989) and the Unified
Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT)

(Venkatesh et al., 2003), which emphasise perceived useful-
ness and ease of use as primary drivers of adoption. While
these models offer valuable insights into initial acceptance,
they provide limited explanation for sustained engagement,
particularly in emotionally and socially rich service contexts
such as hospitality (Cronin et al., 2000; Qiu et al., 2020).
Existing studies have largely focused on the functional ben-
efits of service robots, highlighting usability, reliability and
performance efficacy (Song & Kim, 2022), yet empirical
investigations into the emotional and relational dimensions
of human–robot interaction (HRI) remain scarce (Hu, 2021).
Furthermore, much of this research has been conducted in
artificial or experimental settings, limiting its relevance to
real-world customer experiences and long-term behavioural
responses to robotic services.
Another limitation in current research is the predominant
focus on anthropomorphic or humanoid robots when examin-
ing emotional and relational constructs such as friendliness,
trust and rapport. While humanoid designs may heighten
perceived warmth or sociability, they are not representative
of most service robots currently deployed in commercial
environments, particularly in sectors like food delivery or
retail (Huang & Liu, 2022). Non-humanoid robots, which
are primarily task oriented and designed for efficiency rather
than human resemblance, remain understudied in terms of
how they elicit emotional and relational responses from cus-
tomers. Understanding how such robots influence satisfaction
and revisit intentions is important as businesses increasingly
adopt non-humanoid solutions that do not rely on humanlike
form to deliver service value (Cha et al., 2018).
In addition, sustaining customer engagement beyond
initial novelty remains a persistent challenge. Robotic
services often attract early attention but may struggle to
maintain interest over time, especially among high novelty
seeking individuals who rapidly adapt to new stimuli (El-
Said & Al Hajri, 2022; Kim et al., 2022a, 2022b; Sung
et al., 2016). The role of rapport in robotic service interac-
tions further complicates this landscape. While rapport is
well established as a driver of satisfaction in human deliv -
ered services (Gremler & Gwinner, 2000), its function in
robotic contexts appears to be more ambiguous, potentially
amplifying friendliness while moderating the impact of
trust (Nomura & Kanda, 2016; Song et al., 2022a, 2022b).
These gaps point to the need for a more comprehensive
framework that moves beyond utilitarian predictors to con-
sider how functional, emotional and relational stimuli shape
customer experiences with service robots, particularly in real
world settings involving non-humanoid agents. Addressing
these gaps is important for advancing theoretical understand-
ing of sustained customer engagement and informing the
design and deployment of service robots capable of fostering
long term loyalty in automated service environments.
Research objectives and contributions
To address the limitations of existing models that focus pre-
dominantly on initial adoption, this study draws on the stimu-
lus–organism–response (S–O-R) framework (Mehrabian &
Russell, 1974) in conjunction with the USUS model (Weiss
et al., 2009) to propose a more comprehensive approach
to understanding customer experience in HRI settings. By
examining how functional (e.g. usability, efficiency), emo-
tional (e.g. friendliness, novelty) and relational (e.g. trust,
rapport) dimensions influence key post-adoption outcomes
such as satisfaction and revisit intention, the study offers a
more complete perspective on customer engagement with
service robots. In doing so, it also considers the role of psy-
chological moderators, specifically rapport and individual
novelty-seeking tendencies, that may shape or condition
these effects over time. These contributions aim to extend
current theorisation in both HRI and service marketing by (i)
integrating functional, emotional, and relational stimuli into
a unified explanatory model; (ii) incorporating psychological
boundary conditions that may explain differential customer
responses; and (iii) advancing understanding of the mecha -
nisms through which customer retention evolves in response
to robot-delivered services.
Introduction to HRI
Classifying service robots
Service robots can be categorised based on their functional
roles and levels of interactivity, ranging from task-oriented
functional robots to socially engaging relational robots.
Functional robots are primarily designed for efficiency and
precision, executing specific tasks with minimal human
interaction (Karabegović et al., 2021; Chang & Kim, 2022).
In contrast, transactional robots engage in simple, rule-
based interactions, such as automated check-in services or
self-service kiosks, while relational robots are developed to
facilitate social engagement through both verbal and non-
verbal communication (Park & Jung, 2024). These distinc-
tions show the varying degrees of interactivity across differ-
ent robot types. Table 1 provides a detailed classification of
service robots, outlining their key characteristics, examples,
and interactivity levels to illustrate these variations.

cognitive robots, their effectiveness is evaluated by usability,
efficiency and reliability, rather than emotional mimicry or
social bonding (Chang & Kim, 2022; Park & Jung, 2024).
Despite their low interactivity level, non-humanoid robots
are not devoid of experiential value. Increasing evidence
shows that carefully designed interface elements, such
as polite voice prompts, responsive gestures or cheerful
tones, can positively influence customer perceptions (Shin,
2022). These design features help to humanise task-focused
robots, making interactions feel smoother and more engag-
ing. Functional robots that exhibit such characteristics can
evoke customer satisfaction not only through task per -
formance but also through subtle emotional engagement
(Huang & Liu, 2022; Tussyadiah et al., 2020).
Two constructs are especially salient in this context:
friendliness and rapport. Functional robots can express
friendliness through simple, nonverbal features such as wel-
coming tones, polite phrasing and intuitive responsiveness
(Huang et al., 2021). Even without anthropomorphic form,
such cues can shape positive attitudes, enhance likability and
reinforce brand perceptions (Oğan, 2024; Rana et al., 2025).
Friendliness thus operates as a low-effort affective cue that
improves perceived service quality and contributes to higher
customer satisfaction (Tussyadiah et al., 2020; Shin, 2022).
Rapport, originally conceptualised in human service encoun-
ters as mutual attentiveness and connection (Gremler & Gwin-
ner, 2000), has been adapted to HRI to capture how custom-
ers perceive the warmth and responsiveness of service robots.
While anthropomorphic robots build rapport through emotional
displays and social mimicry, functional robots achieve rapport
differently, i.e., primarily through task reliability, efficiency and
interactional smoothness (Hlee et al., 2022). For example, a
delivery robot that communicates clearly, operates seamlessly
and maintains consistency in service delivery can foster a sense
of rapport even without emotional intelligence or deep social
engagement. This task-based rapport plays a role in elevating
satisfaction and encouraging customers to perceive the service
encounter as competent and pleasant.
Importantly, although both friendliness and rapport are
traditionally associated with socially intelligent robots, their
application in functional and hybrid-functional robots is gain-
ing recognition. These constructs offer a way to bridge the
gap between cold efficiency and engaging service, particu-
larly in contexts where full anthropomorphism is not viable

Theoretical foundations: S–O‑R and USUS
frameworks
The S–O-R framework (Mehrabian & Russell, 1974) pro-
vides a foundational model for explaining how external
stimuli trigger internal cognitive or emotional states, which
in turn drive behavioural responses. Originally developed in
environmental psychology, S–O-R has been widely applied
in consumer behaviour, service management and technol-
ogy adoption research to explain customer decision-making
processes (Cronin et al., 2000; Zhu et al., 2020). This study
extends the S–O-R framework in the context of HRI by
focusing on three key dimensions of service robot experi-
ence—functional, emotional and relational—as the salient
stimuli influencing customer satisfaction and behavioural
responses. These dimensions represent distinct aspects of
robotic service encounters and are conceptualised as follows:
• Functional dimension: Emphasises the robot’s usability,
efficiency and performance reliability, which together
influence the customer’s perception of service effective-
ness (Chopdar & Balakrishnan, 2020 ). This includes
attributes like consistent responsiveness and error-free
operation that ensure the robot performs its tasks safely
and reliably.
• Emotional dimension: Covers the affective responses
elicited by the robot, such as the novelty of the interac-
tion, the robot’s friendliness and the enjoyment the cus-
tomer feels during the encounter. These emotional fac-
tors shape the customer’s overall satisfaction and attitude
toward the service robot (Tussyadiah et al., 2020).
• Relational dimension: Encompasses the sense of trust
and rapport that can develop between the customer and
the service robot. This relational connection influences
how reliable the customer perceives the robot to be and
the customer’s willingness to engage with it in a social
or service context (Gremler & Gwinner, 2000; Song &
Kim, 2022).
However, while the S–O-R framework describes the pro-
cess by which stimuli lead to internal states and responses,
it does not explicitly classify the nature of those stimuli or
how users interpret their interactions with robots. To address
this gap, we integrate the USUS framework (Rana et al.,
2025; Weiss et al., 2009) into our theoretical approach.
USUS (an acronym for Usability, Social Acceptance, User
Experience, and Societal Impact) offers a rounded, user-
centric lens for evaluating human–technology interactions
(Rana et al., 2025). Originally introduced in the HRI lit-
erature (Rana et al., 2025; Weiss et al., 2011), the USUS
framework delineates four complementary dimensions for
assessing user experience and system acceptance. In our
integrated S–O-R–USUS framework, each of the first three
USUS dimensions aligns with one of the core aspects of
service robot experience identified above:
• Usability corresponds to the functional dimension of the
robotic service. It captures the ease of use, efficiency, and
safety of the robot’s performance. Essentially, how well
the robot’s functional attributes meet customers’ needs
(e.g. smooth navigation and error-free task execution).
• Social Acceptance maps onto the relational dimension,
reflecting the customer’s willingness to accept the robot
as a social interaction partner. This includes interpersonal
factors such as the trustworthiness of the robot and the
level of rapport or comfort the customer feels when inter-
acting with it.
• User Experience aligns with the emotional dimension,
encompassing the affective quality of the interaction.
It reflects how engaging, enjoyable or novel the robot
encounter is for the customer, including perceptions of
the robot’s friendliness and the excitement of using a new
technology.
(The USUS framework ’s fourth component , Societal
Impact, involves broader social and ethical implications of
robotics. While important, this aspect lies beyond the scope
of our immediate focus on individual customer experiences.)

precise evaluation of how customers interpret and evaluate

ing S–O-R and USUS in this way yields a comprehensive
theoretical foundation for understanding customer experi-
ences with service robots. The S–O-R framework explains
the causal sequence by which robot attributes (stimuli) evoke
internal evaluations and drive outcomes, while the USUS
framework defines the content of those evaluations and how
customers judge the robot on multiple criteria (Weiss et al.,
2009; Zhu et al., 2020).
This dual-framework approach also extends prior tech-
nology-adoption research, which predominantly empha -

Hypotheses development
Building on the S–O-R framework, this study identifies
functional, emotional and relational dimensions as key
stimuli shaping customer responses to robotic service inter-
actions (Zhu et al., 2020; Rajput & Gahfoor, 2020). Prior
research in human-centric hospitality settings has examined
these dimensions separately (Bartneck et al., 2009 ; Wirtz
et al., 2018), but their integrated influence in robotic set-
tings remains underexplored. Functional attributes, such
as perceived safety, service efficiency and performance
efficacy, shape usability perceptions and build trust in the
service (i.e. confidence that the service will be delivered
reliably) (Chopdar & Balakrishnan, 2020 ; Ivanov et al.,
2020). Emotional attributes (e.g. novelty, enjoyment, friend-
liness) enrich the user experience, although their impact may
diminish over time as customers acclimate to the novelty
(Tung & Au, 2018; Ghazali et al., 2020; El-Said & Al Hajri,
2022). Relational attributes, especially trust, facilitate social
acceptance by giving customers a sense of security during
human–robot interactions (Kim et al., 2022a, 2022b; Tus-
syadiah et al., 2020). In addition, two psychological factors,
customer–robot rapport and customers’ novelty-seeking
tendency, are considered as moderators that can alter the
strength of these relationships over time (Nomura & Kanda,
2016). Based on these theoretical foundations, a series of
hypotheses is developed to predict how the identified stimuli
influence customer satisfaction, attitude, and behavioural
intentions (specifically, revisit intention).
The mediating effects of attitude
Within the S–O-R model, a service robot’s functional fea-
tures are processed cognitively by the customer and can
shape the customer’s attitude toward the service (Park
& Pobil, 2013). Perceived safety is defined as the customer’s
impression of the risk of interacting with a robot and their
degree of comfort with the robot Bartneck et al., (2009).
High perceived safety includes both physical safety and
reduced fear of robotic errors (e.g. not worrying that the
robot might cause harm) (Wirtz et al., 2018). This sense of
safety is crucial for acceptance of robotic services: custom-
ers are more willing to interact with robots when they feel
safe and comfortable doing so (Tung & Law., 2017). Empiri-
cal studies find that when customers believe a robot is safe,
they tend to form a more positive attitude toward using it
(Hlee et al., 2022). In other words, reducing concerns about
mistakes or harm fosters a favourable view of the robot. For-
mally, it is hypothesised that:
H1a. Attitude positively mediates the relationship
between perceived safety and customer’s intention to
revisit a restaurant with robotic service.
Service efficiency, that is the ability of the service
(robot + staff) to minimise wait times and maximise pro-
ductivity, is another functional stimulus expected to influ-
ence attitudes. Efficient use of robots can speed up ser -
vice delivery, as evidenced by reduced waiting times and
increased productivity in robot-assisted settings (Vatan
& Doğan., 2021 ). For example, faster service (such as
quicker reservations or payments) has been linked to
higher customer satisfaction in online service contexts
(Gomes Furtado et al., 2019). In a restaurant, customers
may be impressed by robots that deliver orders promptly,
enhancing their attitude toward the service (El-Said &
Al Hajri, 2022). If robots fail to provide efficiency gains,
however, their added value is questionable (Byrd et al.,
2021). Thus, efficiency improvements via robotics should
translate into more positive customer attitudes, which then
encourage return visits. Hence:
H1b. Attitude positively mediates the relationship
between service efficiency and customer’s intention to
revisit a restaurant with robotic service.
The performance efficacy of service robots, that is how
well they execute their tasks, further contributes to func-
tional value. Performance efficacy includes reliability,
speed, and consistency in task performance (Tojib et al.,
2022). Unlike human workers, robots can operate continu-
ously and perform repetitive jobs without fatigue, poten-
tially outperforming humans on routine tasks (Ivanov et al.,
2020). Technological advances (e.g. in AI and speech rec-
ognition) have improved robots’ capabilities, both function-
ally and even emotionally (to a limited extent) (Go et al.,
2020; Murphy et al., 2019). When customers perceive that
a robot performs its job well and delivers a positive service
experience, they are likely to develop a favourable attitude

The mediating effects of satisfaction
In this study, customer satisfaction represents a key organ-
ismic response in the S–O-R framework, mediating the
influence of emotional and relational dimensions of HRI
on behavioural outcomes. While functional factors are
important for basic service quality, emotional engage-
ment often plays a decisive role in sustaining positive
perceptions in robotic restaurants. One primary emotional
driver in HRI is perceived novelty—the sense of newness
or uniqueness in interacting with robotic servers. Novel
experiences tend to spark initial excitement, making a ser -
vice encounter more memorable and engaging (Qiu et al.,
2020). However, novelty alone may not guarantee repeat
patronage; its effect is largely channelled through satis-
faction, which then shapes whether the customer intends
to return (Albaity & Melhem, 2017). In a robotic restau-
rant context, a customer who finds the experience novel
is likely to report higher satisfaction, and that heightened
satisfaction subsequently increases their intention to
revisit. Thus:
H2a. Satisfaction positively mediates the relationship
between perceived novelty and customer’s intention to
revisit a restaurant with robotic service.
Another crucial emotional stimulus is perceived enjoy -
ment—the pleasure and positive affect a customer derives
from the interaction with robot servers. Enjoyment cap-
tures the intrinsic fun or entertainment value of the experi-
ence (beyond just the novelty) and can stem from factors
such as an intuitive interaction, engaging robot personal-
ity or the high-tech appeal of the service (Davis, 1989;
Venkatesh, 2000). Past technology adoption research
suggests that enjoyable interactions improve overall user
experience, leading to greater engagement and long-term
satisfaction (Ghazali et al., 2020; Saari et al., 2022). In
robotic dining, if customers find the service enjoyable, for
instance, due to smooth interactions, amusing features or
ease of use, they are more likely to feel satisfied; that sat-
isfaction then translates into a stronger intention to return.
Accordingly, the following is proposed:
H2b. Satisfaction positively mediates the relationship
between perceived enjoyment and customer’s intention
to revisit a restaurant with robotic service.
Perceived friendliness of the robot is a relationally ori-
ented stimulus that can also enhance satisfaction. In non-
humanoid service robots (the kind often used for food
delivery in restaurants), ‘friendliness’ is conveyed through
limited but meaningful social cues, for example, polite
language in voice prompts, courteous acknowledgments
or simple interactive displays (smiling icons, lights, etc.)
(Hlee et al., 2022; Shin, 2022). These robots are primarily
task-oriented, lacking advanced emotional intelligence or
human-like conversation, so customers judge their friendli-
ness based on structured interactions such as polite behav -
iour, accuracy in service execution and smooth non-verbal
cues (Hwang et al., 2020). Even minimal social warmth in
a robotic encounter can foster ease and approachability,
making customers feel more comfortable and leading to
higher satisfaction (Tussyadiah et al., 2020). When task-
focused robots exhibit friendly gestures (clear communica-
tion, timely service, polite responses), customers tend to
perceive the interaction positively, which reinforces both
enjoyment and trust in the service (Groom et al., 2010;
Pugh, 2001). In robotic restaurant settings, these positive
emotional responses elevate overall satisfaction, thereby
strengthening the likelihood of customers returning.
Formally:
H2c. Satisfaction positively mediates the relationship
between perceived friendliness and customer’s intention
to revisit a restaurant with robotic service.
Beyond emotional factors, a key relational stimulus
is trust in the robot-delivered service. Trust here refers
to the customer’s confidence in the robot’s reliability,
competence and integrity in performing its service role
(Gaudiello et al., 2016). In technology-mediated inter -
actions, trust is critical for comfort because it reduces
uncertainty and anxiety about the service encounter
(Tussyadiah et al., 2020). When customers trust a robotic
server, believing it will function as intended and not
fail unexpectedly, they are more at ease and more sat-
isfied with the experience (Hwang et al., 2020). Prior
studies in hospitality robotics show that trust can miti-
gate the perceived risk of autonomous services, thereby

increasing satisfaction and the willingness to use such
services (and return) (Tussyadiah et al., 2020). Thus, it
is hypothesised that:
H3. Satisfaction positively mediates the relationship
between perceived trust and customer’s intention to
revisit a restaurant with robotic service.
In sum, H2a–H2c and H3 propose that emotional stimuli
(novelty, enjoyment, friendliness) and a relational stimulus
(trust) each influence revisit intentions via the intermediary
of customer satisfaction. A satisfying robot-service encoun-
ter is expected to translate these experiential and relational
benefits into a greater likelihood of returning.
Sequential mediation of satisfaction and attitude
While satisfaction and attitude are both important organismic
responses in the S–O-R model, they are conceptually dis-
tinct. Satisfaction is an affective, episode-specific response.
It is the customer’s contentment with a particular service
encounter. Attitude on the other hand is a more enduring
evaluation of the service (or provider) on a favourable–unfa-
vourable continuum (Oliver, 1993). It is theorised that these
two responses can form a sequence: a positive immediate
experience (satisfaction) can improve the customer’s overall
attitude toward the service, which in turn drives their behav-
ioural intention to return (Oliver, 1999). A satisfying robot
interaction updates the customer’s general evaluation of the
restaurant (making it more positive), and a favourable atti-
tude is well-known to predict repeat patronage. Thus, after
a satisfying encounter, the customer’s strengthened attitude
toward the robot-assisted restaurant becomes a key driver of
the decision to revisit (Kim & Qu, 2014).
Not all stimuli will trigger this satisfaction →  attitude
→ intention chain equally. It is expected that this sequential
mechanism operates primarily for emotional and relational
stimuli, rather than for purely functional ones. Emotional
and relational cues (novelty, enjoyment, friendliness, trust)
directly engage customers’ feelings and social percep-
tions. For example, a novel interactive feature can excite
and delight, and a friendly, trustworthy robot can foster
a personal bond. Such stimuli tend to heighten affective
satisfaction and subsequently shape a more favourable atti-
tude. In contrast, functional attributes like efficiency or
safety, while important, mostly fulfil baseline utilitarian
expectations. Meeting these expectations can yield satisfac-
tion, but often on a more cognitive level (e.g. ‘the service
was quick, so I’ll come back’) that might influence revisit
intention directly, rather than by fundamentally changing
one’s long-term attitude. In other words, functional perfor -
mance alone might prompt repeat business out of practical
convenience without necessarily enhancing the customer’s
overall affinity toward the service (Brunner-Sperdin et al.,
2012; Falk et al., 2010). Emotional and social stimuli,
however, provide the kind of positive affective experience
that can both satisfy the customer and bolster their atti-
tude toward the robot service. This reasoning aligns with
findings that purely cognitive evaluations (e.g. perceived
usefulness, innovativeness) can drive acceptance intentions
even without strong emotional responses, indicating that
when emotional satisfaction is present it plays a special
role in also shaping attitudes (Chen & Girish, 2023; Sung
& Jeon, 2020).
A serial mediation process is proposed in which certain
stimuli influence revisit intention through a two-step path:
first through satisfaction, and then through attitude (satisfac-
tion feeding into attitude). This is consistent with the S–O-R
framework’s view of multiple internal states linking stimuli
to responses. Accordingly:
H4a-d. Satisfaction and attitude sequentially mediate the
positive relationships between HRI stimulus-perceived
novelty, enjoyment, friendliness, trust-and the customer’s
intention to revisit a robot-serviced restaurant.
In other words, for these emotional and relational factors,
the expectation is that they enhance satisfaction, which then
improves attitude, culminating in higher revisit intentions.
This set of hypotheses (H4a through H4 d) contrasts with the
functional factors (H1 series), where a direct attitude media-
tion was posited without involving satisfaction. The full set
of hypothesised relationships is depicted in the proposed
conceptual model (Fig. 1).
The moderating role of rapport
Customer–robot rapport refers to the sense of mutual under-
standing and connection that develops during their interaction.
In service settings, rapport is known to amplify the effects of
friendly behaviours on customer outcomes. Even for robots
with limited social abilities, incorporating humanlike attrib-
utes (e.g. polite greetings, responsive gestures, a name or
persona) can help establish rapport and enhance the hospital-
ity experience (Kao & Huang, 2023; Kim et al., 2022a; Qiu
et al., 2020). Prior studies have shown that robots exhibiting
anthropomorphic features (like expressive motions, personal-
ised responses or familiarity-building techniques) can increase
perceived intimacy and service quality, thereby strengthening
customers’ emotional engagement (Oğan, 2024; Kim et al.,
2022a, 2022b; Chiang & Trimi, 2020).
In essence, rapport functions as an ‘interactional lubri-
cant’ that makes friendly robot behaviours feel more genuine
and meaningful. Without rapport, a robot’s polite or friendly

actions might seem mechanical and have limited emotional
impact (Artstein et al., 2017). With strong rapport, those
same actions are more likely to elicit positive emotions, as
the customer perceives a warmer, more personal connection.
Indeed, rapport can enable a form of emotional contagion in
HRI: customers ‘catch’ the positive mood from the robot’s
friendly demeanour (Stock & Merkle, 2018), further boost-
ing their satisfaction (Wilson et al., 2017; Tian et al., 2021).
Rapport plays a complex role in shaping satisfaction and
trust in HRI. Generally, higher rapport enhances satisfac-
tion by making interactions more enjoyable and personable
(Gremler & Gwinner, 2000; Nomura & Kanda, 2016). How-
ever, some evidence suggests that very high rapport might
not always increase trust in robotic services. This is because
customers ultimately expect service robots to be efficient
and reliable; if a robot focuses too much on social bond-
ing at the expense of task performance, highly rapport-rich
interactions could paradoxically undermine perceptions of
reliability (Tussyadiah et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2021).
Nonetheless, within moderate levels, rapport clearly
strengthens the emotional bond, turning polite robotic
behaviours into deeper engagement. Instruments like
the Rapport Expectation with a Robot Scale (Nomura &
Kanda, 2013, 2016) highlight how essential rapport is for
maximising the impact of a robot’s friendly behaviour on
customer satisfaction. In this study, rapport is examined as a
moderator of the effect of friendliness and trust on customer
satisfaction. It is a relational perception that conditions other
effects. That is, when rapport is high, the positive influence
of a robot’s friendliness on satisfaction is expected to grow
even stronger, as the customer feels a genuine social con-
nection. Formally:
H5a. The positive relationship between perceived friend-
liness and customer satisfaction will grow stronger as
customer-rapport increases.
Conversely, rapport may also interact with trust. Trust
provides a functional assurance in the robot’s reliabil -
ity (Roy et al., 2024), whereas rapport adds a relational
layer on top of that trust. When both trust and rapport
are present, customers perceive the robot to be not only
competent but also socially engaging (Lee, 2024) (for
example, a customer might initially trust a robot to
deliver food on time, but if rapport is established, the
customer might feel as if the robot genuinely ‘cares’
about their experience). High rapport thus transforms
trust from a purely transactional belief (‘the robot will

could further enhance satisfaction (Lee et al., 2021;

is reinforced by positive social interaction (Kim et al.,

H5b. The positive relationship between perceived trust

The moderating role of novelty seeking
While perceived novelty generally has a positive effect
on customer satisfaction in robotic service encounters
(Ali et al., 2024; El-Said & Al Hajri, 2022; Sharma et al.,
2023), the strength of this effect can vary widely across
individuals (Assaker & Hallak, 2013). Customers differ
in their inherent novelty-seeking tendencies—a personal-
ity trait describing one’s desire for new, varied and unu-
sual experiences. A high novelty-seeker actively pursues
unique experiences and quickly grows accustomed to them,
whereas a low novelty-seeker is more easily delighted
by new experiences and retains that sense of novelty for
longer. Prior work shows that novelty-seeking can moder -
ate satisfaction derived from novel experiences: for some,
novelty greatly enhances enjoyment, while for others (par -
ticularly those who constantly seek novelty) the boost is
weaker or short-lived.
In a robotic restaurant, high novelty-seekers may ini-
tially be excited by the presence of robot servers, but their
enthusiasm can fade relatively quickly. Because these indi-
viduals habitually seek novelty, each exposure reduces the
‘freshness’ of the experience—a phenomenon consistent
with diminishing marginal utility, where repeated expo-
sure to what was once novel yields declining enjoyment
(Gocłowska et al., 2019; Alotaibi et al., 2024). Studies
have found that people with strong novelty-seeking traits
derive less satisfaction once an experience is no longer
new, which aligns with observations that novelty-seeking
dampens the long-term impact of novel features on satisfac-
tion (Hirschman, 1980; El-Said & Al Hajri, 2022). Conse-
quently, the positive relationship between perceived novelty
of a robot service and customer satisfaction is expected to
be weaker for high novelty-seekers, since they adapt quickly
and crave newer stimuli (El-Said & Al Hajri, 2022; Assaker
& Hallak, 2013).
By contrast, low novelty-seekers (who are not constantly
chasing new experiences) tend to find the robot service highly
novel and continue to appreciate its uniqueness for a longer
period. For these customers, dining with robots represents a
significant departure from routine; they do not habituate as
quickly, allowing the novelty to have a stronger and more last-
ing positive effect on satisfaction (Ali et al., 2024).
Empirical evidence shows that for consumers low in
novelty-seeking, the hedonic value of a novel service can
meaningfully enhance delight and loyalty, whereas high
novelty-seekers require ever-new innovations to maintain
the same level of satisfaction (Ali et al., 2024). However,
for high novelty-seekers, these values may lose their
impact as their baseline expectation for unique experi-
ences is higher, resulting in a weaker link between per -
ceived novelty and satisfaction (El-Said & Al Hajri, 2022).
Similar moderation effects have been observed in related
contexts—for instance, in quick-service restaurants, high
novelty-seeking customers showed lower engagement with
robotic services if the novelty did not continually meet
their elevated expectations (Alotaibi et al., 2024; Zemke
et al., 2020).
Given this, novelty-seeking is proposed to moderate the
effects of both novelty and enjoyment on satisfaction. First,
for the novelty–satisfaction link:
H6a. The positive relationship between perceived novelty
and customer satisfaction will be weaker when customer’s
novelty-seeking tendency is higher.
A parallel logic applies to perceived enjoyment. Enjoy -
ment from the robot service, as noted, can stem partly from
its novelty and fun factor (Sharma et al., 2023; Jain et al.,
2023). Customers high in novelty-seeking may enjoy the
robot interaction initially, but their enjoyment diminishes
faster as the experience becomes familiar and they seek new
forms of stimulation (El-Said & Al Hajri, 2022; Alotaibi
et al., 2024). In fact, high novelty-seekers tend to discount or
‘get used to’ enjoyable features over time unless something
new is introduced. On the other hand, low novelty-seekers,
who value consistency and familiarity, can continue deriv -
ing satisfaction from the enjoyable aspects of a robot server
without needing constant novelty (Ali et al., 2024). For
them, the enjoyment from the interaction remains impactful
over repeated visits, strengthening the enjoyment–satisfac-
tion relationship. Therefore, it is hypothesised that:
H6b. The positive relationship between perceived enjoy-
ment and customer satisfaction will be weaker when the
customer’s novelty-seeking tendency is higher.

Research method
Study context
A purposive sampling technique was employed to recruit
participants with relevant and recent experience in robotic
dining service environments. The target population included
individuals aged 18 and above who had interacted with ser-
vice robots in restaurants within the past year. This crite-
rion ensured the collection of accurate and meaningful data
reflecting real-world HRI experiences in structured dining
contexts.
Participants were recruited primarily through social
media platforms and online forums focused on dining and
technology. Recruitment posts targeted urban areas such as
Klang Valley, Johor Bahru and George Town, where robotic
dining services are more prevalent. To ensure eligibility, par-
ticipants completed an initial screening questionnaire, con-
firming their experience with robotic service interactions in
the past year. Responses failing to meet these criteria were
excluded from the final dataset.
The study included a diverse range of restaurant service
robots performing core service functions such as food deliv-
ery and order facilitation (see Appendix 2). However, the
study focused on non-humanoid robots, which are primarily
task-oriented but may incorporate minimal anthropomorphic
features such as screen-based facial expressions, interactive
lighting and polite communication cues (Hlee et al., 2022;
Shin, 2022). Unlike humanoid robots that rely on human-like
gestures or speech, these robots prioritise functionality and
efficiency while enhancing customer engagement through
subtle social cues. This hybrid classification allowed the
study to investigate how functional, emotional, and relational
dimensions interact in non-humanoid robotic dining services.
Through this purposive sampling process, a total of 308
validated responses were obtained, ensuring sufficient data
for strong analysis. Previous studies have relied on hypo-
thetical or simulated interactions to assess customer atti-
tudes toward service robots (Choi et al., 2019; Hu, 2021).
These studies do not capture the complexities and dynamic
of real-world interactions. By focusing on recent, first-hand
experiences, this study minimised recall bias and provided
insights grounded in actual customer-robot interactions. This
approach supports the study’s objective of understanding
the interplay between functional, emotional, and relational
dimensions of HRI in structured service settings.
Survey design and measurement
The questionnaire comprised seven sections including demo-
graphic, control variables and filter items. The first two
sections provided a brief introduction to the study and the
researcher’s background, followed by two filter questions.
Section A gathered preliminary information about respond-
ents’ visits to restaurants using service robots, including the
timing of their last visit, the frequency of visits, and their
companions during the visit.
Survey items were adapted from established literature
to suit the context of service robot restaurants. The func-
tional dimension was assessed through perceived safety,
service efficiency and performance efficacy. Perceived
safety was measured using four items adapted from Bar -
tneck et al. ( 2009 ), while service efficiency was evalu -
ated using four items from El-Said and Al Hajri (2022).
Performance efficacy was also measured with four items
adapted from Lu et al. (2019). The emotional dimension
of HRI included constructs such as perceived friendli-
ness, novelty and enjoyment. Perceived friendliness was
assessed using four items from Hlee et al. (2022), while
perceived novelty was measured with four items adapted
from Li et al. (2021). Perceived enjoyment was assessed
with five items based on Davis (1989) and Venkatesh
(2000). Relational dimension, which are critical for trust
and engagement in HRI, included constructs for perceived
trust and rapport. Perceived trust was measured with four
items adapted from Kim et al., (2022a, 2022b), while rap-
port was evaluated using three items from Gremler and
Gwinner (2000).
To capture customer outcomes, customer satisfaction
and attitude were measured alongside revisit intentions.
Customer satisfaction was assessed with four items from
Kim and Qu (2014), while customer attitude was measured
using three items from Dabholkar and Bagozzi (2002).
Revisit intentions were evaluated using four items adapted
from Song et al., ( 2022a, 2022b). In addition to these pri -
mary constructs, two moderating variables were included
in the study: novelty seeking and rapport. Novelty seeking
was assessed using four items adapted from Dabholkar and
Bagozzi (2002) to capture customers’ tendency to seek out
new and stimulating experiences. Rapport, as a moderat-
ing variable, reflected the quality of interaction between
customers and robots and was measured using three items
from Gremler and Gwinner (2000). All survey items were
measured on a seven-point Likert scale, and full details of
the survey items are provided in Appendix 1.
Sample and data collection
The required sample size was determined using G*Power
3.1, which recommended a minimum of 178 respondents
to achieve 0.15 effect size, 0.05 significance level and 0.95
statistical power. To enhance reliability, 338 responses
were collected. After excluding six ineligible responses
and removing 24 statistical outliers (based on Mahalanobis

distance and extreme inconsistencies in responses), the final
sample comprised 308 valid responses.
Data were analysed using partial least squares struc-
tural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) via SmartPLS
4.1.0.9 software. PLS-SEM was chosen due to the study’s
complex model, which incorporated 12 latent constructs
and 50 observed indicators. This method effectively han -
dles non-normal data distributions and allows for the
simultaneous testing of direct and mediating relation-
ships (Hair et al., 2014).
By integrating real-world customer experiences, non-
humanoid robotic service interactions and structured theo-
retical models, this study advances the understanding of how
functional efficiency, emotional engagement and relational
trust shape customer satisfaction and behavioural intentions
in robotic dining services.
Common method variance
To mitigate potential common method variance (CMV) from
self-report surveys, a marker-based technique was applied
prior to PLS-SEM analysis. A marker variable (MV) com-
prising three items from the ‘attitude toward the colour blue’
scale (Miller & Simmering, 2023) was included in the sur -
vey to detect CMV (Chin et al., 2013; Podsakoff et al., 2003,
2016; Tehseen et al., 2017). Comparing  R2 values for each
endogenous construct, with and without the MV, showed
negligible differences, confirming that CMV was not an
issue (see Table 3).
Results and data analysis
The survey revealed a slight majority of female respondents
(52.6%). Most participants were younger, with 71.4% aged
18–30. In terms of marital status, 73.1% were single, while
26.9% were married; among the married participants, 18.8%
had children and 8.1% did not. Malaysian respondents con-
stituted most of the sample (97.1%).
As for education, 63.3% held undergraduate degrees,
22.1% had advanced degrees (master’s or doctoral) and
14.6% had secondary education or diploma qualifications.
Occupationally, 43.2% were private sector employees,
followed by students (25.3%), public sector employees
(14.6%), self-employed individuals (10.7%), unemployed
participants (2.9%) and 3.2% who did not disclose their
employment status.
Engagement with robotic service restaurants Respondents
reported diverse levels of engagement. Same-day visits
accounted for 29.9%, while 4.2% visited the preceding day.
Visits within the last week, month and 3 months were 14%,
21.1% and 24%, respectively. Most participants (66.2%) had
visited robotic service restaurants more than once, while
33.8% had only visited once. All visits occurred within the
past year, ensuring the recency of experiences and reliability
of the data.
Social dynamics of visits Dining with friends was the most
common scenario, reported by 37.3% of respondents, fol-
lowed by visits with family or relatives (29.9%). These
results highlight the significant role of social contexts in
shaping experiences in robotic dining environments.
Measurement model assessment
The measurement model was evaluated using a two-step
approach. First, reliability and convergent validity were
assessed by analysing average variance extracted (AVE)
and composite reliability (CR). All values met the recom-
mended thresholds (indicator loadings λ > 0.5, AVE > 0.5,
CR > 0.7), confirming strong reliability and validity (Hair
et al., 2019, 2021) (see Table 4). Second, discriminant valid-
ity was tested using the heterotrait-monotrait (HTMT) ratio.
Most HTMT values were below the 0.90 threshold (Table 5),
except for rapport-perceived trust and satisfaction-attitude
pairs. However, bootstrapped 90% bias-corrected and accel-
erated confidence intervals (10,000 subsamples) confirmed
that the intervals did not straddle 0, establishing discrimi-
nant validity for all constructs (Franke & Sarstedt, 2019;
Henseler et al., 2015).

follows established methodological guidance for PLS-SEM

Mediating effects of attitude
For H1a, which hypothesised that perceived safety (SAF)
positively influences RVI through attitude (ATT), the results
revealed no significant direct or indirect effects (β = 0.014,
p = 0.209). This indicates that SAF does not impact revisit
intentions through ATT, rendering the hypothesis not sup-
ported. This suggests that safety, while a critical functional
factor, may not influence attitudes strongly enough to shape
behavioural outcomes in non-humanoid robotic interactions.
In contrast, H1b, which examined the effect of service
efficiency (SEFF) on RVI through ATT, revealed significant
direct (β = 0.081, p  = 0.002) and indirect (β = 0.049, p  =
0.002) effects, supporting complementary mediation. This
confirms that SEFF enhances customer attitudes, which,
in turn, positively influences revisit intentions. The results
stress the importance of efficiency as a functional stimu-
lus within the S–O-R framework in structured hospitality
environments.
For H1c, testing performance efficacy (EFFC) as a predic-
tor of ATT and RVI, a significant direct effect was found (β
= 0.124, p = 0.019), but no mediation effect was observed.
The mediation type is classified as direct-only (DO), which
does not align with the hypothesised mediation. Thus, H1c
was not supported, suggesting that EFFC directly influences
revisit intentions without necessarily shaping customer
attitudes.
Mediating effects of satisfaction
The mediating role of satisfaction (SAS) connects experien-
tial and relational dimensions with behavioural outcomes,
illustrating the interplay of novelty, enjoyment and trust
within the S–O-R and USUS frameworks.
For H2a, perceived novelty (NOVL) was hypothesised to
positively influence RVI through SAS. The findings revealed
significant direct and indirect effects (β = 0.067, p = 0.002),
supporting complementary mediation. This demonstrates
that the novelty of non-humanoid robots enhances satisfac-
tion, which translates into higher revisit intentions, reflecting
the dual experiential and functional value of novelty.
In H2b, perceived enjoyment (ENJ) was expected to
enhance SAT which in turn boosts RVI. However, the results
showed a significant negative indirect effect (β = − 0.035,
p = 0.052) of ENJ on RVI via SAT and no significant direct
effect. The negative direction of the mediation highlights a
potential disconnect between ENJ and satisfaction in robotic
interactions, rendering the hypothesis not supported. In other
words, excessive focus on enjoyment undermined satisfaction,
leading to lower revisit intentions than expected.
For H2c, perceived friendliness (FRI) was tested as a
predictor of SAS and RVI. While a significant indirect
effect was found (β = 0.105, p  < 0.001), the direct effect
was negative (β = − 0.124, p  = 0.028), resulting in com-
petitive mediation. This suggests that while FRI positively
impacts satisfaction, it negatively influences revisit inten -
tions directly, leading to the hypothesis being not supported.
This hypothesis assumes perceived friendliness increases
satisfaction, driving revisit intention. While friendliness
did raise satisfaction, there was a negative direct effect on
revisit intention, creating a competitive mediation (positive
via satisfaction, negative direct). Thus, an overly friendly
robot can backfire on loyalty, as high friendliness conflicted
with customers’ revisit intentions.

Discussion
This study provides critical insights into HRI within the
structured service context of robot-serviced restaurants.
The findings, summarised in Table  9, offer both theo-
retical and practical implications, revealing the complex
relationship between functional, emotional, and relational
dimensions of HRI in shaping customer satisfaction and
revisit intention. While many results align with established
frameworks like the S–O-R and USUS frameworks, oth -
ers challenge existing assumptions and highlight areas for
further exploration.

Functional service factors and revisit intention
The results for the H1 hypotheses highlight a complex role
of functional service qualities in shaping customer loyalty.
Perceived safety (H1a) showed no significant effect on revisit
intention via attitude. In practical terms, this suggests that
basic safety is taken as a given. Customers seem to assume
the robot is safe and instead prioritise other factors. In con-
trast, service efficiency (H1b) emerged as a critical driver:
it had a significant complementary mediation effect, mean-
ing it boosts revisit intention by first improving customer
attitude. When a service robot operates swiftly and reliably
(e.g. delivering food promptly in a restaurant), it leaves a
favourable impression that translates into increased intent
to return. Performance efficacy (H1c), the robot’s compe-
tent task performance, influenced revisit intention through a
direct-only effect. This indicates that customers value a robot
that can effectively perform its job, and this appreciation
directly encourages repeat patronage without necessarily
altering their interim attitude toward the robot. Theoretically,
these findings imply that within the S–O-R framework, not
all functional stimuli feed into the ‘organism’ (attitude)
equally. Service efficiency functions as a stimulus that does
elevate internal evaluations (attitudes) and thus behaviour,
whereas performance efficacy exerts its impact in a more
outcome-driven manner (customers return because the ser -
vice outcome was good, even if their attitude toward the
robot itself is not strongly changed). Meanwhile, the lack of
any effect for safety shows that some functional attributes
might be mere ‘hygiene’ factors, which are necessary but not
sufficient to sway customer attitudes or retention.

that a fun or pleasurable robot interaction enhances satis-
faction and repeat patronage (El-Said & Al Hajri, 2022).
One explanation is that in a task-oriented dining context (e.g.
robotic food delivery), an overly entertaining robot can be
perceived as a novelty gimmick that distracts from core ser-
vice quality. Drawing on expectancy-disconfirmation theory
(Oliver, 1980), customers in structured environments antici-
pate functional attributes, such as reliability and operational
efficiency, over hedonic or relational features. Customers
might enjoy the interaction in the moment but view it as a
one-off experience, satisfying curiosity rather than building
loyalty. In fact, highly novel, anthropomorphic experiences
can stimulate variety-seeking behaviour, prompting patrons
to try something new next time rather than return (Liu et al.,
2025). Thus, consistent with recent cautions in the literature
(Oğan, 2024; Pitardi et al., 2024), our results highlight an
enjoyment paradox: enjoyment alone may not translate into
enduring commitment when it is not aligned with customers’
primary service goals.
Turning to friendliness, H2c/H4c revealed a competitive
mediation: the robot’s friendliness had a positive indirect
effect on revisit intention via increased satisfaction, yet a
negative direct effect. In other words, a friendly demean-
our made people feel satisfied, but at the same time, some
aspect of that friendliness discouraged their return. In plain
terms, while a friendly robot made customers more satisfied,
something about that friendliness also discouraged custom-
ers from returning (the net total effect was not positive). A
possible theoretical reason is that while social robots can
mimic surface-level warmth, they still lack genuine human
empathy and personalisation in service encounters (Wirtz
et al., 2025). This dynamic is supported by role congruity
theory (Eagly & Karau, 2002). The friendly robot might
have improved the immediate experience (e.g. novelty,
polite service), but it could not foster the authentic social
connection or trust that underpins loyalty (El-Said & Al
Hajri, 2022). Indeed, over-anthropomorphism or ‘too much’
friendliness can cross into uncanny or insincere territory,
leading to discomfort or a sense that the experience is gim-
micky (Li et al., 2023). Service context is critical here: din-
ers expecting efficient, seamless service may react negatively
if a robot’s social behaviour feels out of place. For instance,
introducing social robots in culturally themed restaurants
offers novel interactions but can also undermine the per -
ceived authenticity of the experience (Huo et al., 2025). This
context mismatch might explain the direct negative impact
of friendliness on revisit intentions,patrons enjoyed the
robot’s courtesy, yet subconsciously missed the human touch
or felt the concept did not fit the dining atmosphere. From
a social exchange theory perspective (Homans 1958 ; Blau
1964), one could say the robot’s friendliness provided rela-
tional value (a pleasant interaction), but perhaps customers
felt the exchange lacked relational depth. A genuine rela-
tional exchange in services often involves empathy, personal
attention and trust,facets that a robot cannot fully deliver.
A similar pattern is observed in religious consumer con-
texts. For example, Al-Hajla et al. (2019) found that Islamic
brand personality, a symbolic construct, did not significantly
influence adoption behaviour among Muslim consumers
unless it was reinforced by more utilitarian criteria such
as relative advantage and perceived compatibility. In both
cases, symbolic cues alone were insufficient to drive behav-
ioural outcomes when they failed to align with consumers’
core expectations of functionality and appropriateness for
the service context. Therefore, the incremental benefit of
robot friendliness can plateaued quickly. Our findings urge
a reconsideration of the common assumption that simply
making service robots more ‘likable’ (through enjoyment
or friendliness) will automatically boost customer retention.
In the context of human–robot dining interactions, the
findings for H6a and H6b reveal how customers’ novelty-
seeking traits shape satisfaction outcomes. H6a predicted
that the positive effect of perceived novelty on satisfaction
would be weaker for high novelty-seekers. This hypothesis
was supported, indicating a significant negative moderation
effect: high novelty-seeking individuals derive compara-
tively less satisfaction from robotic novelty. The result aligns
with hedonic adaptation theory, which posits that repeated
exposure to novel stimuli yields diminishing enjoyment
over time. High novelty-seekers quickly habituate to the
robot’s novelty, consistent with diminishing marginal util-
ity (Gocłowska et al., 2019; Alotaibi et al., 2024). Moreover,
expectancy-disconfirmation theory (Oliver, 1980) provides a
complementary lens: novelty-seekers’ elevated expectations
for unique experiences mean robotic services often merely
meet rather than exceed their novelty threshold, limiting
satisfaction gains   (El-Said & Al Hajri, 2022 ). In contrast,
H6b posited a similar moderation for perceived enjoyment’s
effect on satisfaction, but this was not supported. The impact
of enjoyment remained stable across novelty-seeking levels,
suggesting that the enjoyment derived from engaging with
a robot server is less contingent on one’s novelty appetite.
Figure 4 illustrates this interaction: the novelty–satisfaction
slope is steep under low novelty-seeking but flattens at high
novelty-seeking levels. Table  8 further confirms this pat-
tern via a moderated mediation analysis: at high novelty-
seeking (+ 1 SD), the indirect effect of novelty on revisit
intentions via satisfaction (and attitude) is substantially
attenuated (β ≈ 0.02, n.s.), whereas at low novelty-seek -
ing (– 1 SD) it remains significant and stronger (β ≈ 0.07,
p < 0.001). This conditional process result (Preacher et al.,
2007) indicates that novelty-driven satisfaction translates
into repeat patronage primarily for customers with lower
novelty-seeking dispositions. A plausible reason is that by
now robotic service is becoming mainstream enough that

via attitude (H4 d: trust → satisfaction → attitude → revisit,
supported), indicating that trust builds favourable attitudes as
well. This aligns with a fundamental tenet of social exchange
and relationship marketing: trust is the foundation of loy -
alty, even in technologically-mediated interactions (Morgan
& Hunt 1994; Kim et al., 2021). However, our moderated
findings qualify this by showing that the importance of trust
can be altered by the level of rapport a customer feels with
the robot.
The findings reveal a complex role of customer–robot
rapport in shaping satisfaction. As hypothesised in H5a,
rapport significantly amplified the effect of perceived robot
friendliness on customer satisfaction. This aligns with prior
service literature showing that warm, friendly interactions
foster rapport, which in turn elevates customer satisfaction
(Borghi et al., 2023). As illustrated in Fig. 3, when custom-
ers felt a strong sense of rapport with the robot, even slight
increases in the robot’s friendliness led to big jumps in sat-
isfaction. Rapport represents a personal connection marked
by enjoyable, friendly engagement (Gremler & Gwinner,
2000). When such a bond is present, friendly behaviours
from the robot are more deeply appreciated and impactful,
leading to higher satisfaction. In essence, rapport creates
an emotional buffer that magnifies positive stimuli, i.e. a
friendly robot seems even friendlier and more satisfying
when the customer feels a social connection with it. This
result reinforces Gremler and Gwinner’s ( 2000) classic
insight that greater rapport yields greater satisfaction in
service exchanges. It also fits with recent robot studies find-
ing that encouraging human–robot rapport (e.g. via con-
versational warmth) boosts customers’ overall experience
(Borghi et al., 2023).
Surprisingly, however, H5b revealed that high rapport
actually weakened the positive influence of trust on satisfac-
tion. Figure  5 illustrates this paradox: at low levels of rap-
port, increases in trust strongly boost satisfaction, but at high
rapport, the trust–satisfaction slope flattens (the service is
already satisfying due to rapport, so additional trust yields
diminishing returns). In other words, when customers felt
a strong personal rapport with the service robot, the usual
importance of trust, defined as belief in the robot’s abil-
ity to deliver on needs (Sharma et al., 2023), was partially
diluted. Several theoretical explanations can be advanced for
this unexpected moderation. One possibility is a redundancy
effect: rapport and trust both contribute to a sense of security
and comfort, so a high level of rapport may have already ful-
filled much of the customer’s psychological need, leaving less
incremental benefit for trust. The customer, feeling emotion-
ally satisfied by the friendly bond, may not derive as much
additional satisfaction from cognitive assurances of the robot’s
reliability. Another, related explanation is shifting focus or
role ambiguity. A highly personable robot could blur the line
between ‘service tool’ and ‘social partner’. When the robot is
treated more like a companion, customers might place greater
weight on relational warmth and less on task-based reliability
in evaluating their experience. This aligns with social cog-
nition research suggesting a warmth–competence trade-off:
extremely friendly (high-rapport) agents might inadvertently
be seen as less task-competent (Schömbs et al., 2023). In
this study’s context, strong rapport may have overshadowed
competence cues, thereby muting the impact of trust on sat-
isfaction. Additionally, an overly social robot could induce
a mild social overload where some patrons might feel over-
whelmed or unsure how to reciprocate, which could further
dampen the influence of trust. Taken together, these interpre-
tations suggest that rapport can at times substitute for, rather
than supplement, the role of trust in driving satisfaction.
These findings show that rapport is fundamentally a per-
ceived relational bond in the interaction. Accordingly, this
study has modelled rapport as a moderator rather than a
direct predictor, capturing how this perception conditions the
influence of friendliness and trust on customer satisfaction.
The mediating roles of satisfaction and attitude
Satisfaction and attitude emerged as important mediators
in translating HRI constructs into revisit intentions. Trust
exhibited indirect-only mediation through satisfaction (H3,
H4 d), highlighting its foundational role in fostering reten-
tion. In contrast, friendliness demonstrated competitive
mediation, reflecting the inherent challenge of balancing its
relational benefits with potential functional drawbacks (H2c,
H4c). The complementary mediation effects of novelty (H2a,
H4a) highlight its role as an experiential driver that can
enhance customer retention when paired with positive sat-
isfaction and attitudes. These findings illustrate the layered
complexity of customer decision-making in robotic service
environments and reinforce the need for multi-dimensional
approaches in designing effective HRI strategies.

Theoretical and practical contributions
Theoretical contributions
This study makes significant contributions to HRI, service
marketing and consumer behaviour literature by refin-
ing existing theoretical frameworks and challenging prior
assumptions about customer engagement with robotic ser -
vices. Unlike previous research that primarily applied the
S–O-R framework to human-like robots, this study demon-
strates its applicability to non-humanoid, hybrid-functional
robots, providing a modified theoretical lens for robotic ser-
vice environments. By integrating S–O-R with the USUS
framework, this study provides a more structured under -
standing of how customers interact with robots that empha-
sise functionality over anthropomorphic features.
While prior research predominantly examined technol-
ogy adoption and initial acceptance (e.g. TAM, UTAUT),
this study shifts the focus toward sustained engagement,
satisfaction and revisit intentions (Davis, 1989; Venkatesh
et al., 2003; Yuen et al., 2022). Specifically, it refines the
S–O-R framework by demonstrating that functional, emo-
tional and relational dimensions do not operate as isolated
factors, but as interdependent drivers of customer engage-
ment. This process-oriented approach challenges traditional
models that treat these dimensions as independent predic-
tors of behavioural outcomes (Mkpojiogu et al., 2024).
Moreover, a key theoretical refinement in this study is
its extension of the integrated S–O-R–USUS framework to
hybrid-functional robots  (i.e. non-humanoid, task-oriented
service robots). Prior studies largely assumed that social
bonding mechanisms in humanoid robots are essential for
customer trust and satisfaction (Oyedele et al., 2007). How-
ever, this study challenges that assumption by demonstrat-
ing that non-humanoid robots can foster relational engage-
ment through functional reliability rather than emotional
expressiveness. This finding aligns with role congruity
theory (Eagly & Karau, 2002), which suggests that robots
deviating too far from expected functional roles may face
user resistance. This aligns with findings in conservative
markets where symbolic brand traits do not always translate
into behavioural loyalty, particularly when utilitarian expec-
tations dominate (Al-Hajla et al., 2019). By examining the
mediating roles of attitude and satisfaction, the study clari-
fies that functional efficiency remains dominant in robotic
service environments. Still, emotional and relational factors
create situational variations in customer responses.
Novelty‑seeking and the limits of sustained engagement
This study also challenges conventional assumptions about
novelty-seeking in HRI engagement models. Previous
research assumed that novelty consistently enhances
engagement (Tussyadiah et al., 2020). However, this study
demonstrates that high novelty-seekers experience dimin-
ishing satisfaction over time, revealing a critical boundary
condition for the Hedonic Adaptation Theory (Frederick
& Loewenstein, 1999). Customers who crave novelty have
a higher threshold for stimulation and quickly acclimate
to novel experiences. Specifically, while novelty sparks
initial excitement, high novelty-seekers exhibit a faster
decline in satisfaction, requiring continuous innovation
to maintain engagement (Chang, 2024). In contrast, low
novelty-seeking customers, who do not regularly seek out
new experiences, derive substantial delight and satisfac-
tion from the novel encounter, which strongly boosts their
revisit intentions. This insight refines prior work by show -
ing that novelty alone does not sustain long-term engage-
ment in robotic service environments.
Trust, rapport, and the complexity of relational dynamics
in HRI
Another significant theoretical contribution is the dual role
of rapport in robotic interactions. While previous research
assumed that rapport-building mechanisms continually
strengthen trust in HRI (Nomura & Kanda, 2016), this study
finds that rapport paradoxically weakens the trust-satisfac-
tion relationship. This supports Trust-Distrust Duality The-
ory (Lewicki et al., 1998), which posits that trust and distrust
can coexist in relational interactions. However, this study
advances that argument by demonstrating that trust forma-
tion in robotic service settings follows a different trajectory
than in human–human interactions.
Rather than reinforcing trust, excessive relational engage-
ment may create unrealistic expectations of social connect-
edness that robots cannot fulfil. This challenges prior service
marketing research assumptions, which treated rapport as a
universally positive factor (Cavedon et al., 2015; Gremler
& Gwinner, 2000). Instead, this study highlights a trade-
off between functional efficiency and perceived relational
authenticity, requiring a complex balance between engage-
ment and credibility in robotic service design.
Refining theories of social perception in HRI
This study also contributes to the ongoing discussions on the
role of social perception in HRI. Recent studies indicate that
acknowledgment and perceived warmth influence customer
engagement with service robots (Frank & Otterbring, 2023).
This study extends that argument by showing that acknowledg-
ment, rapport, and trust function differently in non-humanoid
robotic contexts, shaping customer revisit intentions through
mechanisms distinct from human service encounters.

Furthermore, this study aligns with emerging research on
affective technology acceptance, which suggests that posi-
tive affect enhances robotic acceptance, while negative affect
has mixed effects depending on the measurement approach
(Jessup et al., 2023). By integrating affective, cognitive and
relational perspectives into a structured framework, this
study refines our understanding of customer-robot interac-
tions in service industries, specifically restaurants.
Practical contributions
The findings offer actionable guidance for managers and
designers working in robotic service environments. Restaurant
managers can leverage these insights to balance friendliness
and professionalism in robotic interactions. Robots should
exhibit polite and approachable behaviour while maintaining
task efficiency to meet customer expectations. High novelty-
seekers benefit from periodic updates to robot features, such
as modular functionalities or interactive designs, which sus-
tain engagement and prevent hedonic adaptation (Frederick
& Loewenstein, 1999). In contrast, low novelty-seekers pre-
fer consistent and reliable interactions that emphasise core
functionalities like safety and efficiency (Hlee et al., 2022).
Subtle rapport-building mechanisms, such as continuity in
interactions or adaptive communication, can foster familiarity
without undermining trust (Seo & Lee, 2021).
For designers and coders, the study highlights the impor-
tance of creating adaptive algorithms that support context-sen-
sitive rapport-building, enhancing relational warmth without
overpromising emotional engagement (Lewicki et al., 1998).
Anthropomorphic features should be carefully calibrated to
align with operational goals and avoid creating unrealistic
expectations (Oğan, 2024). Modular software designs that
allow periodic updates can maintain novelty for high novelty-
seekers, while high task-oriented performance ensures satisfac-
tion for low novelty-seekers. Safety features, such as hygienic
handling and advanced navigation, build trust in restaurant set-
tings, while seamless integration with restaurant management
systems improves operational efficiency and provides valuable
data for refinement (Edvardsson et al., 2018).
Managers and designers must align robotic features with
customer expectations by considering both functional and
relational dimensions to optimise customer experience.
Robotic systems can enhance customer satisfaction and fos-
ter long-term loyalty by integrating functional efficiency,
emotional engagement and relational dynamics.
Conclusion
This study advances the understanding of human–robot
interactions in service environments by examining how func-
tional, emotional and relational factors work together to shape
customer experience outcomes. By integrating the S–O-R
framework with the USUS framework, the research provides a
more complete perspective on how service robot features and
behaviours influence customer satisfaction, trust and revisit
intentions. The findings indicate that these dimensions are
interdependent rather than isolated drivers of engagement:
for instance, rapport amplifies the effect of a robot’s friendli-
ness on satisfaction, and the thrill of novelty can taper off
with repeated exposure. Theoretically, the study bridges
human–robot interaction and service marketing literatures,
extending traditional adoption models to consider sustained
engagement and the complex interplay of social and func-
tional cues. Practically, it offers guidance for service providers
deploying robots, highlighting the need to balance operational
efficiency with relational warmth. Robotic services should
deliver reliable, safe performance while fostering appropri-
ate social interaction to meet customer expectations. These
insights contribute an integrated framework for designing
customer-centric robotic services that maximise satisfaction
and encourage loyalty in hospitality settings.
Limitations and directions for future research
This study is not without limitations. First, all measures were
self-reported, which raises the potential for common method
bias, social desirability influences as well as the attitude-behav-
iour gap. Although procedural remedies (e.g. a marker-vari-
able technique) were employed to mitigate bias, future work
should consider complementary data (such as observational or
behavioural metrics) to validate the self-report findings. For
instance, actual return visits or purchase records can verify
whether stated intentions translate into repeat patronage. Sec-
ond, the research was cross-sectional, capturing perceptions
at a single point in time. This design limits the ability to infer
causality or observe how customer responses evolve. Longi-
tudinal studies are needed to examine temporal dynamics. For
example, tracking whether the novelty effect on engagement
stabilises or further declines over multiple interactions (Ali
et al., 2024; El-Said & Al Hajri, 2022). Third, the sample was
drawn from a single regional and cultural context (a Malay-
sian restaurant setting), which may restrict the generalisability
of the results. Cultural norms likely moderate how customers
perceive and accept service robots, so cross-cultural research is
warranted to test the model in diverse settings (Huang & Liu,
2022). Customer expectations and perceptions of robotic inter-
actions vary between Western and Asian markets, particularly
concerning the role of social presence, politeness and emo-
tional engagement in service settings (Huang & Liu, 2022).
Fourth, this study focused on a specific service context (casual
dining) and a particular type of service robot (a non-humanoid,
task-oriented delivery robot). The findings may not fully apply
to other industry settings or to robots with different designs.
Future studies should therefore examine hybrid human–robot

service models where robot assistants work alongside human
staff, to see how such arrangements influence perceived effi-
ciency, service warmth and overall satisfaction (Wang et al.,
2025). Similarly, researchers could investigate the impact of
more anthropomorphic robot designs on customer trust and
comfort, as the optimal degree of human-likeness in service
robots remains an open question (Tussyadiah et al., 2020).
Fifth, the study found that safety had no significant impact in
revisit intentions, perhaps because basic safety is now assumed
by customers; future research could examine if this holds in
scenarios with higher perceived risk or different cultural set-
tings. Finally, the study omitted ‘Societal Impact’, the fourth
dimension of the USUS framework, thereby excluding broader
societal-level considerations from the model. Leaving out
this factor creates a gap in understanding the full spectrum of
impacts. Future research should integrate the Societal Impact
dimension to provide a more complete evaluation of service
robot implementations, examining how these technologies
affect not just individual customers but also wider society.
