Abstract
This study investigates how service assurance and environmental cues in ﬂuence users ’ post-adoption evaluation of
self-service technologies (SSTs) and self-service kiosks (SSKs), incorporating the moderating role of technology
expertise within the technology continuance theory (TCT) framework. Although prior research has examined initial
technology acceptance, post-adoption evaluations in automated service environments remain underexplored. Data
were collected from Korean consumers with prior experience using SSTs/SSKs, and structural equation modeling
(SEM) was applied to test the proposed model. The results revealed that service assurance signi ﬁcantly affects both
environmental cues and post-adoption evaluations, while environmental cues also exert a signi ﬁcant mediating effect
between service assurance and post-adoption evaluation. Moreover, technology expertise moderates the relationship
between environmental cues and post-adoption evaluation, indicating that users with higher technological pro ﬁciency
perceive greater experiential value from well-designed SST environments. This study extends the TCT by integrating
service quality and servicescape perspectives, offering a comprehensive understanding of how users ’ cognitive and
experiential factors interact in technology-mediated services. The ﬁndings also suggest practical strategies for
managers to enhance customer experience by improving service reliability, optimizing physical design and
atmospherics, and tailoring user support based on technology expertise. Overall, this research provides both
theoretical advancement and managerial insights into sustaining positive user engagement in the evolving landscape
of smart service environments.
Introduction
The rapid proliferation of self-service technologies (SSTs) and self-service kiosks (SSKs) has fundamentally trans-
formed the nature of service encounters across industries such as retail, hospitality, and food service. These systems
enable customers to perform transactions autonomously, offering convenience, ef ﬁciency, and operational scalability.
Beyond simple automation, recent advances in arti ﬁcial intelligence and smart interfaces have positioned SSTs and
SSKs as essential components of digital service ecosystems (Singh and Yadav 2025; Wong et al. 2022). As ﬁrms
increasingly adopt these technologies to enhance customer experience and reduce labor dependency, understanding
the determinants of users ’ sustained engagement with such systems has become a pressing research priority. In such
technology-mediated environments, users are required to evaluate service quality and experience without relying on

traditional interpersonal interactions, making the interpretation of service encounters fundamentally different from
conventional service settings.
Despite extensive research on initial technology adoption, relatively fewer studies have examined users ’ post-
adoption evaluations of SSTs and SSKs —namely, how consumers assess, con ﬁrm, and internalize their ongoing
experiences after repeated use (Foroughi et al. 2023; Rahi et al. 2021). Traditional models such as the technology
acceptance model (Davis 1989) and expectation –conﬁrmation theory (Bhattacherjee, 2001) provide useful founda-
tions for understanding technology use, while technology continuance theory (TCT) further explains continued usage
as a function of cognitive con ﬁrmation and affective satisfaction (Liao et al. 2009). However, in SST contexts, users ’
post-adoption evaluations are not shaped solely by system performance or functional ef ﬁciency. Because SSTs
replace or reduce direct interpersonal service encounters, users also rely on experiential and environmental cues —
such as service reliability, assurance, physical surroundings, and perceived service quality —to evaluate whether the
technology-mediated service experience remains satisfactory and worthwhile (Lee et al. 2009; Lin and Hsieh 2011;
Youssef et al. 2025). Thus, examining post-adoption evaluation in SST settings requires attention not only to
cognitive continuance mechanisms, but also to the service and environmental conditions through which users
interpret automated service encounters.
Recent studies reveal a paradigmatic shift in SST/SSK research —from focusing solely on speed and cost reduction
to emphasizing emotional engagement, design aesthetics, and customer delight (Collier and Barnes, 2015; Lee 2023b;
Stead et al. 2021). This shift suggests that users ’ evaluations of SSTs are increasingly shaped by experiential and
interpretive processes rather than purely functional assessments. Moreover, individual differences in technological
capability have emerged as a critical factor in ﬂuencing how users interpret these service experiences (Guan et al.
2021; Nam et al. 2023). Rather than uniformly moderating all relationships, technology expertise may condition the
extent to which users rely on different evaluative cues in automated service environments. Users with greater
technological expertise are more likely to process and integrate environmental and interface-related information into
their overall evaluation, whereas less experienced users tend to depend more on service assurance and reliability cues
to reduce uncertainty and build trust (Galdolage 2021a; Parasuraman 2000). This distinction highlights that post-
adoption evaluation in SST contexts is not only a function of service attributes, but also of how users with different
levels of technological capability interpret and prioritize these attributes.
This study aims to examine how service assurance and environmental cues jointly in ﬂuence users ’ post-adoption
evaluation of SSTs and SSKs, rather than strictly developing a new theoretical framework. Building on the under-
lying logic of the TCT, this study incorporates experiential and contextual factors that are particularly relevant in
technology-mediated service environments. Furthermore, the study investigates the role of technology expertise in
shaping how users interpret and integrate these service experiences. By doing so, this research contributes to the
literature in three ways. First, it shifts the focus of SST research from functional and cognitive evaluation to
experiential interpretation in post-adoption contexts. Second, it integrates service assurance and environmental cues
to explain how users construct their evaluations of automated service encounters. Third, it highlights the role of
individual technological capability in conditioning how users interpret and prioritize different service attributes.
Rather than claiming a full theoretical extension of TCT, this study offers a contextual application and re ﬁnement of
continuance-related perspectives in SST environments, while also providing practical insights into how organizations
can design more inclusive and engaging self-service systems.

Self-Service kiosk
The emergence of SST has transformed traditional service encounters by allowing customers to participate directly in
service production and delivery without human employees. Among SST forms, SSKs have become especially
prominent across retail, hospitality, transportation, and foodservice industries, offering greater ef ﬁciency,

convenience, and autonomy (Lee et al. 2009; Vakulenko et al. 2018). SSKs enable users to perform transactions such
as ordering, payment, and check-in with minimal staff interaction, thereby streamlining operations and reducing labor
costs (Chau et al. 2025). At the same time, the reduced presence of interpersonal interaction raises important
questions about how users perceive, evaluate, and emotionally respond to technology-mediated service encounters
(Collier et al. 2017).
Early research on SST primarily emphasized utilitarian bene ﬁts, such as speed, accuracy, and operational ef ﬁ-
ciency. Prior studies consistently show that service quality dimensions —such as reliability, problem solving, and
responsiveness—are critical determinants of customer satisfaction and behavioral intention in SSK contexts (Lee
et al. 2009; Joshi and Joshi 2020). These ﬁndings suggest that even in automated environments, service assurance
remains a fundamental basis for evaluating technology-enabled services.
Over time, researchers have increasingly examined the experiential and emotional dimensions of SSK use.
Vakulenko et al. ( 2018) identiﬁed multiple value categories —functional, emotional, and social —that shape customer
experience, while Vakulenko et al. ( 2019) further distinguished between instrumental and affective value creation in
SSKs. This stream of research indicates a shift from purely functional evaluation toward value co-creation and
experiential consumption in SST contexts. Lee ( 2023a) and Lee ( 2023b) likewise demonstrated that both utilitarian
and hedonic values in ﬂuence perceived service quality and satisfaction, highlighting that technology-mediated
encounters must deliver not only ef ﬁciency but also emotional engagement.
The conceptual foundation of perceived value in SSKs aligns with Zeithaml ( 1988), who deﬁned customer value as
a trade-off between perceived bene ﬁts and costs. Kumar and Mittal ( 2015) extended this logic to digital service
contexts, showing that consistent service performance and reliability underpin satisfaction. Galdolage ( 2021a, 2021b)
further identi ﬁed usefulness, ef ﬁciency, and information richness as key determinants of customer choice and
satisfaction in SST environments. Together, these studies underscore that effective SSK experiences depend not only
on technological capability but also on users ’ perceptions of service reliability and value.
The servicescape concept (Bitner 1992) has also been extended to SST contexts to explain how environmental cues
shape customer responses. Guan et al. ( 2021) showed that cleanliness, layout, and aesthetics positively in ﬂuence
attitudes toward SST usage, while Youssef et al. ( 2025) proposed the Smart Servicescape model incorporating
aesthetics, physical functionality, and perceived security. Halstead and Richards ( 2014) and Roy ( 2014) further
emphasized that environmental and interface-related cues in ﬂuence both cognitive evaluations and affective
responses to automated services. These ﬁndings highlight that users ’ evaluations of SSKs are shaped not only by
service performance but also by the surrounding overall service environment.
Recent research has further explored how users cognitively and emotionally process SST experiences. Stead et al.
(2021) demonstrated that consumers adapt their cognitive schemas when interacting with new servicescapes, while
Wong et al. ( 2022) conceptualized smart service quality (SSQ) as an integration of physical and technological service
dimensions. Collier and Barnes ( 2015) emphasized the role of enjoyment in shaping customer delight, whereas Nam
et al. ( 2023) and Ivkov et al. ( 2020) highlighted how individual differences and contextual factors in ﬂuence user
responses to SSTs. Chau et al. ( 2025) and Collier et al. ( 2017) further showed that service design and failure recovery
processes can signi ﬁcantly affect user satisfaction in automated environments. Collectively, these studies suggest that
SST experiences are shaped by a combination of functional performance, emotional engagement, and contextual
interpretation.
Expanding beyond individual interactions, Singh and Yadav ( 2025) emphasized the integration of SSKs within
broader omnichannel retail environments, while Zine et al. ( 2016), Joshi and Joshi ( 2020), and Galdolage ( 2021a)
highlighted the importance of ﬂexibility, ef ﬁciency, and customer participation in shaping perceived value. These
ﬁndings reinforce that SSKs function as multidimensional service interfaces that combine technological capability
with experiential and environmental elements.
Despite this growing body of research, existing studies have largely examined these factors in isolation, with
limited attention to how service assurance and environmental cues jointly shape post-adoption evaluation in SST
contexts. Furthermore, relatively little is known about how users with different levels of technological capability
interpret and prioritize these factors when forming evaluations of automated service experiences. To address this gap,
the present study examines how service assurance and environmental cues jointly in ﬂuence post-adoption evaluation

Technology Continuance Theory and Proposed Extended Model
The theory of technology continuance (TCT), introduced by Liao et al. ( 2009), integrates the technology acceptance
model (Davis 1989; Davis et al. 1989) and the expectation –conﬁrmation model (Bhattacherjee 2001) to explain how
users decide whether to continue using a technology after initial adoption. TCT posits that users ’ conﬁrmation of
expectations and perceived usefulness jointly determine satisfaction, which subsequently in ﬂuences continuance
intention. While TCT provides a useful foundation for understanding post-adoption behavior, it has primarily focused
on cognitive and functional evaluations of technology use, with relatively limited attention to the experiential and
service-related dimensions that characterize technology-mediated service environments.
Originally developed in the information systems domain, TCT has been applied to various contexts such as mobile
banking, e-commerce, and SSTs. These studies suggest that continuance behavior is shaped not only by system
performance but also by users ’ overall service experience (Khayer and Bao 2019; Wong et al. 2022). In SST and SSK
contexts, users evaluate technology through a service lens, where functional performance, experiential quality, and
environmental conditions jointly in ﬂuence satisfaction and continued use (Collier and Barnes 2015; Youssef et al.
2025).
Compared to alternative frameworks such as the Uni ﬁed Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology 2
(UTAUT2) (Venkatesh et al. 2012), the Information Systems Success Model (IS Success Model) (DeLone and
McLean 2003), and the Stimulus –Organism–Response (S-O-R) framework (Mehrabian and Russell 1974), TCT is
particularly suitable for this study because it focuses on post-adoption evaluation processes rather than initial
adoption decisions or stimulus-response mechanisms. While UTAUT2 emphasizes behavioral intention driven by
expectancy beliefs and the IS Success Model focuses on system quality and use outcomes, TCT provides a more
appropriate lens for understanding how users form evaluative judgments after repeated technology use in service
contexts.
In the context of SSKs, prior research indicates that users ’ post-adoption evaluations are in ﬂuenced by both
service-related and environmental factors (Guan et al. 2021; Lee et al. 2009; Vakulenko et al. 2018). In this study,
service assurance is conceptualized not as employee-driven interaction, but as users ’ perception of system reliability,
problem resolution capability, and overall trust in the service process (Brady and Cronin 2001; Meuter et al. 2000;
Parasuraman et al. 1988). This distinction is particularly important in SST environments, where assurance is derived
from both the system and the surrounding service context rather than direct interpersonal interaction.
Similarly, environmental cues, based on Bitner ’s( 1992) servicescape framework, refer to users ’ perceptions of
physical and design-related elements such as layout, aesthetics, and ambient conditions of the service setting. While
environmental cues capture the sensory and contextual aspects of the service environment, service assurance re ﬂects
users’ cognitive evaluation of reliability and trustworthiness, highlighting their distinct but complementary roles in
shaping user experience. Recent SST research (Nam et al. 2023; Youssef et al. 2025) shows that these experiential
dimensions signi ﬁcantly in ﬂuence users ’ comfort, trust, and willingness to reuse kiosks.
These ﬁndings suggest that post-adoption evaluation in SST contexts is formed through the combined in ﬂuence of
functional reliability and environmental interpretation, rather than through purely cognitive assessments emphasized
in traditional TCT. Building on this perspective, the present study incorporates service assurance and environmental
cues as key antecedents of post-adoption evaluation, rather than positioning them as direct extensions of the original
TCT structure.
The post-adoption evaluation construct in this study represents an overall assessment of users ’ experience with
SSTs. Although TCT traditionally distinguishes satisfaction and continuance intention as separate constructs, prior
research in technology-mediated service contexts suggests that users often form a holistic evaluation that integrates

Furthermore, this study introduces technology expertise as a moderating factor. Technology expertise refers to an
individual’s perceived ability to effectively use technological systems (Compeau and Higgins 1995; Kim and Gupta

integrates functional reliability, environmental context, and individual capability, offering a contextual application of
TCT that re ﬂects the unique characteristics of technology-mediated service environments.
Hypotheses Development
Service assurance and post-adoption evaluation
Service assurance refers to customers ’ perceptions of a service provider ’s reliability, responsiveness, and competence
in delivering technology-based services (Brady and Cronin 2001; Cronin and Taylor 1992; Meuter et al. 2000;
Parasuraman et al. 1988). Rooted in the SERVQUAL framework (Parasuraman et al. 1988), assurance re ﬂects users’
conﬁdence in the service provider ’s knowledge and problem-solving ability, while responsiveness emphasizes
dependable support. Later studies viewed service quality hierarchically, positioning interaction quality —employees’
behavior and expertise —as central to user evaluation (Brady and Cronin 2001).
In SST and SSK contexts, however, service assurance should be understood beyond employee-driven interaction
and instead as users ’ perception of system reliability, problem resolution capability, and overall trust in the service
process. Even in technology-mediated environments, users continue to seek assurance that the system will function
reliably and that issues can be effectively addressed when they arise. Meuter et al. ( 2000) found that perceived
support and problem-solving capacity strongly in ﬂuence satisfaction, while Zeithaml et al. ( 1996) showed that
assurance and responsiveness drive behavioral intentions. Recent ﬁndings reaf ﬁrm these effects in smart environ-
ments: smart assurance —the perceived reliability and security of automated systems —signiﬁcantly enhances user
comfort and satisfaction (Wong et al. 2022; Youssef et al. 2025). Lee ( 2023a) also demonstrated that perceived
reliability in SST use strengthens overall satisfaction and continuance intention.
From the perspective of TCT, perceived reliability can be viewed as part of users ’ post-use evaluation process,
through which they assess whether the technology continues to meet their expectations. Rather than directly
extending the original TCT structure, this study adopts its underlying logic to explain how reliability-related per-
ceptions contribute to users ’ overall evaluation of SST experiences. Such evaluations enhance users ’ conﬁdence in
the service and reinforce positive judgments regarding continued use.
This study conceptualizes environmental cues as users ’ perceived interpretation of the service environment rather
than as purely objective physical attributes. While traditional servicescape research emphasizes that physical
environments serve as signals from which users infer service quality, perception formation in technology-mediated
contexts may also involve a top-down interpretive process. That is, users ’ prior judgments about system reliability
and trustworthiness can shape how they interpret and evaluate environmental conditions. In SST settings, where
direct human interaction is limited and system performance is central, users who perceive the service as reliable may
interpret the surrounding environment more positively, even when physical attributes are held constant. Accordingly,
service assurance is expected to in ﬂuence not only users ’ overall post-adoption evaluation but also their perception of
environmental cues in SST settings. Therefore, the following hypotheses are proposed:

Environmental cues and post-adoption evaluation
Environmental cues refer to the physical and atmospheric elements of a service setting that shape users ’ sensory and
emotional responses during service encounters (Baker et al. 1994; Bitner 1992; Lin and Hsieh 2011). Originating
from Bitner ’s( 1992) servicescape framework, this concept explains how ambient conditions, spatial layout, and
design symbols in ﬂuence both cognitive and affective evaluations. A clean, modern, and aesthetically pleasing
environment communicates quality and professionalism, thereby enhancing perceived value and satisfaction (Turley
and Milliman 2000). Baker et al. ( 1994) demonstrated that environmental design and cleanliness affect customers ’
quality inferences and store image, while Wake ﬁeld and Blodgett ( 1996) con ﬁrmed that atmospheric appeal fosters
satisfaction and behavioral intentions in service settings.
In SST and SSK contexts, environmental cues become particularly salient because users rely more heavily on
physical and design-related signals in the absence of direct interpersonal interaction. Lin and Hsieh ( 2011) validated
this through the SSTQUAL scale, identifying the physical environment dimension as a strong predictor of satisfaction
and behavioral intention. More recent studies reinforce this view in smart service environments. Youssef et al. ( 2025)
revealed that smart servicescape elements —such as aesthetics, physical functionality, and perceived security —
signiﬁcantly enhance customer inspiration, satisfaction, and loyalty. Similarly, Wong et al. ( 2022) emphasized that
smart service quality depends on the synergy between physical design and interface presentation, highlighting that a
well-designed servicescape elevates users ’ conﬁdence and immersion. Nam et al. ( 2023) further observed that
environmental appeal and convenience in SSKs enhance satisfaction, particularly among users with higher tech-
nology readiness and digital literacy.
While service assurance re ﬂects users ’ cognitive evaluation of reliability and trustworthiness, environmental cues
capture the sensory and contextual aspects of the service environment, underscoring their distinct yet complementary
roles in shaping user experience. From a TCT perspective, environmental cues can be interpreted as part of users ’
experiential evaluation process, through which they form judgments about whether the service environment supports
or enhances their technology use experience. Rather than directly extending the original TCT structure, this study
adopts its underlying logic to explain how environmental perceptions contribute to overall post-adoption evaluation.
Favorable environments thereby reinforce users ’ positive evaluations of SST experiences. Furthermore, environ-
mental cues are expected to function as an intervening mechanism through which service assurance in ﬂuences post-
adoption evaluation. When users perceive a service as reliable and trustworthy, such perceptions are often re ﬂected in
well-maintained, organized, and technologically advanced environments, which in turn enhance their overall eva-
luation of the service experience (Stead et al. 2021). Thus, environmental cues are expected to both directly enhance
users’ post-adoption evaluations and mediate the in ﬂuence of service assurance within the proposed framework.
Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:
H3: Environmental cues positively in ﬂuence users ’ post-adoption evaluation.
H4: Environmental cues mediate the relationship between service assurance and post-adoption evaluation.
Moderating role of technology expertise
Technology expertise refers to an individual ’s perceived knowledge and capability to effectively understand and use
technological systems (Compeau and Higgins 1995; Kim and Gupta 2009; Thatcher and Perrewé 2002). Prior studies
conceptualize it as a personal capability encompassing users ’ familiarity, knowledge, and experience with technol-
ogy, which in turn shapes perceptions of usefulness and ease of use (Shih 2006; Thatcher et al. 2006; Venkatesh et al.
2003).
In this study, technology expertise is operationalized using a self-reported item capturing whether others seek the
respondent’s guidance in understanding new technologies. As such, the construct re ﬂects socially recognized

technological competence rather than objective technical skill or general con ﬁdence. Conceptually, it is closely
related to technology readiness and personal innovativeness, re ﬂecting individuals ’ conﬁdence and enthusiasm
toward engaging with new technologies (Agarwal and Prasad 1998; Parasuraman 2000; Vakulenko et al. 2019).
However, unlike technology readiness, which represents a general attitudinal predisposition toward technology, and
personal innovativeness, which re ﬂects a tendency to adopt new technologies, the present measure captures a form of
socially validated expertise based on others ’ recognition of one ’s ability to understand and explain technological
systems.
In SST/SSK settings, technology expertise in ﬂuences how users interpret and evaluate service experiences rather
than uniformly strengthening all relationships among service attributes. Higher expertise reduces perceived barriers
and enables users to process system-related information more effectively (Galdolage 2021a, 2021b; Joshi and Joshi
2020), whereas low-expertise users tend to rely more heavily on contextual cues such as service assurance and the
servicescape when forming judgments. Recent work shows that technology-oriented or digitally literate users respond
more positively to smart service quality and well-designed servicescapes, with stronger satisfaction and reuse
intentions (Guan et al. 2021; Lee 2023a, 2023b; Nam et al. 2023; Stead et al. 2021; Wong et al. 2022; Youssef et al.
2025).
From a TCT perspective, technology expertise can be understood as a factor that conditions how users evaluate
post-adoption experiences, rather than as a direct extension of the original theoretical structure. Users with higher
expertise are more likely to integrate multiple evaluative cues —such as service reliability and environmental quality
—into a coherent assessment, whereas users with lower expertise may rely more selectively on salient cues to reduce
uncertainty. This suggests that technology expertise functions as a socially grounded indicator of users ’ ability to
interpret and communicate technological information, which in turn shapes how they process and prioritize service-
related cues in SST environments.
Accordingly, the moderating role of technology expertise is conceptualized as shaping the relative in ﬂuence of
different service-related factors, rather than uniformly amplifying all relationships. First, users with higher expertise
are more capable of interpreting how reliable and competent service delivery relates to the surrounding service
environment. Second, users with lower expertise may depend more strongly on service assurance when forming post-
adoption evaluations, as reliability reduces uncertainty in technology use. Finally, the in ﬂuence of environmental cues
on post-adoption evaluation may vary depending on users ’ ability to interpret and appreciate design, functionality,
and symbolic aspects of the service environment. Therefore, the following hypotheses are proposed:
H5: Technology expertise moderates the relationship between service assurance and environmental cues.
H6: Technology expertise moderates the relationship between service assurance and post-adoption evaluation.
H7: Technology expertise moderates the relationship between environmental cues and post-adoption evaluation.

Data collection
Data for this study were collected through a paper-based survey administered in June 2023 targeting Korean
consumers with prior experience using SSTs such as SSKs in restaurants, banks, and airports. While the speci ﬁc
usage context was not controlled as a focal variable in this study, these environments share common characteristics as
technology-mediated service interfaces. Therefore, the analysis focuses on generalizable aspects of SST/SSK
experiences rather than context-speci ﬁc differences.
Participants were recruited through convenience sampling, focusing on adults who had interacted with SST/SSK
systems within the previous six months. The survey aimed to capture users ’ perceptions of service assurance,
environmental cues, post-adoption evaluations, and technology expertise, as well as basic demographic information.
Data were collected anonymously, and participation was voluntary. In total, 218 valid responses were obtained and
used for analysis after screening for missing or incomplete responses. This sample size satis ﬁes the recommended
minimum criteria for structural equation modeling (SEM) (Hair et al. 2018), ensuring adequate statistical power for
the hypothesized model.

Implications
Theoretical Implications
This study provides several insights into the growing body of literature on SST and SSK by building on the logic of
the theory of technology continuance (Liao et al. 2009) and applying it to a service quality –based experiential
framework. Whereas prior studies have primarily emphasized users ’ perceptions of usefulness, ease of use, and
satisfaction as predictors of continuance intention (Foroughi et al. 2023; Rahi et al. 2021), the present study highlights
service assurance and environmental cues as key experiential factors in ﬂuencing users’ post-adoption evaluations in
SST environments. In this respect, the ﬁndings suggest that post-adoption evaluation in SST contexts is shaped not
only by cognitive appraisal but also by experiential and contextual factors.
Second, this study provides empirical support for the mediating role of environmental cues in the relationship
between service assurance and post-adoption evaluation. Although previous research has acknowledged the in ﬂuence
of servicescape and atmospheric design on customer perceptions and behavioral intentions (Baker et al. 1994; Bitner
1992; Lin and Hsieh 2011; Youssef et al. 2025), limited attention has been given to how such environmental
attributes interact with service reliability and assurance in technology-mediated contexts. The results indicate that
environmental cues function as an experiential mechanism through which perceived service reliability is re ﬂected in
users’ overall evaluations, complementing recent discussions emphasizing the integration of physical design and
atmospherics in service environments (Stead et al. 2021; Wong et al. 2022).
Third, this study examines the moderating role of technology expertise to advance the theoretical understanding of
user heterogeneity in technology evaluation. While studies on technology readiness (Parasuraman 2000) and personal
innovativeness (Agarwal and Prasad 1998) have examined individual differences in technology adoption, empirical
exploration of how users ’ technological capability shapes experiential evaluation remains limited. Although the
interaction-based analysis did not provide strong support for a moderating effect, the ﬁndings suggest that users with
different levels of technology expertise may interpret and prioritize service-related cues differently. This pattern may

which tend to be interpreted consistently across users regardless of their level of technology expertise. In contrast,

Finally, by incorporating multidimensional constructs such as service assurance, environmental cues, and tech-

environments and user characteristics jointly shape post-adoption outcomes in digital service ecosystems. Rather than
proposing a full theoretical extension, this study offers a contextually grounded application of TCT that connects
service quality theory, servicescape research, and technology acceptance literature, providing a more context-speci ﬁc
understanding of user evaluation in SST/SSK environments.
Practical Implications
The ﬁndings of this study provide several practical implications for service managers and practitioners implementing
SSTs and SSKs in hospitality, retail, and transportation sectors. First, beyond system functionality, users ’ evaluations
are shaped by their perception of service reliability and their ability to obtain support when needed. Accordingly,
managers should design SST systems that ensure prompt and consistent service recovery when technical errors occur.
As emphasized by Collier et al. ( 2017) and Meuter et al. ( 2000), users expect a swift and effective resolution even in
self-service settings, where human contact is minimized. Providing multiple support channels —such as embedded
help functions, on-site assistance, and remote support —can reduce perceived uncertainty and strengthen trust in the
service process.
Second, environmental cues such as spatial design, cleanliness, and aesthetic appeal were found to mediate users ’
post-adoption evaluations. This ﬁnding implies that users do not evaluate SSTs based on functionality alone, but
rather interpret service quality through the surrounding physical and digital environment. The servicescape therefore
remains a critical touchpoint for user satisfaction (Bitner 1992; Youssef et al. 2025; Wong et al. 2022). In practice,
this suggests that investments in interface design, visual clarity, ambient conditions, and maintenance are not merely
cosmetic but directly related to how users form evaluative judgments. Service providers should therefore enhance
both physical and digital atmospherics —through intuitive kiosk interfaces, clear navigation structures, and well-
maintained environments —to communicate professionalism and technological competence. For example, differ-
entiated kiosk design may include adaptive interface layouts that adjust font size, button complexity, or menu depth
depending on user interaction patterns. Kiosks in high-traf ﬁc environments such as airports may prioritize speed and
minimal steps, whereas kiosks in retail or hospitality settings may incorporate richer visual elements and recom-
mendation features to enhance user engagement.
Third, this study indicates that users ’ technology expertise inﬂuences how they interpret service and environmental
cues. Rather than uniformly enhancing user responses, technology expertise appears to shape which cues users rely
on when forming evaluations. For technologically con ﬁdent users, the design and ambiance of the service setting may
play a more salient role, whereas low-expertise users may depend more on service assurance to reduce uncertainty.
This suggests that a “one-size-ﬁts-all” service design may be insuf ﬁcient in SST environments. Managers may
therefore consider segmenting users based on their technology pro ﬁciency and tailoring their support strategies:
offering simpli ﬁed tutorials, guided interfaces, or hybrid service options for low-expertise users, while enabling
greater autonomy and ef ﬁciency for high-expertise users. Such differentiated approaches can enhance both inclusivity
and user retention (Foroughi et al. 2023; Nam et al. 2023). In this regard, interface personalization can play a critical
role. For instance, systems may provide step-by-step guidance, visual cues, or simpli ﬁed workﬂows for ﬁrst-time or
low-expertise users, while offering shortcut options, saved preferences, or faster navigation paths for experienced

users. Such adaptive interfaces can reduce cognitive load for novice users while maintaining ef ﬁciency for

Finally, the ﬁndings suggest that continuous monitoring of user feedback on SST experiences can support adaptive
improvement of smart service environments. Because users ’ evaluations are shaped by both functional performance
and experiential interpretation, real-time feedback systems can help identify not only technical failures but also
experiential friction points. Integrating real-time analytics or AI-driven feedback systems would allow service pro-
viders to detect issues in user interaction and maintain high-quality experiences over time (Singh and Yadav 2025;
Stead et al. 2021). AI-driven adaptive kiosks can dynamically adjust service environments based on user behavior.
For example, machine learning algorithms can detect hesitation, repeated input errors, or prolonged interaction times
and respond by offering assistance prompts, simplifying the interface, or recommending alternative options. Such
adaptive systems enable real-time personalization of the service experience and enhance both usability and
satisfaction.
Overall, these insights emphasize that the success of SST/SSK adoption depends not only on technological
sophistication but also on how effectively service environments and user characteristics are aligned. Organizations
that integrate reliability, environmental design, and user capability considerations are more likely to sustain
engagement and long-term usage in technology-mediated service settings.
Conclusion
This study builds on the theory of technology continuance by examining how service assurance, environmental cues,
and technology expertise are associated with users ’ post-adoption evaluation of SSTs and SSKs. The ﬁndings indicate
that service assurance and environmental cues play important roles in shaping post-adoption evaluations, with
environmental cues acting as a mechanism through which perceived reliability is re ﬂected in users ’ overall assess-
ments. This suggests that users do not evaluate SSTs solely based on functional performance, but rather form
judgments through a combination of reliability-based perceptions and contextual interpretation of the service
environment. While the moderating effects of technology expertise were not consistently supported in the interaction-
based analysis, the results suggest that users with different levels of technological capability may interpret and
prioritize experiential cues differently. In particular, the ﬁndings imply that user capability in ﬂuences how service-
related information is processed and integrated, rather than uniformly strengthening all relationships within
the model.
Theoretically, this research contributes to SST literature by connecting service quality, servicescape, and tech-
nology continuance perspectives. It highlights the experiential dimension of post-adoption behavior and underscores
the importance of individual technological capability in shaping users ’ evaluation processes. By positioning envir-
onmental cues as an interpretive mechanism and technology expertise as a cognitive conditioning factor, this study
provides a more nuanced understanding of how users construct post-adoption evaluations in technology-mediated
service environments. Rather than proposing a formal extension of TCT, this study provides a contextual application
that re ﬂects the experiential and environmental characteristics of technology-mediated service settings.
Practically, the results suggest that organizations should strengthen reliability and user con ﬁdence through robust
service assurance mechanisms, while simultaneously enhancing the physical and digital environment to improve the
overall service experience. This indicates that effective SST management requires not only technical reliability but
also careful design of the user environment to support positive interpretation and engagement. Segmenting users
based on technological pro ﬁciency and providing differentiated levels of support can foster inclusivity and sustain
engagement in diverse user groups. Such an approach enables ﬁrms to accommodate varying levels of user capability,
thereby improving both user satisfaction and long-term technology adoption.

Limitations
Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations.
First, the data were collected from a single-country context, which may limit the generalizability of the ﬁndings
across different cultural or technological environments. In particular, given that South Korea represents a techno-
logically advanced environment with high levels of digital infrastructure and user familiarity, the distribution of
technology expertise in this sample may be skewed toward higher levels. This reduced variance may attenuate the
observable strength of moderating effects and limit the interpretability of group differences. Future research could
address this limitation by employing cross-cultural designs with more heterogeneous samples to better examine how
contextual differences in ﬂuence the role of technology expertise.
Second, the study relies on self-reported measures, which may introduce common method bias and subjective
evaluation errors. In particular, respondents ’ perceptions of service quality and post-adoption evaluation may be
inﬂuenced by individual response tendencies rather than actual usage behavior. Future studies may incorporate
objective behavioral data, system usage logs, or multi-source data collection approaches to reduce potential bias and
enhance measurement validity.
Third, the use of a single-item measure for technology expertise may constrain the reliability and depth of the
construct. Although the item captures socially recognized technological competence, it may not fully re ﬂect the
multidimensional nature of users ’ technological capability. Future research could adopt multi-item scales or develop
more comprehensive measurement approaches that capture different dimensions of expertise, such as knowledge,
experience, and problem-solving ability.
Fourth, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to capture dynamic changes in users ’ post-adoption evaluation
over time. As users gain experience with SSTs, their perceptions of service quality and environmental cues may
evolve, potentially altering the relationships observed in this study. Future research could employ longitudinal
designs to examine how user evaluations and expertise develop through repeated interactions with technology. In
addition, the use of a single composite construct for post-adoption evaluation may obscure potential differences
between satisfaction and continuance intention. While this approach captures an overall evaluative judgment, it may
limit the ability to identify more nuanced patterns in user behavior. Future studies may separate these constructs to
provide a more ﬁne-grained understanding of post-adoption processes and their distinct drivers.
Fifth, the study did not differentiate between speci ﬁc types of kiosk environments (e.g., retail, food service,
transportation), which may involve varying environmental characteristics and user expectations. As a result, the
inﬂuence of environmental cues may differ across contexts in ways not captured in the present model. Future research
could examine context-speci ﬁc differences in environmental cues and compare their effects across different service
settings.
Finally, the study focuses on a speci ﬁc SST context (SSKs), and the ﬁndings may not fully generalize to other
forms of technology-mediated services such as mobile applications or AI-based service systems. Different interaction
modalities and user interfaces may lead to different evaluative processes. Future research could extend the model to
other technology contexts to assess its applicability and identify potential boundary conditions.
