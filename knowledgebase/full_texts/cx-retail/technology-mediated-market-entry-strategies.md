Abstract
This study examines how technology-mediated market entry strategies contribute to competitive
advantage among small and medium enterprises (SMEs) in Sub-Saharan Africa. Drawing on the
Resource-Based View and the Dynamic Capabilities Framework, the research employs a
quantitative cross-sectional survey design with 412 SME owners and managers drawn from
Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, and Cameroon, stratified across three sectors: retail and trade,
manufacturing, and services and ICT. Data were collected via a structured questionnaire and
analysed using multiple regression, one -way ANOVA with Tukey HSD post -hoc tests, and
moderated regression analysis. Three principal findings emerge. First, digital technology
adoption and technology-mediated market entry strategies jointly explain 56.0% of the variance
in competitive advantage (F(2,409) = 260.5, p < .001), confirming that technology is a significant
enabler of SME competitiveness in the region. Second, significant cross -sectoral differences in
digital adoption exist (F(2,409) = 18.74, p < .00 1, eta² = .084), with services and ICT firms
demonstrating substantially higher adoption than retail or manufacturing SMEs. Third,
institutional and infrastructural barriers significantly moderate the relationship between digital
strategy adoption and comp etitive advantage (Delta R² = .026), attenuating the benefits of
digital investment in environments with weak regulatory and connectivity infrastructure. Social
media marketing (87.6%), mobile payment platforms (80.1%), and digital advertising (70.1%) are
the most widely deployed tools, while AI -assisted analytics remain nascent at 27.9% adoption
across the sample. The findings contribute to the growing body of literature on digital
transformation in emerging markets and carry practical implications for SME  owners,
policymakers, and development finance institutions operating in Sub-Saharan Africa.
Keywords: SME Digital Transformation, Market Entry Strategy, Competitive Advantage, Sub -
Saharan Africa, Dynamic Capabilities, Institutional Barriers.

Introduction
The relationship between digital technology adoption and firm performance has emerged
as one of the central preoccupations of contemporary management research (Bharadwaj
et al., 2013; Teece, 2018). Within the global economy, digital tools have  fundamentally
altered how firms identify markets, reach customers, manage transactions, and sustain
competitive positions. This transformation is neither geographically uniform nor sectorally
consistent. In Sub -Saharan Africa, a region characterised by ra pidly expanding mobile
penetration, a growing youth population, and significant structural constraints on
traditional business infrastructure, small and medium enterprises (SMEs) occupy a peculiar

vantage point. They are, simultaneously, the primary engine s of employment and GDP
contribution in most economies in the region (IFC, 2021; World Bank, 2022) and the
category of firm most exposed to the barriers, financial, institutional, and infrastructural,
that limit technology adoption (Asongu, 2023; Nwankpa et al, 2022).
Sub-Saharan Africa's digital landscape has undergone rapid transformation over the past
decade. The GSMA (2023) reports that mobile internet penetration in Sub -Saharan Africa
reached 26% in 2022, with smartphone connections growing at an annual rate of 8.5%, and
mobile money accounts surpassing 763 million by year end. Kenya's M -PESA ecosystem,
Nigeria's fintech sector, and Ghana's growing e -commerce platforms have each
demonstrated that digital infrastructure, even where incomplete, can cataly se new
commercial behaviours at scale. Asongu et al., (2021)) observed that technology
investment in developing economies does not straightforwardly replicate outcomes seen
in more infrastructure-rich environments, a finding that has been substantiated and refined
by subsequent scholars examining African digital markets specifically ( Adomako et al.,
2024).
Market entry strategy, defined broadly as the set of decisions a firm makes in selecting and
penetrating a new market, has traditionally been theorised around internationalisation
modes such as exporting, licensing, joint ventures, and foreign direct investment (Root,
1994; Anderson & Gatignon, 1986). These frameworks largely assume large-firm resources
and established institutional environments. The real ity of SME market entry in Sub -
Saharan Africa departs markedly from these assumptions. Digital tools, particularly social
media platforms, mobile payment systems, and e -commerce marketplaces, have enabled
SMEs to enter new markets without the capital expen diture historically associated with
physical market presence. This phenomenon, broadly termed digital market entry, remains
under-theorised in the context of African SMEs, with most existing scholarship either
focused on developed -economy firms (Yamin & Si nkovics, 2006; Luo & Tung, 2007) or
treating African SMEs as a residual category in continent-wide digital development studies.
This study addresses that gap directly. By examining how 412 SMEs across four Sub -
Saharan African countries deploy digital tools in market entry and how those deployments
translate to competitive advantage under varying institutional conditions, the research
offers both empirical evidence and theoretical contribution to a field where the demand for
context-specific knowledge is acute (George et al., 2016; Nkamnebe, 2011).

Problem Statement
Despite the well -documented growth of digital infrastructure across Sub -Saharan Africa,
the precise mechanisms through which SMEs leverage digital technology to achieve market
entry and sustain competitive advantage remain inadequately understood in the scholarly
literature (Asongu, 2023; Amadasun & Mutezo  2022). Existing market entry frameworks,
developed largely in the context of Western multinational enterprises, do not adequately
account for the resource constraints, institutional volatility, and infrastructure deficiencies

2008). This theoretical misalignment generates a knowledge gap with direct practica l
consequences: SME owners, policymakers, and development finance institutions lack
evidence-based guidance on which digital strategies generate competitive returns under
which institutional conditions.
A further problem is that research on African SME dig italisation has tended to treat the
continent as a homogeneous unit (Asongu et al., 2021), obscuring significant cross-country
and cross -sector variation in digital maturity, regulatory environment, and consumer
behaviour. Kenya's advanced mobile financial  ecosystem differs materially from
Cameroon's nascent digital economy; Nigerian retail SMEs operate under different
constraints than Kenyan ICT services firms. The failure to account for this heterogeneity
limits the actionability of generalised findings. Additionally, the moderating role of
institutional quality, encompassing regulatory frameworks, access to finance, and digital
infrastructure, on the technology -advantage relationship has received limited empirical
attention in the African SME context, des pite its theoretical centrality in the institutional
economics literature (North, 1990; Acemoglu et al., 2001).
Taken together, these gaps define a researchable problem: the conditions under which
technology-mediated market entry strategies generate sustainable competitive advantage
for SMEs in Sub -Saharan Africa are not well established, and existing theoretical
frameworks require extension to account for the institutional and infrastructural realities of
the regional context.

Research Objectives
The over arching aim of this study is to examine the relationship between technology -
mediated market entry strategies and competitive advantage among SMEs in Sub-Saharan
Africa. The specific objectives are:
1. To identify the digital technologies most commonly deploye d by SMEs in Sub -
Saharan Africa for market entry purposes.
2. To examine how technology -mediated market entry strategies influence
competitive advantage among SMEs in the region.
3. To assess the extent to which institutional and infrastructural barriers moderate the
relationship between digital strategy adoption and competitive advantage.
4. To compare technology -mediated market entry approaches across sectors and
country contexts within Sub-Saharan Africa.

Research Questions
Derived from the foregoing objectives, the following research questions guide this inquiry:
1. What digital technologies do SMEs in Sub -Saharan Africa primarily deploy when
entering new markets?
2. To what extent do technology -mediated market entry strategies contribute to
competitive advantage for SMEs in Sub-Saharan Africa?

Research Hypotheses
Based on the research objectives, literature review, and theoretical framework, the
following hypotheses are advanced:

Significance of the Study
This study contributes to scholarship and practice across several dimensions. Academically,
it extends the Resource -Based View and Dynamic Capabilities Framework into a context,
Sub-Saharan African SME digital market entry, where their application has been limited
and largely untested against empirical data from the region. By introducing institutio nal
quality as a formal moderator, the study also bridges micro -level firm strategy research
with macro-level institutional economics in a way that the SME digitisation literature has
not consistently done. The cross -country, cross -sector design further ad dresses the
homogeneity problem identified in earlier scholarship (Asongu et al., 2021; George et al.,
2016).
For practitioners, the findings offer actionable guidance to SME owners on which digital
tools deliver the greatest competitive returns across dif ferent sectors and institutional
environments. For policymakers and development finance institutions, particularly the
African Development Bank, the World Bank's SME Finance Forum, and national investment
promotion agencies, the evidence on how institution al barriers attenuate the technology-
advantage relationship provides a concrete rationale for targeted infrastructure and
regulatory reform. For researchers, the validated measurement scales and the four-country
comparative design provide a replicable fram ework for future longitudinal and qualitative
follow-on studies.

Literature Review and Theoretical Framework
Theoretical Framework
The Resource-Based View
The Resource-Based View (RBV), developed principally by Barney (1991) and rooted in the
earlier work of Penrose (1959), holds that sustained competitive advantage derives from
resources that are valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable, and  non-substitutable, collectively
referred to as the VRIN criteria. Digital technology, in the form of proprietary data, platform
relationships, algorithmic capabilities, and digital brand equity, has increasingly been
theorised as a VRIN resource, particul arly in contexts where adoption is uneven and
capabilities therefore heterogeneous across firms (Bharadwaj, 2000; Wade & Hulland,
2004). For SMEs in Sub-Saharan Africa, the RBV offers a compelling lens: in markets where
digital infrastructure is expanding but penetration remains incomplete, early and
sophisticated adopters of digital tools may develop capabilities that latecomers cannot
easily replicate, at least within a meaningful competitive time horizon.
Barney and Hesterly (2012) later extended the RBV  to encompass dynamic resource
reconfiguration, acknowledging that the competitive value of resources erodes over time
as markets evolve and imitation occurs. This extension is particularly pertinent to the
African SME context, where the pace of digital diffusion means that first-mover advantages
in specific tools, such as mobile commerce or social media marketing, may narrow rapidly,
requiring firms to continuously upgrade their digital capabilities. Wernerfelt (1984), who
first formalised the resource -based perspective, drew attention to the importance of
resource accumulation paths, a concept that resonates with findings on how SMEs in
emerging markets build digital capabilities incrementally rather than through large capital
investments (Zahra & George, 2002).

The Dynamic Capabilities Framework
Teece, Pisano, and Shuen (1997) advanced the concept of dynamic capabilities as higher -
order routines by which firms sense opportunities in the external environment, seize those
opportunities by mobilising resourc es, and reconfigure their asset base in response to
changing competitive conditions. This framework directly addresses a limitation of the

static RBV: it explains not just what resources a firm has, but how it deploys and renews
them in turbulent environme nts. Teece (2007) subsequently refined the framework into
three clusters of microfoundations: sensing, seizing, and transforming, a taxonomy that
maps cleanly onto the stages of digital market entry.
Applied to SME digital market entry in Sub -Saharan Afric a, the dynamic capabilities
framework suggests that the ability to identify emerging digital platforms (sensing), deploy
appropriate tools and business models (seizing), and adapt those deployments as platforms
evolve or competitive conditions shift (trans forming) is what separates SMEs that sustain
competitive advantage from those that achieve only temporary gains. Zahra and George
(2002) linked dynamic capabilities to absorptive capacity, specifically the ability of firms to
acquire, assimilate, transform , and exploit external knowledge. For African SMEs with
limited formal R&D investment, absorptive capacity built through practical digital
experimentation may be the dominant mechanism through which dynamic capabilities are
developed.
The integration of RBV and dynamic capabilities as a dual theoretical foundation is
consistent with recent scholarship in the SME internationalisation literature
(Weerawardena et al., 2007; Meyer  et al., 2023) and provides the conceptual architecture
for the conceptual model presented in Section 2.3.

Empirical Review
Digital Tools and SME Market Access in Sub-Saharan Africa
The empirical literature on SME digitalisation in Sub-Saharan Africa has grown substantially
since the mid-2010s, driven by the expansion of mobile internet and fintech infrastructure.
Asongu et al. (2021) conducted a panel data analysis across 48 African countries and found
a positive and significant relationship between financial development, proxied in part by
mobile money penetration, an d consumer market expansion, with effects more
pronounced in countries with lower baseline institutional quality. While this study focused
on the macro level, its finding that digital financial tools disproportionately benefit firms
and consumers in weaker institutional environments has direct implications for SME digital
strategy.
The role of social media platforms in SME market entry has attracted particular attention.
Oyewobi et al. (2025), in a study of 243 SMEs in Malaysia that has since been widely
replicated in African contexts, demonstrated that Facebook usage had significant positive
effects on non -financial performance dimensions, including market reach and customer
relationships, but weaker effects on financial performance, a finding consistent wi th the
expectation that digital marketing tools build market presence before they translate to
revenue. Sampson & Annor, (2025) extended this line of research to West Africa, examining
e-commerce adoption among 2,000 online consumers in Nigeria and Cameroo n, and
finding that digital technology adoption was most strongly associated with spending
behaviour change among younger urban consumers. While their study examined consumer

Market Entry Strategy and Emerging Market SMEs
Traditional market entry theory, rooted in transaction cost economics (Williamson, 1985)
and the eclectic OLI paradigm (Dunni ng, 1993), was developed to explain multinational
enterprise internationalisation decisions and presupposes resources, particularly capital
and managerial capacity, that most SMEs in Sub -Saharan Africa do not possess. Scholars
including Peng et al. (2008) and Khanna and Palepu (2010) have argued that this
institutional void argument is central to understanding firm strategy in emerging markets:
where formal institutional frameworks are weak or absent, firms develop alternative
mechanisms for market coordination, many of which are digital in nature.
Luo and Tung (2007) introduced the concept of the springboard strategy to describe how
emerging market firms use internationalisation to overcome domestic institutional
constraints. While applied to large-firm international expansion, the springboard logic has
analogues in the domestic context: African SMEs that use digital platforms to bypass
physical distribution barriers, informal trade barriers, and credit market constraints are
pursuing a structurally similar strategy at the domestic or regional level. Adomako et al.
(2024) examined this dynamic directly in Nigeria, finding that SMEs using digital platforms
to enter Lagos's consumer market achieved faster time -to-market and lower entry costs
than those relying on traditional distribution networks, though with higher failure rates in
the first twelve months, suggesting that digital market entry carries real but different risk
profiles than conventional modes.
Nkamnebe (2011) made an early and still largely unaddressed call for African marketing
scholars to develop contextually appropriate frameworks that move beyond Western -
derived models. His critique specifically targeted the assumption, embedded in standard
market entry theory, that institutional and infr astructural resources can be treated as
exogenous constants. In Sub -Saharan Africa, institutional quality is itself a strategic
variable, one that firms must actively navigate, and that digital technologies can, in some
cases, partially substitute for. This insight is central to the moderation hypothesis tested in
this study.

Competitive Advantage in Resource-Constrained Environments
Porter's (1985) classic typology of competitive strategies, cost leadership, differentiation,
and focus, remains influential but has been critiqued for its assumption that strategic choice
occurs against a backdrop of relatively stable industry structures and accessible
information. In Sub -Saharan African markets, information asymmetries are pronounced,
industry structures are often informal, and the boundary between market entry and
competitive advantage is blurred because firms frequently create the market they enter
rather than compete within pre-existing ones (Prahalad, 2006). Digital tools collapse some

of these information  asymmetries by making price discovery, supplier identification, and
consumer behaviour data accessible to SMEs at negligible marginal cost.
Schoeman & Seymour (2022) examined IT adoption among SMEs in South Africa and found
that firms that adopted IT strategically, as a means of improving market reach and reducing
transaction costs rather than as a compliance or administrative tool, reported stronger
competitive outcomes. Similar patterns have been documented for SMEs by Saah (2022),
who found that strateg ic digital investment was more predictive of competitive
performance than total IT expenditure, reinforcing the dynamic capabilities argument that
deployment logic matters more than investment volume. George et al. (2016) synthesised
this and related evide nce in their call for management research that takes the African
context seriously as a site of theoretical generation rather than application, a framing that
this study adopts explicitly.

Barriers to Digital Adoption Among African SMEs
The literature consistently identifies several categories of barrier to SME digital adoption in
Sub-Saharan Africa. Access to finance is the most cited: IFC (2021) estimates that the SME
finance gap in Sub -Saharan Africa exceeds USD 330 billion, and the capital required for
digital infrastructure investment, including devices, connectivity, software subscriptions,
and staff training, is non -trivial relative to SME balance sheets. Nwankpa et al (2022) )
demonstrated that access to credit is a significant predictor of SME innovation adoption in
Ghana, and this finding has been replicated in Nigerian (Asongu, 2023) and East African
contexts (Saah, 2022).
Infrastructure quality constitutes a second major barrier. Erratic electricity supply, limited
and expensive broadband connect ivity, and low device penetration outside major urban
centres all constrain the deployment and reliability of digital tools (GSMA, 2023; McKinsey
& Company, 2021). The GSMA (2023) estimates that coverage gaps and affordability
constraints leave approximately 900 million people in Sub-Saharan Africa without access to
mobile internet despite living in areas with existing coverage. For SMEs dependent on
digital platforms for market entry, connectivity unreliability introduces operational risks
that partially offset the efficiency gains of digital adoption.
Regulatory and institutional barriers form a third category. Varying e -commerce
regulations, digital payment licensing requirements, and data protection frameworks
across countries in the region create compliance costs that disproportionately burden SMEs
relative to larger firms (Amadasun & Mutezo, 2022)). Acemoglu et al. (2001) established the
foundational empirical case for the long-run importance of institutional quality to economic
performance, a relations hip that later scholars have demonstrated is particularly binding
for firm-level digital investment in developing economies (Peng et al., 2008; North, 1990).

Cross-Country and Cross-Sector Variation in SME Digitalisation
Kenya represents the most frequent ly cited case of advanced SME digitalisation in Sub -
Saharan Africa, anchored by the M-PESA mobile money ecosystem that by 2022 processed
transactions equivalent to approximately 50% of the country's GDP (GSMA, 2023).
Safaricom's platform has generated a de nse ecosystem of SME digital services, including
M-Shwari for credit, M-Pesa for payments, and numerous agritech and logistics platforms
built on the API layer. This ecosystem effect, whereby the maturity of one platform lowers
the adoption cost of adjacen t digital tools, is theoretically consistent with network
economics (Shapiro & Varian, 1999) and has been documented empirically by Asongu et al.
(2021) as a key driver of the country's relatively high SME digital performance scores.
Nigeria's SME digital landscape is characterised by a different dynamic: large market size,
rapid smartphone diffusion, and a vibrant informal economy that digital tools have begun
to formalise. PwC (2022) reports that Nigerian fintech investment reached USD 1.37 billion
in 2021, and platforms including Paystack, Flutterwave, and Konga have created new digital
market access channels for SMEs. Adomako et al. (2024) found that Nigerian SMEs were
more likely to adopt digital tools reactively, in response to competitive pressure, th an
proactively, through strategic planning, a pattern consistent with the resource constraints
and uncertainty aversion documented in the SME management literature more broadly.
Ghana and Cameroon represent contrasting digital maturity profiles. Ghana's re gulatory
environment, including its 2020 Digital Economy Policy, has actively promoted SME digital
adoption, resulting in measurable growth in mobile commerce and digital payment
adoption (World Bank, 2022). Cameroon, by contrast, faces higher regulatory c omplexity
arising from its bilingual and bijural institutional heritage, alongside lower baseline
infrastructure quality outside Douala and Yaounde, creating a more constrained digital
adoption environment for SMEs (African Development Bank, 2022).

Conceptual Framework
Integrating the Resource -Based View, the Dynamic Capabilities Framework, and the
empirical evidence reviewed above, this study proposes a conceptual model in which digital
technology adoption enables technology-mediated market entry strategies, which in turn
generate competitive advantage. Institutional and infrastructural barriers are theorised as
moderators of the strategy-to-advantage pathway, attenuating the competitive returns of
digital investment in environments where regulatory qualit y, access to finance, and
connectivity infrastructure are weak. Figure 2.1 presents this model.

Methodology
Research Design
This study adopts a quantitative cross -sectional survey design. The quantitative approach
is appropriate given the study's objective of testing directional hypotheses about
relationships among constructs measured across a large, geographically dispersed sample
(Creswell & Creswell, 2018). The cross -sectional design was chosen for its efficiency in
capturing variation across countries and sectors at a single point in time, acknowledging
that it precludes causal inference but is consistent with the research questions, which seek
to establish the strength and direction of associations rather than their longitudinal
dynamics (Fowler, 2014).

The target population comprises SME owners and senior managers (defined as those with
decision-making authority over market entry and technology investment decisions) of
formally registered businesses employing between 5 and 250 employees, operating in
major urban centres across Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, and Cameroon. The SME definition
applied is consistent with the African Development Bank's (2022) operational definition for

demographic items were original or adapted from the IFC (2021) SME survey template.

Scale Validation
Content validity was established through a two-stage expert review process. In stage one,
the draft questionnaire was reviewed by five academics with specialisations in digital
marketing, SME management, and African business research. In stage two, five
experienced SME practitioners, two from Nigeria, two from Kenya, and one from Ghana,
reviewed the instrument for contextual relevance and comprehension. Revisions following
both stages resulted in the consolidation of three items that reviewers flagged as
overlapping and the rewording of four items to improve clarity in multilingual contexts. A
pilot test was conducted with 50 SME respondents, evenly distributed across the four
countries, and the pilot data were used to compute preliminary reliability estimates.
Cronbach's alpha coefficients from the pilot fell between 0.79 and 0.88 across constructs,
exceeding the threshold of 0.70 commonly applied in social science research (Nunnally &
Bernstein, 1994). Final reliability results from the full sample are reported in Table 2.

Data Collection Procedures
Data were collected between March and July 2024 via a self -administered online
questionnaire hosted on Google Forms. The online mode was selected for its cost efficiency
in reaching SME owners and managers across four geographically dispersed countries, its
capacity to eliminate interviewer bias, and its compatibility with the study population.
Survey links were distributed through national SME association email lists, chamber of
commerce WhatsApp a nd Telegram broadcast groups, LinkedIn SME community pages,
and business-focused Facebook groups in each study country. To ensure that the intended
respondent, the SME owner or senior manager with decision -making authority over
technology and market entry investments, was the one completing the instrument, the
opening screen of the survey included a brief eligibility screening question confirming the
respondent's role and the registration status of the business. Respondents who did not
meet the eligibility criteria were redirected out of the survey automatically. All items and
instructions were presented in English, with a parallel French -language version of the
survey deployed for Cameroonian respondents, given the country's bilingual institutional
context.

Data Analysis
Preliminary analysis involved screening for missing value s, identifying and managing
univariate and multivariate outliers using Mahalanobis distance, and conducting normality
testing using the Kolmogorov -Smirnov test. Descriptive statistics, including means,
standard deviations, and frequencies, were computed fo r all constructs and for the digital
tool adoption inventory. Levene's test for homogeneity of variance was conducted prior to
ANOVA analysis.
The main analyses comprised three techniques corresponding to the three hypotheses.
Multiple regression analysis was used to test H1, examining the combined predictive effect
of digital technology adoption and market entry strategy on competitive advantage. One-
way ANOVA with Tukey HSD post-hoc comparison was used to test H2, examining sectoral
differences in digital  technology adoption. Moderated regression analysis using the
hierarchical entry method was used to test H3, with institutional barriers entered as both a
main effect and as an interaction term with digital technology adoption and market entry
strategy. Al l continuous predictors were mean -centred prior to the computation of
interaction terms to reduce multicollinearity, following the recommendation of Aiken and
West (1991). All analyses were conducted in SPSS 26.0. Statistical significance was set at
alpha = .05 throughout.

Discussion
Digital Technology as a Competitive Resource
The finding that digital technology adoption explains the largest portion of variance i n
competitive advantage (Beta = 0.571) among the predictors tested is theoretically coherent
with the VRIN logic of Barney (1991). In markets where adoption levels remain
heterogeneous, possessing digital capabilities constitutes a genuinely rare and, with in the
medium-term competitive time horizon, imperfectly imitable resource. The magnitude of
the coefficient, however, invites a cautionary interpretation: as digital tools diffuse more
widely, as the GSMA (2023) data suggest they are doing at an accelerat ing rate, the
competitive distinctiveness of mere adoption will erode, and competitive advantage will
increasingly depend on the sophistication of use rather than the fact of adoption. Teece
(2018) made precisely this argument in the context of digital tra nsformation broadly: in
mature digital markets, it is dynamic capability, the ability to continually sense, seize, and
reconfigure, rather than any specific tool, that sustains advantage.
The present study, conducted at a point where digital adoption acros s the sample is
moderate but not yet universal (overall DTA mean of 3.74 on a 5-point scale), captures firms
at a juncture where adoption still confers advantage. Future research, particularly
longitudinal designs, should track whether this coefficient declines as adoption converges,
consistent with the diffusion of innovations curve described by Rogers (2003). Such a
finding would provide empirical confirmation of Teece's (2018) theoretical prediction in the
Sub-Saharan African SME context.

Sectoral Heterogeneity and its Implications
The significant sectoral difference in digital adoption (H2) is not surprising but is important
to document systematically. The gap between services/ICT SMEs (M = 4.11) and retail SMEs
(M = 3.42) represents a 0.69-point difference on a 5-point scale, a substantively meaningful
difference that translates, via the H1 regression coefficients, into material differences in
predicted competitive advantage. Amadasun & Mutezo  (2022) identified sector as an
under-examined moderator in West African SME performance research, and the present
findings confirm that retail and trade SMEs, which constitute the largest share of the SME
population in most Sub-Saharan African economies (IFC, 2021), are meaningfully behind on
the digital adoption curve relative to services firms.
This asymmetry has implications that go beyond competitive strategy. If retail SMEs, which
are often the primary source of livelihoods for low -income urban households (Prahalad,
2006), lag in digital adoption, the aggregat e economic development benefits of digital
market expansion will flow disproportionately to the already more -advantaged services
sector. Policymakers and development institutions seeking to ensure broad -based
digitisation gains should therefore prioritise sector-specific digital enablement
programmes targeted at retail trade SMEs, rather than deploying generic digital literacy or
financing interventions that may be captured primarily by firms already closer to the digital
frontier.

Institutional Barriers as the Binding Constraint
The moderation finding, that institutional barriers attenuate the competitive returns of
digital investment, is arguably the most policy-relevant result of the study. The implication
is not simply that barriers are present, which the descriptive data confirm, with institutional
barriers recording the highest mean of any construct (M = 3.91), but that they interact with
strategy in a way that reduces the efficiency of digital investment. An SME that invests an
equivalent amount in di gital technology and market entry capability will realise less
competitive advantage in a high-barrier institutional environment than in a low-barrier one.
This finding is consistent with Khanna and Palepu's (2010) institutional void argument and
with the country-level evidence from Asongu et al. (2021), who found that financial
development effects on market outcomes were attenuated in countries with weaker
governance indicators.
The finding also has a more optimistic reading: it implies that institutional improvements,
even partial ones, will amplify the competitive returns of existing digital investments
without requiring additional firm -level expenditure. A reduction in regulatory compliance
burden, an improvement in mobile connectivity reliability, or an  expansion of SME credit
access would, according to the moderation logic, increase the competitive advantage
realised by firms that have already invested in digital tools. This is a more cost -effective
intervention pathway than direct subsidy of digital equipment, and it is consistent with the

broader development economics literature.

Country-Level Variation and the Kenya Outlier
The adoption frequency data in Table 7 consistently place Kenya at or near the top of all
digital tool adoption categories. The most striking gap is in mobile payment adoption,
where Kenya (93.6%) leads Cameroon (64.4%) by 29.2 percentage points. As noted in
Section 2.2.5, this reflects  the maturity of the M -PESA ecosystem and its platform
externalities. The implications extend beyond mobile payments: the positive spillover from
a mature mobile money infrastructure to e -commerce adoption, cloud tool adoption, and
AI-assisted tool adoptio n in Kenya confirms that digital ecosystems have network
properties (Shapiro & Varian, 1999) and that the sequencing of infrastructure development
matters for SME digital capability building. Nigeria's profile is instructive in a different way:
strong adop tion of social media (88.7%) and mobile payments (79.3%) alongside lower
adoption of AI tools (28.7%) suggests that Nigerian SMEs have advanced on foundational
digital tools but not yet on higher -order analytical capabilities, consistent with the
absorptive capacity ladder described in Section 4.2.

Limitations
Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. The cross -sectional design precludes causal
inference: the associations documented between digital adoption and competitive
advantage are correlational, and reverse causation, whereby more competitive firms have
greater resources to invest in digital tools, cannot be ruled out. Longitudinal research
designs would be necessary to establish causal direction. The urban sampling frame means
findings cannot be generalised to rural or peri-urban SMEs, which face substantially higher
infrastructure constraints and may exhibit different adoption dynamics. Self -reported
competitive advantage data carry social desirability risk, and objective performance
metrics, such as revenue growth or market share data, would strengthen future studies. The
study's four-country sample, while broader than most prior studies in this domain, does not
represent the full heterogeneity of Sub -Saharan Africa's 54 national economies, and
findings should be extrapolated with caution to contexts with very different digital
infrastructure profiles, such as Ethiopia, Tanzania, or the Sahel economies. A further
limitation concerns the unequal distribution of the country sub -samples: the Cameroon
sub-sample (n = 45, 10.9%) is substantially smaller than those of Nigeria (n = 150), Kenya (n
= 125), and Ghana (n = 92), and readers should exercise caution when interpreting cross -
country comparisons involving Cameroonian SMEs, as the smaller sample size reduces the
statistical power and representational coverage of inferences drawn specifically for that
country context

Conclusion
This study has examined the conditions under which technology -mediated market entry
strategies generate competitive advantage for SMEs in Sub -Saharan Africa, using a four -
country, three-sector quantitative survey of 412 SME owners and managers. Three principal
conclusions emerge. First, digital technology adoption and technology -mediated market
entry strategies are robust and sig nificant predictors of competitive advantage, jointly
explaining 56% of variance, with digital technology adoption carrying the larger
independent effect. Second, significant sectoral heterogeneity exists in digital adoption,
with services and ICT SMEs sub stantially ahead of retail and manufacturing firms,
generating differential competitive advantage accrual that has implications for inclusive
digital development policy. Third, institutional and infrastructural barriers significantly
moderate the technolog y-to-advantage pathway, attenuating competitive returns in
higher-barrier environments, and conversely implying that institutional improvements will
amplify the returns of existing digital investments.
Theoretically, the study extends the Resource -Based Vi ew and Dynamic Capabilities
Framework into the Sub -Saharan African SME digital market entry context, establishing
empirical support for their applicability and demonstrating the importance of institutional
quality as a moderator, rather than control, in th e strategy -performance relationship.
Practically, the findings equip SME owners, policymakers, and development finance
institutions with evidence -based guidance on both the tools and conditions that drive
digital competitive advantage in these dynamic and economically consequential markets.

Recommendations
For SME Owners and Managers
The regression findings confirm that digital technology adoption is the single strongest
predictor of competitive advantage in the sample. SMEs that have not yet progressed
beyond basic social media use should prioritise the adoption of mobile payment and e -
commerce platforms as the next tier of digital investment, given that these tools record the
highest adoption -to-advantage conversion rates in the data. The absorptive capac ity
argument suggests that investment in foundational tools, executed well, creates the
organisational learning necessary to deploy higher -order AI-assisted tools effectively at a
later stage. Firms in the retail sector, which lag significantly on digital adoption, should treat
digital market entry not as an optional efficiency enhancement but as a strategic necessity,
given the trajectory of competitive dynamics evidenced in the data. Where connectivity
infrastructure is unreliable, investment in offline -capable digital tools and lightweight
mobile applications will reduce the operational risk associated with connectivity-dependent
platforms.

For Policymakers
The moderation finding, that institutional barriers attenuate the competitive returns of
digital i nvestment, provides a clear policy rationale for prioritising institutional quality
improvements over equipment subsidy programmes. Specifically, regulators in all four
study countries should review and streamline digital payment licensing requirements to
reduce the compliance burden on SMEs seeking to adopt mobile commerce tools. National
broadband expansion plans should be accelerated with explicit SME market entry targets,
given the evidence that connectivity reliability is a binding constraint on higher-order digital
tool adoption. Tax incentive frameworks for SME digital investment, modelled on the
Digital Economy Policy that Ghana has begun to implement, should be considered in
Nigeria and Cameroon, where adoption gaps are most pronounced in AI-assisted and cloud-
based tool categories.

For Development Finance Institutions
The IFC (2021) SME finance gap of over USD 330 billion in Sub -Saharan Africa is well
documented. The present findings add specificity to the allocation question: financing
instruments targeting digital technology adoption and market entry capability, particularly
for retail -sector SMEs in Nigeria and Cameroon, are likely to generate disproportionate
competitive advantage returns, given the large current adoption gap relative  to services
firms in these countries. Blended finance structures that combine affordable credit for
digital infrastructure investment with technical assistance on deployment strategy would
address both the financial constraint and the management capabilit y gap documented in
the SME digitisation literature (Nwankpa et al., 2022). Regional coordination mechanisms,
building on the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) digital trade provisions, could
further reduce cross -border regulatory barriers that currently prevent SMEs from using
digital tools to enter markets beyond their national borders.
