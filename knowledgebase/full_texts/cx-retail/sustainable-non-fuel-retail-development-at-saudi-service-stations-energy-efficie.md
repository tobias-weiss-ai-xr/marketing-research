Saudi service stations are evolving  from fuel -dispensing sites into
multi-service mobility and retail platforms. This study extends the
conventional non -fuel retail discussion by integrating energy
efficiency, customer experience, vehicle services, circular waste
management, solar-canopy generation, last-mile logistics, quick-service
restaurant (QSR) partnerships and electric -vehicle charging. The
evidence base combines an integrative review of recent peer -reviewed
literature with Saudi regulatory and industry evidence and a field -
validation protocol for primary research. The proposed primary
component links customer surveys, station -manager interviews,
structured site audits and operational data to secondary findings so that
recommendations can be tested rather than inferred from literature
alone. The synthesis indicates that commercial performance depends on
the interaction between reliable vehicle services, efficient and
comfortable facilities, credible waste handling, renewable -energy
integration, convenient digital and last -mile services,  and partnership
formats that increase dwell time and basket value. Because no
completed survey or interview dataset was supplied with the
manuscript, the paper does not fabricate primary findings; instead, it
specifies the variables, sampling plan, instru ments and analysis needed
to generate them. The resulting framework supports station -level
decisions using joint environmental, service, customer and financial
KPIs and recommends staged pilots before network-wide scaling.

Introduction:-
Service stations are becoming strategically different assets from the forecourts for which many were originally
designed. The conventional model organizes land, circulation and labor around rapid fueling; the emerging model
must also accommodate convenienc e retail, food and beverage, parcel or digital services, longer dwell times and,
increasingly, electric -vehicle charging. Research on petrol -station trip generation already shows that adding
convenience and fast -food functions changes the traffic produced by a station, indicating that non -fuel uses alter
both demand and site operations (Mohd Shafie et al., 2021). Saudi evidence further emphasizes that petrol -station
performance depends on multiple physical and operational dimensions rather than a single fac ility condition

The Saudi context intensifies this integration challenge. Commercial buildings operate under demanding cooling
conditions, so air -conditioning strategy, envelope performance, lighting and controls have direct consequences for
electricity use and peak demand. Saudi studies demonstrate substantial retrofit potential and show why measured
performance matters: post-retrofit commercial-building evidence has documented material energy reductions when
efficiency measures and rooftop photovoltaics are implemented and verified (Ahmed et al., 2023; Asif et al., 2023).
Work on hot -climate HVAC operation similarly shows  that controls and operating strategy can materially change
energy use without requiring complete building replacement (Najid and Al -Homoud, 2023). Building simulation and
stock-level studies reinforce the importance of climate -sensitive efficiency measure s and appropriate baselines
(Krarti et al., 2020; Suliman, 2023). For service stations, therefore, the retail proposition cannot be separated from
the energy intensity required to make that proposition comfortable and reliable.

Retail research provides a second necessary perspective. Customer experience is a dynamic response to multiple
firm-controlled and contextual stimuli rather than a single satisfaction score (Becker and Jaakkola, 2020). In
convenience stores, store image, service quality, price and o mnichannel features influence experience and revisit
intentions (Gibson et al., 2022). Technologies can remove friction or enrich the visit, but their value depends on
purpose and acceptance rather than novelty alone (Grewal et al., 2023; Hoyer et al., 202 0). Sustainability also
reaches customers through store image, perceived quality, credibility and emotions, which means that operational
environmental performance can contribute to commercial value only when customers encounter a coherent and
believable retail experience (Sánchez-González et al., 2022; Godefroit-Winkel et al., 2022).

A third literature concerns the coupling of retail and new mobility infrastructure. Empirical research finds
measurable demand spillovers from electric -vehicle chargers to nea rby businesses, while economic models show
that retailers may have incentives to host charging when additional customer value can be captured (Babar and
Burtch, 2024; Arlt and Astier, 2023). Charging infrastructure nevertheless introduces grid, siting and operational
constraints that require deliberate planning (Mastoi et al., 2022). The practical issue is thus not whether stations
should become more sustainable or more experiential in isolation. It is how energy efficiency, customer experience
and commerci al performance can be designed as mutually reinforcing parts of the same non -fuel retail system.
Existing research is fragmented across these domains, leaving a need for a Saudi -oriented synthesis that identifies
mechanisms, trade-offs, metrics and development priorities.

The expanded scope is particularly relevant in Saudi Arabia because the service -station business model increasingly
extends beyond convenience retail. Automotive services such as oil changes, tire services and battery testing create
additional visit occasions but also introduce regulated workshop requirements and material waste streams. Used
lubricants, empty lubricant containers, scrap tires and spent batteries require controlled segregation, storage,
collection and traceability rather th an disposal as ordinary municipal waste. At the same time, station canopies can
provide usable surface area for photovoltaic generation, while parcel lockers, click -and-collect and other last -mile
functions can convert stations into distributed logistics n odes. QSR and coffee partnerships can increase traffic and
dwell time, but they also add kitchen energy, refrigeration, packaging waste, parking demand and service -queue
interactions. These extensions require a wider operating model than the original energ y-retail-charging framework.

Saudi industry evidence supports this wider interpretation. SASCO reports a multi -service station strategy that
zcombines convenience retail, maintenance services, renewable -energy initiatives, EV charging and strategic
partnerships, while recent Saudi Exchange disclosures show operators adding fast -food restaurants, coffee shops and
traveler services at redeveloped highway locations. The Ministry of Municipalities and Housing has also updated
requirements for fuel stations an d professional workshops, including activities such as tire and oil services and
battery testing, with explicit attention to licensing, safety, waste control and site cleanliness. These developments
justify examining the service station as an integrated co mmercial, environmental and mobility asset rather than a
fuel forecourt with a store attached.

crosses building engineering, retail marketing, automotive services, waste management, service operations and

does not report invented survey, interview or field results where no dataset was provided.

 Mixed Evidence Methodology: Integrative Review and Primary Research Design:
An integrative review design was selected because the focal problem crosses building engineering, retail marketing,
service operations and mobility infrastructure. The method therefore sought conceptual integration rather than a
meta-analysis of a single intervention. The search framework was designed around Scopus and Web of Science Core
Collection coverage and was complemented by publisher discovery environments, including ScienceDirect,
SpringerLink and Emerald Insight, togethe r with Crossref or publisher records for DOI and bibliographic
verification. Because no institutional database export was used to establish a closed record universe, this review
does not report article -screening counts or claim a PRISMA -style census. That choice avoids presenting artificial
precision while retaining transparent eligibility and quality rules.

constructs, while the final reference set was restricted to peer-reviewed works within the core period.

Studies were included w hen they offered empirical, review or theory -building evidence directly relevant to at least
one causal link in the proposed framework: resource efficiency, in -store experience, retail technology, sustainable -
retail perception, service -station operations, charging-driven demand or commercial value. Studies were excluded
when they were non -peer-reviewed, lacked traceable bibliographic metadata, focused on unrelated industrial energy
systems, or discussed sustainability without a mechanism applicable to physi cal retail operations. Geographic
transferability was assessed explicitly: Saudi and hot -climate building studies were prioritized for energy claims,
whereas international retail studies were used for behavioral mechanisms that require subsequent Saudi val idation.
Quality appraisal considered journal peer review, methodological transparency, fit between research design and
claimed outcome, DOI resolvability, and the strength of transfer from the original setting to Saudi service stations.
Evidence was then coded into three primary themes - energy efficiency, customer experience and commercial
performance - plus cross -cutting mechanisms such as credibility, dwell time, technology readiness and
measurement. Synthesis proceeded by comparing convergent findings,  identifying contradictions or boundary
conditions, and building directional relationships rather than averaging heterogeneous effect sizes. This approach
supports a decision framework while preserving differences between engineering savings, behavioral in tentions and
observed commercial outcomes.

Primary Research Component for Saudi Field Validation:
A multi-method primary phase is incorporated to overcome the weakness of relying only on secondary research. The
recommended design uses four sources collect ed at the same stations: (1) a customer intercept survey, (2) semi -
structured interviews with station managers and service/QSR operators, (3) a structured physical and environmental
site audit, and (4) anonymized operational data covering point -of-sale transactions, service-bay activity, energy use,
waste collections, charger utilization and last -mile transactions. The purpose is triangulation: customer responses
explain perceived convenience and value; interviews explain operating constraints; site audits verify whether
claimed practices are physically present; and operational data test whether perceptions translate into measurable
performance.

A practical sampling plan would select approximately 12 -20 stations across at least four formats: urban
neighborhood, highway/travel, large integrated multi -service and emerging charging-led sites. Within those stations,
a target of roughly 300 -500 customer surveys would permit subgroup analysis by visit purpose, while 20 -30
interviews with managers, automot ive-service staff, QSR partners and logistics partners would provide operational
depth. The survey should measure visit purpose, use of vehicle services, QSR purchase, parcel/collection use, dwell
time, perceived cleanliness and comfort, sustainability cre dibility, willingness to revisit, basket value band and
preference for bundled offers. The site audit should record service -bay layout, hazardous -waste storage, labeled
segregation, spill control, tire and battery storage, PV canopy area, charger and parki ng layout, QSR interfaces,
parcel-locker placement and pedestrian safety.

Primary analysis should connect directly to the secondary propositions. Cross -tabulation and regression can test
whether QSR use, vehicle-service use, charging or parcel collection is associated with higher dwell time or non -fuel
spending. Difference-in-differences or matched -site comparisons are preferable where an intervention is introduced
at selected stations. Qualitative interviews should be coded for recurrent barriers such as landlord constraints, waste-
collector reliability, grid capacity, kitchen ventilation, parking conflicts, supplier quality and data -sharing
limitations. Recommendations should then be categorized as evidence -supported, locally validated or still requiring
pilot evidence, preventing the literature review from being presented as if it were primary field proof.

THEMATIC LITERATURE REVIEW AND CRITICAL ANALYSIS:
Energy efficiency as an operating and experience platform:
Evidence from Saudi buildings consistently indicates that retrofit value depends on both technical selection and
operational verification. Ahmed et al. (2023) show that commercial retrofits can combine energy and water
conservation measures with economic assessment, while Asif et al. (2023) demonstrate the importance of post -
retrofit measurement and verification rather than relying only on simulated savings. Rached and Anber (2022)
similarly frame hot -arid retrofitting as a portfolio problem in which envelope, lighting and mechanical measures
should be combined according to building characteristics. At service -station stores, this implies a hierarchy: reduce
avoidable cooling and lig hting loads first, control remaining loads intelligently, and then size on -site generation
against a more efficient baseline. A photovoltaic installation on an inefficient store may reduce purchased electricity
yet leave poor controls, discomfort and peak demand unresolved.

HVAC requires particular attention because energy reduction can conflict with the experiential objective if it is
pursued through indiscriminate temperature increases or reduced ventilation. Najid and Al -Homoud (2023) show
that operatin g strategies materially affect energy use in a hot Saudi climate; Mohammed et al. (2021) likewise
demonstrate that building energy consumption can be related to operational and facility characteristics. For station
retail, the more useful concept is theref ore energy productivity rather than minimum energy use: electricity should
be assessed against transactions, occupied hours and service quality. Sensor -based zoning, scheduled set points, door
management, efficient equipment, shading and maintenance can lo wer cooling intensity while protecting comfort.
Krarti et al. (2020) further show at stock scale that retrofit packages should be tailored to building type and location,
warning against one standard specification for every Saudi station.

Energy efficiency can also affect the asset and brand proposition. Zhang et al. (2024) report that energy performance
is associated with retail property value, although their European property evidence should not be transferred
mechanically to Saudi station economics. More  broadly, Terzis and Gotzamani (2025) show that sustainable retail
performance requires environmental, social and economic indicators rather than energy metrics alone. This supports
an integrated station dashboard including energy -use intensity, peak deman d, energy per transaction, maintenance
calls and customer -comfort indicators. The critical point is that engineering performance becomes commercially
meaningful when it protects service availability and creates a pleasant environment at lower lifecycle cos t.

Customer experience, convenience and credible sustainability:
 Becker and Jaakkola (2020) argue that customer experience emerges across a journey and reflects responses to
multiple stimuli. That perspective is especially useful for service stations because the visit often begins with a
functional mobility need and on ly then becomes a retail opportunity. Gibson et al. (2022) find that convenience -
store image, service quality, price and omnichannel factors shape experience and satisfaction, which then influence
revisit intentions. Station operators should consequently t reat pump-to-store wayfinding, entrance visibility, queue
time, product availability, restroom quality, foodservice speed and payment as one journey. A highly designed store
cannot compensate for a confusing forecourt, while a frictionless forecourt cannot  monetize demand if the retail
offer is weak.

Technology strengthens experience when it reduces effort or adds relevant value. Hoyer et al. (2020) describe how
new technologies can reshape pre -transaction, transaction and post -transaction stages, and Verho ef et al. (2021)
place such changes within broader digital transformation rather than isolated device adoption. Smart -shop research
shows that utilitarian and hedonic motivations interact with customer technology readiness (Chang and Chen, 2021).
In-store technology frameworks likewise distinguish efficiency -enhancing applications from experience -enhancing
ones (Grewal et al., 2023), while field -oriented smart -retailing work shows how integrated digital ecosystems can
reshape physical shopping (Bourg et al. , 2023). For Saudi service stations, practical uses include reliable self -
checkout, digital menus, loyalty recognition, queue management, inventory visibility and personalized offers. The
test should be whether a technology removes time, uncertainty or ser vice failure; installing technology without this
purpose risks creating another point of friction.

Personalization illustrates the trade -off. Riegger et al. (2021) identify drivers such as usefulness and integration but
also barriers including privacy concerns and interaction misfit. Chen and Shang (2021) similarly show that
sustaining smart-retail user experience requires continued attention to technology -related perceptions. Kim (2021)
demonstrates how mobile technology can revitalize physical retail when it connects digital functions with store
experience. These findings suggest that a station loyalty ecosystem should be permission -based, simple and
operationally connected to what customers value - for example, faster payment, relevant food offers or charging-
status information - rather than maximizing data collection. The commercial objective is repeatable convenience, not
digital complexity.

Physical atmospherics remain equally important. Doucé and Adams (2020) show that layering high -arousal sensory
cues can generate overload rather than stronger approach behavior. This is relevant to compact service -station stores
where bright signage, screens, music, food aromas and promotional clutter compete within a small footprint.
Sustainable design can provi de a calmer alternative through daylight control, coherent lighting, acoustics, clean
materials and visible organization. Wang et al. (2023) argue for retail experiences that connect sustainability and
engagement, while Grassi (2022) emphasizes the experie ntial role of socially meaningful retail propositions. The
implication is not that sustainability should become another layer of signage; rather, resource -efficient design and
credible social practices should be experienced through comfort, cleanliness, tr ansparency and service.

Credibility determines whether environmental actions produce customer value. Sánchez -González et al. (2022) link
perceived retailer commitment to sustainable development with image, perceived quality and loyalty, and Godefroit -
Winkel et al. (2022) show that environmental responsibility can affect loyalty through emotional responses. Kumar
and Utkarsh (2023) find that information quality and store credibility are important to consumer engagement in
green retailing. These studies caut ion against superficial claims. A Saudi station that promotes sustainability while
operating uncomfortable spaces, poorly maintained equipment or excessive single -use waste may create a credibility
gap. More defensible communication would connect visible a ctions to specific operational practices, such as
efficient cooling, solar generation, waste separation, low-flow fixtures or verified savings.

Mobility transition, dwell time and commercial capture:
Electric-vehicle charging changes the temporal logic of  the station. Fueling is designed for rapid throughput,
whereas charging can create dwell time that is commercially useful only when customers can move safely and
conveniently between charger, store, foodservice, seating and amenities. Babar and Burtch (20 24) provide causal
evidence that charging stations can increase visits to nearby retailers, with effects concentrated by proximity and
establishment type. Their findings establish a spillover mechanism but do not guarantee profitable retail capture at
every site. Arlt and Astier (2023) show theoretically why retail businesses may invest in public chargers when
charging attracts incremental customers, while also highlighting pricing and incentive issues.

This opportunity introduces operating constraints. Ma stoi et al. (2022) review charging infrastructure planning, grid
integration and siting considerations, implying that chargers cannot be treated as independent retail fixtures. Power
capacity, charging speed, queueing, parking duration and renewable integr ation influence both customer experience
and site economics. In existing Saudi stations, phased deployment may therefore be preferable: establish electrical
capacity and charger placement, align dwell -time services with expected charging duration, and meas ure
incremental store visits and spend before expanding. The relevant metric is not charger utilization alone but the joint
productivity of charging and non-fuel retail.

Mixed uses also alter traffic and layout. Mohd Shafie et al. (2021) demonstrate that petrol stations incorporating
convenience stores and fast -food restaurants require distinct trip -generation consideration. This matters because

Automotive services as a non-fuel revenue and trust platform:
Automotive services materially expa nd the station value proposition because they create a different demand
occasion from convenience shopping. Oil changes, lubricant sales, tire services and battery testing or replacement
can convert the station from a short refueling stop into a maintenanc e destination. Saudi regulatory requirements
now explicitly recognize tire and oil services and battery testing within workshop activities, reinforcing that these
services require appropriate licensing, spatial planning, safety controls and visible operati ng standards.
Commercially, the attraction is repeatable need -based demand and the opportunity to cross -sell food, beverages and
convenience items during service waiting time. Operationally, however, poor workmanship, queuing, fluid handling
or weak parts quality can damage station trust more severely than a weak retail promotion. The correct KPI set
therefore includes service jobs per bay, average service time, comeback/complaint rate, gross margin per job, retail
attachment during service, bay utilization and customer satisfaction.

Waste streams, circularity and environmental control:
Expanding vehicle and food services changes the station waste profile. Used lubricating oil, oily filters and
absorbents, empty lubricant containers, scrap tires and spent lead-acid batteries should not be treated as ordinary
store waste. They require segregation, secure temporary storage, spill prevention, approved collection routes and
documentary traceability. QSR operations add food waste, cooking -oil waste, cardboard an d single-use packaging.
The practical management principle is source separation at the point of generation, followed by clearly assigned
custody, scheduled collection and contractor verification. The environmental benefit is reduced leakage, fire risk and
uncontrolled dumping; the commercial benefit is lower compliance risk, cleaner sites and more credible
sustainability communication. Recommended KPIs include liters of used oil collected versus lubricant sold, tire and
battery return rate, percentage of wa ste transferred through approved collectors, spill incidents, waste -storage
nonconformities and kilograms of food/packaging waste per 1,000 transactions.

Solar photovoltaic canopies as energy and customer infrastructure:
Service-station canopies offer a s tructurally visible opportunity for distributed solar generation, particularly where
daytime electricity demand coincides with cooling, lighting, refrigeration, foodservice and charging loads. Solar
canopies should be evaluated after basic efficiency measu res so that generation is not used to mask avoidable
consumption. Their business case should include annual yield, self -consumption ratio, avoided electricity cost,
structural and fire-safety requirements, cleaning needs, shading benefits and potential int eraction with EV charging.
Canopy PV can also improve customer comfort by shading parking or circulation areas, but the physical
arrangement must preserve fuel -safety zones, visibility, maintenance access and drainage. The primary research
phase should com pare PV -equipped and non -PV stations using kWh purchased per transaction, PV yield per
installed kWp, peak-demand reduction, maintenance downtime and customer perception of visible sustainability.

Last-mile services and the station as a distributed logistics node:
Last-mile functions such as smart parcel lockers, click -and-collect counters, returns points and scheduled pickup can
add visit occasions without requiring the floor area of a full retail concept. Their effectiveness depends on network
location, 24-hour accessibility, parking convenience, security, digital reliability and the extent to which collection
visits convert into store purchases. Saudi research on smart parcel lockers shows that adoption depends on user
acceptance factors including conven ience and trust, suggesting that station deployment should be judged by actual
customer use rather than installation counts. The main commercial question is incremental value: locker transactions
should be linked to store entry, conversion and basket size.  Operational KPIs include parcels per compartment/day,
failed or overdue collections, average stop duration, percentage of locker users entering the store, incremental
transactions during collection windows and service availability.

partnerships within station networks. Partnership models can include fixed rent, revenue share, franchise, concession
or operator-managed concepts; each shifts capital, staffing and demand risk differently. Positive effects may include

Integrating commercial performance:
The expanded framework supports six commercial pathways. Cost productivity comes from efficient HVAC,
lighting, controls and solar generation. Demand productivity comes from service quality, store image, digital
convenience and credible sustainabilit y. Mobility productivity comes from charging and last -mile services that
create additional visits or dwell time. Service productivity comes from vehicle -maintenance revenue and cross -
shopping during waiting time. Partnership productivity comes from QSR and  branded-service arrangements that
bring external demand while sharing investment or operating risk. Circularity productivity comes from avoiding
waste-related incidents and improving material recovery, compliance and brand credibility. The central managem ent
task is to test whether these pathways produce incremental margin after energy, labor, space, partner and waste -
handling costs rather than assuming that more services automatically mean higher profitability.

Commercial measurement should distinguish leading, intermediate and financial outcomes. Leading measures
include HVAC runtime, queue time, stock availability, charger uptime and digital -service reliability. Intermediate
measures include footfall, dwell time, conversion, basket size, foodservice at tachment, loyalty participation and
revisit behavior. Financial measures include gross margin, non -fuel margin per square meter, energy cost per
transaction, maintenance cost, and return on invested capital. This chain prevents attribution errors. For exam ple, a
charger may raise footfall without improving profit if the store is distant or queues are excessive; an efficiency
retrofit may have a sound payback even if customers do not notice it; sustainability communication may improve
perceptions without cha nging actual resource use. Management should therefore evaluate each intervention at the
mechanism it is intended to change and then test downstream effects.

DISCUSSION:-
The synthesis suggests that sustainable non-fuel retail performance is best understood as a coupled operating system.
Engineering studies provide relatively strong evidence for the direction of energy savings in Saudi buildings, but
they rarely measure retail behavior. Retail studies explain behavioral mechanisms, yet most are con ducted outside
Saudi Arabia and often use intentions, perceptions or category -specific samples. Mobility studies supply
increasingly causal evidence on charging -related footfall, but commercial spillovers depend on proximity, service
type and local context . No single literature therefore supports a claim that an energy -efficient station will
automatically sell more non-fuel products. The defensible conclusion is conditional: efficiency can lower the cost of
delivering a high-quality environment; experience design can improve the probability that visits become purchases;
and mobility services can create additional opportunities for conversion.

This conditionality creates several design trade -offs. First, aggressive energy reduction can undermine comfort if
controls are poorly commissioned, turning an engineering gain into an experience loss. Second, digitalization can
shorten queues but exclude customers with lower technology readiness or generate privacy concerns (Chang and
Chen, 2021; Riegger et al., 2021).  Third, stronger sensory merchandising can attract attention but excessive
stimulation may reduce pleasantness (Doucé and Adams, 2020). Fourth, EV charging can extend dwell time but also
occupy valuable parking and electrical capacity. These are not reason s to avoid investment; they show why each
initiative requires an integrated business case with both technical and customer thresholds.

Figure 1 therefore treats Saudi climate, station format, location, mobility mix and operating capability as
moderators. A hot urban station with high turnover may justify advanced controls and rapid digital checkout, while a
highway mobility hub may place greater value on shaded circulation, seating, foodservice and charging. Qasem
(2025) reinforces the need for multi -criteria station assessment, while the wider digital -transformation literature
warns that technology creates value only when organization, processes and capabilities change with it (Verhoef et
al., 2021). The most important managerial shift is consequently from  project-by-project investment to portfolio
governance: every retrofit, retail change and mobility service should be evaluated against a common set of energy,
experience and commercial outcomes.

A further implication is that timing matters: energy savings  accrue across operating hours, whereas retail returns are
concentrated in customer visits, so investment appraisal should compare hourly resource use with transaction
patterns rather than only annual totals. Station managers can use this temporal linkage to identify periods when
cooling, lighting, staffing and promotional activity are misaligned with demand, creating opportunities to improve
both energy productivity and service responsiveness. The same logic supports commissioning after layout or
technology changes, because a technically successful installation can still shift queues, circulation or staff workload
in ways that reduce customer value. Consequently, post -implementation review should combine engineering
verification with operational observation and point-of-sale evidence before a concept is replicated across a network.
This network perspective is particularly important when capital is constrained, since comparative station data can
direct investment toward sites where energy intensity, customer friction and commercial headroom occur together. It
also reduces the risk of treating high revenue as evidence of efficient operations or low energy use as evidence of a
strong customer proposition. A balanced scorecard makes these distinctions visible and  allows management to
preserve useful trade -offs instead of optimizing one metric at the expense of the others. Commercial governance
should also distinguish capital -intensive interventions from operating -process changes. A controls retrofit, a new
foodservice concept and a charging installation have different payback logic, but each should specify the mechanism
expected to change before approval.

This mechanism-first discipline makes post -investment review more informative because managers can ask whether
an underperforming financial result reflects weak technical delivery, insufficient customer response, poor site
selection or unrealistic assumpti ons about conversion. For network operators, station segmentation can further

improve capital allocation. A measure that is attractive at a twenty -four-hour urban site may be inappropriate at a
lower-volume remote site even when both facilities have simila r floor area. The literature also implies that customer
comfort should be treated as a constraint in energy optimization rather than as an unmeasured benefit. Temperature,
queueing and cleanliness failures can erase the perceived value created by faster te chnology or stronger
merchandise. Similarly, the commercial value of sustainability is more likely to persist when it is embedded in
operations. Verified resource savings, reliable equipment and clear customer information provide evidence that can
support credibility beyond a short promotional campaign.

Charging strengthens this need for operational coordination because the customer may remain on site far longer than
during fueling. The longer visit creates an opportunity for foodservice, work, rest or shopping, but also raises
expectations for seating, restrooms and safety. A useful commercial test is therefore incremental margin per added
dwell minute, interpreted alongside charger utilization and energy demand. This metric would reveal whether
extended stays are productive or simply consume space and electrical capacity. Network learning should be
cumulative: pilots should use common definitions for footfall, conversion, energy intensity and margin so that
results can be compared across locations witho ut confusing different measurement conventions. Over time, such
standardized evidence can support scenario planning for changes in fuel demand, charging adoption and electricity
tariffs, allowing non-fuel investment to be treated as a staged transition rather than a one-time redesign.

The framework therefore emphasizes disciplined experimentation. Small, measurable changes can establish local
response before expensive replication, reducing the risk that international retail trends are assumed to operate
identically in Saudi service -station settings. The primary -research issue is therefore central rather than optional.
Secondary research can identify plausible mechanisms, but it cannot establish the magnitude of Saudi customer
response, service -bay economics,  parcel-locker conversion, QSR cross -shopping or the quality of actual waste
handling at a specific network. A credible management recommendation should combine three levels of evidence:
literature support for the mechanism, direct observation or interview  evidence that the mechanism exists locally, and
operational data showing that the intervention changed the intended KPI. Where only the first level exists, the
recommendation should be treated as a pilot hypothesis rather than a proven network standard.

The broadened framework also changes how station space should be allocated. A service bay, QSR drive -through,
locker bank, EV charger and PV -support structure all compete for land, circulation, electrical capacity and
management attention. Network design s hould therefore optimize the portfolio of services by station format instead
of attempting to install every concept everywhere. Urban sites may prioritize rapid services, click -and-collect and
compact food offers; highway sites may justify full QSR, travel er amenities and maintenance; large integrated
stations can support multiple partners and renewable generation; charging -led sites may emphasize seating, food and
digital services that monetize dwell time.

Research Gaps and Future Research:-
The most significant gap is the absence of Saudi station -level studies that simultaneously observe energy, customer,
service, waste and commercial outcomes. Future research should use matched -station or staggered -intervention
designs in which smart meters, PV meters, H VAC sensors, footfall counters, point -of-sale data, service -bay jobs,
charger/locker use, waste -collection records and customer feedback are linked at the same temporal resolution.
Difference-in-differences designs could test whether a specific change - such as adding a QSR, parcel locker, service
bay or PV canopy - changes sales, dwell time, energy intensity or revisit behavior beyond seasonal variation.
A second gap concerns station heterogeneity. Research should compare urban neighborhood stations, highw ay sites,
remote stations, integrated service centers and charging -oriented mobility hubs because their dwell -time economics
and service expectations differ. A third gap is causal evidence on automotive services: the sector needs Saudi data
on service dema nd, margin, repeat intervals, cross -shopping and customer trust. A fourth gap concerns waste
traceability and circularity, particularly used oil, tires, batteries and foodservice packaging. A fifth gap is the
effectiveness of solar canopies under real stat ion load profiles and maintenance conditions. A sixth concerns last -
mile services: locker adoption and parcel -collection traffic should be linked to incremental retail conversion. A
seventh concerns QSR partnership structures and whether fixed -rent, revenu e-share or franchise models produce
different station -level returns. EV transition remains an additional gap requiring joint measurement of charging
duration, retail attachment and electricity demand.

For ope rators, the practical sequence should be baseline, contain risk, optimize, diversify, validate and scale.

performance, distributed solar, well -sited charging, safe workshop operations, traceable waste handling and

evidence review before replication.

Limitations:-
This study has four limitations. First, the evidence base is interdisciplinary and heterogeneous, so effect sizes from
engineering, behavioral, automotive-service, logistics and mobility studies are not statistically pooled. Second, direct
peer-reviewed evidence on Saudi non -fuel retail and station -level waste/service economics remains limited;
international findings are t herefore treated as transferable mechanisms requiring local validation, not as Saudi effect
estimates. Third, official regulations and company disclosures are used for current Saudi context but are not
substitutes for independent causal research. Fourth, n o completed primary survey, interview or station dataset
accompanied the manuscript; consequently, the primary research component is presented as a field -validation
protocol and no fabricated primary findings are reported. Investment forecasting should rel y on the proposed pilots
and measured local data before network-wide commitments are made.

Conclusion:-
Sustainable non -fuel development at Saudi service stations should not be framed only as a relationship between
energy efficiency, customer experience a nd retail growth. The service station is increasingly a portfolio of mobility,
maintenance, hospitality, logistics and environmental functions. Energy efficiency and solar canopies can lower the
resource cost of reliable operations; automotive services can  create repeat need -based visits; QSR partnerships can
increase destination value and dwell time; last -mile services can add digital-logistics traffic; and charging can create
new mobility demand. These opportunities become sustainable only when associated  waste streams, safety
requirements, circulation constraints and partner economics are managed together.

The revised framework therefore positions the station as a measurable operating portfolio rather than a collection of
independent projects. It links l iterature evidence to a primary field -validation process using customer surveys,
manager interviews, site audits and operating data. Recommendations are strongest when all four evidence types
point in the same direction. For Saudi operators, the practical logic is iterative: establish a baseline; control service
and waste risks; optimize energy and customer flow; pilot vehicle services, PV canopies, QSR, last -mile and
charging concepts according to site format; measure incremental environmental and commerci al effects; and scale
only those combinations that create verified value. This approach directly addresses the limitations of secondary -
only analysis while preserving academic integrity where primary data have not yet been collected.
