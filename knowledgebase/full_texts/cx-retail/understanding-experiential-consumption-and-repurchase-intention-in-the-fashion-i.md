Abstract
	 Customer	 experience	(CX)	enables	firms	to	gain	a	competitive	advantage,	especially
in	the	fashion	industry.	The	current	study	focuses	on	analyzing	the	role	of	susceptibility	to
social	influence	and	brand	love	(BL)	in	generating	repurchase	intent	(RPI)	in	the	context	of
experiential	purchases,	explained	through	the	lens	of	Social	Identity	Theory	(SIT).	Snowball
sampling	 was	 employed	 to	 collect	 data	 from	 392	 respondents	 for	 analysis.	 Smart-PLS
Structural	Equation	Modeling	PLS-SEM	was	used.	These	results	are	consistent	with	the	SIT
principles,	 suggesting	 that	 repurchase	in	 fashion	 operates	 through	 social	 validation	 and
emotional	paths,	as	susceptibility	to	social	influence	and	brand	love	both	act	as	mediators
between	repurchase	intention	and	experiential	consumption.	Specifically,	Brand	Love	(BL)
mediates	the	relationship	between	Pleasurable	experiences	(PleE)	and	Repurchase	Intentions
(RPI),	In	contrast,	susceptibility	to	social	influence	(SSI)	mediates	the	relationship	between
Meaningful	Experiences	(MeE)	and	Repurchase	Intentions	(RPI).	This	study	was	conducted
in	the	South	Asian	country	of	Pakistan.	Other	regions	are	encouraged	to	be	included	in	future
studies.	 Practitioners	 can	 focus	 on	 hedonic	 experiences,	 diluted	 with	 a	 social	 validation
element	would	effectively	work	in	generating	long-term	commitment.	Through	SIT	principles,
it	is	explained	that	not	only	BL	but	also	SSI	play	a	strong	role	in	achieving	consumer	long-
term	commitment,	depending	on	the	experiences	provided.
Keywords: Customer experience; fashion; susceptibility to social influence; brand love;
repurchase intention; social identity theory.

1.  Introduction
 According to a study published by Harvard Business Review, for a brand, getting a new
customer would cost around 25% more than retaining an old customer (Amy, 2014). Further,
according to Reichheld (2001), a study found that brands can increase their profitability by 25% to
95% with just a retention rate of 5%. But in continuously evaluating and highly competitive sectors
like the fashion industry, maintaining consumer repeat purchases always remains a challenge.
While Fashion brands bring novelty to their products by offering a wide range of products with
extensive attributes to cope with this challenge (Chowdhury & Akter, 2018) . With continually
evolving customer tastes and preferences in the modern market, customer repurchase cannot be
gained merely through product satisfaction Becker and Jaakkola (2020), but also through overall
customer experience. CX needs to be analyzed in detail and needs to have alignment with the
customer's self, emotional, and social identity (Fujita et al., 2018; Kuuru et al., 2020). Fashion
consumption is used for self-representation and expression, which extends the use of fashion
products beyond just functional value (Pandey & Bajpai, 2025).
 Whereas in the apparel industry, specifically clothing, has different purpose to serve
in different regions; some use it for enhancing appearance, others for association with certain
groups or lifestyles, cultural reflection, etc.(Anyanwu & Chiana, 2022). In shaping purchase or
repurchase intentions, social identity also plays a key role (Kleine, 1993; Khamis et al., 2012).
The bond to pull customers for continued purchase with the brand weakens when brands fail to
tailor consumer inclination to social or group association with the experiences provided (Dawes,
2021), causing negative word of mouth, brand hate, etc. (Ullrich & Brunner, 2015; Zhang Ya et
al., 2020).
 In addition to functional value, customers are becoming increasingly concerned and
seeking exceptional experiences, leaving brands with no choice but to provide emotional and
entertaining experiences (Kim & Sullivan, 2019). Customer experience has been considered a
crucial component and a driving factor in repurchase intent in the fashion industry (Nilsson & Wall,
2017). However, for social and psychological association to align with customers' experiences has
often become a challenge for brands. There are multiple factors influencing the consumption of
fashion-related products, like culture, family, opinion leadership, personality, and lifestyle, etc.,
that combine to affect the need for consumer experience and interpret fashion (Lawan & Zanna,
2013; Taufique, 2017; Abrar et al., 2019; Lynch & Barnes, 2020). Specifically, the apparel industry
is considered a high involvement item; here, customers often want to see, touch, and try before
purchasing for sensory engagement (internally) and social acceptance (externally) (Citrin et al.,
2003). These factors highlight the importance of delivering not only personalized but also socially
and emotionally meaningful experiences for the brand.
 Brands must craft experiences that reflect who consumers are and the groups they belong
to, stimulate brand love, and encourage repurchase. Yet the number of individual differences, such

purchase behavior. Accordingly, this research asks: What is the role of experiential consumption
and SSI in generating BL and RPI in the fashion industry? This study explains the framework

intent and maintaining long-term loyalty that reflects social and identity-based associations rather
than mere satisfaction. Furthermore, this will help practitioners gain a clearer understanding of
how different kinds of experiences can generate customers repurchase intentions, and whether this
effect is always mediated by brand love or if social influence can also be considered as a construct
that contributes to the formation of repurchase intention.

2.1  Customer experience CX
 Traditional business strategies, for example, differentiating products through price,
delivery, lead time, etc., are no longer considered sustainable competitive strategies. Other

emerging differentiators have joined the club, and one of them is CX (Carbone & Haeckel, 2005;
Netigate, 2023). CX has been studied over the past few decades. A vast amount of literature is
present on the topic, with roots lying between the 1960s and 1990s, during which customer focus
shifted to the overall experience related to the product provided by brands (Shaw & Ivens, 2002).
CX is considered the battleground that helps to achieve a sustainable competitive advantage for

 Godovykh and Tasci (2020)  defined customer experience as “the totality of cognitive,
affective, sensory, and behavioral customer responses during all stages of the consumption process,

consumption in two different ways: pleasurable and meaningful. Other studies have shown that
happiness and satisfaction correlate with consumption experiences provided by brands (Schmitt
et al., 2015).
 Pleasurable experiential consumption is focused on sensorial, pleasurable events,
enjoyable moments, episodes, and other such types of experiences (Kahneman et al., 2004;
Schmitt et al., 2015; Brakus et al., 2022). Pleasurable experiences are hedonic in nature, where the
individual tries to get maximum pleasure and minimum pain from the experience; therefore, such
experiences are present-oriented (Baumeister et al., 2013).
 Meaningfulness of experiences could also be considered as eudaimonic; its orientation
is about the past, present, and future, concerned with the meaningful goals and achievements of
the consumer in life (Schmitt et al., 2015). Unlike pleasurable experiences (hedonic) or utilitarian
(functional aspect), meaningful consumption is done by keeping in mind the long-term effect.
Such experiences help customers with identity, life purpose, personal goals, and emotional
attachment  (Huta & Ryan, 2010; Brakus et al., 2022).This study has investigated these two types
of experiential consumption in fashion, specifically clothing/ outfits, to find out their impact on BL
and SSI, and will be explained through the theory of social identity.
2.2  Theoretical underpinning
2.2.1  Social Identity Theory (SIT)
 Consumers often use brands to construct and express their social identity (Mccracken,
1989; Fournier, 1998; Oyserman, 2009; Escalas, 2013). Social identity helps in understanding
customers’ consumption intentions, especially when individuals engage in socially influenced or
collaborative consumption (Elliott, 2020; Małecka et al., 2022). Rooted in social psychology, SIT
was developed by Tajfel (1979) and expanded by Henri Tajfel and Sidanius (2016) explains how
individuals’ cognitions and behaviors are guided by group processes rather than individual factors
(Trepte, 2006).

 As per SIT, individuals see themselves and others as part of various social groups based
on factors such as gender, age, culture, organization, or lifestyle (Ashforth & Mael, 1989). Such
classification helps them to create a feeling of belonging by differentiating between “in-groups”
(those they identify with) and “out-groups” (those they differ with). Thus, through this process,
individuals assimilate group norms and see their group’s outcomes as part of their own self-
concept. By supporting ingroup and differentiating self from out-group, helping individuals
enhance self-esteem, and maintaining social identity (Abrams & Hogg, 1988).
 Tajfel (1979) emphasized three key principles of SIT: 1. social categorization, 2. social
comparison, 3. social identity. In the context of the current study, through social categorization,
consumers cognitively classify themselves and others based on their driving forces, which can
be either internally driven or externally socially driven for experience-seeking. Those seeking
short-term pleasure (e.g., sensory stimulation, which is an internally driven category) represent
pleasurable experiential consumption seekers, while those who seek long-term value, social
recognition, or deeper meaning are grouped into meaningful consumption seekers (which are
externally driven through social cues and influence category). They associate themselves with
certain kinds of experiences, seeking a group based on self-concept, forming social identification.
 As individuals identify with these groups i.e pleasurable experiences seeker consumers
and meaningful experiences seeker consumers, they adopt group-endorsed norms and behaviors,
increasing their SSI and brand love- the degree to which they align their consumption with group
expectations- after creating social identity with these two groups  (Ashforth & Mael, 1989).
Through social comparison, consumers evaluate brands and experience offers relative to others,
deriving satisfaction and validation from conforming to socially accepted consumption patterns.
Finally, self-esteem is reinforced when consumers repurchase experiences that affirm their group
identity and maintain social approval.
2.3  Brand love (BL)
 Brand Love, a psychological construct, originates from Sternberg's Triangular Theory
of Love, introduced in 1986 (Sternberg, 1986) . Carroll and Ahuvia (2006) defined BL as “the
degree of passionate emotional attachment a satisfied consumer has for a particular trade name”.
To create BL among customers, companies should focus on providing positive experiences to
their customers (Suetrong, 2023). In addition, a study conducted by Babu and Abraham (2021)
found evidence that experiences and BL have a direct and positive relationship. Fashion-related
products are considered more associated with branding than other categories of products (Fernie
et al., 1997).
 In the fashion industry, customers buy products that are aligned with their personal
goals (immediate excitement, emotional gratification via pleasurable experiences consumption)
with social acceptance (via meaningful experiential consumption) as explained by the social

2.4  Susceptibility to social influence (SSI)
 Human beings are referred to as social animals because their social groups and relationships
are fundamental to human nature. This results in human interdependency on each other, and due
to this interdependency, they influence each other's decisions on the journey of their lives. A
study on fashion consumption Anyanwu and Chiana (2022) mentioned that normally consumers
make rational decisions based on analysis of information gathered from the pre-purchase phase,
culture, opinion leadership, social class, family, and ethnicity that have an influence on consumer
behavior, especially in fashion consumption. So, social influence also plays an important role
and is considered one of the major constructs that can affect both online and offline groups/
people(Bhukya & Paul, 2023) . Social influence is a two-dimensional construct that is as follows:
 Normative influence is further divided into value-expressive, which is consumer
inclination towards adaptation of the same consumption pattern as the group consumer wants
to associate themselves with for self-concept enhancement Kelman (2017)  and Utilitarian
influence is to comply with the expectations of people for rewards and to avoid punishments
(Burnkrant & Cousineau, 1975; Bearden et al., 1989). Compliance involves conforming to others'
expectations to gain rewards or avoid punishment, which is mediated by others. Deutsch and
Gerard (1955) defined informative influence as “the tendency to accept information from others
as evidence about reality”. Informative influence refers to the process through which individuals
make inferences based on the behavior of others or obtain information from them(Park & Lessig,
1977). Informational influence takes place via the individual process of internalizing information.
Internalization happens when another person's information adds to one's knowledge about
a certain aspect of the surroundings. Research indicates that informational influence is able to
change consumer decision making processes, in particular in the way products are judged (Cohen
& Golden, 1972; Burnkrant & Cousineau, 1975; Pincus & Waters, 1977) and product/brand
selections (Park & Lessig, 1977; Bearden & Etzel, 1982).
 As per SIT, once through social categorization, consumers are placed in a socially relevant
group, creating identification. Social identification pushes customers to obey group expectations
and keep group norms, which increases their allegiance to SSI (with in the group), as consumer

2.5  Repurchase intentions (RPI)

quality, perceived value, and information related to the product influence consumers' buying
intentions (Setyaningrum et al., 2022). On the other hand, customer satisfaction, CX, trust, WOM,

that BL has a significantly positive impact on RPI (Dewi & Kussudyarsana, 2024). As previously
investigated, there are many risks involved in the purchasing process, and today, consumers have
many alternatives in the market, which makes it difficult for consumers to decide; therefore,
this love-like feeling with the brand is the best tool to minimize risks and encourage customer
repurchase (Suetrong, 2023).

clothing retailer, customers’ expectations vary. In the same study, it was emphasized that customer
affordability and customer experience are what customers expect, while for premium clothing
retailers, convenience should be added to provide more value. A study conducted by Nilsson
and Wall (2017) found that customer experience leads to satisfaction and repurchase intention.

3.  Methodology
 This quantitative study was conducted in the twin cities of Pakistan, Islamabad and
Rawalpindi, which were selected for data collection. Due to their complementary urban
characteristics. Islamabad is a well-planned city, whereas Rawalpindi is semi-planned, comprising
both urban and peri-urban areas. Being adjacent, consumers in both cities have nearly equal access
to all leading clothing brands. Collecting data from these cities allows for a better understanding
of the types of experiences consumers prefer and how they evaluate these experiences under
social influence across different sectors of their lives. The sample size was calculated using the
well-known method for a survey, especially when dealing with a large population. The best suited
was Cochran’s formula of 1977, which is calculated to be a 385-sample size that represents the
right accuracy for a large population or any unknown population (Israel, 1992).
 The sampling technique utilized for this study was snowball sampling (it facilitated the
personal collection of data from respondents involved in outfit purchasing in the last 3 months for
their own use, ensuring that participants were directly relevant to the study’s focus on experiential
fashion consumption), and data were collected on 5-point Likert scales for all constructs from
respondents from major shopping malls in the selected cities. Therefore, primary data is used
for this cross-sectional study. The framework of this study consists of five variables. The scale
for pleasurable PleE and meaningful experiences MeE has been adapted from Peterson  et
al.(2005) With 6 items each, these constructs helped understanding customer preferences towards
pleasurable and meaningful experiences, Susceptibility to Social Influence (SII) has been measured
and operationalized with a 12-item scale adapted from Stöckli and Hofer (2020) which helped in
understand customer susceptibility to social influence, a 6-item scale measuring Brand love (BL)

 Smart PLS4 is application is used for data analysis, and PLS-SEM is the analytical

of 50 respondents. The validity and reliability of all reflective constructs were examined using

reflective constructs, including both higher-order and lower-order constructs in the measurement
model, met the required standards and were suitable for further analysis.
 Once the pilot study was completed, data collection for the main study continued from
March to June 2025, and a total of 504 respondents participated. Out of 504 responses, 391 were
considered for final analysis for the study; others were excluded based on screening questions
about whether the respondent had made clothing-related purchases in the previous 3 months or
not, and whether the respondent was from Islamabad or Rawalpindi. Out of 391 respondents, 261
were from Islamabad, and 130 were from Rawalpindi. In addition, 264 respondents were male,
while 127 were female.

5.  Discussion, Implications and Conclusion
 The focus of this study is on understanding the role of experiential consumption
(pleasurable and meaningful experiences) and susceptibility to social influence in generating
repurchase intentions within the fashion industry. This study empirically confirmed that
brand love and susceptibility to social influence both play a significant role in generating
repurchase intentions of experiential consumption in the fashion apparel industry (H2A &
H2B).
 The findings further confirmed that experiential consumption alone does not directly
lead to repurchase intentions (H4A & H4B), thereby addressing the first research objective.
Instead, repurchase intentions are primarily driven through two key mechanisms: brand love
and susceptibility to social influence (i.e., conformity to norms and information from one’s
social circle). In the fashion industry, repurchase can be obtained merely through brand
love (H3A) instead of susceptibility to social influence (H3B) for customers focusing on
pleasurable experiences, while for customers with a focus on meaningful experiences are
strongly influenced by both sustainability and social influence (H3D) and Brand love (H3C).
 Specifically, the study shows that customers engaging in pleasurable experiential
consumption are more likely to repurchase fashion/clothing items when brands foster an
emotional connection through brand love (H3A), rather than through social influence (H3B).
Conversely, customers engaging in meaningful experiential consumption are more inclined
to repurchase similar products under the influence of social norms and peer information,
rather than through brand love (H3D significance level is greater than H3C). This finding
directly addresses the second research objective. Together, the insights from RO1 and RO2
provide a comprehensive answer to the overarching research question.

5.1  Implications
5.1.1  Theoretical Implications
 By extending the Social Identity Theory (Tajfel, 1979; Ashforth & Mael, 1989) further,
this study showed that identity processes are not just based on traditional group memberships
but also on experiential consumption orientations in fashion contexts. Specifically, consumers
think of themselves as cognitive categories belonging to meaningful (MeE) and pleasurable
(PleE) experiential groups, thus extending social categorization to experience-based identity
distinctions.
 The findings also indicate that social identification, measured as Susceptibility to
Social Influence (SSI), is a key mediator in the transformation of meaningful experiences into
brand love and repurchase intention, including significant full and serial mediation effects
(MeE SSI BL RPI). However, pleasurable experiences show a more direct affective pathway
to brand love and repurchase intention and do not mediate much through SSI, this indicates
that hedonic consumption is not as dependent on identity internalization processes.
 Altogether, the study complements SIT by revealing two identity-consumption
mechanisms, an identity-driven one in which meaningful experiences are socially constructed
and maintained through SSI and brand love, and a hedonic one in which pleasurable
experiences are less dependent on social identity structures. It extends SIT into experiential
consumption research by demonstrating that identity relevance determines whether consumer
behavior is socially mediated or emotionally driven in the formation of brand relationships
and loyalty outcomes.

5.1.2  Practical / Managerial Implications
 Results of this study have indicated that loyalty cannot be crafted alone through
pleasurable experiences; in fact, managers need to design experiences that evoke emotional
attachment through pleasurable experiences, by adding comfort, aesthetics, and trendiness,
etc., to the apparel, which would generate brand love. While meaningful experiences create
loyalty through mechanisms of social validation so marketers should invest in strategies that
help consumers get social approval and feel that they belong to their fashion choice by adding
values, social meaning, and lifestyle-related attributes.
 Finally, managers should not only focus on short-term assessment of experience
strategies for the long term, a mix of hedonic experiences with chances of social recognition
may also be a strong path to brand engagement/attachment and loyalty in the fashion industry.
5.2  Conclusion
 The current study illuminated how experiential consumption is converted into
repurchase intentions in the fashion industry. Furthermore, it also confirmed that experiential
consumption does not convert into loyalty in a direct manner; instead, it operates via
social influence and emotional antecedents. Pleasant experiences (PleE) primarily operate
upon repurchase intentions via brand love, while great experiences (MeE) operate upon
repurchase intentions via social influence. Furthermore, postulates of social identity theory,
social categorization, social comparison, and social identification explain both avenues of
experiential consumption to repurchase intentions. Practitioners should take home that in
order to create prolonged brand loyalty in the competitive fashion market, experiences should
not only be enjoyable, but also meaningful and socially significant.
5.3  Limitation
 This study investigated the apparel/clothing sector; to expand generalizability, future
studies are encouraged to include other industries such as hospitality, furniture, and grocery
supermarket retailers. Analyzing various cultural settings would allow for more culturally
sensitive and locally appropriate insights. To understand loyalty over time, future studies
should focus on longitudinal/experimental studies. In the current study, no moderators were
used; future researchers could examine moderators such as gender, income, etc. Scholars
are encouraged to add focus on other experiential dimensions, such as digital experiences
and engagement-related experiences, to dive deep into the topic for better insights. Since
the current study's focus was to examine this framework through the social identity theory,
scholars are encouraged to examine this framework through the lens of other theories, like
symbolic interactionism, self-congruity theory, self-discrepancy theory, etc. This is a cross-
sectional and self-reported study; researchers can also employ a longitudinal setting to
