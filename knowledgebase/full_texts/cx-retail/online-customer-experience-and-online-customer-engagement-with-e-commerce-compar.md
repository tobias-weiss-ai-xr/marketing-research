Abstract
The objective of this research is to investigate the relationship between online customer experience (OCX), online customer
engagement (OCE), and behavioural intention and advocacy in an e-commerce context, comparing online and omnichan-
nel consumer journeys. The SOR paradigm is used. A survey was designed for customers who shopped in an e-commerce
store and followed three different types of consumer journeys. A multi-group analysis was conducted. The results of the
multigroup analysis show that all the causal relationships of the SOR model are fulfilled and that the consumer journey is
a moderating variable. Most of these effects have hardly been investigated in the literature. In addition, the high predictive
power of the model on behavioural intentions and advocacy was tested. Finally, two effects are identified: (a) omnichannel
journey strengthens behavioural OCE and strong advocacy, and (b) visiting multiple channels strengthens the impact of
OCE on behavioural intentions.

Introduction
In 2024, Asia led global e-commerce revenues, with a vol-
ume of 1972 billion US dollars, while the USA and Europe
had, respectively, a volume of 1435 billion and 632.7 billion
US dollars (Statista, 2025). Online purchases have become
highly relevant globally, as evidenced by the fierce competi-
tion in e-commerce and the large amount of resources that
companies are investing in online strategy (Park et al., 2023).
Since 2010, variables such as online customer experience
(OCX) and online customer engagement (OCE) have gained
prominence for their explanatory power of behaviours asso-
ciated with consumer online purchase behaviour (Lim et al.,
2022). Kacprzak and Hensel (2023) conducted a systematic
review of the literature on OCX, noting the large number of
papers published on the topic in the last decade. Keyword
co-occurrence analysis identified customer engagement as
an outcome of OCX, although surprisingly few papers have
examined the relationship between these variables. Even
more surprising is the lack of research into the relationship
between OCX and OCE (Kacprzak & Hensel, 2023).
Conceptually, OCX and OCE are close but distinct con-
cepts. OCX is the “consumer’s cognitive, emotional, and
behavioural response to customer-organisation interactions
that occur through digital channels (e.g. websites, social
media, mobile apps) during the pre-purchase, purchase, and
post-purchase stages of consumption” (Kacprzak & Hen -
sel, 2023). On the other hand, based on Hollebeek et al.’s
(2014) and Lim et al. (2022) definitions of customer engage-
ment, OCE can be defined as a positively valenced cognitive,
emotional, and behavioural commitment of the consumer
to a digital channel (e.g. e-commerce), generated from
interaction with the multi-touchpoints in an online envi-
ronment (Hollebeek et al., 2014; Lim et al., 2022). While
OCX is the consumer’s evaluation of all interactions with

the touchpoints in the online consumer journey, OCE is an
intrinsic motivation, emotional connection, or psychological

which can trigger a range of voluntary behaviours such as
purchase intention, retention, recommendations, loyalty,
self-disclosure, word-of-mouth (WOM), blogging, and

et al., 2022).
Although OCX and OCE with an e-commerce have
received some attention from researchers, the relationship
between the two concepts is a research gap that needs to be
addressed (Lim et al., 2022; Taheri et al., 2024). Recently,
some studies have started to develop OCE by analysing the
impact of social support (Molinillo et al., 2020b) and web-
site characteristics (Taheri et al., 2024) on customer engage-
ment in social media, as well as the impact of OCE on web-
site loyalty (Molinillo et al., 2020b).
Another research gap identified by Kacprzak and Hensel
(2023) is the study of OCX from an omnichannel consumer
journey perspective. OCX has been investigated separately
in online and offline contexts (Kacprzak & Hensel, 2023),
with few studies analysing the omnichannel context (Al-
Nabhani et al., 2022; Gao et al., 2021). Gao et al. (2021)
claim that the degree of incongruence between online
and offline channels influences consumer responses in the
omnichannel consumer journey. In this line, the Marketing
Science Institute (2024) has identified customer journeys
and customer experience as a priority research topic.
Furthermore, a new vision of the consumer journey is
emerging due to the impact of the digital environment and
AI (Rolka & Joachimsthaler, 2025). Contrary to the vision
of a well-defined road trip, with a starting point, a few land-
marks, and a final purchase, reality often shows a chaotic,
unpredictable, and non-linear journey, similar to the flight of
a bumblebee (Rolka & Joachimsthaler, 2025). All phases of
the traditional journey overlap, and consumers switch rap-
idly between channels and moments. Therefore, digital chan-
nels have become the backbone of the consumer journey,
and their characteristics (no physical face-to-face contact, no
switching costs between online channels, consumer conveni-
ence, and no time constraints) have permeated the consumer
journey (Kacprzak & Hensel, 2023).
This study examines three types of online consumer jour-
neys: mono-online journey (the customer only visited the
e-commerce website where they made a purchase), multi-
online journey (the customer visited several websites/apps
before making a purchase on an e-commerce website), and
omnichannel journey (the customer visited several web-
sites/apps and physical shops before making a purchase on
an e-commerce website). The SOR conceptual framework
is adopted, which has been widely used by researchers in
recent years but has had very limited use in analysing the
consumer journey (Hallikainen et al., 2019; Pereira et al.,
2023). A search for academic works that have applied the
SOR framework to the consumer journey yields a limited
number of studies, focusing mainly on tourism (Yadav et al.,
2022) and the adoption of new technologies (Shaikh et al.,
2023; Memon et al., 2025; Koul and Jarotia, 2025).
The objective of this paper is to study the relationship
between OCX, OCE, and behavioural intention and advo-
cacy in an e-commerce context, comparing online and
omnichannel consumer journeys. The stimulus–organ-
ism–response (SOR) paradigm is used, where OCX is the
stimulus from the environment, OCE is the organism (inter-
nal state), and behavioural intention and advocacy are the
responses. The overall objective can be divided into two
research questions:
RQ1: Following the SOR framework, is there a mediating
relationship between OCX and behavioural intention and
advocacy through OCE in an e-commerce context?
RQ2: Are there differences in the relationships of the
SOR model depending on the type of consumer journey
(mono-online, multi-online, and omnichannel)?
The work is structured into several sections. First, the
SOR model is explained and applied to the phenomenon
under study, developing the hypotheses. Second, the meth-
odology of a survey designed for customers who shopped
in an e-commerce store and who followed three different
types of consumer journey (mono-online, multi-online, and
omnichannel) is outlined. Finally, the results are presented
where the relationships between the variables in the total
sample were examined, and a multigroup analysis was then
carried out to identify differences between the three con-
sumer journeys.
This study makes two contributions to the literature.
First, it tests the mediating role of OCE in the relationships
between OCX and behavioural intentions and advocacy in
the context of e-commerce, using the SOR model as a theo-
retical framework. Second, it demonstrates the moderating
role of the type of consumer journey, identifying differences
in the relationships between variables depending on the type
of online journey.
Online customer experience (OCX)
and online customer engagement (OCE)
SOR model
The SOR model comes from the field of environmental psy-
chology and postulates that different aspects of the environ-
ment act as stimuli (S) that affect people’s internal states (O),
which drive their behavioural responses (R) (Luqman et al.,
2017; Mehrabian & Russell, 1974). In consumer behaviour,

stimuli are external to people and stem from both marketing
mix variables and other inputs from the environment (Chang
et al.,  2011; Bagozzi, 1986). Organism refers to the inter -
nal processes and structures involved in connecting exter -
nal stimuli with consumer actions, reactions, or responses
(Chang et al., 2011). They are perceptual, psychological,
feeling, and thinking activities (Bagozzi, 1986). Response
is the end result of consumer decisions, which take the form
of positive or avoidant actions towards a given environment
(Chang et al., 2011).
Taheri et al. (2024) consider that the SOR paradigm is
well suited for studying OCE. Stimulus is the impact of mar-
keting variables and other environmental elements surround-
ing the purchase in an e-commerce (Arora, 1982). Stimulus
is associated with OCX, which implies the evaluation of
touchpoints along the consumer journey. Organism refers
to the cognitive, affective, and conative processing that the
consumer makes of experiences in similar situations and the
relationship of the e-commerce with the individual’s value
system (Arora, 1982). Organism has a close relationship
with customer engagement with e-commerce because it is
a psychological state or emotional connection between the
consumer and e-commerce (OCE). Responses derive from
a complex cognitive and behavioural process developed in
organism, which will result in favourable or unfavourable
actions towards the purchase in an e-commerce (Arora,
1982). The literature establishes consumer engagement
behaviours (WOM, recommendations, helping other cus-
tomers, blogging, writing reviews) as a component closely
linked to customer engagement (Brodie et al., 2011 ; Lim
et al., 2022).
The stimulus (OCX) and the organism (OCE)
The online consumer journey is the set of touchpoints with
which a consumer interacts in digital channels before, dur -
ing, and after a shopping experience (Hamilton & Price,
2019). Several characteristics specific to e-commerce define
the online consumer journey and influence OCX and OCE.
They include the absence of physical face-to-face contact,
the more intense need for information, and the absence of
time constraints (Kacprzak & Hensel, 2023).
Although the dimensionality of OCX has been little stud-
ied compared to the offline context, OCX is considered to be
a multidimensional construct (Kacprzak & Hensel, 2023).
Many studies have followed the dimensionality of customer
experience, such as the hedonic/utilitarian (Al-Nabhani
et al., 2022; Pleyers & Poncin, 2020) or cognitive/affective
dimensions (Barari et al., 2020; Molinillo et al., 2020a), or
have made inferences from these propositions (O’Connor
et al., 2021; Cachero-Martínez & Vázquez-Casielles, 2021).
Bleier et al. (2019) propose four specific dimensions of OCX
that take into account the specific characteristics of digital
channels: (a) informativeness (cognitive dimension), (b)
entertainment (affective dimension), (c) social presence
(warmth, sociability, and sense of human contact), and (d)
sensory appeal (stimulation of the consumer’s senses).
OCE has faced the same issues as OCX in establishing its
dimensionality. In marketing, it has mostly been considered
as having three dimensions (Lim et al., 2022): cognitive,
emotional, and behavioural (Hollebeek et al., 2014; Taheri
et al., 2024). Based on this dimensional structure and taking
into account the specific characteristics of digital channels,
some authors have proposed a more specific dimensional -
ity for OCE (Molinillo et al., 2020b; Taheri et al., 2024):
(a) absorption (customer’s immersion in the online experi-
ence), (b) dedication (customer’s interest in and enthusiasm
for e-commerce), and (c) vigour (consumer’s psychological
attitude and willingness to invest resources).
Regarding the relationship between OCX and OCE in
an e-commerce context, the literature on customer experi-
ence and customer engagement considers the former to be
a precursor of the latter (Ahmad et al., 2022; Brodie et al.,
2011; Hollebeek et al., 2014; Kacprzak & Hensel, 2023;
Roy et al., 2022). Ahmad et al. (2022) tested in Pakistan
that online shoppers who have had a good OCX generate
positive emotions directly related to customer engagement
(involvement, passion, enthusiasm, absorption, and energy).
Roy et al. (2022) tested in Australia that the emotional,
sensory, and social dimensions of customer experience
positively influence customer engagement by increasing
customer commitment. From the perspective of the SOR
theoretical framework, OCE is an organism, while OCX is
the stimulus derived from the environmental factors of the
online consumer journey that influence OCE (Kacprzak &
Hensel, 2023). A satisfying OCX with e-commerce creates
a psychological state, an emotional connection, or a posi-
tive intrinsic motivation that triggers voluntary behaviours
beyond purchase (e.g. behavioural intention, WOM, self-
disclosure, comments, loyalty) (Ahmad et al., 2022; Roy
et al., 2022). Therefore, a direct and positive causal rela-
tionship between OCX (stimulus) and OCE (organism) with
e-commerce is hypothesised (Ahmad et al., 2022; Taheri
et al., 2024).
H1: OCX with e-commerce has a direct and positive
influence on OCE with e-commerce.
The response to OCE
The SOR perspective states that once a stimulus affects the
organism, responses are generated (Taheri et al., 2024).
Behavioural intentions, loyalty, and WOM are the responses
most commonly associated with customer engagement
behaviours (Khan et al., 2020; Lim et al., 2022; Molinillo
et al., 2020b; Park et al., 2023). High levels of customer

engagement are expected to lead to superior marketing out-
comes, including sales growth, cost reduction, referrals,
co-creation experiences, and profitability (Hollebeek et al.,
2014).
The theory of reasoned action defines behavioural inten-
tion as “a degree to which a person, after a subjective evalu-
ation of a particular object, plans to engage in future behav-
iour” (Kacprzak & Hensel, 2023). The engaged customer
develops a strong psychological connection/commitment
with e-commerce, which increases the intention to revisit or
repurchase (Molinillo et al., 2020b). The literature has tested
the existence of a direct and positive relationship between
OCE and behavioural intentions, such that the greater the
customer’s psychological connection with e-commerce, the
greater the loyalty intentions (revisit and repurchase) (Park
et al., 2023; Lim et al., 2022; Molinillo et al., 2020b; Zheng
et al., 2022). Barari et al. (2021), in a meta-analysis of 184
empirical studies, concluded that a positive relationship
between attitudinal engagement, behavioural engagement,
and loyalty can be generalised. In the online context, Ahmad
et al. (2022) tested in Pakistan that customer engagement of
an online shopper generates a commitment in the customer
that favours a positive intention to develop different positive
activities for the brand or company. Mainolfi et al. (2022)
tested that blog engagement positively influences continued
use in Italian and American people who use social media
networks. Finally, Zheng et al. (2022) concluded that cus-
tomer engagement behaviours in live streaming positively
influence purchase intention.
H2: OCE with e-commerce has a positive influence
on a customer’s behavioural intention towards e-com-
merce.
Although WOM has been demonstrated to be a response
to OCE (Molinillo et al., 2020b), advocacy has not been stud-
ied in this context. Sweeney et al. (2020) argue that customer
advocacy is a distinct form of WOM. The main difference is
the level of enthusiasm and passion with which customers
talk about their experiences with a company, making them
highly engaged advocates. Advocacy is an extreme form of
positive WOM, characterised by strongly worded and forceful
recommendations. Although in their seminal work Sweeney
et al. (2020) do not consider customer engagement or OCE as
a precursor to advocacy, the link between OCE and advocacy
is direct, as the greater the customer engagement, emotional
connection, or intrinsic motivation, the greater the likeli-
hood of activating militant and enthusiastic advocacy about
e-commerce (Hoang et al., 2023).
Advocacy reflects a closer relationship between the cus-
tomer and e-commerce and, because of its voluntary nature,
is considered a customer engagement behaviour. Some
studies have tested the relationship between these OCE and
advocacy. Abu-Salih et al. (2024), using natural language
inference (NLI) and AI on Twitter comments in Australia,
identified a positive and significant relationship between
OCE and advocacy. Aljarah et al. (2024) also tested this
relationship in an experimental design, finding that when
a brand is familiar, participants showed a higher level of
online brand advocacy. Finally, Malik and Pradhan (2025)
tested a significant and positive relationship between cus-
tomer engagement and brand advocacy in the context of a
gamified experience on e-tailing websites.
H3: OCE with e-commerce has a positive influence on
customer advocacy of e-commerce.
Consumer journey as a moderating variable
The consumer journey is the set of touchpoints with which
a consumer interacts before, during, and after a shopping
experience (Hamilton & Price, 2019; Li et al., 2025). From
the point of view of the SOR model, the type of consumer
journey (online vs. offline) causes the external stimuli faced
by the consumer to be different, which implies the existence
of a moderating effect.
The online consumer journey is characterised as a shop-
ping behaviour that uses digital channels exclusively, which
implies the absence of face-to-face interactions, and a
greater need for information on the part of the consumer, but
also greater transparency, flexibility, seamlessness, and con-
venience (Gao et al., 2021; Kacprzak & Hensel, 2023). The
omnichannel consumer journey combines online and offline
interactions, taking advantage of both contexts. Engaging
multiple senses and interacting with the product/service and
staff in a physical store enhances customer experience and
engagement (Al-Nabhani et al., 2022).
In omnichannel shopping, offline channels facilitate the
role of digital channels, as long as there is synchronisation
between them (Al-Nabhani et al., 2022; Li et al., 2025).
Consumers want consistent and interchangeable experi-
ences when switching between channels; namely, they
want the OCX to be congruent with the offline customer
experience (Fang & Li, 2025; Verhoef et al., 2015). Gao
et al. (2021) identify customer experience incongruence
in the omnichannel journey when the OCX is better or
worse than the offline customer experience. When customer
experience incongruence in digital and physical channels is
high, the response (behavioural intentions and advocacy) is
negative, whereas when incongruence is low, the response
is positive.
In the meta-analysis of customer engagement behav -
iour, Barari et al. (2021) identify the context of engage-
ment (online versus offline), the type of industry (services
versus manufacturing), the type of product (hedonic versus
utilitarian), the cultural context (distance from power, indi-
vidualism, masculinity, uncertainty avoidance), and control

variables as moderators of the consumer journey. Product
category has also been identified as a moderator: Behnam
et al. (2021) found that the nature of the offering (goods
versus services) moderates the effects of customer engage-
ment, and Lee et al. (2025) found that hedonic products
generate less engagement in consumer journeys on digi-
tal platforms. Finally, consumer characteristics, whether
related to personality or demographic profile, have also
been shown to have a moderating effect on customer com-
mitment behaviours (Barari et al., 2021; Manyanga et al.,
2022).
In this paper, we focus on the moderating effect of the
online versus offline consumer journey, a dimension that has
recently begun to be studied in the literature. Some studies
have demonstrated that brand elements from offline channels
have a significant impact on consumer decisions in online
channels (Al-Nabhani et al., 2022). For example, positive
or negative perceptions of a brand influence the decision
to shop online (Gao et al., 2021; Kwon & Lennon, 2009a,
2009b). In turn, loyalty to the physical store influences
behavioural intention on the retailer’s website (Savila et al.,
2019). Therefore, it can be argued that incongruence in the
omnichannel consumer journey (failure to meet customer
expectations of a seamless shopping process) reduces inten-
tion to shop in e-commerce (behavioural intention and advo-
cacy) (Gao et al., 2021). These characteristics are specific to
the omnichannel consumer journey.
We can therefore hypothesise that the type of consumer
journey will moderate the relationships proposed in the
model.
H4: The type of consumer journey moderates the rela-
tionships of the SOR model.

Methodology
The questionnaire consists of items adapted from scales pre-
viously validated in the literature (Table 1). A 5-point Likert
scale is used (1, totally disagree; 5, totally agree).
To identify the consumer journey, a filter question was
asked: “In your purchase process you may have visited dif-
ferent websites or physical shops seeking information in
order to decide the best purchase option. Pleaseindicate the
option you followed in the online purchase: a) I only visited
the website/app where I made the purchase; b) I visited sev-
eral websites/apps before deciding to buy the service on this
website/app; c) I visited several websites/apps and physical
shops to get information about the product.” The first option
can be described as mono-online-channel consumer journey,
the second as multi-online-channel consumer journey, and
the third as an omnichannel consumer journey (multiroom-
ing) (Table 2).
The final version of the questionnaire was distributed
on Facebook and Instagram, between August and Decem-
ber 2021. We obtained 1440 valid responses from Spanish

model were analysed. Finally, a multigroup analysis based
on the online behaviour of the customers in the sample was
conducted.
Findings
Measurement model
Scale dimensionality through confirmatory factor analysis
was assessed. A full measurement model was tested, includ-
ing all the constructs and control variables. To achieve
discriminant validity, two items (P3.1 and P3.4) from the
behavioural intentions towards the e-commerce construct
and two items (P4.1 and P4.4) from the advocacy variable
were removed. Additionally, the Lagrange multiplier test
suggested a modification, the error correlation between a
pair of items, P2.1–P2.2, from the cognitive dimension of
OCE. In this case, there is a similarity in content perceived
by the respondents. After implementing these changes, the
results of the measurement model showed an acceptable

CFI = 0.94, BBNFI = 0.92, BBNNFI = 0.93, IFI = 0.94,
RMSEA = 0.05). Finally, composite reliability and Cron-
bach’s alpha were used to assess scale reliability. The results
exceed the 0.7 threshold (Table 4).

Multigroup analysis
The sample was divided into three subsamples: mono-
online-channel journey (n  = 317), multi-online-channel
journey (n  = 918), and omnichannel journey (n  = 205). In
SEM, a sample of between 100 and 150 individuals is set as
a minimum sample size to carry out the statistical analyses
(Anderson & Gerbing, 1984, 1988; Ding et al., 1995). Like-
wise, with minimum samples of 200 individuals, estimates
are reliable when factor loadings exceed 0.50–0.60 (MacCal-
lum et al., 2001). The multigroup analysis was performed
using EQS 6.4. Measurement and structural invariances are
examined, in accordance with Hair et al. (2010). Measure-
ment invariance assesses the consistency of factor loadings
across groups, while structural invariance evaluates whether
the structural relationships (paths) between constructs are
equivalent across groups.
Measurement invariance was assessed by examining
configural and metric invariances. The multigroup model
showed different statistics that corroborate an acceptable fit
(Satorra–Bentler χ2 = 4183.52; df = 1803; p-value = 0.00;
CFI = 0.94; BBNFI = 0.90; BBNNFI = 0.93; IFI = 0.94;
RMSEA = 0.05), confirming configural invariance. To assess
metric invariance, the multigroup model, in which the fac-
tor loadings were constrained to be equal across the three
subsamples, was compared. Following Satorra (2001), we
applied the Satorra–Bentler difference scaling test to com-
pare the unconstrained model with the model with the equal-
ity constraint. Specifically, we calculated the transformed
statistic from the Satorra–Bentler χ2 (robust statistic), the χ2
(non-robust statistic) of the unconstrained and constrained
models, along with their respective degrees of freedom and
scaling correction factors. These corrections adjust the χ 2
statistic to account for non-normality in the data. The trans-
formed statistic follows, approximately, a χ 2 distribution
with degrees of freedom equal to the difference between the
two models. We then calculated the corresponding p-value:
values below 0.05 indicate significant differences between
the beta coefficient of the unconstrained and constrained
models, whereas values above 0.05 suggest that these dif-
ferences are not statistically significant. In the present study,
the test result was not statistically significant (p = 0.29).
Structural invariance was then tested. First, the uncon-
strained model was estimated, which allowed structural
relationships to vary freely across the subsamples. Several
statistics showed an acceptable model fit for each subsample
(Table 7). Next, the multigroup model was compared with a
constrained model, in which the structural relationships were
restricted to be equal across the three subsamples. We also
applied the Satorra–Bentler difference scaling test to com-
pare the unconstrained model with the model with the equal-
ity constraint. The result of this test was statistically signifi-
cant (p-value = 0.000) (Table 6), indicating that significant
differences exist in the causal relationships among the three
subsamples. Furthermore, following the same difference
scaling test, all the model paths between two subsamples
were tested separately (Table 7). They were assessed by add-
ing constraints only on the target path and then calculating
the chi-square differences.
The findings of the multigroup analysis revealed that
the relationship between OCX and behavioural intentions
is significantly higher in multi-online-channels customer
journey than in mono-online-channel customer journey. This
relationship is also significantly higher in the omnichannel
journey than in the mono-online-channel journey. However,
no significant differences were observed in this relationship
between customers with multi-online-channel journeys and
those with omnichannel journeys. Additionally, no signifi-
cant differences were found in the other two hypothesised
causal relationships. Therefore,  H4 is partially supported

Discussion and conclusions
Discussion
The objective of this paper is to study the relationship
between online customer experience (OCX), online customer
engagement (OCE), and outcomes (behavioural intention and
advocacy) in an e-commerce context, comparing online and
omnichannel consumer journeys. The test of the model shows
that the stimulus–organism–response (SOR) sequence is ful-
filled, such that OCX influences OCE, which significantly
impacts both responses (behavioural intention and advocacy).
The predictive power of the model for advocacy is extremely
high (R2 = 0.82), whereas it is merely high for behavioural
intentions (R2 = 0.65) and OCE (R2 = 0.63).
These results are consistent with other research. Ahmed et al.
(2022) tested a significant relationship between OCX and OCE,
and Taheri et al. (2024) found that website design is an anteced-
ent of OCE. The relationship between OCE and behavioural
intentions has the most empirical evidence in the literature (Park
et al., 2023; Lim et al., 2022; Molinillo et al., 2020b; Zheng
et al., 2022). On the other hand, the relationship between OCE
and advocacy has not been studied, although Molinillo et al.
(2022b) tested a significant influence between OCE and WOM.
The multigroup analysis shows that the consumer journey
is a moderating variable of the causal relationships and of
the predictive power of the model. The comparison between
omnichannel and multi-online-channel journey shows no sig-
nificant differences in the causal relationships, although the
conative dimension (vigour) of OCE and the strong dimension
of advocacy are significantly higher in the omnichannel jour-
ney. The comparison between omnichannel and mono-online-
channel journey shows that the influence of OCE on behav-
ioural intentions is significantly greater in the former than in
the latter. Finally, when comparing multi-online-channels with
mono-online-channel consumer journeys, it is established that
the influence of OCE on behavioural intentions is greater in the
former than in the latter.
Moreover, the R2 of behavioural intention in omnichannel
journey (R2 = 0.82) is higher than that of the general model
(R2 = 0.65), the multi-online-channels journey (R 2 = 0.67),
and the mono-online-channel journey (R 2 = 0.50). In con-
trast, the R 2 of OCE is higher in the mono-online-chan-
nel journey (R 2 = 0.71) and in the omnichannel journey
(R2 = 0.74) than in the general model (R 2 = 0.63) and the
multi-online-channels journey ( R2 = 0.56). This indicates
that OCX predicts a high percentage of the variance of OCE
in the mono-online-channel journey. In all three groups, the
high R2 for e-commerce advocacy stands out (R2 > 0.80).
The multigroup analysis shows that the consumer journey
moderates the causal relationships and the predictive power
of the model. The explanation for this moderation effect lies,
on the one hand, in the degree of incongruence of online and
offline channels (Gao et al., 2021). Offline experience influ-
ences OCE, enhancing the behavioural dimension of OCE and
the predictive power of the model. Hoang et al. (2023) con-
sider that it is more difficult to provide a positive experience
solely through online platforms because this environment is
not as realistic as brick-and-mortar retailers. Consumers seek
synchronisation between offline and online experiences. When
it occurs, the experience in the physical channel positively
influences the online channel (Gao et al., 2021; Al-Nabhani
et al., 2022; Fang & Li, 2025), strengthening the behavioural
dimension of OCE and the strong dimension of advocacy.
On the other hand, when the consumer visits more than
one channel, the influence of OCE on behavioural inten-
tions with e-commerce intensifies. Although the predictive
power of OCX on OCE increases in the mono-online-chan-
nel journey, when the consumer visits several channels, the
impact of OCE on behavioural intention is strengthened. The
mono-online-channel journey seems to be followed by very
loyal e-commerce customers. A greater influence of OCE on
behaviour intentions would therefore be expected. The most
logical explanation is the existence of switching barriers,
such as switching costs (search costs, transaction costs, learn-
ing costs, cognitive effort, emotional costs, loyalty discounts,
customer habit, and financial, social, and psychological risks)
and less attractive alternatives (Mutum et al., 2014). Some
studies corroborate the importance of switching barriers in
solo e-retailers. Ghazali et al. (2016) found a significant rela-
tionship between switching barriers and customer loyalty in
pure-play retailers. Xue and Jo (2024) also found that switch-
ing costs and alternative attractiveness are two significant
drivers of repurchase intention in Booking.com.

The SOR model had hardly been applied to the consumer

identified in the literature, is the important role played by
switching barriers in the mono-online channel consumer
journey.
Managerial implications
OCX is a key variable for generating customer engagement
with e-commerce and favourable behavioural intention and
advocacy. The findings show that sensory appeal and social
presence are the main dimensions of OCX. Sensory appeal
can be achieved with a dynamic e-commerce site, with high
quality videos and photographs, and 360° views that stimulate
the senses, all in order to provide attractive product presenta-
tions. Integrating ambient music in the site, micro-animations,
or generating immersive experiences with virtual and aug-
mented reality for product presentation are useful resources
to improve OCX. These multisensory resources lead the user
to spend more time on an e-commerce site, which increases
engagement with e-commerce and the likelihood of purchase
(Hoang et al., 2023). However, these technological innova-
tions must enable fast loading and fluid site navigation.
E-commerce should also generate social presence through
tools such as chatbots, live chat, and AI. Another resource
is to equip the e-commerce site with anthropomorphic
visual characteristics and personalise live agents with real
human faces and names (Han, 2021; Yuan & Dennis, 2019).
Encouraging customer reviews and comments, which pro-
vide credibility and usefulness to other users, is also a good
resource to enhance social presence. It is important that all
these tools be equipped with a friendly tone and human-like
messaging that reflects the brand voice.
Creating a brand community of followers and fans helps
to foster the social presence of e-commerce (Hoang et al.,
2023). To that end, contact should be made with advocates
who offer strong, passionate, and explicit recommendations,
and who are proactive in promoting e-commerce (Sweeney
et al., 2020). Although advocates are more valuable than
pWOMers, the latter should also be taken into account when
forming a community of followers.
Omnichannel strategy brings benefits in terms of increas-
ing the behavioural dimension of OCE (vigour) and the
strong dimension of advocacy. It also boosts the influence of
OCE on behavioural intention. E-commerce that only sells
online should consider a physical presence that allows the
customer to have satisfactory “phygital” experiences.
Finally, an effective strategy to encourage consumers’
behavioural intentions towards e-commerce is to create
switching barriers. Loyalty programmes, free shipping,
loyalty discounts, ease of payment, and a wide range of
products and services are basic aspects that must be present
in e-commerce. In addition, it is necessary to create switch-
ing costs with customers by generating search, transaction,
learning, and emotional costs.
Limitations and future research
With respect to the limitations, the different size of the sub-
samples implies different sampling errors (between ± 1.65%
and ± 3.46%), which may affect the multigroup estimates.
Another limitation is that the sample is from a geographic
territory belonging to an advanced western economy
(Spain).
Future research will focus on the three effects identified
from multigroup analysis. Although empirical evidence
shows that the omnichannel consumer journey enhances
engagement with a brand, its application to an e-commerce
site has received little attention. Given that an e-commerce
site is as important as a physical store, more attention from
researchers is needed to further explore OCE. E-commerce
varies considerably, depending on the products and ser -
vices offered and the geographic scope. Another aspect that
needs further exploration is the moderating role of the type
of consumer journey. Few empirical studies have addressed
this issue of the differences between online, offline, and
omnichannel. Along with showrooming, webrooming, and
multirooming, new types of consumer journeys are emerg-
ing that deserve the attention of researchers. Incorporating
offline customer experience together with OCE to explain
the omnichannel consumer journey is also interesting.
Finally, switching barriers must be integrated as a stimulus
to the SOR model, along with OCX. Reality shows that large
online sales platforms use these switching barriers to create
a forced customer loyalty, which is an interesting aspect that
merits further investigation.
