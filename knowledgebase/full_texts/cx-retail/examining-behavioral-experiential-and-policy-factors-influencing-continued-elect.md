Abstract
This study aims to examine the determinants of continued electric vehicle (EV) usage in Indonesia
by integrating behavioral, experiential, innovation, policy, and risk perspectives. Drawing on the
Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), Customer Experience Theor y (CET), Innovation Diffusion
Theory (IDT), and Perceived Risk Theory (PRT), this study develops a structural model involving
attitude (ATT), perceived behavioral control (PBC), driving experience (DE), supportive policy
(SP), product innovativeness (PI), EV services (EVS), perceived risk (PR), and continued usage
(CU). Data were collected from 408 EV users in major Indonesian cities through online and offline
surveys and analyzed using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS -SEM). The
findings show that ATT, PBC, and DE significantly increase CU, while PR has a significant negative
effect. Furthermore, SP, PI, and EVS influence CU indirectly through ATT and DE, confirming the
importance of psychological and experiential mechanisms in sustain ing EV usage. DE emerged as
the strongest direct predictor of CU, while PI and EVS showed the strongest mediated effects
through DE. The novelty of this study lies in shifting the focus from EV adoption intention to post -
adoption continued usage in an emer ging market context. The findings contribute to EV behavior
literature and provide practical insights for policymakers and automakers seeking to sustain
Indonesia’s transition toward electric mobility.

1- Introduction
Climate change remains one of the most pressing global challenges, with serious consequences for ecological systems,
public health, economic stability, and long-term energy security. This urgency has intensified the global transition toward
sustainable energy systems, in which renewable energy and low-carbon technologies are increasingly viewed as essential
for reducing dependence on fossil fuels and supporting sustainable development [1, 2]. International climate
commitments, particularly the Paris Agreement, have strengthened the responsibility of countries to reduce greenhouse
gas (GHG) emissions and limit global temperature increases [3]. In parallel, countries across both advanced and
emerging economies have begun accelerating renewable energy innovation, public awareness, and climate-related policy
initiatives to support this transition [4, 5].
Within this broader transition, the transportation sector occupies a critical position because it remains one of the major
contributors to global GHG emissions. Transportation accounts for a substantial share of global emissions, with road
transport being especially dominant due to its dependence on internal combustion engine vehicles and fossil fuels [6-8].
Rising motorization, urbanization, and dependence on private vehicles further intensify the environmental burden of the
sector [9]. As a result, vehicle electrification has become one of the most widely promoted strategies for reducing

can reduce direct tailpipe emissions and contribute to cleaner urban mobility [10, 11]. However, the environmental

infrastructure, and sustained user engagement after adoption [12, 13].
EVs have therefore emerged as an important strategy for decarbonizing mobility worldwide. They provide zero
tailpipe emissions and, when supported by renewable energy and smart charging systems, can contribute to both

concerns related to range anxiety, charging availability, battery reliability, and vehicle affordability remain  important
barriers to widespread and sustained EV usage [19, 20].
Indonesia represents an important emerging market for EV development because of its rising energy demand, large
vehicle population, dependence on fossil fuels, and strategic ambition to develop a domestic battery -based EV
ecosystem. Coal and natural gas co ntinue to dominate Indonesia’s energy mix, making the transition to cleaner
transportation increasingly important for national decarbonization [21, 22]. The government has committed to achieving
net-zero emissions by 2060, and electric mobility has been positioned as a central component of this transition [23, 24].
Policy support has been strengthened through Presidential Regulation No. 55/2019, fiscal incentives, tax reductions, and
efforts to attract investment in battery and EV production, including part nerships involving global manufacturers and
domestic state-owned enterprises [25, 26]. Despite these initiatives, Indonesia’s EV market remains at an early but
rapidly growing stage. According to the International Energy Agency’s Global EV Outlook 2025, el ectric car sales in
Indonesia tripled in 2024, while the conventional car market contracted by around 20%, raising the electric car sales
share to more than 7% [17]. This growth was partly supported by fiscal incentives, including the reduction of value -
added tax for eligible battery electric vehicles from 11% to 1% [17]. However, Indonesia still lags behind leading EV
markets such as China and Norway, where stronger infrastructure networks, broader model availability, and more mature
consumer ecosystems ha ve accelerated adoption. Within Southeast Asia, Indonesia also trails several neighboring
markets in terms of EV sales share. Recent regional evidence shows that Vietnam and Singapore reached EV sales shares
of around 40% in 2025, while Thailand surpassed 20%, reflecting stronger market acceleration and policy –industry
alignment in parts of ASEAN [18]. By comparison, Indonesia’s EV share remains lower, although its large population,
vehicle market, and nickel -based battery supply chain give it significant l ong-term potential if infrastructure,
affordability, and consumer confidence improve [27]. In the two - and three-wheeler segment, Indonesia has set an
ambitious target of 13 million electric motorcycles by 2030, yet the EV share of two- and three-wheeler sales remained
below 2% in 2024, indicating a large gap between policy ambition and market realization [19]. Charging infrastructure
has also expanded quickly: PLN reported 3,772 public EV charging stations for four-wheelers, 9,956 electricity supply
units for two-wheelers, and 2,240 battery swapping stations as of March 2025 [20]. Nevertheless, uneven infrastructure
distribution, affordability constraints, and consumer concerns about safety, reliability, and charging access continue to
challenge sustained E V usage [28]. These developments show that Indonesia is no longer merely preparing for EV
adoption; it is entering a critical post-adoption phase in which continued usage becomes essential for ensuring that policy
incentives and infrastructure investments generate long-term behavioral change.
Prior studies on EV adoption in Indonesia have mainly focused on pre -adoption outcomes, including purchase
intention, willingness -to-pay, adoption intention, and switching behavior [29, 30]. These studies have shown that
environmental awareness, economic b enefits, charging infrastructure, and government incentives shape consumers’
interest in EVs [25, 26, 31]. The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) has been widely used to explain adoption intention
through attitude (ATT), subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control (PBC) [31, 32]. Other studies have also
examined contextual factors such as charging infrastructure, cost perception, policy incentives, and environmental values
as important determinants of EV adoption in Indonesia [28]. Although these studies provide important insights, they
remain largely focused on whether consumers intend to adopt EVs, rather than whether existing EV users continue using
them over time.
This creates several important gaps in the literature. First, limited attention has been given to continued usage (CU)
after EV adoption, even though the environmental and policy benefits of EVs can only be achieved if users sustain their
usage in everyday mobility. Second, most prior studies have relied primarily on TPB or adoption -based frameworks,
while relatively few have integrated TPB with Customer Experience Theory (CET), Innovation Diffusion Theory (IDT),
and Perceived Risk Theory (PRT). Such integr ation is important because continued usage is not shaped only by
behavioral intention, but also by actual driving experience (DE), product innovativeness (PI), EV services (EVS),
supportive policy (SP), and perceived risk (PR). Third, the roles of DE and PR remain underexplored in the Indonesian
EV context, despite their practical relevance. EV users may initially adopt electric mobility due to incentives or curiosity,

and perceived as safe. Finally, Indonesia is entering a stage in which subsidy policies, infrastructure expansion, and

To address these gaps, this study analyzes the factors influencing CU of EVs in Indonesia by integrating TPB, CET,

to post -adoption continued usage and  by integrating behavioral, experiential, innovation, policy, and risk -based
perspectives. Practically, the study provides insights for Indonesian policymakers, automakers, and EV service providers
by identifying how policy support, product innovation, service infrastructure, and risk reduction can sustain long -term
EV usage. These insights are expected to support Indonesia’s broader transition toward consumer -centered and
sustainable electric mobility.
2- Literature Review
This section reviews the theoretical foundations and core constructs used to explain continued electric vehicle (EV)
usage in Indonesia. The study integrates four complementary perspectives: the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB),
Customer Experience Theory (CET), Innovation Diffusion Theory (IDT), and Perceived Risk Theory (PRT). TPB
explains how attitude (ATT) and perceived behavioral control (PBC) shape behavioral continuation, while CET
highlights the importance of driving experience (DE) and EV services (EVS) in shaping user satisfaction and confidence.
IDT explains how product innovativeness (PI) influences users’ perceptions of technological value, and PRT captures
the negative role of uncertainty and perceived risk (PR). Together, these perspectives provide a broader explanation of
continued usage (CU), which extends beyond initial adoption intention and focuses on whether users sustain EV use
over time. Although TPB traditionally includes subjective norms, this study does not include subjective norm as a core
construct because the focus is on post -adoption continued usage rather than initial purchase intention. In the early
adoption stage, social pressure, peer influence, and perceived social approval may strongly affect whether consumers
consider buying an EV. However, once consumers have already used or owned EVs, their continued usage is more likely
to be shaped by personal evaluation, perceived control, actual driving experience, service accessibility, and risk
perception. Therefore, this study empha sizes ATT and PBC as the TPB -based constructs most directly related to
sustained EV usage, while incorporating experiential, innovation, policy, and risk -related constructs to capture post -
adoption behavior more comprehensively.
2-1- Shaping Perceptions Through Attitude (ATT)
ATT is a central construct in TPB, defined as an individual’s positive or negative evaluation of performing a given
behavior [32]. In the context of EVs, ATT reflects users’ overall appraisal of EV usage, including whether they perceive
EVs as beneficial, convenient, environmentally responsible, and suitable for daily mobility [33]. Favorable ATT can
strengthen behavioral commitment, while unfavorable ATT may reduce the willingness to continue using EVs. Previous
studies show that positive attitudes toward EVs are commonly associated with environmental awareness, expected cost
savings, perceived usefulness, and technological sophistication [34, 35]. Consumers who recognize the environmental
benefits of EVs, such as reduced air pollution and lower greenhouse gas emissions, tend to evaluate electric mobility
more positively [36]. Similarly, economic benefits, including lower fuel and maintenance costs, may reinforce favorable
attitudes toward EV usage. However, attitudes toward EVs are not always positive. Negative evaluations may arise from
high upfront costs, limited driving range, inadequate charging infrastructure, battery -related concerns, or uncertainty
about long-term vehicle reliability [37-39]. In Indonesia, attitudes toward EVs are shaped by both optimism and caution.
On the one hand, increasing public awareness of climate change, government campaigns, and fiscal incentives have
improved consumer perceptions of EVs. On the other hand, limited infrastructure and affordability concerns continue to
weaken positive evaluations among some consumers. This is especially important in continued usage research, because
a favorable attitude must be maintained after adoption. Continued exposure to relia ble services, supportive policy,
positive driving experiences, and visible infrastructure development can reinforce ATT and transform early adoption
into long -term behavioral commitment [40, 41]. Therefore, ATT is expected to play a critical role not only in EV
adoption intention but also in the sustained usage of EVs in Indonesia.
2-2- Building Confidence with Perceived Behavioral Control (PBC)
PBC refers to individuals’ perception of their ability to perform a behavior, including the extent to which they believe
they have the necessary resources, opportunities, and control [32]. In EV usage, PBC reflects consumers’ confidence
that they can continue using an EV effectively. This includes financial capability, access to charging facilities, technical
knowledge, maintenance support, and confidence in daily usability. Higher perceived control increases the likelihood of
sustained behavior, while low perceived control can discourage continued usage even when consumers hold positive

attitudes [39, 42]. In the EV context, affordability and infrastructure are especially important determinants of PBC.
Affordability extends beyond the purchase price and includes operating costs, maintenance expenses, battery
replacement concerns, and acce ss to reliable charging [43]. Limited charging infrastructure can reduce consumers’
perceived control because users may feel uncertain about whether they can complete daily travel without inconvenience
or range anxiety [44, 45]. Conversely, government incentives, public charging expansion, and consumer education can
strengthen PBC by reducing perceived barriers and increasing users’ confidence in EV practicality. For Indonesian
consumers, PBC is particularly important because EV adoption is still developing and infrastructure availability remains
uneven across regions [46]. Many users may be interested in EVs but uncertain about whether charging networks, service
centers, and maintenance support are sufficient for long-term use. Policies that lower ownership costs and infrastructure
programs involving public charging networks can directly strengthen users’ sense of control. Prior studies suggest that
when consumers perceive adequate resources and infrastructure support, they are more likely to adopt and susta in EV
use [47, 48]. Thus, PBC is expected to contribute significantly to CU because consumers who feel capable of managing
EV ownership and operation are more likely to remain committed to electric mobility.
2-3- Learning behind the Wheel from Driving Experience (DE)
CET emphasizes that consumer perceptions and future behavior are strongly shaped by direct interaction with a
product or service [49]. In this study, DE refers to users’ direct experience with EVs, including comfort, ease of
operation, performance, convenience, and overall satisfaction during use. Unlike pre-adoption perceptions, DE is formed
through actual interaction with the vehicle. This makes it especially relevant for continued usage, because users’ long-
term commitment depends on whether their real e xperience confirms or exceeds their expectations. Previous studies
indicate that trial drives and direct usage experiences can reduce uncertainty toward new technologies by allowing
consumers to evaluate vehicle performance, acceleration, drivability, interface usability, and charging-related routines
[50, 51]. A smooth and comfortable experience can strengthen confidence, while poor usability or inconvenience may
create hesitation [52, 53]. EVs may offer distinctive experiential advantages such as quiet op eration, instant torque,
smooth handling, and lower operating costs. However, these advantages may be weakened if users experience range
anxiety, charging delays, or technical unfamiliarity [54]. In Indonesia, DE is especially important because many
consumers are still building familiarity with EV technology. Dealership test drives, ride -hailing EV pilots, rental
services, and government demonstration programs can provide practical exposure and reduce uncertainty. Positive DE
may also strengthen ATT because  users who experience comfort and convenience are more likely to evaluate EVs
favorably. In addition, satisfied users may share their experience with others, contributing to broader social acceptance
[41]. Therefore, DE functions as both a direct predictor of CU and an experiential mechanism that shapes users’ attitudes
toward EVs.
2-4- Policies Shaping Consumer Attitudes through Supportive Policy (SP)
Supportive policy is a major driver of EV development, particularly in emerging markets where EV prices remain
high and infrastructure is still developing. In Indonesia, the government has introduced several policies to accelerate
electric mobility, including subsidies, tax incentives, regulatory support, and industrial development programs aligned
with the national net-zero emissions target [55, 56]. Presidential Regulation No. 55/2019 provides a policy foundation
for accelerating the battery -based EV ecos ystem and supporting domestic production, charging infrastructure, and
investment in EV supply chains [26]. Previous studies suggest that supportive policies can shape consumer attitudes by
reducing perceived financial burden and signaling government commitment to EV development [57]. However, policy
support does not always translate directly into long-term behavior. Incentives may increase initial interest, but sustained
usage depends on whether users perceive EVs as practical, reliable, and beneficial in everyday life [20, 58]. Therefore,
the influence of SP is expected to operate largely through ATT. When consumers perceive that the government
consistently supports EV adoption through subsidies, infrastructure investment, tax reductions, and clear regulation, they
are more likely to develop favorable evaluations of EVs. In Indonesia, SP is especially relevant because consumers
continue to face affordability and infrastructure-related barriers. Policy consistency can reduce uncertainty, improve trust
in the EV ecosystem, and reinforce positive perceptions of electric mobility. As EV subsidies and infrastructure programs
evolve, supportive policies need to move beyond short -term purchase incentives and support long -term ownership
conditions, including charging access, service reliability, safety standards, and public education [59, 60]. Thus, SP is
positioned in this study as a contextual factor that strengthens ATT and indirectly supports CU.
2-5- Inspiring Users with Product Innovativeness (PI)
IDT explains that consumers are more likely to adopt and continue using a technology when they perceive it as
offering relative advantage, compatibility, observability, and practical value [61]. In this study, PI refers to the extent to
which EVs are perceived as technologically advanced and superior to conventional vehicles in terms of features, design,
battery technology, connectivity, and usability [62]. Innovative product features can reduce uncertainty by showing users
that EVs provide not only environmental benefits but also functional and experiential advantages. Examples of PI include

improved battery range, regenerative braking, smart connectivity, mobile application integration, real -time vehicle
diagnostics, advanced safety features, and vehicle -to-grid capabilities. These features can improve user convenience,
increase control, and enhance confidence in the vehicle [58, 63]. In emerging EV markets such as Indonesia, PI may be
especially influential because many consumers are still comparing EVs with conventional internal combustion engine
vehicles. Innovative features may help justif y higher upfront costs by demonstrating added value and modern
functionality. PI is closely connected to both ATT and DE. Innovative features can create positive evaluations by making
EVs appear modern, efficient, and desirable. At the same time, these fea tures can improve DE when users directly
interact with them during daily driving or trial experiences [64]. For example, improved range reduces travel uncertainty,
smart connectivity improves convenience, and user -friendly dashboards enhance perceived usab ility. Therefore, PI is
expected to shape CU indirectly by strengthening ATT and improving DE. This dual role reflects the importance of
innovation not only as a product attribute but also as a driver of user experience and long-term behavioral commitment.
2-6- Sustaining Adoption Through EV Services (EVS)
EVS refer to the supporting services and infrastructure that make EV use practical and sustainable. These include
charging stations, battery swapping facilities, maintenance services, technical support, digital charging applications, and
related service networks. From a service quality perspective, accessibility, reliability, responsiveness, and convenience
are essential for sustaining consumer confidence [65, 66]. Even when consumers hold positive attitudes toward EVs,
limited or unreliable services may re duce their willingness to continue using them. In Indonesia, EV charging
infrastructure has expanded, but its distribution remains uneven [67]. Major urban centers such as Jakarta, Surabaya, and
Bandung have seen gradual growth in public charging facilitie s, including fast -charging services, often supported by
PLN and related public-private initiatives [68]. However, rural and peri-urban areas still face limited access, which can
increase range anxiety and discourage sustained use [69]. This uneven development creates an important challenge for
Indonesia’s EV transition because continued usage depends on whether users can rely on charging and service networks
in everyday mobility. EVS influence CU primarily by improving DE. Reliable and conveniently located charging
infrastructure reduces uncertainty, improves travel confidence, and makes EV use more practical [70, 71]. Service
innovations, such as solar-powered charging hubs, battery swapping systems, and mobile charging services, may further
improve accessibility and convenience [72]. In this sense, EVS are not merely technical infrastructure; they are part of
the user experience. Without reliable EVS, positive attitudes and product innovation may not be sufficient to sustain
long-term use. Therefore, this study positions EVS as a key contextual factor that improves DE and indirectly supports
CU.
2-7- The Weight of Perceived Risk (PR) on Continued EV Usage
PRT argues that consumer decisions are influenced not only by expected benefits but also by uncertainty and the
possibility of negative outcomes [73]. In the EV context, PR includes concerns about battery safety, battery degradation,
charging reliability, maintenance costs, resale value, and long-term vehicle performance [74]. High PR can reduce trust
and weaken consumers’ willingness to continue using EVs, even after initial adoption. Globally, concerns about battery
fires, charging failures, battery replacement costs, and resale uncertainty have influenced consumer perceptions of EVs
[75, 76]. In emerging markets such as Indonesia, these concerns may be more salient because infrastructure is still
developing and many consumers have limited technical familiarity with EVs. Users may worry about whether charging
stations will be available, whether batteries will remain reliable over time, or whether EVs will retain resale value. These
concerns can reduce confidence and undermine continued usage. Addressing PR is therefore essential for sustaining EV
adoption. Manufacturers can reduce perceived risk through battery warranties, transparent information about safety
standards, reliable after-sales service, and clear maintenance guidance [77]. Policymakers can also reduce uncertainty
by strengthening safety regulation, expanding charging infrastructure, and supporting public education about EV
reliability [78]. In this study, PR is treated as a negative determinant of CU because risk perceptions may continue to
influence users even after adoption. This perspective extends EV research by emphasizing that post-adoption behavior
depends not only on positive evaluations but also on the reduction of uncertainty.
2-8- Moving Beyond Adoption to Understand Continued Usage (CU)
EV studies commonly distinguish between adoption intention, switching behavior, and continued usage. Adoption
intention refers to consumers’ willingness to purchase or use an EV in the future, while switching behavior refers to the
actual transition from a conventional vehicle to an EV [79]. CU goes beyond both concepts by examining whether users
continue to integrate EVs into their daily mobility after adoption. This distinction is important because initial adoption
alone does not guarantee long -term environmental, economic, or policy benefits. In this study, CU is the dependent
variable and represents sustained behavioral commitment to EV usage. Theoretically, CU is supported by TPB and
Expectation-Confirmation Theory (ECT). TPB explains how ATT and PBC in fluence behavioral continuation, while
ECT suggests that users are more likely to continue using a technology when their experiences meet or exceed
expectations [39, 80]. This makes CU particularly suitable for studying EVs because long -term use depends on  both

has introduced policies and infrastructure programs to accelerate EV adoption, but the long -term success of these

feedback for infrastructure planning, strengthen market confidence, encourage private investment, and validate
government policy interventions [81]. Conversely, if users discontinue EV usage due to poor experience, limited
services, or high perceived risk, adoption targets may fail to generate lasting decarbonization outcomes. Therefore, this
study moves beyond adoption intention by examining CU as the ultimate be havioral outcome in Indonesia’s EV

3- Method
3-1- Research Design and Hypotheses Development
This study develops an integrated research model to explain the continued usage (CU) of electric vehicles (EVs) in
Indonesia. Unlike much of the existing EV literature, which has primarily focused on adoption intention, purchase
intention, or switching beh avior, this study emphasizes post -adoption behavior, namely whether users continue using
EVs after initial adoption or direct experience. This focus is important because the long-term environmental and policy
benefits of EVs can only be achieved if users sustain their use in daily mobility.
The model integrates four complementary theoretical perspectives: the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), Customer
Experience Theory (CET), Innovation Diffusion Theory (IDT), and Perceived Risk Theory (PRT). TPB explains the
behavioral mechanism through atti tude (ATT) and perceived behavioral control (PBC), which represent users’
evaluation of EV usage and their perceived ability to continue using EVs [32]. CET explains the role of driving
experience (DE) and EV services (EVS), as direct interaction with EVs and supporting services can shape satisfaction,
confidence, and long-term behavioral commitment [49]. IDT explains how product innovativeness (PI), such as battery
range, smart connectivity, and advanced vehicle features, can increase perceived technological value and strengthen both
ATT and DE [61]. Finally, PRT captures the negative influence of perceived risk (PR), including concerns over safety,
battery degradation, resale value, and charging reliability, which may weaken users’ willingness to continue using EVs
[73].
Although subjective norm is a core component of TPB, it was not included in the final model because this study
focuses on continued usage rather than initial adoption intention. Subjective norm is often more relevant in explaining
early-stage adoption deci sions, where social pressure, peer influence, and perceived social approval may shape
consumers’ willingness to try or purchase a new technology. In a post -adoption context, however, continued usage is
more likely to be shaped by users’ own attitudes, perc eived control, direct driving experience, service accessibility,
product evaluation, and risk perception. Therefore, this study emphasizes ATT and PBC as the TPB -based constructs
most directly related to sustained EV usage, while extending the model with e xperiential, innovation, policy, and risk-
related constructs.
The conceptual logic of the model is illustrated in Figure 1. In this framework, behavioral factors such as ATT, PBC,
and PR directly influence CU. Experiential and contextual factors, including DE, PI, EVS, and supportive policy (SP),
shape CU both direct ly and indirectly through ATT and DE. Specifically, PI is expected to strengthen both ATT and
DE, EVS is expected to improve DE, and SP is expected to strengthen ATT. This integrated model therefore explains
CU as the outcome of psychological evaluation, perceived control, direct experience, technological innovation, service
support, policy support, and perceived risk.

Based on the theoretical foundations and prior empirical evidence, nine hypotheses are proposed as follows:
H1: ATT → CU
ATT refers to an individual’s overall positive or negative evaluation of a behavior [32]. In the EV context, favorable
ATT may be shaped by perceived environmental benefits, cost savings, and technological appeal [34, 36]. Negative
evaluations, such as con cerns over high cost, limited range, or inadequate charging infrastructure, may reduce users’
willingness to continue using EVs [38, 39]. In Indonesia, maintaining positive ATT is essential for transforming initial
adoption into long-term behavioral commitment. Therefore, it is hypothesized that ATT positively influences CU.
H2: DE → CU
CET emphasizes that direct experience strongly shapes satisfaction and future behavior [49]. For EV users, trial drives
and daily use allow direct evaluation of comfort, usability, performance, and convenience [50, 53]. In Indonesia, where
EV familiarity is still developing, positive DE may reduce uncertainty and increase commitment to continued EV usage.
Therefore, it is hypothesized that DE positively influences CU.
H3: PBC → CU
PBC represents the extent to which consumers believe they have the resources, ability, and opportunities to perform
a behavior [32]. In EV usage, PBC includes affordability, access to charging facilities, maintenance support, and
technical confidence. Prior studies indicate that stronger PBC is associated with stronger adoption and sustained usage
behavior [42, 43, 45]. Therefore, it is hypothesized that PBC positively influences CU.
H4: PR → CU
PRT suggests that consumers consider uncertainty and potential negative consequences when deciding whether to
adopt or continue using a technology [73]. In the EV context, PR includes concerns about battery safety, long -term
reliability, resale value, and charging availability [74]. These concerns may remain important even after adoption,
especially in markets where infrastructure and user familiarity are still developing [75]. Therefore, it is hypothesized that
PR negatively influences CU.
H5: DE → ATT
Experiential Learning Theory suggests that direct experience plays an important role in shaping attitudes [82]. In EV
usage, hands-on experience can reduce uncertainty and help users form more informed evaluations of the vehicle [20,
50]. Positive experience with comfort, ease of use, and performance may strengthen favorable attitudes toward EVs.
Therefore, it is hypothesized that DE positively influences ATT.
H6: PI → ATT
According to IDT, innovative product features can increase perceived value and generate favorable evaluations [61].
In EVs, features such as smart connectivity, advanced safety systems, interactive dashboards, and battery -related
innovations may improve users’ perceptions of technological superiority [34, 83]. Therefore, it is hypothesized that PI
positively influences ATT.
H7: PI → DE
IDT also suggests that technological innovation can improve user experience by increasing relative advantage and
compatibility with user needs [61]. In EVs, extended range, regenerative braking, smart connectivity, and advanced
vehicle interfaces may improve convenience, comfort, and perceived usability [58, 63]. Therefore, it is hypothesized that
PI positively influences DE.
H8: EVS → DE
EVS, including charging infrastructure, maintenance support, and related service networks, are essential to the
practical use of EVs [65, 66]. Reliable and accessible EV services can reduce range anxiety, improve convenience, and
strengthen confidence during daily use [70, 84]. Therefore, it is hypothesized that EVS positively influences DE.
H9: SP → ATT
Government support, including subsidies, tax incentives, infrastructure investment, and regulatory frameworks, plays
an important role in shaping consumer perceptions of EVs [25, 26]. Prior studies suggest that supportive policies may
strengthen positive attitudes by reducing financial concerns and signaling long-term institutional commitment [20, 58].
Therefore, it is hypothesized that SP positively influences ATT. Together, these nine hypotheses form an integrated
framework that explains continued EV usag e through behavioral, experiential, innovation -related, service -related,
policy-related, and risk-related mechanisms.

3-2- Research Design and Data Collection
All constructs in this study were measured using a 7 -point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7
(strongly agree). A 7-point scale was preferred over a 5-point scale because it provides greater response sensitivity and
allows respondents to express more nuanced perceptions. This is particularly useful for behavioral and post -adoption

The study was conducted in Indonesia, where EV adoption is strategically important for the national transition toward
sustainable mobility and the net -zero emissions target by 2060. The target population consisted of EV users and
consumers with direct EV exposure, including urban middle-class consumers, technology-oriented drivers, EV owners,
EV renters, test-drive participants, and users involved in ride -hailing EV pilot programs. These groups were selected

because they are more likely to have sufficient knowledge or experience to evaluate continued EV usage. A non -
probability sampling approach was used, combining purposive and snowball sampling. Purposive sampling was applied
to target respondents with direc t experience or meaningful exposure to EV usage, while snowball sampling allowed
initial respondents to share the survey with other EV users or EV -related communities. This strategy was practical
because EV users remain a relatively specific and emerging consumer group in Indonesia. However, the use of purposive

to the study sample and should not be generalized to all Indonesian consumers without caution.
Data were collected through both online and offline channels. Online distribution was conducted through social media
platforms, EV community forums, and university alumni groups to reach respondents across several cities. Offline data
were collected through EV dealerships and charging stations in major urban centers, including Jakarta, Surabaya, and
Bandung. These locations were selected because EV infrastructure and EV user activity are more concentrated in urban
areas. This urban focus is appropriate for studying early EV users, but it may underrepresent rural consumers, lower -
income groups, and areas with limited charging infrastructure. A total of 420 responses were received. After removing
incomplete or inconsistent responses, 408 valid responses were retained for analysis. The final sample size exceeds the

3-4- Data Analysis
This study employed Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS -SEM) to examine the proposed
research framework. PLS-SEM was selected because the study is prediction-oriented, focuses on theory extension, and
includes multiple constructs and me diation pathways. Compared with covariance -based SEM (CB -SEM), which is
generally more suitable for strict theory confirmation and global model fit assessment under stronger distributional
assumptions, PLS-SEM is more appropriate for exploratory and predictive models, complex relationships, and data that
may not meet multivariate normality assumptions. Therefore, PLS -SEM was considered suitable for examining
continued EV usage in the Indonesian context. All analyses were conducted using SmartPLS version 4.0.
The data analysis followed two sequential stages. First, the measurement model was evaluated to establish reliability
and validity. Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s Alpha and Composite Reliability (CR), with
values expected to exceed the recommended threshold of 0.70. Convergent validity was examined using the Average
Variance Extracted (AVE), with acceptable values above 0.50. Discriminant validity was assessed using the Heterotrait–
Monotrait (HTMT) ratio, with values expected to remain below 0.90. These procedures ensured that the constructs were
measured reliably and were empirically distinct from one another. Second, the structural model was evaluated to test the

Emerging Science Journal | Vol. 10, No. 5
Page | 3034
hypothesized relationships. Path coefficients (β), t-values, and p-values were estimated to assess the direction, strength,
and significance of each path. The coefficient of determination (R²) was used to evaluate the explanatory power of the
endogenous constructs, while effect size (f²) was used to assess the relative contribution of each exogenous construct.
Predictive relevance (Q²) was assessed using the blindfolding procedure, where values greater than zero indicate that the
model has predictive relevance. A bootstrapping procedure with 5,000 subsamples was specified for estimating standard
errors and assessing the significance of structural paths.
Finally, mediation analysis was conducted to examine whether contextual and technological variables influence CU
indirectly through ATT and DE. Four specified indirect pathways were evaluated: SP → ATT → CU, PI → ATT →
CU, PI → DE → CU, and EVS → DE → CU. Indirect effects were calculated as products of their constituent path
coefficients, and Sobel z-statistics were reported to assess mediation. This procedure was used to evaluate the roles of
ATT and DE in linking supportive policy, product innovation, and EV services to continued EV usage. The interpretation

4-6- Theoretical Implications
This study contributes to EV behavior literature by extending the focus from adoption intention to continued usage.
Building on TPB, the results confirm that ATT and PBC significantly influence CU, supporting the view that users’
evaluations and perceived control remain important beyond initial adoption [32]. The significant negative effect of PR
also confirms the relevance of PRT in explaining post -adoption EV behavior. This indicates that risk perception
continues to shape user commitment even after consumers have experience with EVs. The study also extends CET by
showing that DE is the strongest direct predictor of CU and that DE significantly strengthens ATT. This demonstrates
that actual user experience serves both as a behavioral driver and as a mechan ism for attitude formation. Furthermore,
the findings support IDT by showing that PI improves both ATT and DE. Innovative features therefore contribute not
only to perceived technological attractiveness but also to practical user experience. Finally, the i ndirect role of SP
through ATT shows that policy support functions as a perception -shaping mechanism rather than merely a direct
adoption stimulus. Overall, the study provides a more comprehensive theoretical explanation of sustained EV usage by
integrating TPB, CET, IDT, and PRT into one framework. This integration shows that continued usage is shaped by
psychological evaluation, perceived capability, direct experience, technological innovation, policy support, service
infrastructure, and risk perception. The model therefore offers a useful framework for understanding EV behavior in
emerging markets where infrastructure, policy, and consumer familiarity are still developing.
4-7- Practical Implications
The findings provide several practical implications for policymakers, automakers, and EV service providers. First,
because DE is the strongest direct predictor of CU, EV stakeholders should prioritize programs that allow consumers to
experience EVs directly. Test drives, rental programs, ride-hailing EV pilots, and community-based EV trials can reduce
uncertainty and help users understand the comfort, usability, and practicality of EVs. Automakers and dealers should
therefore treat driving experience as a central part of EV marketing and retention strategy. Second, the significant effects
of ATT and PBC indicate that consumer education and infrastructure development are essential. Public campaigns
should communicate not only environmental benefits but also p ractical benefits, including lower operating costs,
maintenance advantages, charging options, and safety standards. At the same time, improving charging access and
service availability can strengthen users’ perceived control and reduce hesitation toward long-term EV usage. Third, the
strong role of EVS in shaping DE suggests that infrastructure expansion should focus not only on quantity but also on
reliability, accessibility, and convenience. Charging stations should be easy to locate, operationally reliab le, and

policy should therefore provide consistent long -term signals through incentives, regulatory clarity, infrastructure

5- Conclusion
This study examined the determinants of continued electric vehicle (EV) usage in Indonesia by integrating the Theory
of Planned Behavior (TPB), Customer Experience Theory (CET), Innovation Diffusion Theory (IDT), and Perceived
Risk Theory (PRT). Using survey data from 408 EV users and PLS-SEM analysis, the findings show that attitude (ATT),
perceived behavioral control (PBC), and driving experience (DE) significantly increase continued usage (CU), while
perceived risk (PR) significantly reduces it. Among the direct predictors, DE emerged as the strongest factor, indicating
that sustained EV usage depends heavily on users’ practical interaction with the vehicle, including comfort, usability,
convenience, and perceived reliability. The findings also show that product innovativeness (PI), EV services (EVS), and
supportive policy (SP) influence CU indirectly through DE and ATT. In particular, PI and EVS produced the two largest
reported indirect effects through DE (0.195 and 0.183, respectively), confirming that product innovation, charging
accessibility, service reliability, and infrastructure quality are central to sustaining long -term EV usage. The study
contributes to EV adoption literature by shifting attention from purchase intention and switching behavior to continued
usage, which is essential for achieving long -term environmental and policy benefits. Theoretically, the findings
demonstrate the value of combining behavioral, experiential, innovation, policy, and risk perspectives to explain
sustained EV beha vior in an emerging market context. Practically, the results suggest that Indonesian policymakers
should maintain consistent incentives, expand reliable charging infrastructure, strengthen safety standards, and reduce
consumer uncertainty through transparent information. Automakers and EV service providers should also enhance user
experience through innovative product features, dependable after-sales services, and opportunities for direct EV trials.
Future research should extend this model to rural and lower-income populations, compare Indonesia with other emerging
EV markets, and use longitudinal or behavioral data to better capture changes in continued EV usage over time. Together,
these findings support Indonesia’s transition toward consumer-centered electric mobility and its broader net-zero 2060
target.
5-1- Limitations and Future Research
Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study used a cross -sectional survey design, which limits the
ability to observe changes in user attitudes, experiences, and continued usage over time. Future research should use
longitudinal designs to  examine how EV usage behavior changes as infrastructure, policy, and technology develop.
Second, the use of purposive and snowball sampling may introduce selection bias because respondents were more likely
to be connected to EV communities, urban networks, or technology-oriented groups. As a result, the findings should not
be generalized to all Indonesian consumers without caution. Third, the sample is concentrated in urban areas such as
Jakarta, Bandung, and Surabaya and is dominated by relatively educate d respondents. This profile is appropriate for
studying early EV users, but it may underrepresent rural consumers, lower-income groups, and populations with limited
access to charging infrastructure. Future studies should use broader sampling strategies, i ncluding rural regions and

which may be affected by social desirability bias or subjective interpretation. Future research could combine survey data
with behavioral data, such as charging frequency, vehicle usage logs, or actual travel distance. Finally, future studies
may extend the model by including additional variables such as trust, environmental identity, habit, social influence, and
satisfaction to capture broader psychological and behavioral mechanisms.
