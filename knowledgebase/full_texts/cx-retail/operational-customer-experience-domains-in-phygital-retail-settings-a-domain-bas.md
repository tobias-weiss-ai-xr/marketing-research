Abstract. The rapid integration of digital technologies into physical retail environments has intensified the manage-
rial focus on customer experience (CX). However, existing research predominantly conceptualizes CX as a holistic,
journey-based construct, offering limited clarity about where within retail systems experience is structurally shaped
and where managerial interventions can be effectively directed. This conceptual ambiguity complicates strategic deci-
sion-making in digital transformation initiatives. To address this gap, this study systematically identifies and structures
operational CX domains in phygital retail environments and develops a domain-based conceptual framework explain-
ing where experience formation occurs and how managerial interventions can be targeted. Drawing on a structured
integrative literature review of 166 peer-reviewed studies, the research systematically identifies and stabilizes distinct
structural domains within retail systems where CX formation is activated through identifiable experiential mechanisms
and where managerial actions can be meaningfully directed. The analysis results in 9 operational CX domains span-
ning physical, digital, and cross-channel configurations. The resulting framework enables managers to evaluate digital
initiatives by clarifying: (1) which structural domain is being modified, (2) which experiential mechanism is expected
to be activated, and (3) how changes in one domain may affect alignment across others. By making the operational
architecture of CX explicit, the framework supports more coherent, targeted, and strategically consistent digital trans-
formation decisions.
Keywords: customer experience, phygital retail settings, omnichannel retailing, digital transformation, experience
management, decision support framework.

1. Introduction
The rapid digitalization of retail has fundamentally
transformed the in-store shopping journey, blurring the
boundaries between physical and digital experiences.
Traditional brick-and-mortar retailers increasingly inte-
grate digital technologies – such as mobile applications,
interactive interfaces, sensor-based systems, and AI-ena-
bled tools – into physical store environments to enhance
customer engagement, operational efficiency, and com-
petitive positioning (Kumari & Singh, 2024; Perry et al.,
2018; Singh & Y adav, 2025). This convergence has given
rise to a hybrid retail environment in which customer
interactions unfold across intertwined physical and digi-
tal touchpoints.
In the literature, this hybrid configuration is increas-
ingly described as phygital retail, reflecting the fusion of

CX is frequently analyzed either at an abstract level  –
emphasizing overall satisfaction, affective responses, or
perceived value – or at the level of specific technologi -

necessary not only for theoretical advancement but also
for reducing decision fragmentation and enabling more
systematic alignment between digital solutions and CX

To address this gap, the present study seeks to answer
the following main research question:
MRQ: How can operational CX domains be systemati-
cally identified and structured in phygital retail environ-
ments?
To answer this question in a structured manner, the
study is guided by the following sub-research questions:
 – RQ1: How is CX conceptualized in the literature un-
der conditions of phygital retail environments?
 – RQ2: What operational domains at which CX is
shaped can be identified in physical retail settings?
 – RQ3: How do these operational CX domains differ in
terms of their dominant experiential mechanisms and
managerial relevance?
By addressing these questions, this study contributes
to the literature on retail digitalization and CX in three
ways. First, it advances existing CX research by explic -
itly framing CX as a multi-domain phenomenon within
phygital retail environments. Second, it synthesizes frag-
mented insights from CX management, retail digitaliza-
tion, and in-store technology research into a coherent
conceptual structure of operational CX domains. Third,
the study establishes a theoretically grounded foundation
that can inform future research and managerial decision-
making regarding digital interventions, without prescrib-
ing specific technologies or implementation paths.
Methodologically, the study employs a structured
integrative literature review to identify and organize op-
erational CX domains in phygital retail environments.
The aim is to clarify where CX formation occurs and to
provide a conceptually grounded framework for manage-
rial decision-making.
The remainder of the paper is structured as fol-
lows. The next section reviews relevant literature on CX
and phygital retail environments. This is followed by a
methodological description of the literature analysis and
synthesis approach. The subsequent section presents the
identified operational CX domains and discusses their
characteristics and implications. The paper concludes
with theoretical contributions, managerial implications,
and directions for future research.
2. Literature review
Digital transformation of traditional retail and the
emergence of phygital retail environments
Traditional brick-and-mortar retailers currently face
profound and multifaceted pressures to implement digi-
tal solutions across their operations. A convergence of
structural changes in the retail landscape – including in-
tensified competition from e-commerce, evolving con-
sumer expectations, rapid technological advancement,
and external shocks – has fundamentally challenged the
viability of traditional retail models. As highlighted in
the literature, digitalization is no longer optional but has
become a strategic necessity for survival and long-term
competitiveness.
One of the most significant drivers of digital trans-
formation is the rise of e-commerce and digital-native
competitors. Prior research shows that online retail -
ers leverage advanced technologies, data analytics, and
platform-based business models to deliver highly con -
venient, personalized, and efficient shopping experiences
(Vhatkar et al., 2024; Wichmann & Reinartz, 2024). As a
result, digital competitors have captured substantial mar-
ket share, diverting customer traffic and sales away from
physical stores. Traditional retailers have consequently
faced declining footfall, shrinking profit margins, and, in
some cases, store closures or bankruptcies. This competi-
tive pressure has compelled brick-and-mortar retailers to
rethink their value propositions and to integrate digital
capabilities into their business models in order to remain
viable (Hardaker, 2022).
Demand-side pressures further reinforce the need for
digital transformation. Contemporary consumers are in-
creasingly digitally empowered: they are more connect-
ed, better informed, less loyal, and more accustomed to
navigating seamlessly between online and offline chan-
nels (Knapp et al., 2017; Piotrowicz & Cuthbertson, 2014;
Storrie, 2023). Shopping behavior is no longer confined
to a single channel but unfolds across multiple touch-
points, including smartphones, social media platforms,
and physical stores. While younger consumer segments
show a stronger preference for online shopping – con-
tributing to declining physical store visits – older gen -
erations tend to exhibit more stable, yet increasingly hy-
brid, shopping patterns (Tran, 2025). These shifts place
sustained pressure on traditional retailers to deliver con-
venient, integrated, and personalized experiences across
channels. Without digital support, retailers risk failing to
meet rising customer expectations for speed, transpar -
ency, and ease of interaction.

In response to these competitive and behavioral dy-
namics, omnichannel strategies have become a central
element of retail digital transformation. Omnichannel
retailing refers to the integration of physical stores with
digital channels – such as e-commerce platforms, mobile
applications, and social media – to provide a unified and
coherent customer experience (Piotrowicz & Cuthbert-
son, 2014; Storrie, 2023; Vhatkar et al., 2024). Practices
such as buy-online-pick-up-in-store (BOPIS), in-store
returns of online purchases, and digitally supported in-
store services illustrate how retailers seek to combine the
convenience of digital shopping with the experiential
strengths of physical stores. The literature increasingly
emphasizes that such integration is no longer optional
but essential for maintaining competitiveness in contem-
porary retail environments (Aiolfi & Sabbadin, 2019).
Beyond market and consumer forces, technological
and operational pressures also drive digital transforma-
tion in traditional retail. Growing supply chain complex-
ity, rising cost pressures, and the need for operational
efficiency have pushed retailers to adopt digital solutions
for inventory management, logistics coordination, and
process automation (Vhatkar et al., 2024). At the same
time, rapid technological progress has raised industry
standards and redefined expectations regarding what
retailers can offer in terms of service quality, responsive-
ness, and personalization. Innovations in artificial intelli-
gence (AI), big data analytics, and cloud computing have
enabled advanced capabilities, such as real-time inven -
tory visibility and personalized recommendations, that
early adopters increasingly use as sources of competitive
advantage. This dynamic creates a technology-driven
performance gap that traditional retailers are under pres-
sure to close. Consequently, retailers are adopting tools
such as mobile point-of-sale systems, digital payment so-
lutions, augmented reality (AR), and virtual reality (VR)
to enhance in-store interactions and customer engage-
ment (Kovács & Keresztes, 2024; Yim & Y oon, 2025).
External shocks have further accelerated these devel-
opments. The COVID-19 pandemic, in particular, acted
as a critical catalyst for retail digitalization. Lockdowns
and social distancing measures triggered an abrupt shift
toward online shopping, exposing the vulnerability of re-
tailers lacking digital sales channels, delivery integration,
or contactless payment solutions. Many traditional re-
tailers were therefore compelled to rapidly implement or
expand digital capabilities to ensure business continuity.
The literature identifies the pandemic as a turning point
that compressed years of digital adoption into a short
time span and permanently elevated consumer expecta-
tions for digital convenience and seamless cross-channel
service (Nanda et al., 2021; Sah et al., 2024).
Taken together, these converging forces have reshaped
the retail landscape and given rise to a new type of re -
tail environment characterized by the fusion of physical
and digital elements. The term phygital – derived from
the combination of physical and digital – is increasingly
used in the literature to describe retail contexts where
digital technologies are embedded into physical store
environments to create seamless, interactive, and per -
sonalized experiences (Batat, 2024; Corinaldesi, 2025).
In such environments, CX is no longer confined to ei -
ther physical or digital touchpoints but unfolds through
their continuous interaction. Rather than experiencing
isolated encounters with individual touchpoints, custom-
ers navigate a hybrid experiential space in which digital
interfaces (e.g., mobile applications, self-service technol-
ogies, interactive displays) and physical elements (e.g.,
store layout, products, human interaction) jointly shape
perceptions, emotions, and behaviors.
As a result, CX in phygital retail environments emerg-
es through synergistic interactions of physical and digital
components rather than as the sum of separate channel-
specific experiences. This shift challenges traditional
conceptualizations of CX that assume stable boundaries
between channels or touchpoints. Instead, experience
formation becomes distributed and dynamic, as custom-
ers move fluidly across multiple interaction points within
a single shopping episode. For example, decision-making
may begin with digital information accessed online, con-
tinue through physical product interaction, and conclude
via digitally enabled checkout processes. Consequently,
experiential mechanisms are shaped not only by what
customers interact with, but also by where and how these
interactions are orchestrated within the retail system. CX
thus unfolds through the interplay of digital interfaces
(e.g., mobile apps, smart mirrors, kiosks), physical ele-
ments (e.g., store layout, product displays), human actors
(e.g., sales staff), and supporting backend systems (e.g.,
inventory and payment infrastructure). This reconfigu-
ration challenges traditional channel-based conceptual-
izations of CX. Experience becomes distributed, layered,
and dynamically co-constructed across multiple compo-
nents of the retail system.
Absence of CX operational conceptualization
CX has been extensively conceptualized as a multidimen-
sional construct encompassing cognitive, emotional, be-
havioral, sensory, and relational responses arising from
interactions with a firm (Azad Moghddam & Mortimer,
2025; Lemke et al., 2011; Verhoef et al., 2009). This body
of research has significantly advanced understanding of
what CX and how experiential responses influence sat-
isfaction, loyalty, and engagement outcomes. Moreover,
contemporary perspectives emphasize the processual and
dynamic nature of CX, highlighting that experience un-
folds across multiple touchpoints over time rather than
being confined to single transactions (Verhoef et  al.,
2009). Service ecosystem and value co-creation frame-
works further argue that experience emerges from in-
teractions among customers, organizations, technologies,
and institutional contexts (Ozcan & Ramaswamy, 2025;
Ramaswamy & Ozcan, 2016).
While these contributions provide a robust under -
standing of experiential dimensions and co-creation
mechanisms, they primarily focus on psychological and

relational aspects of experience formation. They offer
limited structural clarity regarding where within com -

shaped, particularly in technologically augmented or
phygital environments.
In parallel, digital retail literature has extensively ex-
amined the adoption and impact of specific technologies
such as mobile applications, self-service technologies,
AI-enabled personalization systems, AR, digital pay-
ment solutions, and omnichannel integration (Vhatkar
et al., 2024; Wichmann & Reinartz, 2024). This stream of
research typically analyzes performance outcomes (e.g.,
efficiency, sales, conversion), technology acceptance fac-
tors, channel integration effects, or specific experiential
outcomes (e.g., convenience, enjoyment).
However, digital retail studies frequently adopt a
technology-centric or channel-based perspective. They
tend to examine discrete digital tools or omnichannel
strategies without systematically distinguishing the oper-
ational layers of the in-store environment in which these
technologies intervene. As a result, although we know
much about individual technologies and their potential
effects, we lack a structured understanding of how differ-
ent digital solutions reshape experience formation across
distinct operational loci.
Recent theoretical perspectives – such as service eco-
systems, customer engagement theory, and dynamic rela-
tionality – support this integrative view by emphasizing
multi-actor and multi-level interactions (Ramaswamy &
Ozcan, 2016; Wang et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2017).
Taken together, three observations emerge:
1. CX literature has extensively developed experien-
tial dimensions (cognitive, emotional, behavioral,
etc.) but provides limited clarity on where within
retail systems these experiences are operationally
shaped.
2. Digital retail literature richly documents technolo-
gies and omnichannel strategies but rarely differ -
entiates the operational domains at which digital
interventions influence experience formation.
3. Phygital retail discussions recognize the fusion
of physical and digital elements but do not sys-
tematically conceptualize the layered operational
structure through which CX is co-created in such
environments.
Consequently, existing research lacks a systematic
conceptualization of operational CX domains in phygital
retail environments. Without such a conceptual struc-
ture, it remains unclear how different digital solutions
exert influence at distinct layers of the in-store environ-
ment, how these layers interact, and how experiential di-
mensions are differentially shaped across them. This gap
not only limits theoretical precision but also constrains
managerial reasoning, as retailers lack a structured
framework for understanding where digital interventions
operate within the in-store shopping journey.
Accordingly, this study advances the concept of op-
erational CX domains as a theoretical artifact designed
to structure and systematize the loci of CX formation in
phygital retail settings.
3. Methodology
This study employed a structured integrative literature
review with a theory-building purpose. The objective was
to systematically identify and conceptualize operational
CX domains within phygital retail environments. Follow-
ing established guidelines for integrative reviews (Snyder,
2019; Torraco, 2005; Watson & Webster, 2020), the study
combined transparent search and screening procedures
with iterative conceptual synthesis. The design incor -
porates key principles of systematic reviews  – explicit
search criteria, predefined inclusion rules, and structured
data extraction – while focusing on theory development
rather than statistical aggregation. Accordingly, the aim
was not to conduct a meta-analysis, but to identify and
organize recurring operational loci of CX formation
across studies. The analytical process involved literature
search, screening, open coding, axial clustering, and
domain validation. A working definition of operational
CX domains guided the screening and coding process.
Domain configurations were refined iteratively through
comparison across studies to ensure conceptual clarity
and empirical grounding. The outcome is a theoretically
grounded conceptual artifact: a structured typology of
operational CX domains in phygital retail settings.
Before conducting the literature analysis, a working
definition of the focal construct was established to guide
inclusion and coding:
An operational CX domain is defined as a distinct
structural locus within retail systems that integrate physi-
cal store operations with digital interfaces, where custom-
er experience formation is activated through identifiable
experiential mechanisms and where managerial inter -
ventions (digital, processual, or human-centered)  can be
meaningfully targeted.
To ensure conceptual rigor and analytical consistency,
candidate focal areas identified in the literature were sub-
jected to a multi-criteria screening procedure. To qualify
as an operational CX domain, a focal area had to satisfy
the criteria as presented in the Table 1.
Only those meeting all predefined criteria were re-
tained as operational CX domains.
The literature search was conducted in Scopus  and
Web of Science Core Collection, which are widely recog-
nized as comprehensive and reliable databases for peer-
reviewed academic research in business, marketing, and
retail studies. The search focused on publications from
2010 to 2026, reflecting the acceleration of omnichan -
nel and phygital retail research during this period. Only
English-language articles published in peer-reviewed
journals were included.
The search queries combined three thematic blocks
(see Table  2). The final query combined these blocks
using Boolean operators (A AND B AND C) in title,
abstract, and keywords. The database search identified

this stage, semantic normalization was applied to merge
conceptually equivalent codes and eliminate redundancy.

5. Discussion
The rapid integration of digital technologies into physi-
cal r etail environments has substantially expanded the
conceptual vocabulary surrounding customer experience
(CX). Across the reviewed literature, CX is predominant-
ly described as a holistic, multi-dimensional construct
emerging throughout the customer journey and across
multiple touchpoints (Gahler et  al., 2023; Mele et  al.,
2021). In phygital retail contexts, this holistic framing is
further reinforced by omnichannel and seamless integra-
tion narratives, which emphasize the fluidity of transi -
tions between physical and digital interfaces (Alexander
& Kent, 2022; Rahman et  al., 2022; Rodríguez-Torrico
et al., 2023).
The reviewed corpus further reveals that phygital
retail research frequently employs touchpoint-based de-
scriptions (Stead et al., 2022; Terblanche & Kidd, 2021),
yet touchpoints are often treated as isolated interaction
nodes rather than embedded within broader operational
domains. Similarly, journey-stage models (Kannan &
Kulkarni, 2022; Mele et al., 2021; Rahman et al., 2022)
describe temporal progression but do not consistently
anchor experience formation within controllable mana-
gerial environments. As a result, digital solutions are fre-
quently discussed at a high level of abstraction, detached
from clearly specified operational intervention spaces.
The corpus analysis provided a structured response to
RQ1 by demonstrating that CX in phygital retail litera-
ture is predominantly conceptualized as journey-based
and touchpoint-oriented, while lacking clear structural
anchoring at the level of operational domains.
However, the findings suggest that the literature
implicitly contains recurring structural regularities  –
patterns of operational environments in which CX is
shaped  – but these patterns remain fragmented across
studies. By systematically extracting and clustering these
loci through iterative pattern-based coding grounded in
the literature corpus, this study advances the conceptu-
alization of CX from a predominantly outcome-centered
and journey-centered perspective toward a structurally
anchored, decision-relevant operational framework.
The nine-domain architecture identified in this study –
spanning physical store environment, navigation and tran-
sition spaces, product engagement, human interaction, in-
store technology interfaces, digital platform environments,
transaction operations, fulfillment and post-purchase ex-
ecution, and value communication mechanisms – emerged
through iterative clustering and cross-validation of extract-
ed loci. Importantly, these domains represent analytically
distinct operational environments rather than experiential
outcomes or technological categories.
Several characteristics underscore the structural ro-
bustness of the identified domains.
First, the domains are anchored in managerially con-
trollable environments. Unlike touchpoints or journey
moments, each domain corresponds to a relatively sta-
ble operational configuration within the retail system.
For instance, store environment and atmospherics have
long been examined in retail design literature as spatial
and sensory structures influencing behavior (e.g., atmos-
pheric and servicescape research traditions), yet these
are rarely integrated with digital augmentation discus-
sions within a unified operational architecture. Similarly,
transaction and fulfillment processes are often analyzed
from logistics or operations perspectives, but their role
as experience-shaping domains within phygital retail has
remained fragmented.
Second, the domains demonstrate functional differen-
tiation. Studies focusing on AR applications, interactive
displays, AI or mobile integration (Grewal et al., 2023;
Pantano et al., 2022; Zimmermann et al., 2023) tend to
cluster around technology-mediated in-store interfaces,
whereas research on seamless channel transitions (Alex-
ander & Kent, 2022; Rahman et  al., 2022) consistently
engages navigation and transition mechanisms. Likewise,
work addressing loyalty programs, promotions, and price
framing (Blom et  al., 2021; Haji et  al., 2025; Pai et  al.,
2025) coalesces around value communication structures.
This patterned clustering across otherwise heterogene-
ous studies suggests that the domains reflect underlying
structural consistencies rather than arbitrary grouping.
Third, the architecture captures the dual anchoring
of phygital retail: it preserves the primacy of the physical
store environment while recognizing digitally extended
interfaces as structurally embedded rather than external-
ly appended. Digital platforms and in-store technologies
do not constitute isolated innovation layers; rather, they
function as domain-specific augmentation mechanisms
operating within distinct operational contexts.
In this respect, the proposed architecture differs from
purely journey-based models, which organize experience
temporally, and from channel-based frameworks, which
organize experience by medium. Instead, the nine do-
mains represent spatially and operationally grounded
loci where managerial interventions – particularly digi-
tal solutions – can be targeted with greater precision. By
stabilizing these domains, the study provides an answer
to RQ2: operational CX in phygital retail environments
can be systematically decomposed into analytically dis-
tinct, managerially controllable domains that transcend
individual technologies and specific journey stages. This
structural decomposition forms the basis for more dif-
ferentiated theorizing of experience mechanisms and
managerial decision-making.
While the nine operational CX domains represent
structurally distinct loci within phygital retail environ-
ments, they are not homogeneous in the mechanisms
through which experience is formed. A key insight
emerging from the analysis is that each domain activates
different dominant experiential mechanisms, implying
differentiated pathways through which digital solutions
influence customer perceptions and behaviors.
The study findings suggest that experiential mecha -
nisms are domain-contingent and structurally condi-
tioned.

Physical Store Environment &  Atmospherics domain
(A1) primarily activates sensory–affective mechanisms.
Spatial layout, ambient conditions, visual design, and
digitally enhanced environmental cues influence immer-
sion, mood, and perceived quality. Digital augmentation
here (e.g., dynamic displays, responsive lighting, interac-
tive projections) operates through environmental modu-
lation rather than information processing.
In Navigation, Wayfinding & Transition Spaces domain
(A2) experience formation is predominantly governed
by cognitive–flow mechanisms. Omnichannel synchro -
nization, mobile-assisted navigation, and cross-channel
coordination reduce cognitive load and transition fric-
tion. Digital solutions enhance perceived seamlessness
by structuring movement and minimizing uncertainty
across touchpoints.
Product Information , Engagement &  Evaluation do -
main (A3) engages cognitive–diagnostic and interac-
tive mechanisms. Rich product information, augmented
reality trials, comparison tools, and interactive displays
increase perceived control, transparency, and diagnostic-
ity. Here, digital tools shape experience through informa-
tional empowerment and active engagement.
In Human Service Encounters &  Social Interaction
domain (A4) major mechanisms are social–relational
and affective. Empathy, responsiveness, authenticity,
and relational trust shape experience formation. Digital
augmentation (e.g., clienteling systems, remote expert
consultations) restructures rather than replaces interper-
sonal dynamics, mediating the intensity and continuity
of relational engagement.
Tech-mediated In-store Interfaces domain ( A5) is
analytically distinct. It encompasses self-service kiosks,
smart mirrors, interactive terminals, mobile payment in-
terfaces, in-store apps, and other customer-facing digital
infrastructures embedded within the physical store. The
dominant experiential mechanisms here are mediation
and autonomy regulation. Unlike A3 (product evalua-
tion), which focuses on information and trial, A5 centers
on interaction with technological agents themselves. The
experience is shaped by perceived usability, system re-
sponsiveness, control, and technological fluency. Mecha-
nisms such as perceived ease-of-use, interface transpar -
ency, and technological trust become salient (echoing
but extending beyond traditional technology adoption
logics). Importantly, A5 represents a shift from human-
mediated to system-mediated interaction within the
physical environment. The experiential impact is there-
fore influenced by interface design, responsiveness, error
tolerance, perceived control and agency.
This domain clarifies that digital in-store technolo-
gies are not merely extensions of product information or
environment design; they constitute a structurally dis -
tinct mediation layer within the store ecosystem.
Digital Platforms &  Remote Interfaces domain ( A6)
represents digitally extended environments that operate
beyond the immediate physical store while remaining
structurally integrated into the retail system. It includes
retailer websites, mobile apps, remote consultation tools,
and omnichannel coordination platforms. The dominant
mechanisms here are continuity, integration, and expec-
tation alignment. Unlike A5, which is spatially embed-
ded within the store, A6 extends the retail experience
temporally and spatially. It shapes experience through
cross-channel coherence, information consistency, syn-
chronization of accounts, preferences, and purchase his-
tory, remote accessibility. Experience formation in this
domain depends heavily on perceived ecosystem align-
ment rather than localized interaction quality. Seam-
lessness across platforms influences trust and perceived
organizational competence. Crucially, A6 serves as a
structural bridge between pre-purchase, in-store, and
post-purchase processes. Its experiential logic is systemic
rather than situational.
A closer examination of Transaction & Checkout Op-
erations (A7), Fulfillment, Delivery &  Returns Execution
(8), and Value Communication , Promotions &  Loyalty
Programs (A9) reveals that these domains differ structur-
ally and mechanistically from the experiential construc-
tion domains (A1–A4). While A1–A4 primarily shape
pre-decisional and in-situ experiential construction
through sensory, cognitive, and relational mechanisms,
A7–A9 govern the realization, validation, and evaluation
of exchange. They operate at the level of contractual ex-
ecution and economic meaning rather than atmospheric
engagement or informational exploration.
The transaction and checkout domain (A7) represents
the formalization of economic commitment. Unlike
product evaluation (A3), which concerns decision for -
mation, A7 concerns decision execution under exchange
logic. Experience formation in this domain is governed
primarily by procedural efficiency, reliability, and per -
ceived fairness. Speed, queue management, transparency
of charges, payment accuracy, and system stability influ-
ence customer satisfaction through procedural justice
mechanisms. Here, digital solutions – such as self-check-
out systems, mobile payment interfaces, and contactless
technologies – do not primarily enhance immersion or
relational warmth. Instead, they shape experience by
stabilizing the execution of exchange, reducing temporal
friction, and minimizing perceived risk. This domain is
therefore analytically distinct from both environmental
(A1) and relational (A4) domains. Its experiential logic is
transactional and contract-finalizing rather than atmos-
pheric or social. The customer’s evaluation hinges less on
emotional engagement and more on reliability, transpar-
ency, and operational competence.
Fulfillment, Delivery, and Returns Execution domain
(A8) extends the exchange beyond the point of sale. It
governs the realization of promises embedded in the
transaction. Experience formation in this domain is
dominated by expectation confirmation and recovery
justice mechanisms. Order accuracy, delivery timeliness,
inventory synchronization, return transparency, and re-
fund responsiveness influence perceived organizational
trustworthiness. Digital solutions such as order-tracking

systems, automated lockers, and online return portals

Value Communication, Promotions, and Loyalty Pro-

than the logistical execution of exchange. By shaping
how economic outcomes are interpreted, it plays a dis -

This mechanism-level differentiation provides a di-
rect response to RQ3 by demonstrating that operational
CX domains differ not only structurally but also in the
dominant experiential logics through which digital so-

tomer perception and managerial evaluation criteria.
Overall, the findings demonstrate that CX in phygital
retail cannot be sufficiently understood through journey-
stage or touchpoint-based models alone. While the lit-
erature predominantly conceptualizes CX as holistic and
omnichannel, it lacks clear structural anchoring at the
level of operational environments. By identifying nine
analytically distinct operational CX domains and differ -
entiating them according to their dominant experiential
mechanisms, this study provides a structured answer to
how CX is formed and where digital solutions exert in-
fluence. The resulting domain-based framework offers a
theoretically grounded foundation for evaluating digital
initiatives based on the specific operational contexts and
mechanisms they activate, rather than on abstract no-
tions of seamlessness or technological advancement.
6. Conclusions
This study addresses an important scientific problem in
contemporary CX research within digitally transforming
retail environments. While the literature has extensively
examined experiential outcomes, omnichannel journeys,
and individual retail technologies, it lacks a systematic
conceptualization of where within retail systems custom-
er experience is operationally formed and where mana-
gerial interventions can be targeted. Existing research
tends to conceptualize CX either as a holistic psychologi-
cal construct or as the outcome of specific technologies,
leaving the structural loci of experience formation in-
sufficiently theorized. This conceptual gap is particularly
salient in phygital retail environments, where physical
and digital elements are tightly integrated. To address
this problem, the present study systematically identifies
and structures operational CX domains in phygital re-
tail environments and develops a domain-based concep-
tual framework that clarifies the structural architecture
through which experience formation occurs.
Drawing on a corpus of 166 peer-reviewed stud-
ies, the analysis confirmed that CX in phygital retail is
shaped across nine structurally differentiated domains:
1) Physical Store Environment & Atmospherics,
2) Navigation, Wayfinding & Transition Spaces,
3) Product Information, Engagement & Evaluation,
4) Human Service Encounters & Social Interaction,
5) Tech-mediated In-store Interfaces,
6) Digital Platforms & Remote Interfaces,
7) Transaction & Checkout Operations,
8) Fulfillment, Delivery & Returns Execution, and
9) Value Communication, Promotions & Loyalty
Programs.
Through a multi-stage validation procedure, these
domains were required to satisfy structural qualification
criteria (structural anchoring and experiential activation
linkage) and operational validation criteria (managerial
targetability, conceptual distinctiveness, and cross-study
support). Convergence across datasets and cross-study
reinforcement confirmed that these domains represent
stable architectural components of CX formation rather
than incidental thematic clusters.
The theoretical implications and novelty of this re-
search lies in four principal theoretical contributions to
CX theory. First, it introduces structural clarification  by
distinguishing operational CX domains from experiential
outcomes and from abstract experiential mechanisms.
Whereas prior literature often conflates where experi-
ence is formed with how it is psychologically processed
or how it is measured, this study repositions CX analy-
sis at the structural locus level. Second, it demonstrates
structural stabilization  through inductive convergence
across datasets. Rather than deriving domains from a
single conceptual stream or technological category, the
study shows that stable operational loci emerge across
diverse research traditions within phygital retail schol-
arship. Third, it establishes experiential mechanism dif-
ferentiation, revealing that distinct operational domains
activate different dominant experiential mechanisms.
This differentiation challenges implicit assumptions that
digital solutions uniformly enhance experience; instead,
their effects are domain-contingent. Fourth, the study
introduces an operational targeting logic, linking do-
main architecture to digital intervention precision. By

identifying where experience is structurally shaped, the

The findings have direct implications for managerial
decision-making in phygital retail environments. First,
the domain architecture provides a diagnostic framework
enabling managers to identify which structural locus of
the retail system is being modified by a given digital in-
tervention. This reduces the risk of technology-driven
decision-making detached from experiential objectives.
Second, the differentiation of domains highlights the
need for inter-domain alignment. Investments in tech-
mediated interfaces, for example, cannot compensate for
deficiencies in human service encounters or fulfillment
execution. CX coherence depends on architectural con-
sistency across domains. Third, the model supports the
development of a more systematic digital strategy by en-
couraging retailers to map existing and planned digital
initiatives against the nine domains, thereby detecting
redundancy, fragmentation, or structural blind spots. In
this way, the study contributes to shifting managerial log-
ic from isolated technology adoption toward structured
experiential orchestration, ultimately supporting the
development of more coherent and customer-centered
retail ecosystems.
Several limitations should be acknowledged. First,
the study relied on Scopus  and Web of Science-indexed
sources. Although these databases capture a substan-
tial proportion of high-quality retail research, relevant
insights from practitioner outlets, emerging conference
streams, or regionally indexed journals may not be fully
represented. Second, the focus on phygital retail environ-
ments imposes contextual boundaries. The architecture
may require adaptation in pure online retail, service-
dominant ecosystems, or platform-based marketplaces.
Third, while the coding procedure employed explicit
qualification and validation criteria to enhance analytical
rigor, qualitative clustering and architectural stabilization
inevitably involve interpretive judgment. Although cross-
study convergence mitigates subjectivity, alternative clas-
sifications may be theoretically plausible.
The operational CX domain architecture opens sev-
eral avenues for further investigation:
 – Future research should operationalize the nine do-
mains into measurable constructs and test their
structural validity using confirmatory modeling ap-
proaches. Such work would enable empirical assess-
ment of how domain-level investments influence
CX outcomes.
 – The current study identifies domains as structurally
distinct; however, CX coherence likely depends on
interactions between them. Future research could
examine complementarity, substitution, and ampli-
fication effects across domains, particularly under
conditions of digital integration.
 – Future research should develop structured decision-
support frameworks that require managers to (1)
identify the primary operational CX domain tar -
geted by a digital initiative, (2) assess the dominant
experiential mechanisms activated within that do-
main, (3) evaluate alignment effects across adjacent
domains, and (4) estimate the projected impact on
overall CX coherence. Such frameworks could re-
duce fragmentation and enhance strategic consist-
ency in digital transformation efforts.
 – As AI becomes embedded in phygital retail settings,
future studies should examine whether AI acts as a
reinforcing mechanism within existing domains, or
a cross-domain integrator that reshapes the archi-
tecture itself.
